Summary

In this acrostic psalm of praise, David vows to extol God as his King every day, declaring that his greatness is unsearchable (vv. 1-3). He anticipates one generation telling the next about God's mighty and glorious works (vv. 4-7), and describes the LORD as gracious, compassionate, slow to anger, and good to all he has made (vv. 8-9). David praises God's everlasting kingdom and his care for those who are falling or bowed down, providing food to every living thing in its season (vv. 10-16). He closes by praising the LORD's righteousness and nearness to all who call on him in truth, promising to fulfill the desires of those who fear him while preserving those who love him (vv. 17-21).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Acrostic Hymn of Praise (The last Davidic Psalm) — begins the Final Hallelujah sequence (145-150)
Attributed Author: David (superscription: "David's Psalm of praise")
Key Themes: God's greatness beyond comprehension, His kingdom that endures, God's compassion to all creation, His faithfulness and nearness to all who call on Him, declaring God's praise to all generations
Historical Context: Psalm 145 is the last psalm attributed to David and the only one called "tehillah" (praise) in its superscription — giving its name to the entire book (Tehillim in Hebrew). It is an acrostic (each verse begins with successive Hebrew letters) and was considered so important that the Talmud states: "Everyone who recites Psalm 145 three times daily is assured a place in the world to come." It is David's final, comprehensive declaration of who God is — His greatness (v.3), His compassion (v.8-9), His kingdom (vv.11-13), His providence (vv.15-16), His nearness (v.18), and His preservation (v.20). This psalm launches the final Hallelujah sequence (145-150) that concludes the entire Psalter.
His Greatness Is Unsearchable (vv.1-3): I will extol thee, my God, O king; and I will bless thy name for ever and ever. The last psalm in the psalter attributed to David, and an acrostic running the Hebrew alphabet a line at a time. It is the core of the Ashrei, said three times daily in Jewish prayer. Every day will I bless thee. Then a claim that limits everything the psalm goes on to say, and his greatness is unsearchable, so the twenty verses following are offered as a report from a subject that cannot be finished.
One Generation Shall Praise Thy Works to Another (vv.4-7): One generation shall praise thy works to another, and shall declare thy mighty acts. Praise is described as a chain of transmission rather than an emotion, and the psalm keeps alternating between what I will say and what they shall say. And men shall speak of the might of thy terrible acts, where terrible keeps its older sense of awe-inspiring. They shall abundantly utter the memory of thy great goodness.
His Tender Mercies Are over All His Works (vv.8-9): The LORD is gracious, and full of compassion; slow to anger, and of great mercy. Exodus 34:6 again, the sentence the Old Testament quotes about God more than any other. Then the psalm pushes it further than most of its uses go, the LORD is good to all: and his tender mercies are over all his works, which extends the kindness past Israel to everything made.
Thy Kingdom Is an Everlasting Kingdom (vv.10-13): All thy works shall praise thee, O LORD; and thy saints shall bless thee. The subject for four verses is the kingdom, and what is said of it is duration, thy kingdom is an everlasting kingdom, and thy dominion endureth throughout all generations. Something is missing here. The acrostic goes from mem at verse 13 to samekh at verse 14 and skips nun, and a line beginning with that letter, faithful is the LORD in all his words, appears in the Septuagint and in the Dead Sea scroll of the psalms. English Bibles differ over whether to print it, some giving it in the text and some in a note, so two readers comparing versions at this verse will not find the same number of lines. The Hebrew tradition preserved the gap and did not fill it.
Thou Openest Thine Hand (vv.14-16): The LORD upholdeth all that fall, and raiseth up all those that be bowed down. After four verses on an everlasting kingdom the psalm turns to people who cannot stand up, which is the sequence it wants. The eyes of all wait upon thee; and thou givest them their meat in due season. Then a line Psalm 104 also uses of the animals, thou openest thine hand, and satisfiest the desire of every living thing, so the feeding is not confined to the congregation.
The LORD Is Nigh unto All Them That Call upon Him (vv.17-20): The LORD is righteous in all his ways, and holy in all his works. What follows is the psalm's warmest claim and it comes with a qualification attached, the LORD is nigh unto all them that call upon him, to all that call upon him in truth. In truth rules out the calling that is only noise, and it is the one condition the psalm sets. He will fulfil the desire of them that fear him. The last line is the psalm's only hard sentence and it is not softened, the LORD preserveth all them that love him: but all the wicked will he destroy.
Let All Flesh Bless His Holy Name (v.21): My mouth shall speak the praise of the LORD: and let all flesh bless his holy name for ever and ever. The acrostic ends where it began, with one man's mouth, and then hands the sentence to everything alive. All flesh is the widest term available, and this last verse of the last Davidic psalm sets up the five hallelujah psalms that close the book by making the congregation the whole of creation.

Deep Dive

Setting: Psalm 145 is the last psalm in the Psalter with David's name on it, and it closes his final collection (Psalms 138–145). The four laments just before it (140–144) were cries for rescue. This one is pure praise, and it opens the door to the five "Hallelujah" psalms (146–150) that end the book. Its heading, "A Song of Praise. Of David," is unique: it is the only psalm titled tehillah, "praise," the singular of tehillim, the Hebrew name for the whole book of Psalms. It is also an acrostic: each verse begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, from aleph to tav, like an A-to-Z of praise. It is the last of the alphabetic psalms (9–10, 25, 34, 37, 111, 112, 119, 145). One letter, nun, is missing from the standard Hebrew text, and the ESV prints the missing line in brackets after v13 (see below). The psalm has long been prized in Jewish worship. A saying in the Talmud holds that whoever recites it three times a day has a share in the world to come, and it is still prayed daily in the synagogue, introduced by the last line of Psalm 144 ("Blessed are the people whose God is the LORD"). The Tyndale notes trace two themes woven through it: God as King of all (vv1–3, 10–13), and God's care for all he has made (vv14–20). The word "all" runs through it again and again. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanzas as the ESV spaces them.

I Will Extol You, My God and King (vv1–3)

  • "I will extol you, my God and King" (v1). "Extol" ('aromimkha) means to lift high, to exalt. The great king of Israel calls God "King," and in the Hebrew it is "the King," with the definite article. David knew he ruled under a higher throne. JFB: "God is addressed as king, alluding to His government of men." The Tyndale notes connect it to Psalm 47:2, where God is the great King over all the earth. Kingship is the psalm's theme (vv11–13).
  • "And bless your name forever and ever" (v1). To "bless" God (barak) is to praise him, to speak well of him with grateful reverence. God's "name" is God as he has made himself known. "Forever and ever" appears in vv1, 2 and 21, bracketing the psalm. Praise that starts in this life is meant to go on without end.
  • "Every day I will bless you" (v2). Forever starts today. The resolve is daily and ordinary, not only for special occasions. Henry says those who "abound in fervent prayer" will in time abound in grateful praise, which is "the true language of holy joy."
  • "Great is the LORD, and greatly to be praised" (v3). This line also opens Psalm 48:1 and appears in Psalm 96:4 and 1 Chronicles 16:25. Praise should match its object, and God's greatness is great.
  • "His greatness is unsearchable" (v3). Literally "there is no searching out" ('en cheqer) of it. Isaiah 40:28 says the same of God's understanding, and Job 5:9 of his wonders. Paul ends his great argument about God's plan with the same awe: "How unsearchable are his judgments" (Rom 11:33). No one will ever reach the end of knowing God, which is why praise can go on "forever and ever."
  • Commentators: Guzik counts David's four resolutions in vv1–2 (extol, bless, bless every day, praise) and passes on Spurgeon's remark that a firm resolve is wise when it is a resolve to praise God. He also cites Morgan on the move from "my God" to "the LORD," God's covenant name. JFB adds that God's greatness is known "as displayed in His works," which leads straight into the next stanza.

One Generation Shall Commend Your Works (vv4–7)

  • "One generation shall commend your works to another" (v4). "Commend" (yeshabbach) means to praise, to laud. Faith is handed down. Parents tell children, and children tell their children (Deut 6:6–7; Ps 78:4–7). The Tyndale notes connect it with Psalm 22:30, which foresees the Lord being made known to the coming generation. Every generation that hears the story adds its own chapters, as Spurgeon puts it through Guzik.
  • "Your mighty acts" (v4). The gevurot are God's acts of power, especially the great saving acts of Israel's history: the exodus, the sea, the conquest.
  • "On the glorious splendor of your majesty… I will meditate" (v5). Three words are piled up: glory, splendor, majesty. Language strains to describe God. "Meditate" ('asichah) is the same root as the "complaint" or musing of Psalm 142:2. The mind that once circled anxiously around its troubles now circles around God's glory. Note the pattern in vv4–7: "they" speak, "I" meditate, "they" speak, "I" declare. Public praise and private reflection feed each other.
  • "They shall speak of the might of your awesome deeds" (v6). "Awesome" (nora'ot) means fear-inspiring, deeds that make people tremble. JFB: deeds "which produce dread or fear."
  • "They shall pour forth the fame of your abundant goodness" (v7). "Pour forth" (yabbi'u) means to bubble up, to gush like a spring. Proverbs 18:4 uses it of "a bubbling brook." The picture is of praise that cannot be held in. "Fame" is literally "remembrance," the memory of God's goodness kept alive by retelling.
  • "And shall sing aloud of your righteousness" (v7). Here goodness and righteousness stand side by side. God's righteousness is not a threat to his people but a reason to sing. He always does what is right, and that includes keeping his promises. JFB reads it as goodness "according to covenant engagement."
  • Commentators: Guzik passes on Poole's point that the heaped-up words for God's glory in v5 show that no language is equal to the subject, and Trapp's urging that each generation pass on what God has done so that his praise does not die out. Henry finds God's greatness and justice shown most clearly not in creation but in redemption, in the cross of Christ.

Gracious and Merciful (vv8–9)

  • "The LORD is gracious and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love" (v8). This is God's own description of himself to Moses on Mount Sinai, after the golden calf: "The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Exod 34:6). It is the most quoted verse about God's character in the Old Testament (Num 14:18; Neh 9:17; Ps 86:15; 103:8; Joel 2:13; Jonah 4:2). "Gracious" (channun) means showing favor that is not earned. "Merciful" (rachum) is related to rechem, "womb," and carries the tender compassion a mother feels for her child (Isa 49:15). "Slow to anger" is literally "long of nostrils": it takes a long time for his anger to flare. "Steadfast love" (chesed) is his loyal covenant love.
  • The chosen words. After the extravagant language of majesty and awesome deeds (vv5–6), the heart of God's glory turns out to be his kindness. Maclaren, as Guzik reports, says God's moral qualities, not his power, are the core of his glory. Moses asked to see God's glory, and God answered by proclaiming his name and his mercy (Exod 33:18–19; 34:6).
  • "The LORD is good to all, and his mercy is over all that he has made" (v9). God's goodness is not only for Israel. He gives sun and rain to all (Matt 5:45; Acts 14:17). Theologians call this "common grace," God's kindness to every creature, believer or not. "His mercy is over all" pictures his compassion spread out over creation like a covering. The Tyndale notes link it to Psalm 136:25, where God gives food to all flesh.
  • Commentators: Henry sees Christ as the fullest expression of this goodness and mercy, a merciful High Priest who came to save sinners. JFB groups vv8–9 together and says God's mercy "rests on all His works." Guzik names this common grace and passes on Horne's note on the womb-related word for mercy.

Your Kingdom Is an Everlasting Kingdom (vv10–13)

  • "All your works shall give thanks to you, O LORD, and all your saints shall bless you!" (v10). Two circles of praise. The wider circle is all creation, which praises God simply by being what he made it (Ps 19:1; 148). The inner circle is "your saints" (chasidekha), "your faithful ones," those who have received God's chesed (v8) and answer with loyal love. Creation thanks him silently. The saints put it into words. JFB says to "bless" is "to praise with reverence, more than merely to praise."
  • "They shall speak of the glory of your kingdom and tell of your power" (v11). In the Hebrew, vv11–13 form a little acrostic within the acrostic. The letters for vv11, 12 and 13 are kaf, lamed, mem. Read backwards, as mem-lamed-kaf, they spell melekh, "king." Many scholars think this is deliberate, a hidden signature at the center of the psalm. Whether intended or not, "kingdom" is the word that dominates these verses, used four times.
  • "To make known to the children of man your mighty deeds" (v12). The saints do not keep their knowledge of God to themselves. Their speech has a purpose: that all people, "the children of man," come to know the King. This is mission in the Psalms (Ps 67:2; 96:3).
  • "Your kingdom is an everlasting kingdom, and your dominion endures throughout all generations" (v13). Earthly kingdoms rise and fall. David's own kingdom would split within a generation of his death. God's never ends. Nebuchadnezzar, humbled, came to the same confession: "his kingdom is an everlasting kingdom" (Dan 4:3, 34). Meyer, as Guzik reports, mentions an inscription with this verse over the door of an old church in Damascus that later became a mosque.
  • The missing nun line (v13). In the standard Hebrew text, each verse begins with the next letter, but there is no verse for nun (N), which should come between mem (v13) and samekh (v14). The ESV prints the line in brackets: "The LORD is faithful in all his words and kind in all his works." It comes from one Hebrew manuscript, the Greek Septuagint and the Syriac, and a Dead Sea Scroll of the Psalms from Qumran (11QPsa) has it too, beginning with the Hebrew word ne'eman, "faithful," which starts with nun. Most scholars think a scribe's eye skipped it early in copying, and that the scrolls preserve the original. Some think it was added later to fill the gap, since its second half matches v17 exactly. Either way, nothing taught by the line is new: God's faithfulness and kindness are affirmed throughout Scripture.
  • Commentators: Guzik passes on Maclaren's point that those who receive grace become its messengers, and Spurgeon's complaint that Christians talk too little about their King. He cites Kidner on the missing nun verse and its recovery from the ancient translations and the Dead Sea Scrolls. JFB says the speaking of v11 "is for the extension of His knowledge and perfections in the world," and gives only cross-references (Dan 4:3, 34) on v13.

The LORD Upholds All Who Are Falling (vv14–20)

  • "The LORD upholds all who are falling and raises up all who are bowed down" (v14). The great King's power is shown in how he treats the weak. "Upholds" (somekh) means supports, holds up, as a hand under the arm of someone stumbling (Ps 37:24). "Bowed down" (kefufim) is used of people bent double under a heavy load. Psalm 146:8 repeats the line. Earthly kings care about the strong and useful. This King stoops to lift the fallen.
  • "The eyes of all look to you, and you give them their food in due season" (v15). Picture animals looking up to the one who feeds them (Ps 104:27–28, nearly the same words). "In due season" means at the right time, as each creature needs it. Verses 15–16 have long been used as a prayer before meals. Luther's Small Catechism gives them as the table blessing.
  • "You open your hand; you satisfy the desire of every living thing" (v16). Feeding the whole world is as easy for God as opening a hand. "Satisfy" means to fill full. JFB reads v15 as looking "with expecting faith." Jesus taught the same: the Father feeds the birds, so his children need not be anxious (Matt 6:26), and he taught them to ask, "Give us this day our daily bread" (Matt 6:11).
  • "The LORD is righteous in all his ways and kind in all his works" (v17). "Kind" (chasid) is the adjective from chesed: loyally loving. God is never one without the other: never righteous at the cost of love, never loving at the cost of what is right. Paul says the cross shows God to be both "just and the justifier" (Rom 3:26).
  • "The LORD is near to all who call on him, to all who call on him in truth" (v18). God is everywhere, but he is near in a special way to those who pray to him (Deut 4:7; Ps 34:18; Jas 4:8). "In truth" (be'emet) means sincerely, honestly, not with mere words. Jesus said the Father seeks those who worship "in spirit and truth" (John 4:23–24).
  • "He fulfills the desire of those who fear him; he also hears their cry and saves them" (v19). The circle narrows. Verse 16 promised to satisfy the desire of every living thing. Verse 19 promises more to "those who fear him": he hears their cry and saves them. Those who fear God want what he wants, and he gives it (Ps 37:4; 1 John 5:14).
  • "The LORD preserves all who love him, but all the wicked he will destroy" (v20). "Preserves" (shomer) means guards, watches over, the word of Psalm 121 ("he who keeps you"). The Tyndale notes say those who fear God are those who love him, take refuge in him and seek him as their only Savior. The verse is honest about the other side: the King's kindness is not indifference to evil. Those who refuse him will face him as Judge. Kidner, as Guzik reports, warns that the word means watches over, not a promise that nothing hard will ever happen.
  • Commentators: Henry says God is near to his people in a special way, dwelling in their hearts by faith, and that his destruction of the wicked is part of his righteousness. JFB gives only cross-references on vv14 and 18–19. Guzik passes on Maclaren's observation that the word all comes again and again through these verses, and notes Dickson's warning, through Spurgeon, that calling on God in truth rules out false worship.

My Mouth Will Speak the Praise of the LORD (v21)

  • "My mouth will speak the praise of the LORD" (v21). The psalm returns to where it started: David's personal resolve (vv1–2). The verse begins with tav, the last letter of the Hebrew alphabet. David has used every letter to praise God. Boice, as Guzik reports, calls this verse David's last will and testament: the final words of the last psalm bearing his name are a vow to praise.
  • "And let all flesh bless his holy name forever and ever" (v21). "All flesh" means every living creature, especially every human being. David's private praise becomes a call to the whole world. This sets up the next five psalms, each of which begins and ends with "Praise the LORD!" (Hallelu Yah), and the very last verse of the book: "Let everything that has breath praise the LORD!" (150:6).
  • Commentators: JFB says all flesh is invited to the praise "in which the pious will ever delight to join." Henry reads the verse as the psalmist's closing resolve to praise God himself, joined to a call for all people to bless his holy name.

Pointing to Christ

Psalm 145 praises a King who is gracious and merciful, near to all who call on him, who lifts the fallen and opens his hand to feed the hungry. In Jesus that King stepped into his own world. He announced, "The kingdom of God is at hand" (Mark 1:15). He raised up those bowed down, literally, when he healed a woman bent double for eighteen years (Luke 13:11–13). He took bread, looked up to heaven, gave thanks, and fed five thousand from his open hands (Mark 6:41–44). He came near, so near that God took on flesh. And in him the gracious and merciful God of Exodus 34 is seen most clearly: "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14). His kingdom is the everlasting kingdom of v13, and one day every creature in heaven and on earth will bless his name forever and ever (Rev 5:13).

Reflection

  • Praise Without End (vv.1-3) — David commits to extolling God every single day, declaring His greatness as ultimately unsearchable. Is your praise a daily discipline, or something you only reach for in extraordinary moments?
  • Passing Praise to the Next Generation (vv.4-7) — David expects one generation to commend God's works to the next, carrying the testimony forward in time. Who in the next generation is hearing about God's works from you specifically?
  • Compassion Extended to All (vv.8-9) — God's tender mercies are said to reach over all He has made, not just a select few. Do you extend that same generous view of God's compassion to people outside your own circle?
  • A Kingdom That Will Never Fall (vv.10-13) — David describes God's kingdom as everlasting, standing through every generation regardless of what earthly kingdoms rise and fall. When the news feels unstable, does the permanence of God's kingdom actually steady you?
  • An Open Hand for Every Living Thing (vv.14-16) — God upholds those who fall and opens His hand to satisfy the desire of every living thing. Where do you need to trust that open hand instead of trying to provide for yourself entirely on your own?
  • Near to Everyone Who Calls (vv.17-20) — The psalm promises that the Lord is near to all who call on Him in truth, without needing a priest or special system to gain access. Do you actually call on Him with that kind of confidence, or do you feel like you need to earn the right to approach?
