Summary

This chapter continues Israel's case law with regulations on theft and restitution, requiring thieves to repay what they stole, often multiple times over (vv. 1-15). It then addresses seduction, moves to capital offenses such as sorcery, bestiality, and sacrificing to other gods, and commands compassionate treatment of foreigners, widows, and orphans, warning that God Himself will hear their cry if they are mistreated (vv. 16-24). Further laws forbid charging interest on loans to the poor and require a borrowed cloak taken in pledge to be returned by sunset, and the chapter closes with instructions on offering the firstfruits and firstborn to God and a call to be a holy people (vv. 25-31).

Authorship & Background

Author: Moses (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 22 continues the Book of the Covenant with laws covering property crimes, social responsibility, and moral/religious duties. The chapter moves from theft and restitution (vv.1-15) to sexual ethics and capital religious offenses (vv.16-20) to the protection of the vulnerable (vv.21-27) and duties to God (vv.28-31). These laws reveal God's character: He cares about property rights, sexual purity, the vulnerable (strangers, widows, orphans), and economic justice (no usury against the poor). The chapter ends with the call to holiness: "ye shall be holy men unto me" (v.31).
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: restitution scaled to the animal and its recoverability, four-fold for a sheep and five-fold for an ox already sold, capital penalties reserved for sorcery, bestiality and sacrifice to other gods, a repeated appeal to Israel's own memory of being strangers as the reason to treat strangers well, a widow's or orphan's cry promised a direct hearing from God himself, and a cloak taken in pledge required back by sunset because it is the only covering its owner has
Laws of Theft and Restitution (vv.1-6): Stealing an ox: restore five-fold; a sheep: four-fold (v.1). A thief killed during a nighttime break-in: no guilt; killed in daylight: guilt (vv.2-3). Stolen animal found alive: double restitution (v.4). Damage to fields by livestock or fire: full restitution from the best of one's own property (vv.5-6).
Laws of Trust and Custody (vv.7-15): Property entrusted and stolen: the thief pays double; if no thief found, the custodian swears before God (vv.7-9). Animals in custody that die or are injured: an oath settles it (vv.10-11). If stolen: the custodian pays (v.12). If torn by beasts: evidence suffices (v.13). Borrowed items damaged: full restitution unless the owner was present (vv.14-15).
Sexual and Religious Laws (vv.16-20): Seduction of an unbetrothed virgin: the man must pay the bride-price and marry her (vv.16-17). Three capital offenses: sorcery (v.18), bestiality (v.19), and sacrificing to other gods (v.20).
Protection of the Vulnerable (vv.21-27): Do not oppress strangers — "ye were strangers in the land of Egypt" (v.21). Do not afflict widows or orphans — God will hear their cry and His wrath will burn (vv.22-24). No usury/interest on loans to the poor (v.25). Return a poor man's cloak by sunset — "for I am gracious" (vv.26-27).
Duties to God (vv.28-31): Do not revile God or curse rulers (v.28). Do not delay firstfruits or firstborn offerings (vv.29-30). "Ye shall be holy men unto me" — do not eat flesh torn by beasts (v.31).

Setting: Israel is camped at Mount Sinai, and Moses is still in the thick darkness on the mountain receiving the "Book of the Covenant" (20:22–23:33; 24:7), the case laws that apply the Ten Commandments to everyday life. Chapter 21 dealt with slaves, violence and injuries. Chapter 22 carries on with property and theft, applying "You shall not steal" (20:15), and then moves to a mix of laws about sexual conduct, false worship, and care for the poor and powerless. The two halves belong together. A people who had just been rescued from forced labor and poverty were to build a society where property was respected, the weak were protected, and God was honored in all of it. (In Hebrew Bibles the chapter starts one verse later, so English 22:1 is Hebrew 21:37, and every verse number here is one higher than in Hebrew.)

Laws About Restitution (vv1–15)

  • Restitution, not prison. Israel's law had no prisons for punishment. When someone stole or damaged property, he paid it back, often several times over. The victim was made whole, the thief paid a real cost, and he stayed in the community where he could work. Zacchaeus knew this law well when he promised to repay anyone he had cheated "fourfold" (Luke 19:8).
  • "He shall repay five oxen for an ox, and four sheep for a sheep" (v1). If the thief had already killed or sold the animal, the penalty was higher, because he had gone further in his crime and the animal could not be returned. An ox was worth more as a work animal, trained to plow, so the penalty for it was higher still. When the prophet Nathan told David about a rich man who stole a poor man's lamb, David said the man "shall restore the lamb fourfold" (2 Sam 12:6), quoting this law.
  • "If a thief is found breaking in and is struck so that he dies, there shall be no bloodguilt for him" (v2). "Breaking in" is literally "digging through." Houses were often built of mud brick, and a burglar could tunnel through a wall (compare Matt 6:19, where thieves "break in," literally "dig through"). At night, the homeowner could not see whether the intruder was armed or what he intended, so killing him in defense was not murder.
  • "But if the sun has risen on him, there shall be bloodguilt for him" (v3). In daylight, the homeowner could see the thief, recognize him, call for help, or stop him without killing. Then killing him would be murder. Even a thief's life has value. The law draws a careful line between defending one's household and taking revenge.
  • "He shall surely pay. If he has nothing, then he shall be sold for his theft" (v3). A thief who could not repay worked off the debt as a servant, under the time limits of 21:2. The money went to the victim, not the state.
  • "If the stolen beast is found alive in his possession… he shall pay double" (v4). Double means he returns what he took and pays its value again. He loses exactly what he hoped to gain.
  • "If a man… lets his beast loose and it feeds in another man's field" (v5). Grazing animals on someone else's crops was a common source of conflict between farmers and herdsmen. "From the best in his own field." Restitution is not paid with leftovers.
  • "If fire breaks out and catches in thorns" (v6). Farmers burned off weeds and thorns, and in dry seasons a fire could spread to a neighbor's grain. Even if he did not mean it, "he who started the fire shall make full restitution." Carelessness that harms a neighbor is still his responsibility. Samson later used fire on the Philistines' fields deliberately (Judg 15:4–5), a picture of how devastating it could be.
  • "If a man gives to his neighbor money or goods to keep safe" (v7). With no banks, people left valuables with a trusted neighbor while traveling. If the goods were stolen and the thief was caught, he paid double.
  • "The owner of the house shall come near to God" (v8). If no thief was found, the man who had been keeping the goods had to come before God to show he had not taken them himself. "God" here is ha-elohim, which the KJV translates "the judges." Either way, the case was settled at the sanctuary, before God's appointed judges, perhaps with an oath. The Tyndale notes add that we are not told how the decision was reached.
  • "For every breach of trust… of which one says, 'This is it'" (v9). "Breach of trust" is the Hebrew pesha, a word for rebellion or transgression, usually used for sin against God. Betraying a neighbor's trust is a serious sin. "This is it" is the moment when the owner spots his lost property in someone else's hands. "The one whom God condemns shall pay double." If the accuser was lying, he paid; if the keeper was guilty, he paid.
  • "An oath by the LORD shall be between them both" (v11). When an animal died or vanished with no witnesses, the keeper swore an oath before the LORD that he was innocent, and the owner had to accept it. This only works in a society that takes God's name seriously (20:7). A false oath would bring God's own judgment.
  • "If it is torn by beasts, let him bring it as evidence" (v13). A shepherd could bring back the remains to prove a wild animal killed it. Amos uses the picture: "As the shepherd rescues from the mouth of the lion two legs, or a piece of an ear" (Amos 3:12). Jacob complained to Laban that he had borne the loss of torn animals himself, without this protection (Gen 31:39).
  • "If a man borrows anything… and it is injured or dies, the owner not being with it, he shall make full restitution" (v14). Borrowing carries responsibility. "If the owner was with it, he shall not make restitution" (v15), because the owner could have looked after his own animal. "If it was hired, it came for its hiring fee." If the animal was rented, the rental price covered the risk.
  • Commentators: JFB explains the higher penalty for a slain or sold animal and the night-and-day rule, saying a robber at midnight "might, in self-defense, be slain with impunity," but killing him after sunrise was treated as murder. JFB also notes that the word for "God" in this chapter is "several times… rendered 'judges' or magistrates." Henry draws the lesson: "We must answer to God, not only for what we do maliciously, but for what we do heedlessly," and adds that when we have harmed a neighbor "we should make restitution, though not compelled by law." Guzik praises the restitution system for restoring the victim and keeping the offender productive instead of locked up.

Laws for a Just Community (vv16–31)

  • "If a man seduces a virgin who is not betrothed and lies with her" (v16). Seduction here means persuading, not force. Rape is treated separately and more severely (Deut 22:25–27). "He shall give the bride-price for her and make her his wife." The bride-price (mohar) was a payment to the bride's family when a marriage was arranged (Gen 34:12; 1 Sam 18:25). In that world, a young woman who had lost her virginity outside marriage would have had great trouble finding a husband. The law makes the man take responsibility: he cannot use her and walk away.
  • "If her father utterly refuses to give her to him" (v17). The woman is not simply handed to the man who wronged her. Her father can refuse the marriage, and the man still pays the full bride-price. Deuteronomy 22:28–29 adds that if he marries her, he may never divorce her.
  • "You shall not permit a sorceress to live" (v18). The word is mekashephah, someone who practices magic or witchcraft, the same root used for Pharaoh's "sorcerers" (7:11). Sorcery tried to control the spiritual world by spells, curses and contact with other powers. It belonged to the pagan world God was rescuing Israel from (Deut 18:9–14). It is feminine here probably because women were commonly associated with such practices, but the law applied to men as well (Lev 20:27). This was a law for Israel's courts in a theocracy. It has been terribly misused in later history, especially in the European witch trials, which went far beyond anything the Bible authorized. The New Testament response to sorcery is the gospel, confession and the burning of magic books (Acts 19:18–19).
  • "Whoever lies with an animal shall be put to death" (v19). Bestiality was practiced in some Canaanite and Egyptian cults. It overturns the order of creation, in which humans alone bear God's image (Lev 18:23–25).
  • "Whoever sacrifices to any god, other than the LORD alone, shall be devoted to destruction" (v20). "Devoted to destruction" is charam, the word for something set apart wholly to God, either for his use or for destruction, with no human benefit allowed. It is the same word later used for the conquest of Jericho (Josh 6:17). Sacrificing to another god breaks the first command at its root (20:3). The three crimes in vv18–20 all have to do with seeking power or life from something other than the LORD.
  • "You shall not wrong a sojourner or oppress him, for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt" (v21). A "sojourner" (ger) was a foreigner living among Israel, without land or family protection. "Wrong" can mean to cheat or mistreat, and "oppress" is the word used for what Egypt did to Israel (3:9). Israel's own memory was to shape its laws. The command comes again and again (23:9; Lev 19:33–34; Deut 10:18–19), and Leviticus goes further: "You shall love him as yourself." Guzik notes that caring for the sojourner does not mean a nation has no borders, but it does mean those who live among God's people must be treated justly.
  • "You shall not mistreat any widow or fatherless child" (v22). In that society, a woman without a husband and a child without a father had no one to defend them in court and often no means to live. The sojourner, the widow and the fatherless are a trio throughout the Old Testament, the classic picture of the vulnerable (Deut 10:18; 24:17–21; Ps 146:9; Isa 1:17). James says true religion is "to visit orphans and widows in their affliction" (Jas 1:27).
  • "If… they cry out to me, I will surely hear their cry" (v23). This echoes Israel's own story: "I have heard their cry because of their taskmasters" (3:7). God is the defender of those who have no defender. "My wrath will burn, and I will kill you with the sword, and your wives shall become widows" (v24). This is measure for measure. Those who prey on widows and orphans will leave their own families widowed and fatherless.
  • "If you lend money to any of my people with you who is poor" (v25). Loans in Israel were not mainly business loans but help for someone in trouble, such as a farmer after a bad harvest. "You shall not be like a moneylender to him." "You shall not exact interest from him." The word for interest is neshek, which the lexicon describes as literally "something bitten off." Interest rates in the ancient Near East commonly ran 20 percent or more for silver and higher for grain. Israel was not to profit from a brother's poverty. Deuteronomy 23:20 allowed interest on loans to foreigners, which most read as covering trade. Christians have long debated how this applies to modern banking. The core principle is plain: do not take advantage of a poor person's need (Ps 15:5).
  • "If ever you take your neighbor's cloak in pledge, you shall return it to him before the sun goes down" (v26). A lender could hold something as security. But for a poor man, his outer cloak was also his blanket at night. "For that is his only covering… in what else shall he sleep?" (v27). God asks the question himself. The lender may keep it during the day, but must give it back at night. Amos later condemned those who "lay themselves down beside every altar on garments taken in pledge" (Amos 2:8). "If he cries to me, I will hear, for I am compassionate." "Compassionate" is channun, gracious, the word God uses of himself when he proclaims his name to Moses (34:6). The law is shaped by God's own character.
  • "You shall not revile God, nor curse a ruler of your people" (v28). "God" is again elohim, and the KJV translates it "the gods," which some take as "judges." Most modern translations take it as God himself, set in parallel with the human ruler. "Ruler" is nasi, a chief or leader of a tribe. Paul quoted this verse when he realized he had spoken harshly to the high priest: "You shall not speak evil of a ruler of your people" (Acts 23:5). Respect for those in authority is part of respect for God, who stands behind all authority (Rom 13:1).
  • "You shall not delay to offer from the fullness of your harvest and from the outflow of your presses" (v29). "Fullness" and "outflow" are poetic words, probably for grain and for wine or olive oil. The first and best belong to God, and he should not be kept waiting for them (Prov 3:9).
  • "The firstborn of your sons you shall give to me" (v29). God had claimed every firstborn when he spared Israel's firstborn in Egypt (13:2, 12–15). Firstborn sons were not sacrificed. They were "redeemed," bought back with an offering (13:13; 34:20), and later the Levites served in their place (Num 3:11–13). This sharply separated Israel from Canaanite religion, where children were sometimes sacrificed.
  • "Seven days it shall be with its mother; on the eighth day you shall give it to me" (v30). Firstborn animals were sacrificed, but not until they had a week with their mothers. The Tyndale notes point out God's care even for animals (compare Deut 22:6–7; Jonah 4:11). The eighth day was also the day of circumcision for Israel's sons (Gen 17:12).
  • "You shall be consecrated to me. Therefore you shall not eat any flesh that is torn by beasts in the field" (v31). "Consecrated" is literally "men of holiness." An animal killed by wild beasts would not have had its blood properly drained (Lev 17:10–15), and blood stands for life, which belongs to God. "You shall throw it to the dogs." The holy people do not eat what scavengers eat. Holiness for Israel reached the dinner table.
  • Commentators: Henry gives the spirit of the section: "The people of God should ever be ready to show mildness and mercy." JFB has a good note on the pledged cloak: "From the nature of the case, this is the description of a poor man," who slept in his day clothes and covered himself with his cloak at night. On v28 JFB takes "gods" as judges or magistrates and applies "ruler" to the high priest of Paul's day (Acts 23:5). JFB passes over vv18–25, 29 and 31 without comment. Guzik also reads elohim in v28 as possibly meaning judges, though most modern translations take it as God. He notes that the penalty for false worship was rarely carried out in Israel's history, citing Elijah and the prophets of Baal (1 Kgs 18:40) as one of the few cases, and he passes on Adam Clarke's comment that the word for interest suggests "biting usury," which the lexicon supports.

Pointing to Christ

These laws reveal a God who notices the stolen sheep, the burned field, the borrowed ox, the widow without a defender, and the poor man shivering at night without his cloak. "I will hear, for I am compassionate." Jesus is that compassion in the flesh. He came to "proclaim good news to the poor… liberty to the captives" (Luke 4:18), he defended widows against those who "devour widows' houses" (Mark 12:40), and he told a story about a despised foreigner who loved his neighbor (Luke 10:29–37). He also paid the debt we could not pay. We are the thief who has nothing, rightly "sold for his theft" (v3), and Christ himself became the price, so that we might be redeemed like a firstborn son and made "consecrated" to God (1 Pet 1:18–19; Heb 12:23).

Reflection

  • Repayment That Goes Beyond the Debt (vv.1-4) — A convicted thief must repay more than what was originally stolen, not simply return it. Does your repentance stop at "I'll stop doing that" or does it move toward actively making things right?
  • Remembering What You Once Were (v.21) — Israel is told not to mistreat foreigners because they themselves were once foreigners in Egypt. How does remembering your own past need for mercy shape the way you treat people who need it now?
  • God Hears the Cry of the Vulnerable (vv.22-24) — God promises to personally hear and avenge widows and orphans who are mistreated. Are you someone who notices and protects the vulnerable, or someone who adds to what burdens them?
  • Compassion Over Technical Rights (vv.26-27) — A lender must return a poor man's cloak by sunset even though he had a legal claim to hold it. Where do you insist on a "right" at the cost of showing compassion?
  • Called to Be Holy in the Details (v.31) — This whole list of everyday civil laws ends with the summary command to be a holy people. Does your holiness show up in ordinary weekday decisions, or only in how you act on Sunday?
