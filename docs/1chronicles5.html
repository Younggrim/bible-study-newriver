1These were the sons of Reuben the firstborn of Israel. Though he was the firstborn, his birthright was given to the sons of Joseph son of Israel, because Reuben defiled his father’s bed. So he is not reckoned according to birthright.

2And though Judah prevailed over his brothers and a ruler came from him, the birthright belonged to Joseph.

3The sons of Reuben, the firstborn of Israel: Hanoch, Pallu, Hezron, and Carmi.

4The descendants of Joel: Shemaiah his son, Gog his son, Shimei his son,

5Micah his son, Reaiah his son, Baal his son,

6and Beerah his son, whom Tiglath-pileser king of Assyria carried into exile. Beerah was a leader of the Reubenites.

7His relatives by their clans are recorded in their genealogy: Jeiel the chief, Zechariah,

8and Bela son of Azaz, the son of Shema, the son of Joel. They settled in Aroer and as far as Nebo and Baal-meon.

9They also settled in the east as far as the edge of the desert that extends to the Euphrates River, because their livestock had increased in the land of Gilead.

10During the days of Saul they waged war against the Hagrites, who were defeated at their hands, and they occupied the dwellings of the Hagrites throughout the region east of Gilead.

11The descendants of Gad lived next to the Reubenites in the land of Bashan, as far as Salecah:

12Joel was the chief, Shapham the second, then Jaanai and Shaphat, who lived in Bashan.

13Their kinsmen by families were Michael, Meshullam, Sheba, Jorai, Jacan, Zia, and Eber—seven in all.

14These were the sons of Abihail son of Huri, the son of Jaroah, the son of Gilead, the son of Michael, the son of Jeshishai, the son of Jahdo, the son of Buz.

15Ahi son of Abdiel, the son of Guni, was head of their family.

16They lived in Gilead, in Bashan and its towns, and throughout the pasturelands of Sharon.

17All of them were recorded in the genealogies during the reigns of Jotham king of Judah and Jeroboam king of Israel.

18The Reubenites, the Gadites, and the half-tribe of Manasseh had 44,760 warriors—valiant men who carried the shield and sword, drew the bow, and were trained for battle.

19They waged war against the Hagrites, as well as Jetur, Naphish, and Nodab.

20And because they cried out to God in battle, they were helped against their enemies, and the Hagrites and all their allies were delivered into their hands. Because they put their trust in God, He answered their prayers.

21They seized the livestock of the Hagrites—50,000 camels, 250,000 sheep, and 2,000 donkeys. They also took 100,000 captives,

22and many others fell slain, because the battle belonged to God. And they occupied the land until the exile.

23Now the people of the half-tribe of Manasseh were numerous. They settled in the land from Bashan to Baal-hermon (that is, Senir, also known as Mount Hermon).

24These were the heads of their families: Epher, Ishi, Eliel, Azriel, Jeremiah, Hodaviah, and Jahdiel. They were mighty men of valor, famous men, and heads of their families.

25But they were unfaithful to the God of their fathers, and they prostituted themselves with the gods of the peoples of the land, whom God had destroyed before them.

26So the God of Israel stirred up the spirit of Pul king of Assyria (that is, Tiglath-pileser king of Assyria) to take the Reubenites, the Gadites, and the half-tribe of Manasseh into exile. And he brought them to Halah, Habor, Hara, and the river of Gozan, where they remain to this day.

Summary

The tribe of Reuben's genealogy opens with a note that his birthright passed to Joseph's sons because he defiled his father's bed, though the ruling scepter belonged to Judah (vv. 1-2). Reuben's descendants are traced down to Beerah, who was carried into exile by Assyria, and the tribe's settlements in Gilead are described (vv. 3-10). Gad's genealogy and territory in Bashan follow (vv. 11-17), then the combined forces of Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh — over 44,000 warriors — defeat the Hagrites in battle because they trusted God (vv. 18-22). The chapter closes with the half-tribe of Manasseh's leaders and the sobering report that all three tribes were later exiled by Assyria for turning to idolatry (vv. 23-26).

Authorship & Background

Author: Traditionally attributed to Ezra the scribe. Originally one book with 2 Chronicles. Written post-exile (approximately 450-400 BC) for the returned remnant. The Chronicler retells Israel's history from a priestly/worship perspective, emphasizing the Davidic covenant, Temple worship, and God's faithfulness.
Historical Context: Chapter 5 covers the Transjordan tribes — Reuben (vv.1-10), Gad (vv.11-17), and the half-tribe of Manasseh (vv.23-26) — along with a remarkable joint military victory (vv.18-22) and a devastating theological verdict on their exile (vv.25-26). This chapter presents a miniature theology of history: trust God and prosper (vv.18-22), forsake God and face exile (vv.25-26).
Classification: Historical Narrative — Genealogy
Key Themes: A birthright forfeited in the opening verses and explained rather than merely noted, Reuben's line ending with a name carried away captive by Assyria, a war credited explicitly to prayer rather than to the numbers the register has just finished counting, because they put their trust in him, and the three eastern tribes named first to go into exile with the reason stated as directly as the earlier victory, they transgressed against the God of their fathers
Reuben's Forfeited Birthright (vv.1-10): The register interrupts itself in its first two verses to explain a demotion. Reuben was the firstborn, but forasmuch as he defiled his father's bed, his birthright was given unto the sons of Joseph, and the Chronicler adds the further complication that the genealogy is not to be reckoned after the birthright, for Judah prevailed above his brethren. Two tribes divide what one man lost, Joseph taking the double portion and Judah the rule. Then the line, ending with Beerah whom Tiglath-pileser carried away captive.
The Sons of Gad (vv.11-17): Gad is recorded by territory before family, they dwelt over against them in the land of Bashan unto Salecah, and the list of names is short. The section closes with a note on method that is worth having: all these were reckoned by genealogies in the days of Jotham king of Judah. The Chronicler is telling the reader which census he is copying from.
The War Against the Hagrites (vv.18-22): Five verses of narrative in the middle of a list, and they read like an argument. Forty-four thousand seven hundred and sixty men able to bear buckler and sword, and they made war with the Hagrites, and they were helped against them, and the Hagrites were delivered into their hand, because they cried to God in the battle, and he was intreated of them, because they put their trust in him. The register pauses to record that the outcome was credited to prayer rather than to the numbers it has just given.
Half-Manasseh, and the Captivity (vv.23-26): The eastern half of Manasseh is placed geographically, from Bashan unto Baal-hermon, and its men described as famous men, and heads of the house of their fathers. Then the section turns on them: they transgressed against the God of their fathers, and went after the gods of the people of the land. The consequence is stated with the same causal directness as the victory four verses earlier, and the God of Israel stirred up the spirit of Pul king of Assyria, and he carried them away. The three eastern tribes are the first to go, and the register says why.

Deep Dive

Setting: Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. Having traced Judah (chapters 2–4) and Simeon, the tribe inside Judah's land (4:24–43), the Chronicler now crosses the Jordan to the east. This chapter covers the two and a half tribes who settled in Transjordan, the high plateau and pasture country east of the Jordan River and the Dead Sea: Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh. Moses had let them take that land, which was ideal for cattle, on condition that their fighting men crossed over and helped conquer Canaan (Num 32). They did, but they lived on the far side of the river from the rest of Israel, and from the place of worship. They were also the first tribes taken into exile, by Assyria around 733 BC, more than a century before Judah fell to Babylon. The chapter's shape is a warning for the Chronicler's readers: trust in God brought victory, and unfaithfulness brought exile.

Descendants of Reuben (vv1–10)

  • "Reuben the firstborn of Israel (for he was the firstborn, but…)" (v1). The Chronicler stops before he can even start the list. Reuben was Jacob's eldest son, but "he defiled his father's couch": he slept with Bilhah, his father's concubine (Gen 35:22). Taking a father's woman was a bid to take his place, as Absalom later did with David's concubines (2 Sam 16:21–22). Jacob's last words to Reuben were "Unstable as water, you shall not have preeminence, because you went up to your father's bed" (Gen 49:3–4).
  • "His birthright was given to the sons of Joseph" (v1). The firstborn's birthright normally meant a double share of the inheritance (Deut 21:17). Jacob gave that double share to Joseph by adopting Joseph's two sons, Ephraim and Manasseh, as his own, so that Joseph's family became two tribes (Gen 48:5). JFB notes that the birthright "implied not only dominion, but a double portion," and that the two were split.
  • "Judah became strong among his brothers and a chief came from him" (v2). Joseph got the double share, but the leadership went to Judah. "Chief" is nagid, leader or prince, the title God gave David: "I took you from the pasture… that you should be prince over my people Israel" (2 Sam 7:8). Jacob had said, "The scepter shall not depart from Judah" (Gen 49:10). This parenthesis explains why the Chronicler began with Judah rather than Reuben. The order of the lists follows God's choices, not birth order.
  • "Hanoch, Pallu, Hezron, and Carmi" (v3). Reuben's four sons, as in Genesis 46:9 and Numbers 26:5–6. Dathan and Abiram, who rebelled against Moses with Korah and were swallowed by the earth, were Reubenites from Pallu's line (Num 16:1; 26:8–10).
  • "Beerah… whom Tiglath-pileser king of Assyria carried away into exile" (v6). The line of Joel ends with a deported chief. Tiglath-pileser III ruled Assyria from 745 to 727 BC and built it into an empire. He was the first Assyrian king to deport whole populations as a policy, moving conquered peoples away from their homelands so they could not rebel. His campaign against the northern kingdom in about 733 BC took Gilead and Galilee (2 Kings 15:29).
  • "Aroer, as far as Nebo and Baal-meon" (v8). These towns were on the plateau east of the Dead Sea, on the border with Moab. Nebo is the mountain region where Moses looked over the promised land and died (Deut 34:1). The Mesha Stele (the Moabite Stone, about 840 BC), an inscription by a king of Moab, names Aroer, Nebo and Baal-meon and boasts of taking them from Israel. It also mentions "the men of Gad" who had lived in that region "from of old." The border country changed hands more than once.
  • "As far as the entrance of the desert this side of the Euphrates, because their livestock had multiplied" (v9). Reuben's herds pushed east into the steppe that runs toward the Syrian desert. Cattle needed grass, and grass meant land.
  • "In the days of Saul they waged war against the Hagrites" (v10). The Hagrites were a nomadic people of the desert edge, whose name may connect them with Hagar, Ishmael's mother. They appear among Israel's enemies in Psalm 83:6. This verse is a preview of the battle described in more detail in vv18–22.

Descendants of Gad (vv11–22)

  • "The sons of Gad lived over against them in the land of Bashan as far as Salecah" (v11). Gad lived north of Reuben, in Gilead and reaching into Bashan, the fertile highland east of the Sea of Galilee known for its strong cattle (Ps 22:12; Amos 4:1). Salecah was on the far eastern edge of the territory (Deut 3:10).
  • The pasturelands of Sharon (v16). Not the well-known coastal plain of Sharon west of Samaria, but a pasture region east of the Jordan with the same name. The Mesha Stele also mentions a place called Sharon in this area.
  • "Recorded in genealogies in the days of Jotham king of Judah, and in the days of Jeroboam king of Israel" (v17). A real date for a real record. Jeroboam II of Israel (about 793–753 BC) and Jotham of Judah (about 750–735 BC) overlapped briefly around 750 BC. This was a prosperous time, just before the Assyrian storm broke. The Chronicler had access to a census taken then.
  • "44,760, able to go to war" (v18). The three eastern tribes together. "Who carried shield and sword, and drew the bow, expert in war." These were tough frontiersmen. Deuteronomy 33:20 describes Gad as one who "crouches like a lion." David's Gadite warriors had "faces like the faces of lions" and were "swift as gazelles" (1 Chr 12:8).
  • "The Hagrites, Jetur, Naphish, and Nodab" (v19). Jetur and Naphish were sons of Ishmael (1:31; Gen 25:15). Jetur may be the origin of the later name Ituraea, a region named in Luke 3:1. So this was a war with Ishmaelite tribes, kin to Israel through Abraham.
  • "For they cried out to God in the battle, and he granted their urgent plea because they trusted in him" (v20). This is the heart of the chapter, and the theme of the whole book. The verb "trusted" (batach) means to lean one's whole weight on something. Chronicles tells story after story where a king or army facing a stronger enemy cries out to God and wins: Abijah (2 Chr 13:14–18), Asa ("we rely on you," 2 Chr 14:11), Jehoshaphat ("our eyes are on you," 2 Chr 20:12), Hezekiah (2 Chr 32:20–22). The eastern tribes are the first example. Their victory came not from being "expert in war" (v18) but from crying out in trust.
  • "50,000 of their camels, 250,000 sheep, 2,000 donkeys, and 100,000 men alive" (v21). The numbers are huge. Some scholars think the word for "thousand" (eleph) can also mean a clan or military unit, which would shrink these figures considerably, and that is possible here. Either way, the point is a total victory and an enormous haul of livestock, which mattered most to herding tribes.
  • "For many fell, because the war was of God" (v22). God fought for them, and so they won. The same God who answered their cry was the God who could send them away. "And they lived in their place until the exile" looks ahead to v26, with a quiet warning built in. Land given by God was held only as long as his people kept faith.
  • Commentators: Henry sees the eastern tribes as a picture of unity, since "they all shared in a victory," and of the happiness of people who help each other and call on the Lord. Guzik links the cry of v20 to Jabez's prayer in the previous chapter, both answered because people called on God. He notes that "the war was of God" means a war of judgment, which only God's command could justify. JFB identifies the Hagrites as a name "originally synonymous with 'Ishmaelites,'" and later applied to a particular Arab tribe.

The Half-Tribe of Manasseh (vv23–26)

  • "From Bashan to Baal-hermon, Senir, and Mount Hermon" (v23). The northern half of Transjordan, up to the slopes of Mount Hermon, the snowy peak on the northern border. Senir is the Amorite name for Hermon (Deut 3:9). This was some of the best land in the region.
  • "Mighty warriors, famous men, heads of their fathers' houses" (v24). The Chronicler gives them full honor. Then the next word turns everything.
  • "But they broke faith with the God of their fathers" (v25). "Broke faith" is maal again, the word used of Achan (2:7) and of Saul (10:13), and the Chronicler's word for the unfaithfulness that leads to exile (9:1; 2 Chr 36:14). "And whored after the gods of the peoples of the land" uses the language of adultery. Israel's covenant with God was like a marriage, and worshiping other gods was unfaithfulness to a husband (Hos 2; Jer 3). The irony is pointed: they worshiped "the gods of the peoples… whom God had destroyed before them." They turned to gods who had not even been able to save their own worshipers.
  • "So the God of Israel stirred up the spirit of Pul king of Assyria" (v26). Assyria is not acting on its own. God is behind it. The same phrase, "stirred up the spirit," is used at the very end of Chronicles of Cyrus king of Persia, whom God moved to send the exiles home (2 Chr 36:22; Ezra 1:1). The same God who stirred a pagan king to send his people into exile later stirred another pagan king to bring them back. The Chronicler's first readers were living proof of the second half.
  • Pul and Tiglath-pileser (v26). "Pul" was the name Tiglath-pileser III used as king of Babylon, and Babylonian king lists call him Pulu. So these are two names for one man. The Hebrew grammar agrees: "the spirit of Pul… the spirit of Tiglath-pileser, and he took them," with a singular verb. 2 Kings 15:19 and 15:29 use each name separately. Older commentators such as JFB took Pul to be Tiglath-pileser's father, but the Assyrian and Babylonian records discovered since have shown they were the same king.
  • "Halah, Habor, Hara, and the river Gozan, to this day" (v26). Places in northern Mesopotamia, along the Habor River (the Khabur, in modern northeast Syria) and the region of Gozan. 2 Kings 17:6 names the same places for the deportation of the rest of the northern kingdom after Samaria fell in 722 BC. "Hara" appears only here. JFB calls this "the first captivity." "To this day" means these tribes never came back as tribes. When the exiles returned from Babylon two centuries later, they were mostly from Judah, Benjamin and Levi.
  • Commentators: Henry says the eastern tribes "shared in captivity" just as they had shared in victory, and that they had chosen the best land without thinking that it was also the most exposed. He draws a warning: love of earthly things pulls people away from God's ordinances and toward ruin. Guzik notes that the half-tribe began as brave and godly men, and that settling east of the Jordan was not wrong in itself. But living far from the center of worship seems to have weakened their devotion (compare the altar dispute in Joshua 22). JFB places the exile in "Upper Media" and dates it as the first of the captivities.

Pointing to Christ

This chapter puts two paths side by side. The same tribes who "cried out to God… because they trusted in him" later "broke faith" and were carried away. The difference was not strength or skill but trust. Israel's history showed again and again that God's people could not stay faithful on their own. The birthright note at the start points to the answer. Leadership was given to Judah, and "a chief came from him." From that tribe came the faithful Son of David, who trusted his Father perfectly, even in the hour when he too "cried out" (Matt 27:46; Heb 5:7). His faithfulness is what brings the scattered home.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes the theological framework of the chapter: the same tribes that experienced supernatural victory through faith (vv.18-22) later experienced supernatural judgment through apostasy (vv.25-26). He notes that the Chronicler presents this as a warning pattern for the post-exilic community: your restoration is real, but it can be lost.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Privilege Forfeited by Sin (vv.1-2) — Reuben was Israel's firstborn but lost his birthright through one act of unfaithfulness with his father's bed. What privileged position in your life are you presuming will always be yours, regardless of how you live?
  • Victory Won by Crying Out, Not Weapons (v.20) — Tens of thousands of trained warriors went to battle, but the text credits the win to their cry to God and their trust in Him. Are you relying on your own preparation and strength, or genuinely crying out to God when you face a fight?
  • Chasing Gods Already Proven Powerless (v.25) — These same tribes later turned to worship the gods of the very peoples God had destroyed before them. What are you tempted to chase after that has already shown itself unable to save or satisfy anyone?
  • God's Sovereign Hand in the Discipline (v.26) — Assyria's king had no knowledge of Israel's God, yet Scripture says God stirred him up to carry His people into exile. Can you trust that the hard circumstances in your life might be God's purposeful discipline rather than meaningless chaos?
