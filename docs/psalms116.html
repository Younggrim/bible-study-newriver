Summary

This Hallel thanksgiving psalm opens with love and devotion to God for hearing the psalmist's cry (vv. 1-2). He recalls being surrounded by the anguish of death until he called on the LORD for rescue (vv. 3-4), praising God's graciousness, righteousness, and mercy toward the helpless (vv. 5-6). Having been delivered from death, tears, and stumbling, he can now rest and walk confidently before the LORD in the land of the living (vv. 7-9), his faith holding even through affliction (vv. 10-11). Unable to repay God's kindness, he resolves to publicly offer thanksgiving and fulfill his vows before God's people (vv. 12-14, 17-18), noting that the death of God's faithful ones is precious to him (v. 15) and closing with a declaration of devoted service and praise in Jerusalem (vv. 16, 19).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Individual Thanksgiving / Hallel Psalm
Attributed Author: Anonymous (part of the Egyptian Hallel)
Key Themes: Love for God because He heard prayer, deliverance from death, rest for the soul, paying vows publicly, the preciousness of saints' deaths to God, gratitude in worship
Historical Context: Psalm 116 is part of the Egyptian Hallel (113-118) sung at Passover. It is deeply personal — the psalmist nearly died and was rescued by God. The psalm's language of deliverance from death, taking the "cup of salvation" (v.13), and paying vows in the presence of God's people makes it especially meaningful in the context of Jesus' last Passover. When Jesus took the cup at the Last Supper, this psalm would have been fresh on His lips — yet for Him, the "sorrows of death" would not be averted but embraced. Paul quotes v.10 in 2 Corinthians 4:13: "I believed, therefore have I spoken."
I Love the LORD, Because He Hath Heard (vv.1-2): I love the LORD, because he hath heard my voice and my supplications. The psalm gives a reason for love, which is unusual, and the reason is a specific answered prayer rather than God's character in general. Because he hath inclined his ear unto me, therefore will I call upon him as long as I live. One hearing produces a lifetime policy.
The Sorrows of Death Compassed Me (vv.3-4): The sorrows of death compassed me, and the pains of hell gat hold upon me: I found trouble and sorrow. Hell here renders Sheol, the place of the dead rather than a place of punishment, so what is described is coming close to dying and not to damnation. The prayer that follows is four words long in effect, O LORD, I beseech thee, deliver my soul, which is what a man in that condition has breath for.
The LORD Preserveth the Simple (vv.5-6): Gracious is the LORD, and righteous; yea, our God is merciful. The psalm generalizes from its own case, and the group it names as protected is not the wise or the strong, the LORD preserveth the simple. Simple means open and untrained rather than foolish. I was brought low, and he helped me.
Return unto Thy Rest, O My Soul (vv.7-9): Return unto thy rest, O my soul; for the LORD hath dealt bountifully with thee. The singer instructs himself, as Psalm 103 does, and what he orders is a return to a settled state he had left. Three deliverances are then counted off in one verse, my soul from death, mine eyes from tears, and my feet from falling. And the conclusion is about where he will live rather than how he will feel, I will walk before the LORD in the land of the living.
I Said in My Haste, All Men Are Liars (vv.10-11): I believed, therefore have I spoken: I was greatly afflicted. Paul quotes the first clause in 2 Corinthians 4:13 as the pattern of his own preaching under pressure, taking it from the Greek, which reads it more smoothly than the Hebrew does. Then a confession that the psalm makes no attempt to justify, I said in my haste, All men are liars. It is recorded as a thing said while panicking, which is why the clause about haste is there, and the psalm neither endorses it nor deletes it.
What Shall I Render unto the LORD (vv.12-19): What shall I render unto the LORD for all his benefits toward me? The last eight verses answer that question, and every item in the answer is a public act in the temple rather than a private feeling. I will take the cup of salvation, and call upon the name of the LORD, and I will pay my vows unto the LORD now in the presence of all his people, which is said twice. Between them stands the verse most often read at funerals, precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints, and in context it is a statement that God does not spend their lives cheaply, which is why this man is alive to sing. The self-description is a servant's, I am thy servant, and the son of thine handmaid: thou hast loosed my bonds. And the place is named at the end, in the courts of the LORD'S house, in the midst of thee, O Jerusalem.

Setting: Psalm 116 is the fourth psalm of the "Egyptian Hallel" (Psalms 113–118), the praise psalms sung at Passover. It is one of the four sung after the meal, so it was probably on the lips of Jesus and his disciples as they left the upper room for the Mount of Olives (Matt 26:30). Its author and date are not given. Unlike the psalms on either side of it, it is intensely personal, full of "I" and "me." A single worshiper has come close to death, cried out to God, and been rescued, and now he comes to the temple to keep the promises he made in his trouble. Whether the danger was illness, enemies or something else is not said, which makes the psalm easy for anyone to pray. Its language draws on earlier psalms, especially Psalm 18 and Psalm 27. The ancient Greek translation (the Septuagint) divides it into two psalms at v10, and Paul quotes that Greek version of v10 in 2 Corinthians 4:13. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

I Love the LORD, Because He Has Heard My Voice (vv1–4)

  • "I love the LORD" (v1). No other psalm begins quite this way. The command to love God (Deut 6:5) appears here as a simple confession. And the reason is given at once: "because he has heard." This is love that grows out of experience. The psalmist has been heard and helped, and his heart has answered. John says the same of Christian love: "We love because he first loved us" (1 John 4:19).
  • "My pleas for mercy" (v1). The Hebrew word, tachanun, means a cry for grace or favor, the prayer of someone who has no claim and asks only for kindness.
  • "Because he inclined his ear to me" (v2). The picture is of a great person bending down to catch a weak voice. It echoes Psalm 113's God who "looks far down" (113:6). The result is a lifelong resolve: "therefore I will call on him as long as I live." Answered prayer makes for more prayer, not less.
  • "The snares of death encompassed me; the pangs of Sheol laid hold on me" (v3). The language closely matches Psalm 18:4–5. "Snares" (or "cords") of death picture death as a hunter's rope pulling tight. "Pangs" can also be translated "straits" or "distresses," the tight, narrow place with no way out. "Sheol" is the Hebrew name for the realm of the dead. "Laid hold" is literally "found," and the same verb comes again in the next line: the pangs "found" him, and he "found" distress and anguish. Death had tracked him down.
  • "Then I called on the name of the LORD" (v4). His prayer is just four words in Hebrew: "Please, LORD, rescue my soul!" In a crisis there is no time for long prayers, and God hears short ones. "Call on the name of the LORD" appears four times in the psalm (vv2, 4, 13, 17). In trouble he calls, and in thanks he calls again.
  • Commentators: JFB says "a truly grateful love will be evinced by acts of worship," and compares v3 to the same figures for distress in Psalm 18:4–5. It also notes the wordplay on the verb "found." Henry says believers are "most affected by his loving-kindness when relieved out of deep distress." Guzik notes that Peter uses a closely related phrase, the "pangs of death," when he speaks of God raising Jesus (Acts 2:24), and he passes on Spurgeon's admiration for the short, direct prayer of v4 as a model in emergencies.

Gracious Is the LORD, and Righteous (vv5–7)

  • "Gracious is the LORD, and righteous; our God is merciful" (v5). The psalmist turns from his story to who God is. "Gracious" and "merciful" come from God's own self-description at Sinai (Exod 34:6) and were just sung in Psalm 111:4. "Righteous" stands between them. God's kindness is never at odds with his justice. Notice the shift from "I" to "our": personal rescue leads to shared praise.
  • "The LORD preserves the simple" (v6). "Simple" is peti, a person who is open, inexperienced and easily led. In Proverbs it is often a warning (Prov 1:22; 14:15). Here it is meant tenderly: God protects those who have no cleverness or strength to protect themselves. Psalm 19:7 says God's word makes "wise the simple," and Jesus thanked his Father for revealing his truth to "little children" (Matt 11:25).
  • "When I was brought low, he saved me" (v6). This is the same pattern as Psalm 113: God raising the poor from the dust.
  • "Return, O my soul, to your rest" (v7). The psalmist speaks to himself, as in Psalm 42:5 ("Why are you cast down, O my soul?"). His heart has been racing in fear, and now he calls it home. The "rest" is not only the end of danger but rest in God (62:1, 5; Matt 11:28–29). The reason: "the LORD has dealt bountifully with you." The verb means to treat someone fully and generously.
  • Commentators: JFB says the relief comes from "God's known pity and tenderness," not from the writer's merit, and that he now assures his soul of rest and peace. Henry says "neither ignorance nor guilt will hinder their salvation, when they put their trust in the Lord." Guzik passes on Poole's definition of the "simple" as sincere, humble people, and Spurgeon's point that the rest of v7 is found in God himself, not in circumstances.

I Will Walk Before the LORD in the Land of the Living (vv8–11)

  • "From death, my eyes from tears, my feet from stumbling" (v8). God rescued all of him: his life from death, his eyes from weeping, his feet from falling. The line closely follows Psalm 56:13. The verb switches to "you": the psalmist turns from talking about God to talking to him.
  • "I will walk before the LORD in the land of the living" (v9). To "walk before" someone means to live in their sight and under their care. God told Abraham, "walk before me, and be blameless" (Gen 17:1). "The land of the living" means this present life, as opposed to Sheol (27:13; 142:5). The man who almost died now means to live every remaining day in God's presence.
  • "I believed, even when I spoke: 'I am greatly afflicted'" (v10). This verse is hard to translate, and versions differ. The ESV's reading means that his faith held even while he was saying how badly he was suffering. Faith and honest lament are not opposites. The Greek Septuagint reads "I believed, therefore I spoke," and that is the version Paul quotes: "Since we have the same spirit of faith according to what has been written, 'I believed, and so I spoke,' we also believe, and so we also speak" (2 Cor 4:13). Paul writes that in the middle of describing his own sufferings, "afflicted in every way, but not crushed" (2 Cor 4:8). Both readings land in the same place: real faith speaks, even from under heavy pressure.
  • "I said in my alarm, 'All mankind are liars'" (v11). "Alarm" (KJV "haste") means panic or agitation. In his terror, he felt that no one could be trusted. People had failed him, or he expected they would. The line is honest about what fear does to the heart. It may be a hasty overstatement, as many readers take it. It is also true in its own way: human help is unreliable and no human being is wholly true (Rom 3:4). Either way, the God of v5 is the one who does not lie.
  • Commentators: JFB says "walk before the Lord" means living under God's favor and guidance, and explains "haste" as literally "terror" or "agitation" caused by the affliction. It reads vv10–11 as a contrast between confidence in God and distrust of men "as not reliable." Guzik follows Kidner in calling v10 "faith hard-pressed," notes that Paul's Greek form is stronger than the Hebrew, and agrees with Spurgeon that v11 was a hasty word from bitter experience rather than a settled conclusion.

What Shall I Render to the LORD? (vv12–14)

  • "What shall I render to the LORD for all his benefits to me?" (v12). "Render" means to give back or repay. The question has no adequate answer. Nothing can repay God, and he needs nothing from anyone (50:9–12; Acts 17:25). But gratitude cannot stay silent, so the psalmist finds ways to respond.
  • "I will lift up the cup of salvation" (v13). The answer to "what shall I give?" is, first, "I will take." The best response to God's gifts is to receive more from him. "Salvation" is plural in Hebrew, "the cup of salvations," meaning many rescues or full deliverance. The cup was probably a drink offering poured out with a thank offering at the temple (Num 15:5–10), or a cup raised and drunk in the thanksgiving meal that followed. Scripture also speaks of a cup of God's wrath (75:8; Jer 25:15). This is its opposite, a cup full of rescue. At Passover, several cups of wine were drunk during the meal. It was likely the cup after supper that Jesus took and called "the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20), shortly before the group sang the second half of the Hallel.
  • "And call on the name of the LORD" (v13). He called on God's name for help (v4). Now he calls on it in thanks. The same act of prayer serves both.
  • "I will pay my vows to the LORD in the presence of all his people" (v14). In danger people often promise God something: an offering, a sacrifice, a public act of praise (66:13–14; Jonah 2:9). Now he keeps his word in public. Thanksgiving in Israel was not private. It was a testimony to the whole congregation (22:22, 25).
  • Commentators: JFB identifies the cup as "the drink offering" that was part of the thank offering (citing Numbers 15:3–5) and reads v14's "now" as an eager wish to do it before the people. Henry says plainly, "We cannot in any way profit him," yet believers should give themselves to his service all the same. Guzik passes on Boice's insight that God is repaid by taking more from him, compares the psalmist to the one healed leper who came back to give thanks (Luke 17:15–18), and notes that Jesus instituted the cup of the new covenant on the night he sang these words.

Precious in the Sight of the LORD Is the Death of His Saints (vv15–19)

  • "Precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints" (v15). "Precious" is yaqar, which means costly, rare, highly valued, the word for precious stones (1 Kings 10:2). God does not treat the death of his people lightly or cheaply. In context, the point is that God cared too much about the psalmist's life to let him die easily. That is why he rescued him (72:14: "precious is their blood in his sight"). Christians have also rightly drawn comfort from the verse when believers die. Their death is not unnoticed. It is watched over and valued by God (Rev 14:13). "Saints" here is chasidim, people of God's chesed, his faithful, loyal ones.
  • "I am your servant, the son of your maidservant" (v16). He says "your servant" twice. "The son of your maidservant" describes a slave born into the household, not bought from outside. It means he belongs to God from birth, and it may hint that he had a godly mother. The law described a slave who, though free to go, chose to stay with a master he loved (Exod 21:5–6).
  • "You have loosed my bonds" (v16). The "snares of death" (v3) have been untied. The irony is deliberate: freed from death's ropes, he binds himself as God's servant. True freedom is belonging to the right Master.
  • "I will offer to you the sacrifice of thanksgiving" (v17). The thank offering (todah) was a sacrifice of gratitude, eaten in a joyful meal with family and friends (Lev 7:11–15). Hebrews uses the same idea for Christian worship: "let us continually offer up a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that acknowledge his name" (Heb 13:15).
  • "In the courts of the house of the LORD, in your midst, O Jerusalem" (vv18–19). Verse 18 repeats v14 word for word and then names the place: the temple courts in Jerusalem. The private rescue ends in public worship among God's people. The psalm closes with "Praise the LORD!"
  • Commentators: JFB reads the "homeborn servant" of v16 as a plea for God's covenant care, and calls vv17–19 "an ampler declaration of his purpose," set in the Lord's house in Jerusalem. Henry: "Such are true saints of God, in whose lives and deaths he will be glorified," and "doing good is sacrifice, with which God is well pleased." Guzik, with Maclaren, links v15 to Psalm 72:14 and notes that God does not treat his people's death lightly. He passes on Spurgeon's and Manton's warning that being the child of a godly parent does not make anyone God's servant without a personal choice.

Pointing to Christ

On the night before he died, Jesus most likely sang this psalm. He sang of the snares of death closing in, hours before his own arrest, and of lifting "the cup of salvation" after he had just lifted the cup of the new covenant. Then he went out to Gethsemane and prayed for another cup to pass from him, the cup of wrath, and drank it so that his people might drink the cup of salvation instead. His death was precious in the Father's sight beyond any other. Peter says God raised him, "loosing the pangs of death, because it was not possible for him to be held by it" (Acts 2:24). Now he walks before the LORD in the land of the living forever, and those joined to him, with Paul, can say, "I believed, and so I spoke."

Reflection

  • Returning to Rest (v.7) — After the LORD's deliverance, the psalmist commands his soul to return to rest. Once a crisis has passed, do you actually let your soul rest, or do you stay braced for the next one?
  • What Shall I Render? (v.12) — Facing all that God has done, the psalmist asks what he can possibly give back. When you consider God's goodness to you, what is your honest answer to that question?
  • Receiving the Cup of Salvation (v.13) — The psalmist responds to God's deliverance by taking the cup of salvation and calling on His name. Are you trying to earn what God is simply offering you to receive?
  • Precious in His Sight (v.15) — The death of the LORD's saints is called precious to Him — not something He treats lightly. Does it change how you view your own life to know God considers it costly and valuable?
  • Paying Vows in Public (v.18) — The psalmist commits to fulfilling his vows to the LORD in front of all His people, not just privately. Is your gratitude to God something others can see, or does it stay hidden?
