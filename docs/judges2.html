1Now the angel of the LORD went up from Gilgal to Bochim and said, “I brought you up out of Egypt and led you into the land that I had promised to your fathers, and I said, ‘I will never break My covenant with you,

2and you are not to make a covenant with the people of this land, but you shall tear down their altars.’ Yet you have not obeyed My voice. What is this you have done?

3So now I tell you that I will not drive out these people before you; they will be thorns in your sides, and their gods will be a snare to you.”

4When the angel of the LORD had spoken these words to all the Israelites, the people lifted up their voices and wept.

5So they called that place Bochim and offered sacrifices there to the LORD.

6After Joshua had dismissed the people, the Israelites went out to take possession of the land, each to his own inheritance.

7And the people served the LORD throughout the days of Joshua and of the elders who outlived him, who had seen all the great works that the LORD had done for Israel.

8And Joshua son of Nun, the servant of the LORD, died at the age of 110.

9They buried him in the land of his inheritance, at Timnath-heres in the hill country of Ephraim, north of Mount Gaash.

10After that whole generation had also been gathered to their fathers, another generation rose up who did not know the LORD or the works that He had done for Israel.

11And the Israelites did evil in the sight of the LORD and served the Baals.

12Thus they forsook the LORD, the God of their fathers, who had brought them out of the land of Egypt, and they followed after various gods of the peoples around them. They bowed down to them and provoked the LORD to anger,

13for they forsook Him and served Baal and the Ashtoreths.

14Then the anger of the LORD burned against Israel, and He delivered them into the hands of those who plundered them. He sold them into the hands of their enemies all around, whom they were no longer able to resist.

15Wherever Israel marched out, the hand of the LORD was against them to bring calamity, just as He had sworn to them. So they were greatly distressed.

16Then the LORD raised up judges, who saved them from the hands of those who plundered them.

17Israel, however, did not listen to their judges. Instead, they prostituted themselves with other gods and bowed down to them. They quickly turned from the way of their fathers, who had walked in obedience to the LORD’s commandments; they did not do as their fathers had done.

18Whenever the LORD raised up a judge for the Israelites, He was with that judge and saved them from the hands of their enemies while the judge was still alive; for the LORD was moved to pity by their groaning under those who oppressed them and afflicted them.

19But when the judge died, the Israelites became even more corrupt than their fathers, going after other gods to serve them and bow down to them. They would not give up their evil practices and stubborn ways.

20So the anger of the LORD burned against Israel, and He said, “Because this nation has transgressed the covenant I laid down for their fathers and has not heeded My voice,

21I will no longer drive out before them any of the nations Joshua left when he died.

22In this way I will test whether Israel will keep the way of the LORD by walking in it as their fathers did.”

23That is why the LORD had left those nations in place and had not driven them out immediately by delivering them into the hand of Joshua.

Summary

The angel of the LORD comes up from Gilgal to Bochim, rebuking Israel for making covenants with the Canaanites instead of tearing down their altars, and warns these nations will remain as thorns and their gods a snare (vv. 1-3); the people weep and offer sacrifices there (vv. 4-5). After Joshua's generation dies, a new generation arises that does not know the LORD or His works, and Israel serves the Baals and Ashtaroth, provoking His anger (vv. 6-13). God hands them to raiders, then raises up judges who deliver them, yet each generation relapses into idolatry once the judge dies (vv. 14-19). The chapter closes with the LORD leaving the remaining nations to test Israel's obedience (vv. 20-23), establishing the cycle of sin, oppression, and deliverance that structures the rest of Judges.

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Samuel. Judges covers approximately 350 years (1380-1050 BC) between Joshua's death and the rise of the monarchy. Hebrew title: 'Shophetim' — 'Judges' (deliverers/ rulers raised by God). The book records Israel's repeated cycle: sin → oppression → crying out → deliverance → rest → sin again. The refrain: 'In those days there was no king in Israel: every man did that which was right in his own eyes' (17:6, 21:25). The book demonstrates humanity's need for a righteous king — ultimately fulfilled in Christ.
Historical Context: Chapter 2 is the theological introduction to the entire book of Judges. It contains three critical elements: (1) The Angel of the LORD's rebuke at Bochim (vv.1-5), (2) the death of Joshua's generation and the rise of a generation "which knew not the LORD" (vv.6-10), and (3) the introduction of the recurring cycle that defines the book: sin → oppression → crying out → deliverance → rest → sin again (vv.11-23). This chapter explains the theological framework for everything that follows. Israel's failure in Chapter 1 (incomplete conquest) now receives divine commentary — God declares He will no longer drive out the nations before them. The remaining Canaanites will serve as a test and a consequence. The chapter also introduces the concept of the "judges" — deliverers raised by God in response to Israel's groaning under oppression. Yet even this grace is met with ingratitude: each generation becomes "more corrupt than their fathers" (v.19).
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: An angel's rebuke delivered at a place later named for the weeping it caused, a covenant renewed at Gilgal answered by disobedience rather than gratitude, a generation that knew not the LORD rising the moment eyewitnesses to His works had died, a cycle of sin, oppression, crying out, deliverance and rest introduced as the book's whole shape, and each turn of the cycle spiraling downward rather than circling back to where it started
The Angel's Rebuke at Bochim (vv.1-5): "An angel of the LORD came up from Gilgal to Bochim" (v.1). This is likely a theophany — the Angel of the LORD (pre-incarnate Christ) speaks in the first person as God: "I made you to go up out of Egypt... I said, I will never break my covenant with you" (v.1). He moves from Gilgal (the place of Israel's first encampment and circumcision — a place of covenant renewal) to Bochim (which means "weepers"). God's indictment: "Ye shall make no league with the inhabitants of this land; ye shall throw down their altars: but ye have not obeyed my voice" (v.2). The consequence: "I will not drive them out from before you; but they shall be as thorns in your sides, and their gods shall be a snare unto you" (v.3). The people weep — hence "Bochim" — but their tears produce no lasting repentance. They sacrifice to the LORD (v.5) but do not change their behavior.
Joshua's Death and the Lost Generation (vv.6-10): The narrative flashes back to Joshua's dismissal of the people (v.6; cf. Joshua 24:28-31). Israel served the LORD during Joshua's lifetime and during the lifetime of the elders who had witnessed God's mighty works (v.7). Joshua dies at 110 (v.8) and is buried in Timnath-heres (v.9). Then the devastating statement: "there arose another generation after them, which knew not the LORD, nor yet the works which he had done for Israel" (v.10). This is the hinge of the book — the failure to transmit faith to the next generation. The generation that saw God's works believed; the generation that only heard about them did not. This indicts the parents who failed to teach (cf. Deuteronomy 6:6-9).
The Cycle Introduced (vv.11-23): Israel "did evil in the sight of the LORD, and served Baalim" (v.11). They "forsook the LORD" and followed the gods of surrounding nations (v.12). God's anger burns; He delivers them to "spoilers" and "enemies" (v.14). They cannot stand before their enemies (v.14). Yet in mercy, "the LORD raised up judges, which delivered them" (v.16). God was "moved to pity by their groaning" (v.18, ESV). But when the judge died, "they returned, and corrupted themselves more than their fathers" (v.19). Each cycle spirals downward — not a circle but a descending spiral. God's response: He will leave the nations as a test (v.22) — to prove whether Israel will walk in His ways or not.

Deep Dive

Setting: Chapter 1 showed the tribes failing, one after another, to drive out the Canaanites. Chapter 2 explains why and what it led to. It opens with God's own verdict, delivered at a place that came to be called Bochim, "weepers." Then the writer steps back to the death of Joshua and gives a summary of the whole period of the judges, perhaps three hundred years, in a few verses. This chapter is the key to the book. Every story from Othniel to Samson follows the pattern set out here: Israel turns from God, God hands them over to enemies, they cry out, God raises up a deliverer, and when the deliverer dies they turn away again, each time a little worse.

The Angel of the LORD at Bochim (vv1–5)

  • "The angel of the LORD went up from Gilgal to Bochim" (v1). Gilgal was Israel's first camp in the land, where the people were circumcised, kept Passover, and where Joshua met "the commander of the army of the LORD" (Josh 4:19; 5:2–15). JFB says the angel's coming from Gilgal "gave emphatic force to his rebuke," reminding Israel of how it began. Bochim's location is unknown. The Greek Septuagint adds "and to Bethel," and some connect it with the "oak of weeping" near Bethel (Gen 35:8).
  • Who is the angel? The word mal'ak means "messenger," human or heavenly. But this messenger speaks as God himself: "I brought you up from Egypt… I said, 'I will never break my covenant with you.'" In several Old Testament passages "the angel of the LORD" speaks and is treated as God (Gen 16:7–13; 22:11–18; Exod 3:2–6; Judg 6:11–24; 13:21–22). Many Christian interpreters, from the early church on, have seen these as appearances of the Son of God before his birth. Others think he is a created angel who speaks with God's full authority, as a royal herald spoke in the first person for his king. A few have suggested a human prophet, since prophets are sometimes called "messengers" (Hag 1:13), but this one is never named and does not act like one.
  • "I will never break my covenant with you" (v1). God's side of the covenant is unbreakable (Lev 26:44; Deut 31:6). Israel's side is the problem.
  • "You shall make no covenant with the inhabitants of this land; you shall break down their altars" (v2). These were the terms Israel received at Sinai and in Moab (Exod 23:32–33; 34:12–13; Deut 7:2–5). The altars mattered because the Canaanites' gods were the real danger. "What is this you have done?" echoes God's question to Eve in the garden (Gen 3:13).
  • "They shall become thorns in your sides, and their gods shall be a snare to you" (v3). God now announces what Joshua had warned: "a snare and a trap for you, a whip on your sides and thorns in your eyes" (Josh 23:13; Num 33:55). Because Israel would not drive them out, God will not either.
  • "The people lifted up their voices and wept" (vv4–5). Their grief seems real, and they name the place for it and offer sacrifices. But the rest of the chapter shows that weeping did not change their lives. JFB says, "the reformation was but temporary." There is a difference between sorrow over consequences and sorrow that turns from sin (2 Cor 7:10).
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry, following Bishop Patrick, holds that this "was not a created angel, but the Angel of the covenant," and concludes he was "the Son of God appearing as an angel," because only God could say "I made you go up out of Egypt." He calls Bochim, "weepers," a fitting name for a religious assembly, but warns that the tears did not bring a full reform. Guzik also takes the angel as the pre-incarnate Christ, while noting that "messenger" is a possible reading. He points out that God reminds Israel of his faithfulness before he rebukes them, and that the root problem in chapter 1 was spiritual, not military. JFB adds that Israel "had forfeited all claim to the stipulated benefits" of the covenant.

The Death of Joshua (vv6–10)

  • "When Joshua dismissed the people" (v6). The story steps back in time. Verses 6–9 repeat, almost word for word, Joshua 24:28–31. This is the second introduction to the book, and it starts again from Joshua's death to show the long view.
  • "Who had seen all the great work that the LORD had done" (v7). Israel stayed faithful as long as people who had seen God's acts for themselves were alive. Joshua 24:31 says "had known." Judges says "had seen." The faith of that generation rested on what they had witnessed.
  • "Timnath-heres" (v9). The same place as Timnath-serah (Josh 19:50; 24:30), with two Hebrew letters switched. Heres means "sun." Either spelling may be the original.
  • "Another generation after them who did not know the LORD or the work that he had done" (v10). One of the saddest verses in the Bible. "Know" (yada') in Hebrew means more than knowing facts. It means personal acquaintance and relationship. This generation knew about the LORD. They did not know him. Moses had commanded parents to teach their children about God and what he had done (Deut 6:6–7, 20–25), and the stones at the Jordan were set up so children would ask (Josh 4:6–7). Somewhere the passing on failed.
  • Commentators: Henry says the new generation "had so little sense of religion" and were "so entirely devoted to the world, so intent upon the business of it" that they were easily drawn after idols. Guzik presses the point that faith cannot be inherited secondhand. JFB calls the new generation "strangers to all the hallowed and solemnizing experience of their fathers."

Israel's Unfaithfulness (vv11–23)

  • "Did what was evil in the sight of the LORD" (v11). This exact phrase opens nearly every cycle in the book (3:7, 12; 4:1; 6:1; 10:6; 13:1). The standard is God's sight. At the end of the book, the standard will be "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (21:25).
  • "Served the Baals" (v11). Baal means "lord," "owner," or "husband." He was the Canaanite storm god, the rider of the clouds who brought rain and so fertile fields and herds. Texts from the ancient city of Ugarit in Syria describe him in detail. In a land that depended on rain, worshiping him looked like good farming. The plural "Baals" refers to the many local forms of Baal worshiped at different shrines (Baal-berith, 8:33; Baal-peor, Num 25:3). JFB says "the plural is used to include all the gods of the country."
  • "The Baals and the Ashtaroth" (v13). Ashtaroth is the plural of Ashtoreth, the Hebrew form of Astarte, a goddess of love, fertility and war. Her worship was tied to sexual rites. JFB calls the name one "denoting all the female divinities." Henry: "Baal and Ashtaroth, he-gods and she-gods."
  • "They abandoned the LORD" (vv12–13). Said twice. Israel probably did not think of it as abandoning the LORD, only adding gods. But the first commandment allows no additions (Exod 20:3). The Hebrew word for "abandon" ('azab) is the word for a husband deserting his wife.
  • "He gave them over to plunderers… he sold them" (v14). God did not just step back. He handed them over, as a master sells a slave. "The hand of the LORD was against them" (v15). This is what the covenant warned (Lev 26:17; Deut 28:25). JFB notes that "all these calamities were designed only as chastisements." The aim was to bring Israel back.
  • "Then the LORD raised up judges, who saved them" (v16). The Hebrew shophet means more than a courtroom judge. It means a leader who sets things right, who rules and delivers. Some judges held court (Deborah, 4:4–5), but most are known as military rescuers. They were raised up one at a time as God chose. JFB: "there was no regular, unbroken succession of judges," and "they had no power to make laws."
  • "For they whored after other gods" (v17). Strong language, used often in the Prophets. Israel was bound to the LORD like a wife to a husband, and idolatry was adultery (Exod 34:15–16; Hos 1:2).
  • "The LORD was moved to pity by their groaning" (v18). The word (naham) can mean to be sorry, to relent, or to feel compassion. God acted not because Israel had repented, since the text says only that they groaned, but because he pitied them. Every rescue in Judges is mercy. The same words describe God hearing Israel's groaning in Egypt (Exod 2:24).
  • "More corrupt than their fathers" (v19). The cycle is not a circle but a downward spiral. Each generation goes further. By the end of the book, Israel's worst sins are being done by Israelites to Israelites (chs 19–21).
  • "I will no longer drive out before them any of the nations" (vv20–21). God now confirms what he announced at Bochim. Israel broke the covenant, so the nations stay.
  • "In order to test Israel by them" (v22). "Test" (nasah) means to prove, to bring out what is really there. The nations left in the land would show whether Israel would walk in God's ways. God does not tempt anyone to sin (James 1:13), but he does allow situations that reveal the heart (Deut 8:2). Chapter 3:1–4 gives a second reason: to teach war to a generation that had never fought.
  • "He did not give them into the hand of Joshua" (v23). This connects back to Joshua's time. The nations that remained were not left by accident or because God failed. God had always planned a gradual conquest (Exod 23:29–30), and he now uses the remaining nations for his purposes.
  • Commentators: Henry lays out the book's pattern of sin, judgment, groaning, deliverance and relapse, "the same repeated in the same order," and notes that after each judge died, "they corrupted themselves more than their fathers." Guzik explains that Baal was tied to rain and harvest, so his worship promised prosperity, and he adds that Israel probably thought it could keep the LORD while adding other gods. He says the judges succeeded only because the LORD was with them, and he warns that God sometimes gives people what their hearts set on, as he left the nations Israel wanted to keep. The Tyndale notes add that although God planned this test, he does not set his people up to sin.

Pointing to Christ

Judges 2 shows the limits of every deliverer God raised up. The judges saved Israel "all the days of the judge," but when the judge died, the people fell back further than before. Israel needed a deliverer who would not die and leave them, and a change of heart that would last beyond one generation. Jesus is that deliverer. He "always lives to make intercession" for his people (Heb 7:25), and so he can save them "to the uttermost." The God who was "moved to pity" by Israel's groaning is the same God who in Christ "had compassion" on the crowds (Matt 9:36). And the next generation's fate does not have to repeat the cycle, because God promised to give his people a heart to know him (Jer 24:7; 31:34).

Reflection

  • Broken Covenant Despite God's Faithfulness (vv.1-2) — The Angel of the LORD reminds Israel of every promise He kept, then confronts them for allying with the very nations He told them to remove. Where has God's consistency exposed an inconsistency in your own obedience?
  • Tears Without Repentance (vv.4-5) — Israel wept bitterly at Bochim, yet nothing in their behavior actually changed afterward. Do you mistake feeling bad about sin for actually turning from it?
  • A Generation That Didn't Know the LORD (v.10) — After Joshua's generation died, the next one grew up ignorant of what God had done, apparently because no one had passed it on. What are you doing today to make sure your faith reaches the next generation, not just your own?
  • Mercy in the Middle of Judgment (vv.16,18) — Even as Israel kept sinning, God raised up judges to rescue them because He was moved by their suffering, not because they'd earned it. Where in your life is God's mercy showing up before you've cleaned yourself up?
  • A Downward Spiral Left Unchecked (v.19) — Each generation in the cycle grew more corrupt than the last once the judge who held them accountable was gone. What sin have you stopped resisting that's quietly gotten worse over time?
