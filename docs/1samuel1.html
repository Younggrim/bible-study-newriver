1Now there was a man named Elkanah who was from Ramathaim-zophim in the hill country of Ephraim. He was the son of Jeroham, the son of Elihu, the son of Tohu, the son of Zuph, an Ephraimite.

2He had two wives, one named Hannah and the other Peninnah. And Peninnah had children, but Hannah had none.

3Year after year Elkanah would go up from his city to worship and sacrifice to the LORD of Hosts at Shiloh, where Eli’s two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, were priests to the LORD.

4And whenever the day came for Elkanah to present his sacrifice, he would give portions to his wife Peninnah and to all her sons and daughters.

5But to Hannah he would give a double portion, for he loved her even though the LORD had closed her womb.

6Because the LORD had closed Hannah’s womb, her rival would provoke her viciously to taunt her.

7And this went on year after year. Whenever Hannah went up to the house of the LORD, her rival taunted her until she wept and would not eat.

8“Hannah, why are you crying?” her husband Elkanah asked. “Why won’t you eat? Why is your heart so grieved? Am I not better to you than ten sons?”

9So after they had finished eating and drinking in Shiloh, Hannah stood up. Now Eli the priest was sitting on a chair by the doorpost of the temple of the LORD.

10In her bitter distress, Hannah prayed to the LORD and wept with many tears.

11And she made a vow, saying, “O LORD of Hosts, if only You will look upon the affliction of Your maidservant and remember me, not forgetting Your maidservant but giving her a son, then I will dedicate him to the LORD all the days of his life, and no razor shall ever touch his head.”

12As Hannah kept on praying before the LORD, Eli watched her mouth.

13Hannah was praying in her heart, and though her lips were moving, her voice could not be heard. So Eli thought she was drunk

14and said to her, “How long will you be drunk? Put away your wine!”

15“No, my lord,” Hannah replied. “I am a woman troubled in spirit. I have not had any wine or strong drink, but I have poured out my soul before the LORD.

16Do not take your servant for a wicked woman, for all this time I have been praying out of the depth of my anguish and grief.”

17“Go in peace,” Eli replied, “and may the God of Israel grant the petition you have asked of Him.”

18“May your maidservant find favor with you,” said Hannah. Then she went on her way, and she began to eat, and her face was no longer downcast.

19The next morning they got up early to bow in worship before the LORD, and then they returned home to Ramah. And Elkanah had relations with his wife Hannah, and the LORD remembered her.

20So in the course of time, Hannah conceived and gave birth to a son. She named him Samuel, saying, “Because I have asked for him from the LORD.”

21Then Elkanah and all his house went up to make the annual sacrifice to the LORD and to fulfill his vow,

22but Hannah did not go. “After the boy is weaned,” she said to her husband, “I will take him to appear before the LORD and to stay there permanently.”

23“Do what you think is best,” her husband Elkanah replied, “and stay here until you have weaned him. Only may the LORD confirm His word.” So Hannah stayed and nursed her son until she had weaned him.

24Once she had weaned him, Hannah took the boy with her, along with a three-year-old bull, an ephah of flour, and a skin of wine. Though the boy was still young, she brought him to the house of the LORD at Shiloh.

25And when they had slaughtered the bull, they brought the boy to Eli.

26“Please, my lord,” said Hannah, “as surely as you live, my lord, I am the woman who stood here beside you praying to the LORD.

27I prayed for this boy, and since the LORD has granted me what I asked of Him,

28I now dedicate the boy to the LORD. For as long as he lives, he is dedicated to the LORD.” So they worshiped the LORD there.

4And when the time was that Elkanah offered, he gave to Peninnah his wife, and to all her sons and her daughters, portions:

5But unto Hannah he gave a worthy portion; for he loved Hannah: but the LORD had shut up her womb.

6And her adversary also provoked her sore, for to make her fret, because the LORD had shut up her womb.

7And as he did so year by year, when she went up to the house of the LORD, so she provoked her; therefore she wept, and did not eat.

8Then said Elkanah her husband to her, Hannah, why weepest thou? and why eatest thou not? and why is thy heart grieved? am not I better to thee than ten sons?

10And she was in bitterness of soul, and prayed unto the LORD, and wept sore.

11And she vowed a vow, and said, O LORD of hosts, if thou wilt indeed look on the affliction of thine handmaid, and remember me, and not forget thine handmaid, but wilt give unto thine handmaid a man child, then I will give him unto the LORD all the days of his life, and there shall no razor come upon his head.

13Now Hannah, she spake in her heart; only her lips moved, but her voice was not heard: therefore Eli thought she had been drunken.

14And Eli said unto her, How long wilt thou be drunken? put away thy wine from thee.

15And Hannah answered and said, No, my lord, I am a woman of a sorrowful spirit: I have drunken neither wine nor strong drink, but have poured out my soul before the LORD.

16Count not thine handmaid for a daughter of Belial: for out of the abundance of my complaint and grief have I spoken hitherto.

17Then Eli answered and said, Go in peace: and the God of Israel grant thee thy petition that thou hast asked of him.

18And she said, Let thine handmaid find grace in thy sight. So the woman went her way, and did eat, and her countenance was no more sad.

19And they rose up in the morning early, and worshipped before the LORD, and returned, and came to their house to Ramah: and Elkanah knew Hannah his wife; and the LORD remembered her.

20Wherefore it came to pass, when the time was come about after Hannah had conceived, that she bare a son, and called his name Samuel, saying, Because I have asked him of the LORD.

21And the man Elkanah, and all his house, went up to offer unto the LORD the yearly sacrifice, and his vow.

22But Hannah went not up; for she said unto her husband, I will not go up until the child be weaned, and then I will bring him, that he may appear before the LORD, and there abide for ever.

23And Elkanah her husband said unto her, Do what seemeth thee good; tarry until thou have weaned him; only the LORD establish his word. So the woman abode, and gave her son suck until she weaned him.

24And when she had weaned him, she took him up with her, with three bullocks, and one ephah of flour, and a bottle of wine, and brought him unto the house of the LORD in Shiloh: and the child was young.

25And they slew a bullock, and brought the child to Eli.

26And she said, Oh my lord, as thy soul liveth, my lord, I am the woman that stood by thee here, praying unto the LORD.

27For this child I prayed; and the LORD hath given me my petition which I asked of him:

28Therefore also I have lent him to the LORD; as long as he liveth he shall be lent to the LORD. And he worshipped the LORD there.

Summary

Elkanah, an Ephraimite with two wives, favors the childless Hannah even as her rival Peninnah torments her year after year on their family's trips to Shiloh (vv. 1-7). Deeply distressed, Hannah prays silently at the tabernacle and vows that if the LORD gives her a son she will dedicate him to God for life, with no razor ever touching his head (vv. 10-11). Eli the priest first mistakes her moving lips for drunkenness, then blesses her once she explains her grief (vv. 12-17). The LORD remembers Hannah, and she conceives and names her son Samuel (vv. 19-20). After he is weaned, she brings the boy to Eli and leaves him at Shiloh to serve the LORD for as long as he lives, fulfilling her vow (vv. 24-28).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Samuel (for the early chapters), Nathan, and Gad (1 Chronicles 29:29). Originally one book with 2 Samuel in the Hebrew Bible. Written approximately 1050-950 BC. The book covers the transition from the judges to the monarchy — from the birth of Samuel through the death of Saul (approximately 1105-1010 BC).
Historical Context: Chapter 1 opens in the closing days of the judges period, when Israel had no king and spiritual leadership was failing. The tabernacle is at Shiloh, where it had been since Joshua's day (Joshua 18:1). Eli serves as both priest and judge, but his house is corrupt — his sons Hophni and Phinehas are introduced here (v.3) and will be exposed in chapter 2. Into this setting of spiritual decline, God begins His work of renewal not through a mighty warrior or a political revolution, but through the prayer of a barren woman. Hannah's barrenness follows a biblical pattern: Sarah (Genesis 11:30), Rebekah (Genesis 25:21), Rachel (Genesis 30:1), and later the mother of Samson (Judges 13:2). In each case, God opens the womb to produce a child of special purpose. The name "Samuel" (Shemu'el) is explained as "asked of the LORD" — connecting to the Hebrew "sha'al" (to ask). Hannah's vow that "no razor shall come upon his head" (v.11) indicates a Nazirite dedication (Numbers 6:1-21), consecrating Samuel to God's service from birth, like Samson before him (Judges 13:5). But unlike Samson, Samuel will fulfill his calling faithfully. The chapter establishes the theological foundation for the entire book: God works through human weakness, answers desperate prayer, and raises up leaders according to His sovereign purpose.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: Renewal beginning not with a warrior or a political revolution but with a barren woman's prayer, the LORD stated twice to have shut up her womb rather than left it to chance, lips moving with no voice heard mistaken by the priest for drunkenness, a name explained by a wordplay, because I have asked him of the LORD, and a vow kept by lending the child back to the very God who gave him
Elkanah's Family and Hannah's Barrenness (vv.1-8): Elkanah is from Ramathaim-zophim in the hill country of Ephraim — though he is called an "Ephrathite" (likely meaning he is of the clan of Zuph, a Levite who settled in Ephraim; see 1 Chronicles 6:33-38). He has two wives: Hannah (meaning "grace" or "favor") and Peninnah (meaning "pearl" or "coral"). The polygamy here, as always in Scripture, produces conflict. Peninnah has children; Hannah has none. The text states twice that "the LORD had shut up her womb" (vv.5-6) — this is not random misfortune but divine sovereignty. Peninnah "provoked her sore" (v.6) — the Hebrew suggests persistent, deliberate irritation. This happened "year by year" (v.7) at the annual pilgrimage to Shiloh. Elkanah's love for Hannah is evident — he gives her a "worthy portion" (KJV) or "double portion" (ESV) — but his question "am not I better to thee than ten sons?" (v.8) reveals that he cannot fully understand her grief. Hannah's pain is not merely social (childlessness brought shame) but spiritual — she longs to participate in God's covenant promises through offspring.
Hannah's Prayer and Eli's Rebuke (vv.9-18): Hannah rises after the sacrificial meal and goes to pray at the tabernacle. She is "in bitterness of soul" (v.10) — the same phrase used of Naomi in Ruth 1:20. Her prayer is silent — lips moving, no voice heard. This was unusual; prayer in the ancient world was typically spoken aloud. Eli, sitting by the doorpost, assumes she is drunk — a sad commentary on what he has grown accustomed to seeing at Shiloh (perhaps reflecting the general moral decline). His rebuke is harsh: "How long wilt thou be drunken?" (v.14). Hannah's response is dignified and corrective: she is not a "daughter of Belial" (worthless woman) but a woman pouring out her soul before God. Her vow (v.11) is remarkable: if God gives her a son, she will give him back — dedicated as a Nazirite for life. She asks for a child only to surrender him. Eli's response shifts from rebuke to blessing: "the God of Israel grant thee thy petition" (v.17). Hannah's transformation is immediate — "her countenance was no more sad" (v.18). Faith has received the answer before the evidence appears.
Samuel's Birth and Dedication (vv.19-28): "The LORD remembered her" (v.19) — the same language used of Rachel (Genesis 30:22) and of God's covenant faithfulness generally. Samuel is born, and Hannah names him with a wordplay: "Because I have asked him of the LORD" (v.20). She keeps her vow — after weaning (typically age 2-3 in the ancient world), she brings Samuel to Shiloh with generous offerings: three bulls (or a three-year-old bull, per ESV), an ephah of flour, and wine. She identifies herself to Eli and declares: "For this child I prayed" (v.27). Her final statement uses the word "lent" — "I have lent him to the LORD" (v.28). The Hebrew "sha'al" (asked/lent) creates a wordplay throughout: she asked, God gave, and now she returns what was asked. Samuel's entire life is framed by prayer — asked in prayer, given through prayer, returned through prayer.

Map & Geography

  • Ramathaim-zophim (Ramah): In the hill country of Ephraim — Elkanah and Hannah's home, about 5 miles north of Jerusalem
  • Shiloh: The central sanctuary where the tabernacle resided, approximately 20 miles north of Jerusalem in Ephraim
  • The annual pilgrimage route: Ramah northward to Shiloh through the central hill country

Setting: The last days of the judges, roughly 1100 BC. Judges ended with "there was no king in Israel" (Judg 21:25), and Ruth ended with the name David (Ruth 4:22). The books of Samuel (one book in the Hebrew Bible) tell how Israel moved from judges to kings: through Samuel, the last judge and a prophet; Saul, the first king; and David, the king after God's heart. The writer is not named. Jewish tradition credited Samuel with the early part, but the book records his death (25:1), so others finished it. The story does not begin with a battle or a palace. It begins in a troubled household, with a woman who cannot have children and prays. The tabernacle stands at Shiloh in the hill country of Ephraim, where it has been since Joshua (Josh 18:1), served by the aging priest Eli and his corrupt sons.

The Birth of Samuel (vv1–20)

  • "A certain man of Ramathaim-zophim… whose name was Elkanah" (v1). The opening is almost identical to the openings of the stories of Samson's father (Judg 13:2) and Micah (Judg 17:1). The reader expects another dark Judges story. This one goes a different way. Ramathaim, "the two heights," is the full name of Ramah, Samuel's hometown (v19). Its exact site is debated, but it lay in the hills north of Jerusalem. "Zophim" connects it to the district named after Elkanah's ancestor Zuph (9:5).
  • "An Ephrathite" (v1). Here this means an Ephraimite, a man living in Ephraim, not someone from Ephrathah near Bethlehem as in Ruth 1:2. First Chronicles 6:22–28, 33–38 lists Elkanah and Samuel among the Levites of the Kohath clan. Levites lived in towns among the other tribes, so he could be a Levite by descent and an Ephraimite by residence. The long family line suggests a man of standing.
  • "He had two wives… Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children" (v2). Hannah means "grace" or "favor." Peninnah may mean "pearl" or "coral." Hannah is named first, probably the first wife, and Peninnah was perhaps taken because Hannah had no children. Scripture records polygamy without approving it, and every such household in Genesis is full of rivalry: Sarah and Hagar, Rachel and Leah. The writer simply states the painful fact.
  • "Year by year… to worship and to sacrifice to the LORD of hosts at Shiloh" (v3). The law called men to appear before the LORD at three festivals a year (Exod 23:14–17; Deut 16:16). Elkanah's "yearly" trip may be a family pilgrimage at one of them, probably the autumn Feast of Booths. This is the first time in the Bible that God is called "the LORD of hosts," YHWH tseva'ot. "Hosts" means armies, especially the armies of heaven. The title will run all through the prophets (more than 200 times). In a book about Israel's wars, it reminds the reader who commands every army.
  • "Where the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, were priests" (v3). They are mentioned in passing, but chapter 2 will expose them as "worthless men" who "did not know the LORD" (2:12). Phinehas bears the name of Aaron's faithful grandson (Num 25:7–13; Judg 20:28), which only makes his corruption sadder. Elkanah worships faithfully even though the priests are wicked.
  • "Portions… but to Hannah he gave a double portion, because he loved her" (vv4–5). Peace offerings were shared: part was burned, part went to the priest, and the worshiper's family ate the rest as a festive meal (Lev 7:11–18). The Hebrew of "double portion" is difficult. It may mean a double share or one choice portion. Either way it showed his love, and it probably made Peninnah's jealousy worse.
  • "The LORD had closed her womb" (vv5–6). The narrator says it twice. Hannah's barrenness is not an accident, and it is not presented as punishment. It is God's doing for a purpose not yet revealed. Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel and Samson's mother were all barren before bearing a child God had a special role for.
  • "Her rival used to provoke her grievously" (vv6–7). "Rival" (tsarah) is the technical word for a co-wife, and it sounds like the word for distress. "Provoke" means to vex or irritate, and it happened "year by year," at the very place and moment of worship. Hannah wept and would not eat the festival meal.
  • "Am I not more to you than ten sons?" (v8). Elkanah's love is real, and his question is tender. But it misses her grief. Being loved by her husband did not remove the shame of barrenness in that culture, or her longing for a child. Compare Ruth 4:15, where the women tell Naomi that Ruth "is more to you than seven sons."
  • "Eli the priest was sitting on the seat beside the doorpost of the temple of the LORD" (v9). "Temple" (hekal) means palace or sanctuary. By now the tabernacle at Shiloh had some permanent structure, with doors (3:15). Eli is sitting on a "seat," the word often used for a throne. He will fall from this same seat when he dies (4:18).
  • "She was deeply distressed and prayed to the LORD and wept bitterly" (v10). "Deeply distressed" is literally "bitter of soul," the same phrase used of the "angry fellows" of Judges 18:25 and close to Naomi's "call me Mara" (Ruth 1:20). Hannah takes her bitterness to God rather than to Peninnah.
  • The vow (v11). "If you will indeed look on the affliction of your servant and remember me and not forget your servant." She uses the language of God's care in Genesis: he "looked on" Leah's affliction (Gen 29:32) and "remembered" Rachel (Gen 30:22). She calls herself "your servant" three times. She promises that her son will be given "to the LORD all the days of his life, and no razor shall touch his head." That is the sign of a Nazirite, a person set apart to God (Num 6:1–8). Most Nazirite vows were for a set time, and a Levite's service ran from age 25 or 30 to 50 (Num 8:24–25). Hannah promises her son to God for life, like Samson (Judg 13:5). A Dead Sea Scroll of Samuel and the Greek translation make the Nazirite link explicit.
  • "Hannah was speaking in her heart; only her lips moved" (vv12–13). People in the ancient world normally prayed aloud. Silent prayer was unusual enough that Eli misread it.
  • "How long will you go on being drunk?" (v14). Festival feasting at Shiloh evidently included drinking, and some got drunk. Eli is quick to rebuke an anguished woman praying, yet he will be slow to rebuke his own sons' real sins (2:22–25; 3:13). His spiritual sight is as dim as his eyes will become (3:2).
  • "I have been pouring out my soul before the LORD" (v15). Her answer is respectful and clear. "Pouring out" is the picture of emptying a vessel completely (compare Ps 62:8, "pour out your heart before him"). She asks not to be counted "a worthless woman," literally "a daughter of Belial" (v16), the very phrase chapter 2 will use of Eli's sons (2:12). The irony is sharp: the woman Eli suspected is godly, and the sons he trusted are worthless.
  • "Go in peace, and the God of Israel grant your petition" (v17). Eli does not know what she asked for, but he blesses her. "Petition" is she'elah, from the verb sha'al, "to ask." This word will echo through the rest of the chapter.
  • "Her face was no longer sad" (v18). Nothing had changed yet. She was still childless, and Peninnah was still there. But Hannah had poured out her heart and received a word of blessing, and she believed it. She went and ate.
  • "The LORD remembered her" (v19). God had never forgotten her. "Remember" in Scripture means to act on behalf of someone in faithfulness to a promise, as God "remembered" Noah (Gen 8:1) and his covenant with Abraham (Exod 2:24).
  • "She called his name Samuel, for she said, 'I have asked for him from the LORD'" (v20). The name Samuel (Shemu'el) most likely means "name of God" or "his name is God." It sounds partly like sha'al, "ask," and partly like shama', "hear," as in "God heard." Hebrew name explanations are often wordplay on sound rather than strict etymology, and Hannah's point is clear: this child is God's answer to her asking. Some scholars notice that "asked" (sha'ul) is exactly the name of Saul, and the wordplay quietly anticipates the king Israel will soon "ask" for (8:10; 12:13).
  • Commentators: Henry traces the household's trouble to "Elkanah's marrying two wives, which was a transgression of the original institution of marriage." He describes Peninnah as one who "upbraided Hannah with her affliction," and observes that "being in bitterness of soul, she prayed." He takes the name to "signify asked of God." Guzik notes that the title "LORD of hosts" appears for the first time here and some 260 times in all, that Hannah's lifelong vow went beyond a Levite's limited term of service, and that God can speak a true blessing through an imperfect priest, as he later did through Caiaphas (John 11:49–52). He also reads Hannah's changed face as faith receiving the promise before it was fulfilled. JFB says Elkanah "practised polygamy," contrary to the original law, explains Ramathaim as a "double city," and notes that Hannah's portion was the honor given a favored guest. The Tyndale notes add that Elkanah was a Levite and suggest Hannah may have hoped her son would be a deliverer like Samson.

Samuel Given to the LORD (vv21–28)

  • "To offer to the LORD the yearly sacrifice and to pay his vow" (v21). Elkanah may have made his own vow, or he is joining in Hannah's. Under the law, a husband could cancel his wife's vow on the day he heard it (Num 30:6–8). By going up "to pay his vow," Elkanah confirms hers.
  • "As soon as the child is weaned" (v22). Children in the ancient Near East were usually weaned at around two or three years old (compare 2 Macc 7:27, "nursed you for three years"). Weaning was celebrated (Gen 21:8). Hannah keeps him for those precious years and then will bring him "so that he may appear in the presence of the LORD and dwell there forever."
  • "Only, may the LORD establish his word" (v23). No word from God has been recorded yet, only Eli's blessing and Hannah's vow. Elkanah's prayer is that God will bring his purpose for this child to pass. The Greek translation and the Dead Sea Scroll read "your word," meaning Hannah's vow.
  • "A three-year-old bull, an ephah of flour, and a skin of wine" (v24). The traditional Hebrew text has "three bulls," while the Dead Sea Scroll and the Greek have "a three-year-old bull." The ESV follows the second, which fits v25 ("they slaughtered the bull"). JFB agrees it is "probably the true rendering." A bull was an expensive offering, an ephah of flour was more than half a bushel, and the wine went with the sacrifices (Num 15:8–10). Hannah gives generously. "And the child was young" is a simple line that carries a mother's whole heart: he was very small.
  • "I am the woman who was standing here in your presence, praying" (v26). Some three years later she returns to the same spot. Eli may not remember her. She reminds him.
  • "For this child I prayed, and the LORD has granted me my petition" (v27). "Petition" is again she'elah. Her prayer was not general. She asked for this child, and God gave him.
  • "Therefore I have lent him to the LORD" (v28). "Lent" is a form of the same verb sha'al, "ask." It means "to give what was asked," or "to grant on request." So the verse runs: "I asked, the LORD gave what I asked, so I give back to the LORD what was asked." "Lent" can mislead English readers, as if she expected him back. "As long as he lives, he is lent to the LORD" means the gift is permanent. The chapter has played on sha'al seven times.
  • "And he worshiped the LORD there" (v28). "He" probably means Samuel, the small boy, bowing before the LORD, though some manuscripts read "they." Either way, the chapter that began with a woman too sad to eat at the place of worship ends with worship. Chapter 2 opens with Hannah's song of praise.
  • Commentators: Henry reads "lent" as a permanent gift: "I have lent him to the Lord as long as he liveth." He notes the size of her offering, taking the traditional reading of three bulls, "no less than three bullocks." JFB prefers the reading "a bullock of three years old" and says Hannah waited because his age would then "enable her to fulfill her vow." Guzik explains that the Hebrew behind "lent" means she had asked for him, so Samuel was the answer to her prayer and is now returned to the God who gave him. He also notes that Elkanah supported Hannah's vow when he could have overruled it, and that the family ends by worshiping together.

Pointing to Christ

Hannah's story follows a pattern that runs through the Bible: when God's people are in a dark time, he begins his rescue with a barren woman and a miraculous birth. Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Samson's mother and Hannah all lead up to two births in the Gospels: John the Baptist, born to barren Elizabeth, and Jesus, born to a virgin. Mary's song (Luke 1:46–55) borrows heavily from Hannah's song in the next chapter. Hannah gave her son to the LORD to serve at his house for life. The Father gave his Son, and the Son gave himself fully, to bring us into God's presence for good. And Hannah's poured-out prayer and changed face before anything had changed point to the access every believer has through Christ: "Let us then with confidence draw near to the throne of grace, that we may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need" (Heb 4:16).

Reflection

  • Enduring Grief in God's Presence (vv.6-7) — Peninnah provoked Hannah year after year over her barrenness, yet Hannah kept returning to worship at Shiloh rather than staying away in bitterness. Do you keep bringing your unresolved pain to God, or does disappointment quietly push you away from Him?
  • Honest, Desperate Prayer (vv.10-13) — Hannah poured out her soul in silent anguish before the LORD, making no effort to hide her grief or perform composure. Are you willing to bring God your raw, unedited struggles instead of a polished version of yourself?
  • Faith Before the Evidence (v.18) — After Eli's blessing, Hannah's sadness lifted and she began eating again before she had actually conceived. Do you let God's word settle your heart before you can see how things will turn out?
  • Giving Back What Was Asked (vv.27-28) — Hannah's entire purpose in asking for a son was to dedicate him back to the LORD's service. What has God given you that He may be calling you to release back into His hands?
