14O my dove in the clefts of the rock, in the crevices of the cliff, let me see your face, let me hear your voice; for your voice is sweet, and your countenance is lovely.

17Before the day breaks and shadows flee, turn, my beloved, and be like a gazelle or a young stag on the mountains of Bether.

Summary

The Bride compares herself to a common wildflower, and her Beloved calls her a lily among thorns, while she likens him to a fruit tree offering shade and sweetness (vv. 1-3). She recalls being brought to the banquet hall under his banner of love, so lovesick she asks to be revived, and she charges the Daughters of Jerusalem not to stir up love before its time (vv. 4-7). She then hears her Beloved's voice and pictures him bounding over the hills, calling her to come away now that winter has passed and spring has arrived (vv. 8-13). He longs to see her face and hear her voice, and she asks him to catch the little foxes spoiling their vineyards (v. 15). The chapter closes with her confident claim that they belong to each other and her invitation for him to return swiftly, like a gazelle on the mountains (vv. 16-17).

Authorship & Background

Author: Solomon (1:1). Also called 'Canticles' or 'Song of Songs' (the greatest song). A love poem celebrating marital love — the only extended treatment of romantic/sexual love in Scripture. Interpreted on multiple levels: (1) literal — celebrating human love within marriage; (2) allegorical — picturing God's love for Israel or Christ's love for the Church (Ephesians 5:25-32).
Classification: Wisdom Literature — Love Poetry
Key Themes: Spring as the reason to come away, a banner of love over the banqueting house, a beloved compared to a gazelle on the mountains, and small foxes named as the threat to a vineyard in bloom
Speakers: The Bride (Shulammite): vv.1, 3-6, 8-17; The Bridegroom (Solomon): v.2, 10-15 (quoted by Bride); The Bride to Daughters of Jerusalem: v.7; The Bridegroom's invitation (quoted): vv.10-14
Historical Context: Chapter 2 is among the most beloved and quoted chapters in all of Scripture's poetry. The Bride and Bridegroom exchange descriptions of mutual delight. The imagery shifts from the court (Chapter 1) to the countryside — flowers, orchards, vineyards, mountains, and springtime. The Bride describes herself with humility ("rose of Sharon, lily of the valleys") and the Bridegroom elevates her ("as the lily among thorns"). The central images include the banqueting house with love as his banner, the intimate embrace (v.6), the springtime invitation to "come away," and the covenant declaration "My beloved is mine, and I am his." The chapter also introduces warnings: the "little foxes" that spoil the vines (threats to love), and the charge not to awaken love before its time. Allegorically, this chapter pictures the joy of communion with Christ — His invitation to leave the old life and enter new life with Him, the mutual belonging of Christ and the believer, and the need to guard the relationship from small sins that would destroy intimacy.
The Rose of Sharon and the Lily (vv.1-3): She calls herself the rose of Sharon and the lily of the valleys, and the point is modesty rather than grandeur — these are common wildflowers of the coastal plain. He answers by turning it into distinction: as the lily among thorns, so is my love among the daughters. Her reply matches it with the apple tree among the trees of the wood, so the compliments are being traded in the same currency.
The Bride: His Banner Over Me Was Love (vv.4-7): The banqueting house and the banner are military images used domestically: a standard is what an army rallies to, and here it is love. Verse 5's request to be stayed with flagons and comforted with apples is the language of someone overwhelmed rather than merely pleased. Verse 7 is the first of three charges to the daughters of Jerusalem not to stir up love before it pleases, a refrain that acts as the book's brake.
The Bridegroom Comes Leaping (vv.8-9): She hears him before she sees him — the voice of my beloved, he cometh leaping upon the mountains, skipping upon the hills. The comparison to a roe or young hart is speed and lightness. Then the detail that makes it a scene rather than a description: he stands behind the wall, looking in at the windows, showing himself through the lattice.
Rise Up, My Love: The Winter Is Past (vv.10-14): The longest speech of his so far, and she quotes it rather than reporting it. The argument is seasonal: the winter is past, the rain over, the flowers appear, the turtledove is heard, the fig puts forth green figs. Spring is offered as the reason to come away, which is as close as the book comes to giving a motive for anything. The clefts of the rock and the secret places of the stairs suggest she is not yet out.
Take Us the Little Foxes (vv.15-17): The chapter turns abruptly to pests: take us the foxes, the little foxes that spoil the vines, for our vines have tender grapes. Small damage at a vulnerable stage, and the line has been read as everything from a folk song fragment to a warning about minor faults in a new relationship. Verse 16's “my beloved is mine, and I am his” is the book's most quoted statement of mutual possession, and it recurs in variations at 6:3 and 7:10.

Setting: Chapter 1 ended with the couple praising each other in the countryside, their "house" built of cedar branches. Chapter 2 continues the exchange and then moves into one of the best-loved poems in Scripture, a spring song in which the beloved comes bounding over the hills and calls her to "come away" (vv8–17). For the ways the Song has been read (as a celebration of married love, an allegory of God and his people, a two- or three-person story or an anthology of poems), see the Setting of chapter 1. The chapter is full of the plants and animals of the Israelite countryside: flowers of the coastal plain, fruit trees, gazelles, doves in the cliffs, foxes in the vineyards. It also contains the first of three refrains warning not to "stir up or awaken love until it pleases" (v7; 3:5; 8:4), one of the Song's main lessons. The ESV prints no section headings in the Song. It labels the speakers, and the headings below follow those exchanges and the ESV's paragraph breaks.

A Lily Among Brambles (vv1–3)

  • "I am a rose of Sharon, a lily of the valleys" (v1). She is speaking about herself. The famous hymn applies "rose of Sharon" to Jesus, but in the Song these are her words, and Guzik is blunt that the title belongs to her, though he notes Spurgeon's devotional use of it. The Sharon was the fertile coastal plain between Joppa and Mount Carmel, carpeted with wildflowers in spring. The "rose" (chavatselet) was almost certainly not a rose but a bulb flower, perhaps a crocus, asphodel or narcissus. The same word appears in "the desert shall… blossom like the crocus" (Isa 35:1). JFB argues for meadow saffron, a plant with a pungent bulb. The "lily" (shoshannah) may be a lotus, anemone or wild lily. Either way she is saying something modest: I'm just one wildflower among thousands. The Tyndale notes read it as humility.
  • "As a lily among brambles, so is my love among the young women" (v2). He takes her modest words and turns them into a compliment. Yes, a lily, but a lily in a thornbush. Next to her, every other woman is brambles. Guzik: the gift here is the experience of being preferred. The Tyndale notes: he agrees with her picture but says her beauty is rare.
  • "As an apple tree among the trees of the forest, so is my beloved among the young men" (v3). She answers in the same form. Wild forest trees give no fruit, but he stands out among the young men like a fruit tree. The "apple" (tappuach) may have been an apple, apricot or citron, and JFB calls it a general word for such fruit. "With great delight I sat in his shadow, and his fruit was sweet to my taste." In a hot climate, shade can save a life (Jonah 4:6), as the Tyndale notes observe. Back in 1:6 the sun had burned her as she worked the vineyards. Now she rests in his shade. "Shadow" is also a Bible picture of God's protection: "in the shadow of your wings I will take refuge" (Ps 57:1).
  • Commentators: Henry: "The world is a barren tree to the soul; but Christ is a fruitful one." JFB passes on Luther's warning that the bride must take care not to become a thorn herself through impatience, rash judgment and pride. Guzik, passing on Glickman, connects the shade with her earlier labor in the sun: she is no longer at others' mercy.

Sick with Love (vv4–7)

  • "He brought me to the banqueting house" (v4). Literally "the house of wine," a place of feasting and joy. The Tyndale notes: she now pictures him as her host and provider at a feast. Guzik suggests it may be a secluded outdoor spot.
  • "And his banner over me was love" (v4). A "banner" (degel) was a military standard, the flag each tribe camped under in the wilderness (Num 2:2). It showed whose army you belonged to. His love is that public. He isn't ashamed of her and wants everyone to know she is his. The Tyndale notes: he enjoys telling others how much he loves her. Some scholars suggest, from a related Akkadian word, "his intention toward me was love." Either way his love is plain to see.
  • "Sustain me with raisins; refresh me with apples, for I am sick with love" (v5). "Raisins" are raisin cakes, a festival food (2 Sam 6:19). Love has made her faint, dizzy and weak in the knees, like someone who needs to eat. "Lovesick" is an old idea, and the Song is honest about how overwhelming love can be. The Tyndale notes add that raisin cakes and apples were thought in the ancient world to stir desire.
  • "His left hand is under my head, and his right hand embraces me!" (v6). The picture is a tender embrace as they lie together, repeated in 8:3. The Tyndale notes take it as an intimate embrace. Guzik, passing on Kinlaw and Estes, reads vv4–6 as a dream or daydream of the embrace she longs for, not yet happening.
  • "I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, by the gazelles or the does of the field, that you not stir up or awaken love until it pleases" (v7). This is the first of three times she says this (3:5; 8:4). "Adjure" means put someone under a solemn charge, as under oath. JFB: "not an oath but a solemn charge." She swears not by God but by gazelles and does, graceful, shy creatures of the open country. Many scholars notice a wordplay. "Gazelles" (tseva'ot) sounds like "hosts" in "the LORD of hosts" (YHWH tseva'ot), and "does of the field" ('ayelot hassadeh) sounds like 'El Shaddai, "God Almighty." She may be invoking God's names in a playful, veiled way, suited to a love poem.
  • "Until it pleases" (v7). Love has its time. Don't wake it early by rushing into intimacy before it can rightly be fulfilled in marriage. The Tyndale notes: virginity is praised here, as in 8:8–9, and the young women should be cautious and not rush into love. Some take the line differently, as "don't disturb us now," but most see a lesson about timing. Guzik, passing on Glickman, distinguishes healthy restraint from shameful repression. Desire is good. It just needs to wait for its proper time. Paul's words fit: "it is better to marry than to burn with passion" (1 Cor 7:9).
  • Commentators: Guzik describes the difference between the rush of early attraction and the deeper attachment that grows over time, and warns that hurrying the first before the second has formed brings harm. JFB pictures a hunter moving gently so as not to scare off timid game, and applies it to a tender conscience before Christ. Henry: "Let those who have comfort, fear sinning it away." He also says the church is weak in herself but "strong in Him that supports her."

The Voice of My Beloved (vv8–9)

  • "The voice of my beloved! Behold, he comes, leaping over the mountains, bounding over the hills" (v8). She hears him before she sees him. "Behold" signals excitement. He is not dawdling. Nothing, hills or mountains, slows him down.
  • "My beloved is like a gazelle or a young stag" (v9). Gazelles are fast, graceful and sure-footed on steep ground (2 Sam 22:34; Hab 3:19). The Tyndale notes: a fast, graceful animal that overcomes obstacles to reach its goal. The gazelles of v7 come back, now as a picture of him.
  • "Behold, there he stands behind our wall, gazing through the windows, looking through the lattice" (v9). He has arrived at her family's house and is peeking in. Windows had no glass. A lattice was a wooden grille that let in air and light but kept out direct view. He can see a little, and wants to see more.
  • Commentators: JFB applies the lattice to how believers see Christ now: "He is only seen by faith, through the windows of His Word," not yet face to face (1 Cor 13:12). Henry links his coming over the hills to the hope of the Old Testament saints, who saw Christ coming through sacrifices and promises, and adds that Christ's absence "is but for a moment."

Arise, My Love, and Come Away (vv10–15)

  • "Arise, my love, my beautiful one, and come away" (v10). He invites her out of the house into the spring countryside. The invitation frames the whole poem, repeated word for word in v13.
  • "For behold, the winter is past; the rain is over and gone" (v11). In Israel, winter is the rainy season, roughly November to March, cold, wet and grey. The rains end and the land bursts into life. The Tyndale notes: after winter's gloom, spring's sights, sounds and smells stir romance.
  • "The flowers appear on the earth, the time of singing has come, and the voice of the turtledove is heard in our land" (v12). "Singing" (zamir) can also mean "pruning," and the ESV footnote gives that. Vines were pruned in spring. The Hebrew may hold both: the season of pruning and songs. Turtledoves are migrating birds that return to Israel in April, and their soft cooing was a sure sign of spring (Jer 8:7).
  • "The fig tree ripens its figs, and the vines are in blossom; they give forth fragrance" (v13). Figs set small early fruit in spring, and grape blossoms have a light, sweet scent. Everything is new and growing. Jesus used the fig tree the same way: "as soon as its branch becomes tender and puts out its leaves, you know that summer is near" (Mark 13:28).
  • "O my dove, in the clefts of the rock, in the crannies of the cliff, let me see your face, let me hear your voice" (v14). Rock doves nest in cliffs, hard to reach. She is shy, or hiding, or just out of his reach, and he wants to see and hear her. The Tyndale notes suggest playful hide-and-seek, and Guzik reflects on how much a beloved voice means. The cleft of the rock is also where God hid Moses as his glory passed by (Exod 33:22).
  • "Catch the foxes for us, the little foxes that spoil the vineyards, for our vineyards are in blossom" (v15). Foxes and jackals dug among the vines, gnawed the roots and ate the grapes. It is not clear who speaks: perhaps she, perhaps he, perhaps the two together, or her brothers. The "vineyards" are probably also their love itself (1:6). The Tyndale notes: the foxes may stand for any threat to the relationship, whether the usual ups and downs of love, rivals, or anything else that spoils it. Small, sly things do the most damage to a young love, and the plural "catch… for us" suggests both partners guard it together.
  • Commentators: JFB on the foxes: "'Little' sins are parents of the greatest," naming spiritual pride and an uncharitable spirit. Henry: "Whatever we find a hinderance to us in that which is good, we must put away." Guzik passes on Glickman's list of little foxes in marriage: uncontrolled desire, mistrust and jealousy, selfishness and pride, and an unforgiving spirit. He also passes on Hudson Taylor's point that the foxes are small, but the damage they do is great. JFB reads the winter as the old covenant of works now past, and the turtledove as an emblem of the Holy Spirit. Henry: "Arise then, and come away from the world and the flesh, come into fellowship with Christ."

My Beloved Is Mine, and I Am His (vv16–17)

  • "My beloved is mine, and I am his" (v16). This is the heart of the Song, and it returns twice, the second time in reverse (6:3; 7:10). Love belongs both ways. Each gives himself or herself to the other, and neither owns the other alone. It sounds like God's covenant words to Israel: "I will be your God, and you shall be my people" (Jer 7:23; Lev 26:12).
  • "He grazes among the lilies" (v16). He is a shepherd again (1:7). The Tyndale notes suggest the lilies may be the woman herself, since she called herself a lily (v1) and is often pictured as a garden (4:12–16).
  • "Until the day breathes and the shadows flee" (v17). "The day breathes" may be the evening breeze, the "cool of the day" (Gen 3:8), when shadows lengthen and vanish into dark. Or it may be dawn, when the morning breeze rises and night's shadows flee. Readers differ. Either way she wants him with her until then.
  • "Turn, my beloved, be like a gazelle or a young stag on cleft mountains" (v17). "Cleft mountains" translates bater, which may mean "mountains of separation," hills split by ravines, or may be a place name, Bether (so the KJV). The ESV footnote gives Bether. She calls him to come bounding to her over whatever separates them. The scene ends with longing still unfulfilled, which leads into the night search of chapter 3.
  • Commentators: Guzik takes v16 as mutual belonging and exclusivity, the search not for a perfect person but for the person who belongs to you. He notes that Spurgeon preached eight sermons on these two verses and asked which is the greater wonder, that Christ should be mine, or that I should be his. JFB reads "Bether" as "divisions," the mountains that separate the bride from Christ, which he crossed at his first coming. Henry: "Christ will come over every separating mountain to take us home to himself."

Pointing to Christ

Chapter 2 is about the joy of love, its right timing, and what threatens it, and it is first about a man and a woman. But its pictures echo through Scripture. A Bridegroom who comes leaping over the mountains, who calls "Arise, my love… come away, for behold, the winter is past," sounds like the gospel: the long winter of sin and exile ending, the new creation breaking out like spring (Isa 35:1–2; 2 Cor 5:17). The banner over his people is love, shown publicly on a cross (Rom 5:8). "My beloved is mine, and I am his" is the covenant made personal: "I am my beloved's" because "you are not your own, for you were bought with a price" (1 Cor 6:19–20), and he is ours because he gave himself for us (Gal 2:20). And the longing of v17 for the shadows to flee is the church's own prayer, "Come, Lord Jesus" (Rev 22:20).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik highlights the progression from longing (Ch.1) to enjoyment (Ch.2). The Bride has moved from self-doubt to confidence in her beloved's love. Guzik emphasizes the "little foxes" as the most practical warning in the Song: it is rarely the great crises that destroy marriages or spiritual life, but the accumulated small neglects and compromises.

Reflection

  • A Love That's Publicly Declared (v.4) — She describes his banner over her as love — a public, unashamed claim rather than something hidden. Are you willing to make your affection visible, or do you keep love guarded and private?
  • A Warning Against Rushing Love (v.7) — She charges the daughters of Jerusalem not to stir up or awaken love before its proper time. Where are you tempted to force something into being — a relationship, a decision, a blessing — before it's actually ready?
  • An Invitation Out of Winter (v.10) — Her beloved calls her to rise up and come away now that winter is past and spring has come. What dead or barren season are you still lingering in, when you've actually been invited into something new?
  • The Little Foxes That Spoil the Vines (v.15) — She calls for the small foxes to be caught before they ruin the blossoming vineyard. What small, easy-to-ignore habit is quietly eroding a relationship or your walk with God?
  • Mutual, Total Belonging (v.16) — She declares that her beloved is hers and she is his, a complete and exclusive claim on each other. Is there a part of yourself you're holding back from full belonging — to your spouse, or to God?
