2The godly man has perished from the earth; there is no one upright among men. They all lie in wait for blood; they hunt one another with a net.

4The best of them is like a brier; the most upright is sharper than a hedge of thorns. The day for your watchmen has come, the day of your visitation. Now is the time of their confusion.

Summary

Micah laments that Israel is like a picked-over vineyard with no good fruit left — the godly have vanished and people hunt one another for gain, with even judges and rulers demanding bribes (vv. 1-4). No one can be trusted, not even close family, yet Micah chooses to wait on the Lord, confident God will hear him (vv. 5-7). He tells his enemy not to gloat, for though he falls he will rise, and the Lord will be his light in the darkness until God vindicates him and shames those who mocked his faith (vv. 8-10). A day of rebuilding is coming for Israel even as the land now suffers for its sin, and Micah prays for God to shepherd His people again as in days of old, with wonders like those at the Exodus (vv. 11-15). The nations will watch in fear and humiliation before the Lord (vv. 16-17). The chapter closes in praise of a God unlike any other, who delights to forgive, casts sins into the depths of the sea, and keeps His ancient promises to Abraham and Jacob (vv. 18-20).

Authorship & Background

Author: Micah of Moresheth. Prophesied approximately 735-700 BC to both Israel and Judah. Contemporary with Isaiah. Key theme: social justice, true religion, and Messianic hope. Famous verses: 'But thou, Bethlehem Ephratah... out of thee shall he come forth' (5:2) and 'What doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?' (6:8).
Classification: Lament and Doxology — From Darkness to Triumphant Hope
Key Themes: Moral collapse of society, personal faith amid corruption, hope in God's faithfulness, forgiveness of sin, covenant faithfulness, incomparable God
Historical Context: Chapter 7 concludes the book of Micah with a dramatic emotional arc—from the depths of lament over social and moral collapse to the heights of doxological praise for God's incomparable mercy. The prophet surveys his society and finds it utterly corrupt: no godly person remains, every relationship is poisoned by distrust, and even family bonds have disintegrated. The description of societal breakdown (vv. 1-6) paints a picture so dark that Jesus Himself quoted verse 6 when describing the cost of discipleship (Matthew 10:35-36). In a world where no human can be trusted, the prophet makes his decisive turn: "Therefore I will look unto the LORD" (v. 7). This is the pivot point of the entire chapter—and arguably of the entire book. The final section (vv. 18-20) is one of the most beautiful passages on God's forgiving character in all of Scripture. The rhetorical question "Who is a God like unto thee?" is a wordplay on Micah's own name (which means "Who is like the LORD?"). The prophet's very identity is wrapped up in the answer: there is no God like YHWH—a God who pardons iniquity, delights in mercy, and casts sins into the depths of the sea.
Woe Is Me: No Upright Man Remains (vv.1-6): The prophet compares himself to someone gleaning after the harvest and finding no cluster to eat. What he cannot find is a good man: the best of them is as a brier, the prince asks for a bribe, the judge is for reward. Then the breakdown reaches the household — trust no friend, put no confidence in a guide, the son dishonors the father, a man's enemies are the men of his own house. Jesus quotes v.6 in Matthew 10 when describing what following him will cost.
Therefore I Will Look unto the LORD (v.7): The turn of the whole book, and it is a decision rather than a change of circumstances: therefore I will look unto the LORD, I will wait for the God of my salvation, my God will hear me. Nothing in vv.1-6 has improved. The word “therefore” reasons from the collapse to the looking, which is the opposite of what it should do.
Rejoice Not Against Me, O Mine Enemy (vv.8-10): The voice becomes the city's, addressing a gloating enemy: when I fall, I shall arise; when I sit in darkness, the LORD shall be a light unto me. The confession in v.9 is unusual in that it accepts the sentence as deserved — I will bear the indignation of the LORD, because I have sinned against him — while still expecting vindication. Both at once, rather than one instead of the other.
The Day for Building Thy Walls (vv.11-13): A short passage on reversal of scale: the day for building the walls, the decree far removed, and people coming from Assyria, Egypt, the river and the sea. Boundaries that had shut Judah in are described as widening. Verse 13 keeps it honest by leaving the land desolate in the meantime, for the fruit of their doings, so the promise does not cancel the judgment already pronounced.
Feed Thy People with Thy Rod (vv.14-17): A prayer rather than an oracle: feed thy people with thy rod, the flock of thine heritage. The request is for shepherding in Bashan and Gilead as in the days of old, and the answer promises marvels as in the day of coming out of Egypt. The nations are described licking the dust and coming with fear, which is the counter to the enemy gloating in v.8.
Who Is a God Like unto Thee? (vv.18-20): The book ends in a question that is also a pun. Micah's own name means who is like the LORD, so the closing doxology plays on it — his identity and his last words are the same claim. What follows is the reason: a God who pardons iniquity, does not retain his anger for ever, delights in mercy, subdues iniquities and casts all their sins into the depths of the sea. A book that opened with mountains melting closes with sins sunk out of reach.

Setting: Judah, late 700s or early 600s BC. This is the last chapter of Micah's third and final round of messages (chs 6–7). Chapter 6 put God's people on trial: God reminded them of everything he had done, set out what he requires, "do justice… love kindness… walk humbly with your God" (6:8), and pronounced sentence on a city of rigged scales and lying tongues. Chapter 7 begins with the prophet's lament over a society where no one can be trusted, not even family. Then, in a voice that seems to speak for faithful Israel as a whole, it turns to trust, confession and hope. The book ends with a prayer, God's answer, and a hymn of praise that plays on Micah's own name, "Who is like the LORD?" Jews still read these closing verses on the Day of Atonement.

Wait for the God of Salvation (vv1–17)

  • "Woe is me! For I have become as when the summer fruit has been gathered" (v1). The prophet pictures himself as a hungry man walking through an orchard after the harvest. Every grape and fig has been picked. "No first-ripe fig that my soul desires." The early figs of June were a delicacy (Isa 28:4; Hos 9:10). The fruit he is looking for is righteousness, and there is none. Jesus did the same when he came to a fig tree in leaf and found nothing on it, a sign of God searching his people for fruit (Mark 11:12–14).
  • "The godly has perished from the earth" (v2). "Godly" is chasid, a person marked by chesed, the faithful love God asked for in 6:8. Such people have vanished. Psalm 12:1 laments the same: "the godly one is gone." Compare God's challenge to Jeremiah to search Jerusalem for one person who does justice (Jer 5:1).
  • "They all lie in wait for blood, and each hunts the other with a net" (v2). Society has become a hunting ground. "The other" is literally "his brother." Fellow Israelites trap each other like birds or animals.
  • "Their hands are on what is evil, to do it well" (v3). The Hebrew is literally "both hands on evil, to do it well." They are skilled and diligent in wrongdoing. "The prince and the judge ask for a bribe, and the great man utters the evil desire of his soul; thus they weave it together." The three powers of the city, the official, the judge and the wealthy man, work together like weavers twisting threads into a rope. One wants something, another signs off on it, the court rubber-stamps it.
  • "The best of them is like a brier, the most upright of them a thorn hedge" (v4). Even the best of them wounds anyone who comes near, like a thorn bush that tears at your clothes. JFB pictures the thorn piercing whoever touches it.
  • "The day of your watchmen, of your punishment, has come" (v4). The "watchmen" are the true prophets who warned of judgment (Ezek 3:17). The day they foretold has arrived. "Now their confusion is at hand." Panic and bewilderment will seize those who scorned the warnings.
  • "Put no trust in a neighbor; have no confidence in a friend; guard the doors of your mouth from her who lies in your arms" (v5). When justice breaks down, trust breaks down too. A man cannot even confide in his wife without the risk that it will be used against him.
  • "For the son treats the father with contempt… a man's enemies are the men of his own house" (v6). The fifth commandment, "Honor your father and your mother," is overturned. Jesus quotes this verse when he warns his disciples that following him will divide families: "I have come to set a man against his father, and a daughter against her mother… And a person's enemies will be those of his own household" (Matt 10:35–36; Luke 12:53). The Mishnah, an early Jewish law collection, also quotes Micah 7:6 as a sign of the dark days before the Messiah comes. JFB reads it the same way.
  • "But as for me, I will look to the LORD; I will wait for the God of my salvation; my God will hear me" (v7). The chapter turns on this verse. "But as for me" is emphatic in Hebrew, just as in 3:8. When no one else can be trusted, the prophet turns to the one who can. "Look" is the word for a watchman scanning the horizon (Hab 2:1). "Wait" is patient, expectant hope. Habakkuk ends his book the same way: "yet I will rejoice in the LORD; I will take joy in the God of my salvation" (Hab 3:18). "My God will hear me." In 3:4 God refused to hear the corrupt rulers. He hears the one who waits for him.
  • "Rejoice not over me, O my enemy; when I fall, I shall rise" (v8). The speaker now seems to be Zion, the faithful people, speaking as one. The "enemy" is feminine in Hebrew, probably a hostile city or nation personified, such as Babylon, Nineveh or Edom (Obad 12). Falling is not the end: "the righteous falls seven times and rises again" (Prov 24:16).
  • "When I sit in darkness, the LORD will be a light to me" (v8). Darkness is exile and judgment. God himself is the light in it (Ps 27:1; Isa 9:2). The speaker is not denying the darkness but refusing to believe it is final.
  • "I will bear the indignation of the LORD because I have sinned against him" (v9). Here is real repentance. No excuses, no blame-shifting. The suffering is deserved, and it is accepted. Guzik draws on G. Campbell Morgan to distinguish remorse, which is sorry for its own pain, from penitence, which grieves the wrong done to God.
  • "Until he pleads my cause and executes judgment for me" (v9). This is the great reversal of the book. In 6:1–2 the LORD brought his "case" (rib) against his people. Now the same word is used: he will "plead my case" (yariv rivi), taking up their cause as their advocate. The Judge who prosecuted them becomes their defender. "He will bring me out to the light; I shall look upon his vindication." "Vindication" is tsedaqah, his righteousness, here meaning his faithful saving action (compare "righteous acts" in 6:5).
  • "Shame will cover her who said to me, 'Where is the LORD your God?'" (v10). That taunt was the cruelest one an enemy could throw at Israel (Ps 42:3, 10; 79:10; Joel 2:17). It mocked both the people and their God. When God acts, the question will be answered. "She will be trampled down like the mire of the streets." The enemy who trampled Zion will be trampled.
  • "A day for the building of your walls! In that day the boundary shall be far extended" (v11). The voice changes again, now addressing Zion with a promise. The walls are probably the stone walls around a vineyard (gader), a picture of God replanting his vineyard (Isa 5:5). The return from Babylon and Nehemiah's rebuilding of Jerusalem's walls fulfilled it in part (Neh 6:15). The word for "boundary" (choq) usually means "decree," so JFB weighs two readings: the hostile decree is removed, or the border is pushed out. Most modern translations follow the ESV.
  • "They will come to you, from Assyria and the cities of Egypt… from sea to sea and from mountain to mountain" (v12). Assyria and Egypt were the two great powers on either side of Israel. People will stream in from every direction. Some read this as the scattered exiles coming home. Others see the nations coming to Zion, as in 4:1–2, and Isaiah's promise that Egypt and Assyria will one day worship alongside Israel (Isa 19:23–25).
  • "But the earth will be desolate because of its inhabitants, for the fruit of their deeds" (v13). Before that day, the earth is judged. "Fruit" comes back from v1: the prophet found no good fruit, and the world reaps the fruit of what it has done.
  • "Shepherd your people with your staff, the flock of your inheritance" (v14). The prophet prays. The image goes back to 2:12 and 5:4: God is the Shepherd. The staff guided and protected the sheep, "your rod and your staff, they comfort me" (Ps 23:4). JFB notes it is a shepherd's rod, not the rod of punishment in 6:9.
  • "Who dwell alone in a forest in the midst of a garden land" (v14). "Dwell alone" recalls Balaam's blessing on Israel, "a people dwelling alone, and not counting itself among the nations" (Num 23:9), the same Balaam mentioned in 6:5. JFB points out the link. "Garden land" is literally "Carmel," a name for fertile ground. The flock lives in a thicket, a small remnant, but surrounded by good pasture.
  • "Let them graze in Bashan and Gilead as in the days of old" (v14). Bashan and Gilead, east of the Jordan, were famous cattle country (Deut 32:14; Amos 4:1). Assyria had taken them in 733 BC and deported the people (2 Kgs 15:29), within Micah's lifetime. The prayer asks God to give back the lost lands, as in the days of Moses and Joshua.
  • "As in the days when you came out of the land of Egypt, I will show them marvelous things" (v15). God answers the prayer directly. "Marvelous things" (niphla'ot) is the word for the wonders of the exodus (Exod 3:20; 34:10; Ps 78:12). He promises a new exodus, rescue as great as the first. Micah's whole final round has been built on the exodus: "I brought you up from the land of Egypt" (6:4).
  • "The nations shall see and be ashamed of all their might; they shall lay their hands on their mouths" (v16). Hand over mouth is the gesture of stunned silence (Job 40:4). The nations that boasted will have nothing to say. "Their ears shall be deaf," perhaps from the thunder of God's acts.
  • "They shall lick the dust like a serpent, like the crawling things of the earth" (v17). To lick the dust is the posture of total defeat (Ps 72:9; Isa 49:23). The serpent recalls the curse in Eden, "on your belly you shall go, and dust you shall eat" (Gen 3:14). The Tyndale notes connect this with the promise that the woman's offspring would crush the serpent's head (Gen 3:15).
  • "They shall come trembling out of their strongholds; they shall turn in dread to the LORD our God" (v17). Fear drives them out of their fortresses. "Turn in dread to the LORD" may mean surrender, but it also opens a door. The nations who come to the LORD trembling are not far from the nations of 4:2 who come asking to be taught his ways.
  • Commentators: JFB sees the family breakdown of v6 as the lawless state expected before Messiah comes, and Israel's refusal to despair in v8 as an echo of the Messiah's own faith in his hour of desertion. He reads the enemy as a woman and the trampling as retribution in kind. Guzik notes that when every human support fails, God can use it to drive people to the only one who will never let them down. With Morgan, he contrasts remorse and penitence in v9. He also sees in v15 a renewed relationship with the Shepherd bringing new wonders like the exodus. The Tyndale notes read "that day" in vv11–12 as including both the return from Babylon in 538 BC and the final restoration.

God's Steadfast Love and Compassion (vv18–20)

  • "Who is a God like you" (v18). Micah's name means "Who is like the LORD?" The book ends by answering its author's name with praise. The question also echoes the song Israel sang after crossing the Red Sea: "Who is like you, O LORD, among the gods?" (Exod 15:11). The new exodus of v15 calls for a new song. What makes God unlike every other god is not first his power but his pardon.
  • "Pardoning iniquity and passing over transgression for the remnant of his inheritance" (v18). "Pardoning" is literally "lifting" or "carrying" iniquity (nose' 'awon). These words come from the great self-description God gave Moses on Sinai: "The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love… forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin" (Exod 34:6–7). Micah has spent seven chapters naming sin. He ends by naming the God who forgives it.
  • "He does not retain his anger forever, because he delights in steadfast love" (v18). God's anger is real, as the whole book shows, but it is not his deepest delight. "He will not always chide, nor will he keep his anger forever" (Ps 103:9). "Delights in steadfast love" is chapets chesed. In 6:8 God asked his people to "love chesed." Here is why: he loves it first. Guzik stresses the point: the reason for God's mercy lies in God himself, not in his people.
  • "He will again have compassion on us; he will tread our iniquities underfoot" (v19). "Compassion" (racham) is related to the word for a mother's womb, the tender love of a parent (Isa 49:15). "Tread underfoot" is kavash, "subdue," the word used for conquering enemies (2 Sam 8:11). Sins are pictured as enemies, and God stamps them down. He does not only forgive sin. He defeats its power.
  • "You will cast all our sins into the depths of the sea" (v19). "Depths" (metsolot) is the same word used in the song of the sea for Pharaoh's army: "they went down into the depths like a stone" (Exod 15:5). As God drowned Israel's enemies in the Red Sea, he will drown their sins, never to rise again. JFB: sins buried "out of sight in eternal oblivion," not left on the shore where the tide could bring them back. Guzik passes on the well-known saying that God then puts up a sign: "No fishing." Psalm 103:12 has a similar picture: "as far as the east is from the west."
  • Tashlikh. On the Jewish New Year (Rosh Hashanah), many Jews still go to a river or the sea, recite Micah 7:18–20, and empty bits of bread from their pockets into the water. The ceremony is named Tashlikh, "you will cast," from the Hebrew of v19. And on the afternoon of the Day of Atonement, these verses are read after the book of Jonah, the story of a God who pardoned even Nineveh.
  • "You will show faithfulness to Jacob and steadfast love to Abraham, as you have sworn to our fathers from the days of old" (v20). The two great covenant words close the book: 'emet, faithfulness or truth, and chesed, steadfast love. God swore an oath to Abraham after the near-sacrifice of Isaac: "By myself I have sworn… in your offspring shall all the nations of the earth be blessed" (Gen 22:16–18). The book that began with God coming down in judgment (1:3) ends with God keeping his oldest promise. "From the days of old" echoes 5:2, the ruler "whose coming forth is from of old."
  • Commentators: Henry: "The pardon of sin is the foundation of all other covenant-mercies," and "God's people are a pardoned people, and to this they owe their all." He says God casts their sins into the sea "never to rise again," and pardons "because he delights in mercy." JFB says God subdues Israel's iniquities and buries them where they cannot resurface. Guzik makes several pastoral points. God's mercy does not shame his justice, because judgment itself can be a mercy, like a surgeon cutting out a cancer. Drawing on Spurgeon, he urges believers to trust God's word over their fears when doubts say they cannot be forgiven. If God is this merciful, believers have no excuse to withhold forgiveness from others. And God "subdues" iniquity, saving his people from sin's power, not only loving them in it.

Pointing to Christ

The book ends where the gospel begins. When John the Baptist was born, his father Zechariah sang that God had come "to show the mercy promised to our fathers and to remember his holy covenant, the oath that he swore to our father Abraham" (Luke 1:72–73). Mary sang the same: God "has helped his servant Israel, in remembrance of his mercy, as he spoke to our fathers, to Abraham" (Luke 1:54–55). They were singing Micah 7:20. The God who "pleads my cause" (v9) is the God who gives us "an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous" (1 John 2:1). The iniquity he "lifts" and carries away was carried by the Lamb of God (John 1:29). Where Micah saw sins thrown into the sea, Paul saw them nailed to the cross (Col 2:14). And the answer to Micah's question is still the same: there is no God like him.

Reflection

  • When Trust Disappears From Society (v.2) — Micah laments a culture where no godly person remains and even brothers hunt each other. When the people around you seem untrustworthy, does that drive you toward cynicism or toward a deeper reliance on God?
  • A Deliberate Choice to Look to God (v.7) — in the middle of total moral collapse, Micah makes a decision rather than waits for a feeling: he will look to the LORD. In your own discouraging seasons, are you waiting to feel like trusting God, or choosing to look to Him regardless?
  • Falling Is Not Final (v.8) — Micah tells his gloating enemy that even though he falls, he will rise, because the LORD will be his light in the darkness. When you fail, do you stay down out of shame, or get up because you know where your light comes from?
  • A God Who Delights to Forgive (vv.18-19) — the chapter closes marveling that God doesn't just tolerate sin but delights in mercy, casting iniquity into the depths of the sea. Are you still carrying guilt for something God has already thrown into the deep?
