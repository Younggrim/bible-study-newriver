1The Spirit of the Lord GOD is on Me, because the LORD has anointed Me to preach good news to the poor. He has sent Me to bind up the brokenhearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives and freedom to the prisoners,

2to proclaim the year of the LORD’s favor and the day of our God’s vengeance, to comfort all who mourn,

3to console the mourners in Zion—to give them a crown of beauty for ashes, the oil of joy for mourning, and a garment of praise for a spirit of despair. So they will be called oaks of righteousness, the planting of the LORD, that He may be glorified.

4They will rebuild the ancient ruins; they will restore the places long devastated; they will renew the ruined cities, the desolations of many generations.

5Strangers will stand and feed your flocks, and foreigners will be your plowmen and vinedressers.

6But you will be called the priests of the LORD; they will speak of you as ministers of our God; you will feed on the wealth of nations, and you will boast in their riches.

7Instead of shame, My people will have a double portion, and instead of humiliation, they will rejoice in their share; and so they will inherit a double portion in their land, and everlasting joy will be theirs.

8For I, the LORD, love justice; I hate robbery and iniquity; in My faithfulness I will give them their recompense and make an everlasting covenant with them.

9Their descendants will be known among the nations, and their offspring among the peoples. All who see them will acknowledge that they are a people the LORD has blessed.

10I will rejoice greatly in the LORD, my soul will exult in my God; for He has clothed me with garments of salvation and wrapped me in a robe of righteousness, as a bridegroom wears a priestly headdress, as a bride adorns herself with her jewels.

11For as the earth brings forth its growth, and as a garden enables seed to spring up, so the Lord GOD will cause righteousness and praise to spring up before all the nations.

Summary

Isaiah speaks as the Spirit-anointed Servant, sent to bring good news to the poor, bind up the brokenhearted, proclaim liberty to captives, and announce the Lord's favor — the year of Jubilee (vv. 1-2), a passage Jesus later read aloud in the Nazareth synagogue and declared fulfilled in himself (Luke 4:16-21). Those who mourn receive beauty instead of ashes and praise instead of despair, and they rebuild the ruins of generations past (vv. 3-4). God's people become priests and ministers named among the nations, sustained by others' wealth (vv. 5-6), and God promises them an everlasting covenant because he loves justice and hates wrongdoing (vv. 7-9). The chapter closes with the speaker rejoicing like a bridegroom, clothed in salvation and righteousness, as God causes righteousness and praise to spring up before all nations (vv. 10-11).

Authorship & Background

Author: Isaiah son of Amoz (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Messianic Oracle — The Anointed Preacher
Key Themes: The Spirit-anointed Messiah, good news to the poor, liberty to captives, the Year of Jubilee, beauty for ashes, the garment of praise, rebuilding ruins, priests of God, everlasting covenant, salvation and righteousness as garments
Historical Context: This is the chapter Jesus read in the Nazareth synagogue (Luke 4:16-21), stopping mid-sentence at "the acceptable year of the LORD" — because "the day of vengeance" (second half of v.2) belongs to His second coming, not His first. The speaker is the Servant/Messiah Himself, declaring His Spirit-empowered mission. The language draws on the Year of Jubilee (Leviticus 25): debts canceled, slaves freed, property returned. The Messiah is the ultimate Jubilee — setting everything right.
The Spirit of the Lord GOD Is upon Me (vv.1-3): The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me, because the LORD hath anointed me to preach good tidings unto the meek. This is the passage Jesus reads in the synagogue at Nazareth in Luke 4, and where he stops reading is part of the account: he closes the book after to proclaim the acceptable year of the LORD, leaving out the next clause, and the day of vengeance of our God. The commission is a list of beneficiaries rather than of duties, the meek, the brokenhearted, the captives, the bound, them that mourn. And the exchanges at the end are all three of the same shape, beauty for ashes, the oil of joy for mourning, the garment of praise for the spirit of heaviness.
They Shall Build the Old Wastes (vv.4-5): And they shall build the old wastes, they shall raise up the former desolations, and they shall repair the waste cities, the desolations of many generations. The word desolations occurs three times in one verse, and what is promised is construction work on ground that has been derelict for lifetimes. Then the labor supply, and strangers shall stand and feed your flocks, and the sons of the alien shall be your plowmen, which is the same reversal as 60:10.
Ye Shall Be Named the Priests of the LORD (vv.6-7): But ye shall be named the Priests of the LORD, men shall call you the Ministers of our God. The whole community is given the office, which Exodus 19:6 had promised and which 1 Peter 2:9 takes up. Then the arithmetic of restitution, for your shame ye shall have double, and for confusion they shall rejoice in their portion, therefore in their land they shall possess the double. The double of 40:2 was punishment received; the double here is compensation, and the same word is doing both jobs.
An Everlasting Covenant (vv.8-9): For I the LORD love judgment, I hate robbery for burnt offering, which puts the two halves of this book's argument in one sentence: the objection was never to the offerings but to what funded them. And I will make an everlasting covenant with them. Then the visibility of it, and their seed shall be known among the Gentiles, all that see them shall acknowledge that they are the seed which the LORD hath blessed.
As a Bridegroom Decketh Himself (vv.10-11): I will greatly rejoice in the LORD, my soul shall be joyful in my God, for he hath clothed me with the garments of salvation, he hath covered me with the robe of righteousness. The comparison is drawn from a wedding and it names both parties, as a bridegroom decketh himself with ornaments, and as a bride adorneth herself with her jewels. And the closing image is botanical and returns to the growth language of 45:8, for as the earth bringeth forth her bud, so the Lord GOD will cause righteousness and praise to spring forth before all the nations.

Deep Dive

Setting: Chapters 60–62 are the bright center of Isaiah's last section. Chapter 60 pictured Zion flooded with light and the nations streaming to her. Chapter 61 introduces the one who brings that good news, speaking in his own voice: "The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me." He sounds like the Servant of the earlier songs, on whom God put his Spirit (42:1), whom God called to restore Israel (49:5–6), and who was taught to sustain the weary with a word (50:4). He also sounds like the herald of 40:9 and 52:7. About seven centuries later, Jesus stood up in the synagogue of his hometown, Nazareth, read the opening of this chapter, rolled up the scroll, and said, "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:16–21). The whole chapter is one ESV section.

The Year of the LORD's Favor (vv1–11)

  • "The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me" (v1). Who is speaking? Some take it as the prophet himself describing his own calling. But the words fit the Servant too closely to miss: "I have put my Spirit upon him" (42:1), and the shoot of Jesse on whom "the Spirit of the LORD shall rest" (11:2). Jesus settled the question for Christians by claiming these words for himself. At his baptism the Spirit came down on him like a dove (Luke 3:22), and Luke says he returned to Galilee "in the power of the Spirit" just before reading this passage (Luke 4:14).
  • "Because the LORD has anointed me" (v1). "Anointed" is mashach, the root of Mashiach, "Messiah." In Greek it is Christos, "Christ." Kings and priests were anointed with oil to mark them out for their office (1 Sam 16:13; Exod 28:41). Here the anointing is with the Spirit. Peter preached "how God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power. He went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil" (Acts 10:38). JFB says he was anointed "as Messiah, Prophet, Priest, and King."
  • "To bring good news to the poor" (v1). "Bring good news" is basar again, the gospel word of 40:9 and 52:7. "The poor" (ʿanawim) means the afflicted and humble, people with no power who depend on God. When John the Baptist sent from prison to ask whether Jesus was the one, Jesus answered with this list: "the blind receive their sight… and the poor have good news preached to them" (Matt 11:5). The Beatitudes open with the same people: "Blessed are the poor in spirit… Blessed are those who mourn" (Matt 5:3–4).
  • "He has sent me to bind up the brokenhearted" (v1). "Bind up" is the word for bandaging a wound (1:6; 30:26). The "brokenhearted" are people whose hearts are shattered, like the "crushed in spirit" of Psalm 34:18 and the "contrite" of 57:15. God "heals the brokenhearted and binds up their wounds" (Ps 147:3).
  • "To proclaim liberty to the captives" (v1). "Liberty" (deror) is a technical word for the release of slaves and the cancelling of debts in the Year of Jubilee. Every fiftieth year, Israel was to "proclaim liberty throughout the land" (Lev 25:10), the line inscribed on the Liberty Bell. Debts were cancelled, slaves went free, and land returned to the families that had lost it. Jeremiah uses the same word for the slave release that Jerusalem's leaders broke (Jer 34:8–17). The Anointed One announces a Jubilee.
  • "And the opening of the prison to those who are bound" (v1). The Hebrew is literally "opening" and is normally used of opening eyes (42:7: "to open the eyes that are blind, to bring out the prisoners from the dungeon"). Prisoners kept in dark dungeons came out blinking. The Greek version translates it "recovery of sight to the blind," and Luke quotes that Greek form (Luke 4:18). Both readings capture part of the picture: release from darkness into light.
  • "To proclaim the year of the LORD's favor" (v2). "Favor" (ratson) means goodwill and acceptance. "The year" points back to the Jubilee year. 49:8 called it "a time of favor… a day of salvation," and Paul quotes that line: "now is the favorable time; behold, now is the day of salvation" (2 Cor 6:2).
  • "And the day of vengeance of our God" (v2). Jesus stopped reading in the middle of this verse. He read "to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor," then "rolled up the scroll and gave it back to the attendant and sat down" (Luke 4:20). He did not read "the day of vengeance." Most Christian interpreters see this as deliberate. His first coming brought the time of favor, and the day of judgment waits for his return (Acts 17:30–31; 2 Thess 1:7–10). JFB: "the interval from His first to His second coming is 'the acceptable year.'" Notice too the proportions: a year of favor, a day of vengeance (34:8; 63:4). Guzik makes the same point.
  • Vengeance and comfort together (v2). "Vengeance" here does not mean spite. It means God putting things right, punishing oppressors and setting the oppressed free (35:4: "your God will come with vengeance… he will come and save you"). For the captives, the day their captors are judged is good news. "To comfort all who mourn." The keynote word of 40:1 again.
  • "To give them a beautiful headdress instead of ashes" (v3). The Hebrew is a wordplay. "Headdress" is peʾer and "ashes" is ʾepher, almost the same letters rearranged. Mourners put ashes on their heads (2 Sam 13:19). Wedding guests and priests wore a turban or garland (Exod 39:28; Isa 61:10). The KJV's "beauty for ashes" is famous. The point is that the sign of grief on the head is replaced by the sign of celebration.
  • "The oil of gladness instead of mourning" (v3). Mourners did not anoint themselves with oil (2 Sam 14:2). Oil on the head was a sign of joy and honor at a feast (Ps 23:5; 45:7). Hebrews applies Psalm 45:7 to Christ: "God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness beyond your companions" (Heb 1:9). The Anointed One shares his anointing with mourners.
  • "The garment of praise instead of a faint spirit" (v3). The KJV says "the spirit of heaviness." "Faint" means dim or failing, like a smoking wick about to go out (42:3 uses the same word: "a faintly burning wick he will not quench"). Grief clothes people in heaviness. God gives a new set of clothes, praise.
  • "That they may be called oaks of righteousness, the planting of the LORD, that he may be glorified" (v3). "Oaks" (ʾelim) are great trees, terebinths or oaks, the largest and longest-living trees in the land. Mourners whose spirits were a flickering wick become tall, deep-rooted trees. "The planting of the LORD" repeats 60:21. Psalm 1:3 pictures the righteous as "a tree planted by streams of water." "That he may be glorified" also repeats 60:21. The goal of it all is God's glory.
  • "They shall build up the ancient ruins" (v4). The ones who were comforted become the rebuilders. The people restored in v3 restore their cities (58:12). Grace does not just rescue. It puts the rescued to work.
  • "Strangers shall stand and tend your flocks; foreigners shall be your plowmen and vinedressers" (v5). Foreigners take over the farm work. That is not to make Israel lazy but to free her for her real calling.
  • "But you shall be called the priests of the LORD… the ministers of our God" (v6). At Sinai God called Israel "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exod 19:6). A priest stood between God and people, teaching God's ways and bringing people near. Israel was meant to be that for the whole world. Here the calling is fulfilled. Peter applies Exodus 19:6 to the church: "you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood… that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light" (1 Pet 2:9). Revelation says Christ "made us a kingdom, priests to his God and Father" (Rev 1:6).
  • "You shall eat the wealth of the nations" (v6). Priests in Israel had no land of their own. They lived on the offerings of the other tribes (Num 18:20–21). In the same way, Israel as priest to the world is supported by the nations. Compare 60:5, 11.
  • "Instead of your shame there shall be a double portion" (v7). The firstborn son received a double portion of the inheritance (Deut 21:17). Elisha asked for "a double portion" of Elijah's spirit (2 Kings 2:9). Zion "received from the LORD's hand double for all her sins" (40:2). Now she receives double blessing in its place. Job's fortunes were restored twice over after his suffering (Job 42:10). "They shall have everlasting joy." The same promise as 35:10 and 51:11.
  • "For I the LORD love justice; I hate robbery and wrong" (v8). This is the reason behind the promises. God is not random. His people were robbed and wronged, and he loves justice, so he will put it right. The traditional Hebrew text reads "robbery with burnt offering," and the KJV follows it, meaning offerings brought from stolen goods (compare 1:11–15; Mal 1:13). With a change of vowels only, the same letters read "robbery and wrong," which the ESV and most modern versions prefer. JFB suggests "the spoil of iniquity." Either way God hates injustice, whether it is done to his people or dressed up as worship.
  • "I will make an everlasting covenant with them" (v8). This is the third time these chapters promise an everlasting covenant (55:3, with "the sure mercies of David"; 59:21, with the Spirit and the word). Hebrews speaks of "the blood of the eternal covenant" by which God raised Jesus (Heb 13:20).
  • "All who see them shall acknowledge them, that they are an offspring the LORD has blessed" (v9). God promised Abraham, "I will bless you… and in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:2–3). Now the nations see it plainly. Jesus: "By this all people will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another" (John 13:35).
  • "I will greatly rejoice in the LORD; my soul shall exult in my God" (v10). A new voice sings. It may be Zion answering, rejoicing in what she has received. It may be the Anointed One of v1, rejoicing in what God has done through him. Mary's song echoes this verse: "My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior" (Luke 1:46–47). Paul: "Rejoice in the Lord always" (Phil 4:4).
  • "He has clothed me with the garments of salvation; he has covered me with the robe of righteousness" (v10). Clothes in the Bible often picture a person's standing. Adam and Eve's fig leaves failed and God clothed them (Gen 3:7, 21). The high priest Joshua's filthy garments were removed and replaced with clean ones (Zech 3:3–5). The spider-web clothes of 59:6 could not cover anyone. These garments are a gift, "he has clothed me." Paul speaks of having "a righteousness from God that depends on faith" (Phil 3:9), and of putting on Christ (Gal 3:27). In Jesus' parable, a wedding guest without the wedding garment is thrown out (Matt 22:11–13).
  • "As a bridegroom decks himself like a priest with a beautiful headdress" (v10). "Decks himself like a priest" is one Hebrew verb formed from the word kohen, "priest." A bridegroom wore a splendid turban like a priest's. "Headdress" is peʾer again, the same word as in v3. The one who received a headdress instead of ashes now wears it to a wedding. "As a bride adorns herself with her jewels." Chapter 62 will develop the wedding picture: "as the bridegroom rejoices over the bride, so shall your God rejoice over you" (62:5). Revelation ends with "the holy city, new Jerusalem… prepared as a bride adorned for her husband" (Rev 21:2).
  • "As the earth brings forth its sprouts… so the Lord GOD will cause righteousness and praise to sprout up before all the nations" (v11). Growth is slow and quiet, but certain. Seeds do not grow by being pulled. They grow because God makes them grow (Mark 4:26–29; 1 Cor 3:6–7). Righteousness and praise will spring up as surely as spring follows winter. Guzik notes that we cannot manufacture this growth, only tend the soil. The "branch" and "planting" images of 60:21 and 61:3 finish here.
  • Commentators: Henry reads vv1–3 as "the grace of Christ, published by himself to a lost world in the everlasting gospel," with the release from Babylon as a type of it. He reads the priests of v6 as the church, "a royal priesthood, maintained by the riches of the Gentiles," and says righteousness and salvation are "the eternal matter of the church's rejoicing and thanksgiving." JFB and Guzik both see Jesus stopping at "the year of the LORD's favor" as dividing his first coming from his second. Guzik adds that prophecy can jump across long stretches of time without warning. JFB reads "oaks" as terebinth trees and the bridegroom's headdress as "a priestly headdress," which matches the Hebrew verb. Guzik explains "beauty" as an ornamental headdress, comparing Exodus 39:28, and notes that all believers share in Christ's anointing (1 John 2:20). JFB, unlike Henry, ties the promises of v7 to literal Israel in her land.

Pointing to Christ

This is the passage Jesus chose for his first sermon. Anointed by the Spirit at his baptism, he stood up in Nazareth and claimed every line up to "the year of the LORD's favor," then sat down and stopped. He preached good news to the poor, bound up broken hearts, freed captives of every kind, and opened blind eyes. He stopped before "the day of vengeance" because his first coming was for mercy, and the day of judgment waits for his return. Meanwhile he gives his people what this chapter promises: a headdress for ashes, oil of gladness for mourning, and the robe of righteousness for the rags they came in.

Reflection

  • The Spirit's Anointing for Mission (v.1) — The Messiah declares that the Spirit of the Lord is on Him, anointing Him to bring good news to the poor, bind up the brokenhearted, and proclaim liberty to captives. Do you rely on the Spirit's power for the tasks God gives you, or try to carry them in your own strength?
  • Beauty for Ashes (v.3) — Those who mourn are promised a crown of beauty instead of ashes, gladness instead of mourning, and praise instead of a spirit of despair. What ashes are you still wearing that you haven't let God exchange for something beautiful?
  • Rebuilding Ancient Ruins (v.4) — God's people will rebuild the ancient ruins and restore the places long devastated. Is there something broken from your past that God wants to use you to rebuild rather than just move on from?
  • Called Priests of the Lord (v.6) — God's people are called priests of the Lord and ministers of God, given a role that goes beyond what they could have earned. Do you see your calling before God as a genuine priesthood, or as something less significant?
  • Clothed with Salvation (v.10) — The speaker rejoices greatly in the Lord because He has clothed them with garments of salvation and covered them with a robe of righteousness. Do you try to produce your own righteousness, or receive it as something God has already put on you?
