1At the beginning of the reign of Zedekiah son of Josiah king of Judah, this word came to Jeremiah from the LORD.

2This is what the LORD said to me: “Make for yourself a yoke out of leather straps and put it on your neck.

3Send word to the kings of Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon through the envoys who have come to Jerusalem to Zedekiah king of Judah.

4Give them a message from the LORD of Hosts, the God of Israel, to relay to their masters:

5By My great power and outstretched arm, I made the earth and the men and beasts on the face of it, and I give it to whom I please.

6So now I have placed all these lands under the authority of My servant Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon. I have even made the beasts of the field subject to him.

7All nations will serve him and his son and grandson, until the time of his own land comes; then many nations and great kings will enslave him.

8As for the nation or kingdom that does not serve Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon and does not place its neck under his yoke, I will punish that nation by sword and famine and plague, declares the LORD, until I have destroyed it by his hand.

9But as for you, do not listen to your prophets, your diviners, your interpreters of dreams, your mediums, or your sorcerers who declare, ‘You will not serve the king of Babylon.’

10For they prophesy to you a lie that will serve to remove you from your land; I will banish you and you will perish.

11But the nation that will put its neck under the yoke of the king of Babylon and serve him, I will leave in its own land, to cultivate it and reside in it, declares the LORD.”

12And to Zedekiah king of Judah I spoke the same message: “Put your necks under the yoke of the king of Babylon; serve him and his people, and live!

13Why should you and your people die by sword and famine and plague, as the LORD has decreed against any nation that does not serve the king of Babylon?

14Do not listen to the words of the prophets who say, ‘You must not serve the king of Babylon,’ for they are prophesying to you a lie.

15For I have not sent them, declares the LORD, and yet they are prophesying falsely in My name; therefore I will banish you, and you will perish—you and the prophets who prophesy to you.”

16Then I said to the priests and to all this people, “This is what the LORD says: Do not listen to the words of your prophets who prophesy to you, saying, ‘Look, very soon now the articles from the house of the LORD will be brought back from Babylon.’ They are prophesying to you a lie.

17Do not listen to them. Serve the king of Babylon and live! Why should this city become a ruin?

18If they are indeed prophets and the word of the LORD is with them, let them now plead with the LORD of Hosts that the articles remaining in the house of the LORD, in the palace of the king of Judah, and in Jerusalem, not be taken to Babylon.

19For this is what the LORD of Hosts says about the pillars, the sea, the bases, and the rest of the articles that remain in this city,

20which Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon did not take when he carried Jeconiah son of Jehoiakim king of Judah into exile from Jerusalem to Babylon, along with all the nobles of Judah and Jerusalem.

21Yes, this is what the LORD of Hosts, the God of Israel, says about the articles that remain in the house of the LORD, in the palace of the king of Judah, and in Jerusalem:

Summary

God instructs Jeremiah to make a wooden yoke, wear it, and send similar yokes to the kings of Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon, with the message that God has given all these lands into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar, and that any nation that submits to Babylon's yoke will be allowed to remain in its land, while resistance means destruction (vv. 2-8). Jeremiah warns Judah's own king and people not to listen to prophets, diviners, and dreamers who claim they will not serve Babylon, since those prophets are lying (vv. 9-10, 12-15). Regarding the temple vessels not yet taken to Babylon, God declares that they too will be carried off and will remain there until He decides to bring them back (vv. 19-22).

Authorship & Background

Author: Jeremiah son of Hilkiah (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Sign-Act — The Yoke of Submission
Key Themes: Nebuchadnezzar as God's "servant," submit to Babylon or die, false prophets opposing submission, the temple vessels going to Babylon, God's sovereignty over all nations
Historical Context: During Zedekiah's reign (likely 594-593 BC), envoys from Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon came to Jerusalem — likely to discuss a joint rebellion against Babylon. God sends Jeremiah with yokes and a message to each king: submit to Nebuchadnezzar (whom God calls "my servant") or be destroyed. The most controversial of Jeremiah's messages: calling for submission to the pagan enemy. This made him look like a traitor.
Make Thee Bonds and Yokes (vv.1-2): Thus saith the LORD to me, Make thee bonds and yokes, and put them upon thy neck. This sign is not performed and finished like the smashed bottle, it is worn, and worn continuously in public. That detail is what makes chapter 28 possible: a symbol you carry on your body is a symbol somebody else can take off you.
Send Them to the Kings (vv.3-11): The message goes out by the hand of the ambassadors which come to Jerusalem, to the kings of Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre and Sidon. What that describes is a summit: five states with delegations in Jerusalem negotiating a coalition against Babylon, and a prophet walking into the middle of it wearing a yoke. The argument put to them starts from creation rather than from politics, I have made the earth, the man and the beast, and have given it unto whom it seemed meet unto me, and now I have given all these lands into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar the king of Babylon, my servant. And the practical counsel is survival, bring your neck under the yoke of the king of Babylon, and serve him and his people, and live.
To Zedekiah, Serve Him and Live (vv.12-15): The same counsel is given to the king of Judah in the same words, and then the warning about the advice competing with it, hearken not to the words of your prophets that speak unto you, saying, Ye shall not serve the king of Babylon, for they prophesy a lie unto you. What is being urged looks like collaboration and is presented as the only policy anyone survives, which is the position that makes Jeremiah hated by the war party and useful to nobody.
To the Priests, the Vessels Will Not Come Back Yet (vv.16-22): The specific claim circulating in Jerusalem is quoted, behold, the vessels of the LORD's house shall now shortly be brought again from Babylon, that is, the deportation of 597 was temporary and the temple furniture would be home within the year. The reply is that the remaining vessels will follow the first lot, and then a clause that is easy to miss, and there shall they be until the day that I visit them, then will I bring them up, and restore them to this place. Ezra 1 records them brought back and inventoried, seventy years later. So the prophecy agreed with the peace-prophets about the eventual return and disagreed with them only about when, which was the whole of the argument.

Map & Geography

  • Jerusalem (v.3, v.18, v.20): Capital of Judah; the holy city where the Temple stood.
  • Edom (v.3): Land south of the Dead Sea; descendants of Esau.
  • Moab (v.3): Land east of the Dead Sea; often in conflict with Israel.
  • Babylon (v.6, v.8, v.9): Capital of the Babylonian empire; place of Judah's exile (modern Iraq).

Setting: Jerusalem, 594–593 BC, the fourth year of Zedekiah (28:1). Three years earlier Nebuchadnezzar had taken King Jeconiah, the nobles and the temple's gold to Babylon and put Zedekiah on the throne under oath (2 Chr 36:13). Now there was talk of revolt. The Babylonian Chronicle records a rebellion inside Babylon itself in 595–594 BC, and the western kingdoms may have taken it as a sign that Nebuchadnezzar was weak. Envoys from five neighboring kings came to Jerusalem to plan a joint uprising. In this setting God sends Jeremiah into the streets wearing an ox yoke. Chapters 27 and 28 belong together. Chapter 27 is the sign and the sermon, and chapter 28 is the public clash it provoked with the prophet Hananiah.

The Yoke of Nebuchadnezzar (vv1–22)

  • "In the beginning of the reign of Zedekiah" (v1). Most Hebrew manuscripts read "Jehoiakim" here, which repeats 26:1 word for word. A few Hebrew manuscripts and the Syriac read "Zedekiah," and the ESV follows them with a footnote. The rest of the chapter clearly speaks of Zedekiah (vv3, 12), and 28:1 dates "that same year" to Zedekiah's fourth year. A scribe probably copied the opening of chapter 26 by mistake. The Greek version leaves the verse out entirely. "Beginning of the reign" here seems to mean the early part of the reign in a broad sense, not the accession year, since 28:1 says the fourth year.
  • "Make yourself straps and yoke-bars, and put them on your neck" (v2). An ox yoke was a wooden crossbar or frame laid over the necks of a pair of animals, held on by leather straps under their throats. The word for yoke-bar, motah, is the one Hananiah will break in 28:10. Jeremiah wore it in public as a living sermon. Isaiah walked barefoot as a sign (Isa 20:2–4), and Ezekiel lay on his side for months (Ezek 4:4–6). The message of the yoke was simple: bow your neck and serve.
  • "Send word to the king of Edom, the king of Moab, the king of the sons of Ammon, the king of Tyre, and the king of Sidon" (v3). All five appear in the cup list of 25:21–22. Their ambassadors had come to Jerusalem to plan the revolt. Egypt was likely encouraging them in the background. Jeremiah seems to have made yokes for the envoys to take home as well, which explains the plural in v2. A small kingdom like Judah hosting a summit of rebels was risky. God's prophet showed up with a message no one wanted.
  • "Give them this charge for their masters" (v4). God speaks here to foreign kings, who did not worship him. He is not only Israel's God. He rules the nations.
  • "It is I who by my great power and my outstretched arm have made the earth" (v5). The argument starts at creation. The Maker owns the earth, and he can give it "to whomever it seems right to me." Daniel learned the same lesson in Babylon: "the Most High rules the kingdom of men and gives it to whom he will" (Dan 4:17, 25, 32). Jeremiah prays in similar words in 32:17.
  • "Nebuchadnezzar, the king of Babylon, my servant" (v6). The title from 25:9 again. "I have given him also the beasts of the field to serve him." Even the wild animals fall under his rule. Daniel says almost exactly the same to Nebuchadnezzar's face: "into whose hand he has given… the beasts of the field" (Dan 2:37–38). The language recalls Adam's dominion in Genesis 1:28. For a time, God had placed the world under Babylon.
  • "Him and his son and his grandson, until the time of his own land comes" (v7). Babylon's rule would not be permanent. It would last about three generations. In fact Nebuchadnezzar's son Evil-merodach (Amel-Marduk) reigned two years (2 Kgs 25:27). He was murdered by his brother-in-law, and within twenty-five years of Nebuchadnezzar's death the empire fell to Cyrus in 539 BC. "Son and grandson" may be meant loosely, as a Hebrew way of saying "a few generations." This verse is missing from the Greek version, which is shorter throughout this chapter.
  • "But if any nation or kingdom will not serve this Nebuchadnezzar…" (v8). Resistance would bring "the sword, famine, and pestilence," the three killers of a siege (21:7, 9; 24:10).
  • "Your prophets, your diviners, your dreamers, your fortune-tellers, or your sorcerers" (v9). Five kinds of religious adviser, much like the list of forbidden practices in Deuteronomy 18:10–14. Diviners read omens, for example in animal livers or arrows (Ezek 21:21). Dreamers interpreted dreams. "Fortune-tellers" ('onenim) is uncertain. It may mean those who read clouds or signs. Sorcerers cast spells. Every royal court had experts like these, and all of them were saying the same thing: "You shall not serve the king of Babylon."
  • "It is a lie… with the result that you will be removed far from your land" (v10). The irony is sharp. The advisers promised freedom and independence, but listening to them would bring exactly what they claimed to prevent: exile.
  • "I will leave on its own land, to work it and dwell there" (v11). In Hebrew "serve" and "work" are the same verb, 'abad, used of tilling the soil (Gen 2:15). Serve Babylon, and keep working your own land. Refuse to serve, and lose the land altogether.
  • "To Zedekiah king of Judah I spoke in like manner" (v12). Jeremiah turns from the envoys to his own king. "Bring your necks under the yoke… and live." The message is the same as 21:9 and 38:17, given here years before the final siege. The road to life was humbling and unpopular, but it was open.
  • "Why will you and your people die?" (v13). This echoes the question God asks through Ezekiel at the same time: "Why will you die, O house of Israel?" (Ezek 18:31; 33:11).
  • "I have not sent them… they are prophesying falsely in my name" (v15). The prophets in Jerusalem used the LORD's name, not Baal's, and that made them more dangerous (23:21, 25). "You and the prophets who are prophesying to you." Those who listen to lies share the fate of those who tell them.
  • "The vessels of the LORD's house will now shortly be brought back from Babylon" (v16). In 597 BC Nebuchadnezzar had carried off the temple's gold vessels (2 Kgs 24:13), and some had already been taken in 605 BC (Dan 1:2). Their return would have been the clearest sign that the exile was over. The temple prophets promised it "shortly." Hananiah would soon name a date: two years (28:3).
  • Why the vessels mattered. When ancient armies conquered a city, they often carried off the statues of its gods as proof of victory. The LORD had no image in his temple, so the sacred vessels stood in its place. Losing them felt like losing God's presence. Getting them back would feel like getting God back.
  • "Serve the king of Babylon and live. Why should this city become a desolation?" (v17). Even now Jerusalem did not need to be destroyed.
  • "If they are prophets… then let them intercede with the LORD of hosts" (v18). A true prophet was also a man of prayer. Abraham (Gen 20:7), Moses (Exod 32:11–14) and Samuel (1 Sam 12:23) prayed for the people. If these prophets really had God's ear, they should be praying that what was still left would not be taken. Instead they were promising the return of what was already gone. The challenge is something like Elijah's on Carmel: let the true prophet be shown by what God does.
  • "The pillars, the sea, the stands" (v19). These were the great bronze works Solomon made for the temple: the two bronze pillars named Jachin and Boaz at its entrance, each about 27 feet tall (1 Kgs 7:15–22). There was the huge bronze basin called "the sea," resting on twelve bronze oxen, which held thousands of gallons of water (1 Kgs 7:23–26). And there were ten wheeled bronze stands for smaller basins (1 Kgs 7:27–37). They were too heavy to carry off easily in 597 BC. In 586 BC the Babylonians broke them up and hauled away the bronze (52:17–23; 2 Kgs 25:13–17).
  • "They shall be carried to Babylon and remain there until the day when I visit them" (v22). "Visit" is paqad again, God attending to something, here in mercy. "Then I will bring them back and restore them to this place." And he did. When Cyrus let the exiles go home in 538 BC, he gave back 5,400 gold and silver vessels to Sheshbazzar (Ezra 1:7–11). Belshazzar had drunk from some of them on the night Babylon fell (Dan 5:2–4, 30). The vessels went out, as Jeremiah said, and came back, as Jeremiah said. The false prophets' timetable was wrong, but God's promise came true.
  • What about the ark? The list never mentions the ark of the covenant. Scripture does not record its fate. A later Jewish tradition says Jeremiah hid it in a cave on Mount Nebo (2 Macc 2:4–8), but that is legend, not Scripture. Jeremiah himself had said a day would come when people would no longer miss the ark (3:16).
  • Commentators: Henry says, "Those that will bend shall not break," and that "prophets must be praying men." He adds that "those that encourage sinners to go on in their sinful ways will in the end perish with them." JFB favors "Zedekiah" in v1 and calls the yokes an "appropriate symbol" for kings planning to throw off Babylon. He also notes that "serve" and "till" are the same Hebrew root in v11, which the lexicon supports. JFB warns that "the devil often makes God's name the plea for lies." Guzik, citing Harrison, treats "Jehoiakim" in v1 as a copying error. He cites Cundall on why the vessels mattered in a temple with no idol. He contrasts this heavy yoke with Jesus' invitation to take his easy yoke (Matt 11:28–30).

Pointing to Christ

Jeremiah walked through Jerusalem with a yoke on his neck, calling proud nations to bow and live. The surrounding kings wanted freedom on their own terms, and the false prophets promised it, and it led to ruin. Jesus offers a different yoke and a truer freedom: "Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light" (Matt 11:29–30). Nebuchadnezzar's rule lasted three generations. The kingdom of God's true Servant, the one to whom "all authority in heaven and on earth" has been given (Matt 28:18), lasts forever. The temple vessels came back from Babylon as God promised. The true Temple, Christ's own body, was destroyed and raised on the third day (John 2:19–21), so God's people would never again need to fear that his presence could be carried away.

Reflection

  • God's Sovereignty Over the Nations (v.6) — God hands the surrounding lands over to Nebuchadnezzar, a pagan king, and calls him "my servant" even though Nebuchadnezzar doesn't know Him. Where do you need to trust that the powers you fear are still under God's control?
  • The Command to Submit (vv.8-11) — Nations that resist Babylon face destruction, while those who accept the yoke are preserved in their own land. Is there a hard circumstance where accepting rather than fighting is actually the wiser, more faithful path?
  • Discerning True Voices from Comfortable Ones (vv.14-15) — The false prophets tell the people exactly what they want to hear — resist, don't submit — even though God never sent them. How do you tell the difference between a message that's popular and a message that's true?
  • A Future Day of Restoration (v.22) — Even as the temple vessels are carried off to Babylon, God promises a day when He will visit His people and bring them back. What current loss are you waiting on God to reverse in His timing?
