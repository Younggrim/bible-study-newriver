Summary

A hymn combining creation and God's law. The heavens are described as declaring God's glory without needing words, their message going out to the whole earth, with the sun pictured as a bridegroom running its joyful course across the sky (vv. 1-6). The psalm then turns to praise God's instruction: his law revives the soul, his testimony makes the simple wise, his precepts bring joy, and his commands give light to the eyes (vv. 7-9), describing them as more desirable than gold and sweeter than honey, and a great reward to those who keep them (vv. 10-11). David asks to be cleared of hidden faults and kept from willful sin, and closes with the well-known prayer that the words of his mouth and the meditation of his heart would be pleasing to the LORD, his rock and redeemer (vv. 12-14).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Hymn of Praise / Torah Psalm
Attributed Author: David — "To the chief Musician, A Psalm of David"
Key Themes: Two books of revelation (creation and Scripture), the heavens' wordless testimony, the perfection and value of God's law, hidden faults, presumptuous sins, acceptable worship
Historical Context: C.S. Lewis called this "the greatest poem in the Psalter and one of the greatest lyrics in the world." The psalm moves from general revelation (creation, vv.1-6) to special revelation (Torah, vv.7-11) to personal response (prayer, vv.12- 14). The two halves use different names for God: "El" (God the Creator) in vv.1-6 and "Yahweh" (LORD, the covenant God) in vv.7- 14 — reflecting the two modes of revelation. Paul quotes v.4 in Romans 10:18 to argue that God's message has reached all peoples. The six descriptions of God's Word in vv.7-9 (law, testimony, statutes, commandment, fear, judgments) with six qualities and six effects form one of the most structured and beautiful passages in Scripture.
The Heavens Declare (vv.1-6): The heavens declare the glory of God, and the firmament sheweth his handywork. What the passage is careful about is the manner of the speech, and the paradox is stated outright, there is no speech nor language, where their voice is not heard. A wordless communication with universal range, and Paul takes up verse 4 in Romans 10:18. Then the sun is described as a runner and a bridegroom, which rejoiceth as a strong man to run a race, and its circuit is total, from the end of the heaven, and there is nothing hid from the heat thereof.
The Law of the LORD Is Perfect (vv.7-11): The subject changes from the sky to the scriptures and the form changes with it: six lines, each naming the law with a different word and pairing it with an adjective and an effect. The law is perfect, converting the soul, the testimony is sure, making wise the simple, the statutes are right, rejoicing the heart, the commandment is pure, enlightening the eyes, the fear is clean, enduring for ever, the judgments are true and righteous altogether. Then two comparisons, more to be desired are they than gold, sweeter also than honey and the honeycomb, so value and pleasure are both claimed.
Cleanse Thou Me from Secret Faults (vv.12-14): Who can understand his errors, cleanse thou me from secret faults. The turn to the first person is the third movement of the psalm, and the sins named are the ones the previous section's clear law cannot reach: the ones the singer cannot see. Then a request for restraint, keep back thy servant also from presumptuous sins. And the closing verse asks for something unusual, that the words of my mouth, and the meditation of my heart, be acceptable in thy sight, which puts speech and thought forward as an offering after eleven verses about other people's.

Deep Dive

Setting: "A Psalm of David," given to the choirmaster for Israel's worship. After the noise of battle in Psalm 18, this psalm steps outside under the open sky. C. S. Lewis called it the greatest poem in the Psalter. It moves in three steps: what the heavens say about God (vv1–6), what God's written word says (vv7–11), and what that word does to the person who hears it (vv12–14). Christians have long called these God's two books, creation and Scripture. The first book shows that God is there and that he is glorious. The second shows who he is and what he wants, and it leads the psalmist to see his own sin and his need of a redeemer. A small detail marks the shift. In vv1–6 God is called El, the general word for God. From v7 on he is called YHWH, "the LORD," his personal covenant name, seven times.

The Heavens Declare (vv1–6)

  • "The heavens declare the glory of God" (v1). "Declare" is a participle in Hebrew: the heavens are declaring, all the time, without a break. "Glory" (kavod) is weight, splendor, God's greatness made visible. The sky does not argue for God like a philosopher. It simply shows his work, the way a painting shows a painter.
  • "The sky above proclaims his handiwork" (v1). "Sky" here is raqia, the "expanse" or "firmament" of Genesis 1:6–8, the great dome overhead. "Handiwork" means the work of his hands. David has already sung, "When I look at your heavens, the work of your fingers" (8:3).
  • "Day to day pours out speech" (v2). The verb means to bubble up or gush like a spring (Prov 18:4). Each day hands the message on to the next, and each night hands it on to the next night, a relay that has never stopped since creation. Night has its own message: only in the dark can the stars be seen.
  • "There is no speech, nor are there words, whose voice is not heard" (v3). The Hebrew is very terse: "No speech, no words, their voice not heard." The ESV reads it as "there is no language where their voice is not heard." Most modern versions read it as a paradox: the heavens have no speech and no words, and their voice is not heard, yet (v4) their message goes out through all the earth. Both readings are possible, and both make the same point. The sky's message needs no translation.
  • "Their voice goes out through all the earth" (v4). The Hebrew says "their line" (qav), a measuring line, perhaps meaning their message has staked out the whole world. The ancient Greek translation read "their sound," and Paul quotes that in Romans 10:18. He applies it to the gospel's spread: Israel cannot say it never heard, because the message has gone out as widely as the sky's. Paul also says creation leaves everyone "without excuse" (Rom 1:20).
  • "In them he has set a tent for the sun" (v4). The surrounding nations worshipped the sun. In Babylon he was Shamash, god of justice, who saw everything as he crossed the sky and was pictured handing down the law on Hammurabi's famous stone. The psalm does not mention a sun god. The sun lives in a tent God pitched for it. It is a creature, not a god (Deut 4:19).
  • "Like a bridegroom leaving his chamber… like a strong man" (v5). Two pictures of joy and vigor: a bridegroom on his wedding morning, radiant and eager, and a runner who loves to race. Every sunrise looks like that.
  • "There is nothing hidden from its heat" (v6). Nothing escapes the sun. This line is the hinge of the psalm. In the very next breath David turns to God's law, which searches and warms in the same way, and by v12 he will be praying about "hidden" faults (the same root word).
  • Commentators: Henry calls creation and Scripture "two excellent books which the great God has published," and says the heavens expose the folly both of atheists, "who see the effect and yet say, 'There is no cause,'" and of idolaters, who give glory to "the lights of heaven which those very lights directed them to give to God only." JFB: "uttereth—pours forth as a stream; a perpetual testimony," and he notes Paul's use of the Greek "sound" in Romans 10, "which gives the same sense." Guzik passes on Kidner's comment that the sky is simply the sun's tent and track, with no myth in it, and Kidner's point that "knowledge" is well matched with night, since the night sky shows what daylight hides.

The Law of the LORD Is Perfect (vv7–11)

  • Six names, six qualities, six effects (vv7–9). Each line names God's word in a different way, says what it is like, and says what it does. The names: law (torah, instruction), testimony (God's own witness to himself), precepts (specific charges), commandment, fear, and rules (mishpatim, judgments or decisions). Psalm 119 uses the same family of words for 176 verses. David has the books of Moses in mind, but the words fit all of Scripture.
  • "The law of the LORD is perfect, reviving the soul" (v7). "Perfect" (tamim) means whole, complete, lacking nothing, the same word used of God's way in 18:30. "Reviving" means bringing back, restoring. The KJV's "converting" is possible, but the sense is closer to Psalm 23:3: "he restores my soul." The word brings a weary or wandering soul back to life.
  • "The testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple" (v7). "Sure" means firm, reliable, confirmed. The "simple" (peti) is the open-minded, easily led person of Proverbs (Prov 1:4; 14:15), not a fool, just untaught. God's word gives that person a steady footing. Paul told Timothy the Scriptures "are able to make you wise for salvation" (2 Tim 3:15).
  • "The precepts of the LORD are right, rejoicing the heart" (v8). "Right" means straight, upright. God's commands are not a burden that kills joy. They make the heart glad, because they show the way life actually works.
  • "The commandment of the LORD is pure, enlightening the eyes" (v8). "Pure" (bar) is clear, like a cloudless sky, and it gives light, like the sun of v6. "Enlightening the eyes" meant renewed strength and life, as when Jonathan ate honey and "his eyes became bright" (1 Sam 14:27). The same honey turns up two lines later.
  • "The fear of the LORD is clean, enduring forever" (v9). "Fear" is an unusual name for God's word. It means the reverent awe the word produces, the right response to it. Unlike pagan religion, which could be unclean and cruel, true worship is "clean" and does not wear out.
  • "The rules of the LORD are true, and righteous altogether" (v9). Not just true here and there, but true taken all together. There is no part of God's word that is false or unjust.
  • "More to be desired are they than gold… sweeter also than honey" (v10). Gold was the most precious thing a person could own. Honey was the sweetest thing a person could taste. God's word is worth more than the one and sweeter than the other (119:72, 103). Ezekiel ate a scroll and found it "as sweet as honey" (Ezek 3:3).
  • "By them is your servant warned; in keeping them there is great reward" (v11). The word does two things: it warns, like a sentry calling out danger, and it rewards. "Reward" (eqev) means the result at the end, what follows on. Obeying God has its own fruit.
  • Commentators: JFB sums up the law's effects: it "revives those depressed by doubts, makes wise the unskilled… rejoices the lover of truth," and "provides permanent principles of conduct." Henry compares the pleasures of sense, which "will soon surfeit, and yet never satisfy," with the pleasures of God's word, which "are substantial and satisfying, and there is no danger of exceeding in them." Guzik passes on Kidner's link to Kant, who marveled at "the starry heavens above and the moral law within," and Spurgeon's line that the wisest person reads both the world-book and the Word-book.

Hidden Faults and Presumptuous Sins (vv12–13)

  • "Who can discern his errors?" (v12). The word has done its work. Once David looks into God's perfect law, he sees himself, and he is not sure he sees everything. "Errors" (shegiot) are sins of wandering or ignorance. The Law of Moses provided sacrifices for sins committed "unintentionally" (Lev 4:2; Num 15:27–29), sins a person did not realize at the time were sins.
  • "Declare me innocent from hidden faults" (v12). "Hidden" here means hidden from David himself, not from God (90:8). These are the blind spots every believer has. He asks God to acquit him of them, to clear him, since he cannot even list them to confess them.
  • "Keep back your servant also from presumptuous sins" (v13). "Presumptuous" (zedim) means proud, arrogant, insolent. The Law said that someone who sinned "with a high hand," knowingly and defiantly, had no sacrifice to cover it (Num 15:30–31). The Hebrew can also be read as "proud men," arrogant people who pull a believer into sin (as in 86:14). Either way, David knows his own heart could drift from wandering into willful defiance, and he asks God to hold him back.
  • "Let them not have dominion over me!" (v13). Sin can rule a person like a master (Gen 4:7). Paul uses the same picture: "sin will have no dominion over you, since you are not under law but under grace" (Rom 6:14).
  • "Then I shall be blameless, and innocent of great transgression" (v13). "Blameless" again (tamim), the same word used for God's perfect law in v7. "Great transgression" (pesha) is open rebellion, breaking with God altogether. David's hope is not to become sinless but to be kept from sin's rule and from that final break.
  • Commentators: Henry says secret faults are "such as were hidden from his own observation of himself," and that "even secret faults are defiling" but are cleansed through pardon (1 John 1:7). Presumptuous sins, he says, are those "which most offend God and wound conscience." JFB: "The clearer our view of the law, the more manifest are our sins." Guzik passes on Clarke's list of secret faults, sins forgotten, sins of ignorance and sins done in private, and Spurgeon's reminder that this was the prayer of a believer, not a careless sinner.

My Rock and My Redeemer (v14)

  • "Let the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart" (v14). Outward words and inward thoughts. The heavens spoke without words (v3). Now David offers words. "Meditation" (higgayon) is the murmuring, pondering thought of 1:2.
  • "Be acceptable in your sight" (v14). "Acceptable" (ratson) is the word for a sacrifice God accepts with favor (Lev 1:3; 22:20). David offers his words and thoughts as an offering. Hebrews 13:15 calls praise "the fruit of lips."
  • "O LORD, my rock and my redeemer" (v14). "Rock" (tsur) recalls the strong, sheltering God of 18:2. "Redeemer" (goel) is the family member who pays to buy back a relative sold into slavery, recovers lost land, or rescues a widow's family line, as Boaz did for Ruth (Lev 25:25, 47–49; Ruth 4:4–6). The psalm that began with the vast sky ends with a God close enough to be family, the one who pays the price to buy his servant back.
  • Commentators: JFB says that only by grace can "our words and thoughts" become "acceptable to God." Guzik passes on Kidner's point that "acceptable" belongs to the language of sacrifice, and Morgan's comment that if our Rock were not our Redeemer, we should be without hope.

Pointing to Christ

The heavens show God's glory without words, and the law shows his will in words. But the psalm ends with a need neither book can meet by itself. It ends with a sinner who cannot see all his own faults and asks for a redeemer. John's Gospel begins where this psalm begins, with creation, and then says that the Word through whom all things were made "became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory" (John 1:3, 14). Jesus is the light that no one can hide from (John 1:9; 3:20–21), the one who kept God's law perfectly, and the kinsman-redeemer who paid the price to buy his people back (1 Pet 1:18–19). In him the God of the skies becomes "my rock and my redeemer."

Reflection

  • Creation Preaches to Everyone (v.1) — The heavens declare God's glory without a single word being spoken, reaching every person regardless of language. Do you still notice God's voice in creation, or has it become background noise?
  • God's Word Does Specific Work (v.7) — Scripture is described as reviving the soul and making the simple wise, not just informing the mind. Where in your life do you need God's Word to revive or instruct you right now?
  • Valuing Scripture Above Gold and Honey (v.10) — David describes God's words as more desirable than wealth and sweeter than the richest food. Does your actual time and attention reflect that Scripture holds this kind of value for you?
  • Asking God to Reveal Hidden Faults (v.12) — David acknowledges sins he cannot even see in himself and asks God to cleanse him from them. Are you willing to ask God to search out blind spots you cannot identify on your own?
  • Guarding Against Deliberate Sin (v.13) — David prays not just for cleansing from hidden faults but for God to keep him from willful rebellion. What deliberate sin do you need God's help resisting before it takes hold?
