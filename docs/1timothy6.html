Summary

Paul instructs slaves to honor their masters, warns against those who teach otherwise for personal gain, and identifies the love of money — not money itself — as a root of all kinds of evil that has led some away from the faith (vv. 1-10). He calls godliness paired with contentment great gain, noting that believers brought nothing into the world and can take nothing out, so food and covering should be enough (vv. 6-8). Timothy is charged to flee these temptations and instead pursue righteousness, godliness, faith, love, endurance, and gentleness, to fight the good fight of faith, and to keep the command he has received without fault until Christ appears, a charge Paul reinforces with praise to God as the only sovereign Ruler (vv. 11-16). He instructs the wealthy not to trust in their riches but in God, and to be generous and ready to share, storing up treasure for the life to come (vv. 17-19). The letter closes with a final charge to guard what has been entrusted to him and avoid the godless chatter and false knowledge that some have wandered into (vv. 20-21).

Authorship & Background

Author: Paul the Apostle (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 6 concludes 1 Timothy with instructions about servants and masters (vv.1-2), a warning against false teachers who use religion for profit (vv.3-5), the famous passage on contentment and the love of money (vv.6-10), a personal charge to Timothy (vv.11-16), instructions for the wealthy (vv.17-19), and a final appeal to guard the deposit (vv.20-21).
Classification: Epistle — Pauline, Pastoral Epistle
Key Themes: Servants instructed to honor believing masters more rather than less, gain mistaken for godliness named as the error underneath the false teaching, contentment paired with godliness as great gain against a love of money called a root of all evil, a charge to fight the good fight framed by a doxology to the King of kings, and a closing command to guard a deposit rather than to add to it
Servants and Masters (vv.1-2): Servants should honor their masters so God's name isn't blasphemed (v.1). Those with believing masters should serve even better — not less — because they're brothers (v.2).
False Teachers and Greed (vv.3-5): Those who teach differently are proud, ignorant, and obsessed with controversies (vv.3-4). They suppose "gain is godliness" — using religion for profit (v.5).
Contentment and Money (vv.6-10): "Godliness with contentment is great gain" (v.6). We brought nothing in and take nothing out (v.7). Food and clothing are enough (v.8). Those who desire riches fall into temptation (v.9). "The love of money is the root of all evil" (v.10).
Charge to Timothy (vv.11-16): Flee greed; pursue righteousness, godliness, faith, love, patience, meekness (v.11). "Fight the good fight of faith" (v.12). Keep the commandment until Christ's appearing (v.14). Magnificent doxology to God as King of kings (vv.15-16).
Instructions for the Rich (vv.17-19): Don't be arrogant or trust in wealth. Trust in God. Be generous, ready to share (vv.17-18). Store up treasure for the future (v.19).
The Final Charge and Benediction (vv.20-21): O Timothy, keep that which is committed to thy trust. The letter ends on the word guard rather than on an instruction to do anything new, and what is to be avoided is named as vain babblings and oppositions of science falsely so called. Some have erred concerning the faith by professing it. Then four words to close, grace be with thee.

Deep Dive

Setting: Paul is writing to Timothy, his younger co-worker, whom he left in Ephesus to deal with false teachers (1:3), probably in the early 60s AD after his first Roman imprisonment. Ephesus was one of the richest cities of the empire, a port and banking centre where the Temple of Artemis held deposits for half of Asia. Chapter 5 dealt with groups in "the household of God" (3:15): older and younger members, widows and elders. Chapter 6 finishes that list with slaves, then turns back to where the letter began, the false teachers. This time Paul exposes their motive: they treat religion as a way to make money. That leads into one of the Bible's fullest passages on contentment and wealth, a stirring personal charge to Timothy, a burst of worship, a word for the rich, and an abrupt last warning. The Tyndale notes point out that the closing section mirrors the shape of chapter 1: false teachers, a charge to Timothy, a doxology, and a warning about those who have wrecked their faith.

Instructions for the Church (vv1–2)

  • The section began in 5:1. The ESV heading "Instructions for the Church" runs from 5:1 to 6:2, and the chapter break falls in the middle of it. Slaves are the last group in Paul's survey of the church family.
  • "All who are under a yoke as bondservants" (v1). "Bondservant" translates doulos, slave. Estimates for the Roman Empire run into the millions, perhaps a fifth or more of the population of a city like Ephesus. Roman slavery was not based on race. People became slaves through war, debt, birth or abandonment as infants, and they worked as farm hands, but also as doctors, teachers and household managers. Many churches were full of slaves, sitting beside free members and sometimes beside their own masters.
  • "Regard their own masters as worthy of all honor" (v1). These are masters who are not believers, as the contrast with v2 shows. The yoke is real, and Paul does not pretend otherwise. But a slave's conduct is a witness.
  • "So that the name of God and the teaching may not be reviled" (v1). Christianity was already suspected of upsetting households. A slave who became insolent after conversion would confirm the worst fears of outsiders and get the gospel called a revolutionary cult. Paul uses the same reasoning for young widows (5:14) and for younger women and slaves in Titus 2:5, 10. Behaviour either adorns the teaching or brings it into disgrace (compare Isa 52:5, quoted in Rom 2:24).
  • "Those who have believing masters must not be disrespectful on the ground that they are brothers" (v2). "Be disrespectful" is literally "despise" or "look down on." The danger here is the opposite one: a Christian slave reasoning that since we are equals in Christ, I owe this man less. Paul says the shared faith is a reason to serve better, because the one benefiting is "believing and beloved."
  • Paul and slavery. Paul regulates slavery, as Colossians 3:22–4:1 and Ephesians 6:5–9 also do, and he never calls for a revolt, which in Rome would have been crushed in blood. But he also condemns "enslavers" (literally "man-stealers") in 1:10, tells slaves to gain freedom if they can (1 Cor 7:21), and treats master and slave as brothers under one Lord (Philem 16). Christians have long wrestled with why the New Testament did not say more. The seeds it planted later grew into the Christian abolition movements.
  • Commentators: Guzik observes that the Bible regulates slavery without endorsing it, and that the gospel attacked its roots (pride, greed, contempt for others) without a violent uprising. He applies the passage to ordinary work: a lazy or resentful Christian employee drives people away from Christ, and a Christian boss is not owed less effort. Henry agrees that the gospel "does not cancel" the duties of a servant and adds that Christian masters should be served "the rather" because they share the same faith. JFB thinks the danger in v1 was slaves feeling spiritually superior to heathen masters.

False Teachers and True Contentment (vv3–10)

  • "Teach and urge these things" (v2b). This line closes the household instructions and opens the next paragraph. Timothy is not merely to hold these views but to press them.
  • "A different doctrine" (v3). One Greek verb, heterodidaskaleō, "to teach otherwise," which Paul used in 1:3 and which is not found in any earlier surviving Greek writing. The test is twofold: "the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ" and "the teaching that accords with godliness." "Sound" means healthy, a medical word that runs through these letters (1:10; 2 Tim 1:13; Titus 1:9). True teaching makes people spiritually healthy and produces a godly life. False teaching does neither.
  • "Puffed up with conceit and understands nothing" (v4). "Puffed up" is a word for being clouded or wrapped in smoke, the same word used of a new convert who becomes proud (3:6). The irony is sharp: the men who claim special knowledge (v20) actually understand nothing.
  • "An unhealthy craving for controversy and for quarrels about words" (v4). "Unhealthy craving" is noseō, to be sick, used only here in the New Testament. The lexicon gives the classical sense "to have a morbid craving." It stands in deliberate contrast to the "sound" (healthy) words of v3. These teachers are sick, and their sickness is an appetite for argument. "Quarrels about words" (logomachia) means fights over terms and fine points rather than truth (compare 1:4; 2 Tim 2:14, 23).
  • "Envy, dissension, slander, evil suspicions, and constant friction" (vv4–5). This is what such teaching produces. Look at the fruit: not love from a pure heart (1:5), but a church at war with itself. "Constant friction" suggests people rubbing against each other continually, like an irritation that never heals.
  • "Depraved in mind and deprived of the truth" (v5). The ESV catches a word play. "Deprived" is passive: they have been robbed of the truth, as the Tyndale notes point out. They once had access to it and lost it.
  • "Imagining that godliness is a means of gain" (v5). Here is the motive. Travelling teachers who lectured for fees were common in the Greek world, and they were often mocked for teaching for money and fame. The Tyndale notes suggest these men brought that habit into the church, or taught a faith that promised success, and found a ready audience among wealthy members (vv17–19; 2:9). Paul had refused to make money from preaching in Ephesus itself (Acts 20:33–35). The KJV adds "from such withdraw thyself" at the end of v5, but the earliest manuscripts lack it, so the ESV leaves it out.
  • "But godliness with contentment is great gain" (v6). Paul turns their phrase around. Godliness is gain, enormous gain, but not the kind they mean. "Contentment" is autarkeia, the Stoic and Cynic philosophers' favourite word for self-sufficiency, a wise man needing nothing outside himself. The lexicon gives "sufficiency" and "contentment." Paul takes the word and changes its centre. Christian contentment is not self-sufficiency but Christ-sufficiency: "I have learned in whatever situation I am to be content… I can do all things through him who strengthens me" (Phil 4:11–13; compare 2 Cor 9:8, the word's only other use).
  • "We brought nothing into the world, and we cannot take anything out" (v7). This echoes Job 1:21 ("Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked shall I return") and Ecclesiastes 5:15. Every possession is on loan for the length of a life. The KJV's "and it is certain" is not in the oldest manuscripts.
  • "If we have food and clothing, with these we will be content" (v8). "Clothing" is literally "coverings," which can include shelter. Paul names bare necessities. Compare Agur's prayer, "give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with the food that is needful for me" (Prov 30:8), and Jesus' teaching that the Father knows we need these things (Matt 6:25–33).
  • "Those who desire to be rich" (v9). The problem is not having wealth but wanting it, a settled ambition to be rich. That desire is open to poor people as much as rich ones.
  • "Fall into temptation, into a snare… plunge people into ruin and destruction" (v9). The verse moves downward like a fall: temptation, then a trap, then many foolish and hurtful desires, then sinking. "Plunge" is bythizō, to sink, used elsewhere only of boats so full of fish that they began to sink (Luke 5:7). The picture is a ship overloaded until it goes under. "Ruin and destruction" piles up two words for loss, likely meaning ruin in this life and loss in the next.
  • "The love of money is a root of all kinds of evils" (v10). "Love of money" is one Greek word, philargyria, literally "fondness for silver," found only here in the New Testament. The KJV's "the root of all evil" is often misquoted as "money is the root of all evil." Paul says neither. Money is not the problem but the love of it. And the Greek has no article before "root," so "a root" is a fair rendering: greed is not the only source of sin, but there is no kind of evil it cannot feed. The Pharisees, "who were lovers of money," mocked Jesus for this very teaching (Luke 16:14).
  • "Wandered away from the faith and pierced themselves with many pangs" (v10). "Pierced" is peripeirō, found only here, which the lexicon says first meant to put something on a spit. Those who reach for money end up skewered by it, and they do it to themselves. Judas, who sold his Lord for thirty pieces of silver, is the Bible's darkest example (Matt 26:14–16; 27:3–5).
  • Commentators: Guzik warns against presenting the gospel as a product that will fix people's problems or make them prosper, and says people should follow Jesus because he is God, not for what they get. He notes that the desire for riches is more dangerous than riches, since Abraham and David were wealthy and godly. Henry on the false teachers: "Commonly those are most proud who know least; for with all their knowledge they do not know themselves." JFB carefully points out that the Greek says these men treat godliness as a source of gain, not that gain is godliness as the KJV reads. JFB also insists, rightly, that the article is missing in v10, so love of money is "a" root, not the only one.

Fight the Good Fight of Faith (vv11–21)

  • "But as for you, O man of God" (v11). The phrase "man of God" is an Old Testament title for prophets and leaders: Moses (Deut 33:1), Samuel (1 Sam 9:6), Elijah and Elisha (1 Kings 17:18; 2 Kings 4:9). Paul gives Timothy that dignity, set against the money-loving teachers. In 2 Timothy 3:17 the same title is used more broadly of anyone equipped by Scripture.
  • "Flee these things. Pursue righteousness, godliness, faith, love, steadfastness, gentleness" (v11). Run from greed and its company, and run after six virtues. "Steadfastness" (hypomonē) is endurance under pressure. "Gentleness" is a rare word for a meek, unruffled spirit, an important trait for someone who must correct argumentative people (2 Tim 2:24–25). Paul gives the same flee-and-pursue command in 2 Timothy 2:22.
  • "Fight the good fight of the faith" (v12). The image is the athletic contest, agōn, the word behind "agony," not a battlefield. Ephesus held famous games. The verb is present tense: keep on contending. Paul will say of himself at the end, "I have fought the good fight" (2 Tim 4:7).
  • "Take hold of the eternal life to which you were called" (v12). "Take hold" is a decisive grip. Eternal life is God's gift and his call, yet Timothy is to seize it and live in it now. The same verb returns in v19.
  • "The good confession in the presence of many witnesses" (v12). When Timothy made it is not said. Many think of his baptism, where believers publicly confessed "Jesus is Lord" (Rom 10:9). The Tyndale notes take that view. Others, including JFB, think of his ordination, when elders laid hands on him (4:14; 1:18). A few suggest a time Timothy stood trial for his faith (compare Heb 13:23). Whichever it was, people had heard him say where he stood.
  • "God, who gives life to all things" (v13). The best manuscripts use a word meaning to keep alive or preserve. Timothy serves in the sight of the One who holds every life, including his, so he need not fear men who threaten it.
  • "Christ Jesus, who in his testimony before Pontius Pilate made the good confession" (v13). The same phrase, "good confession," links Timothy's confession to Christ's. Before Pilate, Jesus affirmed that he was a king, that his kingdom is not of this world, and that he came "to bear witness to the truth" (John 18:33–37), and then he went silently to the cross. Naming Pilate also ties the gospel to a real Roman official and a datable event, as the creeds would later do ("suffered under Pontius Pilate"). The Greek for "testimony" is related to martys, witness, the word that later came to mean martyr.
  • "Keep the commandment unstained and free from reproach" (v14). "The commandment" probably means the whole charge Timothy has been given, the gospel and the life that goes with it. Keep it spotless, so no one can fairly fault it.
  • "Until the appearing of our Lord Jesus Christ" (v14). "Appearing" is epiphaneia, a favourite word of these letters (2 Tim 1:10; 4:1, 8; Titus 2:13). In the Greek world it was used of a god showing himself, or of an emperor's visit to a city. The Tyndale notes observe that the letters use it for both of Christ's comings, so the church lives between two "appearings." Timothy is to be faithful until the second, however far off.
  • "Which he will display at the proper time" (vv15–16). Paul cannot speak of Christ's coming without breaking into worship. Each phrase lifts God above every rival:
  • "The blessed and only Sovereign" (v15). "Sovereign" is dynastēs, ruler or potentate, used in Mary's song of the rulers God brings down from their thrones (Luke 1:52). In a world where emperors were hailed as lords and the imperial cult was strong in Ephesus, there is only one true Sovereign.
  • "King of kings and Lord of lords" (v15). An Old Testament title for God (Deut 10:17; Ps 136:2–3; Dan 2:47), which Revelation gives to the returning Christ (Rev 17:14; 19:16).
  • "Who alone has immortality, who dwells in unapproachable light" (v16). Angels and redeemed humans will live forever, but only by God's gift. God alone has immortality in himself. "Unapproachable light" recalls Moses, who could not see God's face and live (Exod 33:20), and Psalm 104:2, where God covers himself with light as a garment. "Whom no one has ever seen or can see" echoes John 1:18, which goes on to say that the only Son "has made him known." Many think this doxology, like 3:16, may come from the worship of the early church.
  • "As for the rich in this present age" (v17). Paul returns to money, this time to believers who already have it, and there were wealthy members in Ephesus (2:9). He does not tell them to give it all away. "In this present age" quietly marks the limit of their wealth: it belongs to an age that is passing.
  • "Not to be haughty, nor to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches" (v17). Two dangers: pride and false security. Wealth tempts people to look down on others and to trust in what can vanish overnight (Prov 23:4–5; Luke 12:16–21, the rich fool).
  • "On God, who richly provides us with everything to enjoy" (v17). This is not a grim, anti-pleasure faith. God gives "richly," and he gives things "to enjoy." Paul has already rejected false teachers who forbade marriage and certain foods (4:3–5). The answer to greed is not contempt for good things but gratitude to the Giver.
  • "Rich in good works… generous and ready to share" (v18). A wordplay: let them be rich in a different currency. "Ready to share" is related to koinōnia, fellowship, the sharing that marked the first church (Acts 2:44–45).
  • "Storing up treasure… a good foundation for the future" (v19). Paul is echoing Jesus: "lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven" (Matt 6:19–21; Luke 12:33). Generosity is not buying salvation. It is putting wealth where the heart already is. The phrase mixes images, treasure and foundation, but the meaning is clear: give now, and build on something that lasts.
  • "That which is truly life" (v19). This picks up v12. The KJV reads "eternal life," but the oldest manuscripts have "that which is really life." The rich man thinks he has life. Paul says real life is something else, and it is still to be seized.
  • "O Timothy, guard the deposit entrusted to you" (v20). A heartfelt personal appeal, the only place Paul calls him "O Timothy." "Deposit" is parathēkē, a legal and banking word for property left with someone for safekeeping, which they are bound to return intact. The Greek Old Testament uses it for goods entrusted to a neighbour (Lev 6:2, 4). In a city whose temple served as a bank, the image would be vivid. The deposit is the gospel itself, handed to Timothy. He is not to improve it or add to it but to keep it safe and pass it on (2 Tim 1:12–14; 2:2).
  • "Irreverent babble and contradictions of what is falsely called 'knowledge'" (v20). "Contradictions" is antithesis, opposition, the root of our word "antithesis," found only here. The lexicon gives "opposition." These may be counter-arguments set against the gospel, or a system of contrasting pairs. "Knowledge" is gnōsis. In the second century a movement called Gnosticism claimed secret knowledge for a spiritual elite, taught that matter was evil, and denied that Christ came in the flesh. Most scholars think the full system came later, but early forms of its ideas may already be at work here: the ban on marriage and foods (4:3) and the claim that the resurrection had already happened (2 Tim 2:18) fit that pattern. The KJV's "science falsely so called" used "science" in its old sense of knowledge, not modern science.
  • "Some have swerved from the faith" (v21). "Swerved" means missed the mark, as in 1:6 and 2 Timothy 2:18. The letter that opened with men who "swerved" (1:6) and made "shipwreck of their faith" (1:19) closes the same way.
  • "Grace be with you" (v21). "You" is plural. The letter was addressed to Timothy, but Paul expected it to be read aloud to the whole church. There are no greetings or travel plans, and the Tyndale notes suggest that in a tense situation, with Timothy's authority under challenge, Paul let the charge itself be the final word.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Those who will get to heaven must fight their way thither." He also says "Christ died not only as a sacrifice, but as a martyr," and on the rich, "Those are truly rich who are rich in good works." Guzik sees three things in Jesus' good confession before Pilate: he owned his kingship, he told Pilate his power came from above, and he submitted silently to his Father's will. On v19 Guzik makes the point that a hand cannot grip two things at once, so Timothy must let go of the pursuit of money to lay hold of real life. He also argues that giving matters for the giver's own heart, since God does not need our money. JFB follows the oldest manuscripts in reading "that which is really life" and in v13 "preserveth alive," and links the "oppositions" of v20 to early forms of the dualism that Gnosticism later developed. JFB reads the good confession as Timothy's ordination, where the Tyndale notes prefer his baptism.

Pointing to Christ

The chapter runs between two pictures of wealth. The false teachers want religion to make them rich, and they end up pierced and sunk. Paul points instead to the one who "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor" (2 Cor 8:9), who stood before Pilate with nothing but the truth and made the good confession all the way to the cross. He is the King of kings who will appear at the proper time, and the one in whom the unseen God in unapproachable light is made known. Contentment is possible because he is enough, and "that which is truly life" is found in him.

Reflection

  • Contentment as True Gain (v.6) — Paul redefines what real profit looks like: not accumulation, but godliness paired with a satisfied heart. Where in your life are you chasing more instead of learning to be content with what you already have?
  • The Trap of Wanting to Be Rich (v.9) — Paul warns that the desire to get rich, not wealth itself, is what leads people into temptation and ruin. Is the pursuit of more shaping your decisions in ways you haven't fully noticed?
  • The Root of All Kinds of Evil (v.10) — Paul traces a wide range of harm back to the love of money, which has led people to wander from their faith altogether. Where does money's pull show up most in your own choices and anxieties?
  • Fleeing and Pursuing (v.11) — Paul gives Timothy two commands at once: run from the love of money, and run toward righteousness, godliness, faith, love, endurance, and gentleness. What do you need to actively flee right now, and what should you be chasing instead?
  • Fighting the Good Fight (v.12) — Paul charges Timothy to take hold of eternal life the way a competitor fights for a prize, not passively but with full effort. Are you engaging your faith with that kind of intensity, or coasting through it?
  • Generosity From the Wealthy (vv.17-19) — Paul instructs those with resources not to put their hope in wealth but in God, and to be generous and ready to share. If you have more than enough, is your first instinct to hold on tighter or to give more freely?
