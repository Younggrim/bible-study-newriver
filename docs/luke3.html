1In the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar, while Pontius Pilate was governor of Judea, Herod tetrarch of Galilee, his brother Philip tetrarch of Ituraea and Trachonitis, and Lysanias tetrarch of Abilene,

2during the high priesthood of Annas and Caiaphas, the word of God came to John son of Zechariah in the wilderness.

3He went into all the region around the Jordan, preaching a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins,

4as it is written in the book of the words of Isaiah the prophet: “A voice of one calling in the wilderness, ‘Prepare the way for the Lord, make straight paths for Him.

5Every valley shall be filled in, and every mountain and hill made low. The crooked ways shall be made straight, and the rough ways smooth.

6And all humanity will see God’s salvation.’”

7Then John said to the crowds coming out to be baptized by him, “You brood of vipers, who warned you to flee from the coming wrath?

8Produce fruit, then, in keeping with repentance. And do not begin to say to yourselves, ‘We have Abraham as our father.’ For I tell you that out of these stones God can raise up children for Abraham.

9The axe lies ready at the root of the trees, and every tree that does not produce good fruit will be cut down and thrown into the fire.”

10The crowds asked him, “What then should we do?”

11John replied, “Whoever has two tunics should share with him who has none, and whoever has food should do the same.”

12Even tax collectors came to be baptized. “Teacher,” they asked, “what should we do?”

13“Collect no more than you are authorized,” he answered.

14Then some soldiers asked him, “And what should we do?” “Do not take money by force or false accusation,” he said. “Be content with your wages.”

15The people were waiting expectantly and were all wondering in their hearts if John could be the Christ.

16John answered all of them: “I baptize you with water, but One more powerful than I will come, the straps of whose sandals I am not worthy to untie. He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and with fire.

17His winnowing fork is in His hand to clear His threshing floor and to gather the wheat into His barn; but He will burn up the chaff with unquenchable fire.”

18With these and many other exhortations, John proclaimed the good news to the people.

19But when he rebuked Herod the tetrarch regarding his brother’s wife Herodias and all the evils he had done,

20Herod added this to them all: He locked John up in prison.

21When all the people were being baptized, Jesus was baptized too. And as He was praying, heaven was opened,

22and the Holy Spirit descended on Him in a bodily form like a dove. And a voice came from heaven: “You are My beloved Son; in You I am well pleased.”

Summary

John the Baptist begins his ministry in the wilderness, fulfilling Isaiah's prophecy of one preparing the way of the Lord (vv. 1-6). He preaches repentance to the crowds, warning against relying on descent from Abraham, and gives specific instructions to different groups: sharing with the needy, tax collectors collecting no more than required, and soldiers being content with their wages and avoiding extortion (vv. 7-14). He points ahead to one mightier than himself who will baptize with the Holy Spirit and fire (vv. 15-17), and Luke notes Herod later imprisons John (vv. 19-20). Jesus is baptized, and as he prays, the heavens open, the Spirit descends like a dove, and the Father's voice declares him his beloved Son (vv. 21-22). The chapter closes with Jesus' genealogy traced backward through David and Abraham all the way to Adam, the son of God (vv. 23-38).

Authorship & Background

Author: Luke (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 3 records the ministry of John the Baptist and the baptism of Jesus, followed by Luke's genealogy of Christ. Luke the historian anchors the narrative in specific historical time — naming the emperor (Tiberius), the governor (Pilate), the tetrarchs (Herod, Philip, Lysanias), and the high priests (Annas and Caiaphas). This is approximately AD 26-27.
Classification: Gospel — Narrative
Key Themes: a historian's precision naming seven office-holders from Caesar down to the high priests before saying the word of God came to none of them but to John, Isaiah quoted at greater length than any other Gospel keeps, all flesh shall see the salvation of God, practical instructions unique to Luke given to specific groups, crowds, tax collectors, soldiers, each told exactly what repentance looks like in their own work, John's story finished, including his imprisonment, before Luke ever records the baptism itself, and a genealogy traced backward all the way to Adam and to God rather than forward from Abraham
The Word Came to John (vv.1-9): Luke dates the ministry with the precision of a historian and names seven office-holders to do it, from Tiberius Caesar down to Annas and Caiaphas, and then says the word of God came to none of them. It came unto John the son of Zechariah in the wilderness. Isaiah is quoted at greater length here than in the other Gospels, and Luke keeps the clause the others drop, all flesh shall see the salvation of God. The preaching itself is unsparing: O generation of vipers, and the demand is for evidence rather than sentiment, bring forth therefore fruits worthy of repentance, with the ancestry defense refused in advance, begin not to say within yourselves, We have Abraham to our father.
John's Practical Ethics (vv.10-14): Unique to Luke — John gives specific, practical instructions to different groups: the crowds (share your possessions), tax collectors (don't extort), soldiers (don't abuse power). Repentance produces concrete behavioral change, not just religious feeling.
The Baptism, and Herod's Prison (vv.15-22): The people are in expectation and wondering whether John is the Christ, which he denies in terms of relative standing, one mightier than I cometh, the latchet of whose shoes I am not worthy to unloose. Then Luke does something the other Gospels do not: he finishes John's story before the baptism, noting the rebuke of Herod over Herodias and that Herod shut up John in prison. Only after that does he record the baptism, and he puts it in a subordinate clause, when all the people were baptized, it came to pass, that Jesus also being baptized, and praying, the heaven was opened. The praying is Luke's addition. The voice says, Thou art my beloved Son, in thee I am well pleased.
Luke's Genealogy (vv.23-38): Unlike Matthew's genealogy (which goes from Abraham forward to Jesus through Joseph's legal line), Luke traces Jesus' lineage backward from Jesus all the way to Adam and to God. Most scholars believe Luke traces the biological line through Mary (Heli being Mary's father), while Matthew traces the legal line through Joseph. Luke's genealogy emphasizes Jesus' connection to all humanity — He is the Son of Adam, the Son of God.

Map & Geography

  • The Jordan River: John baptized in the region around the Jordan, likely near Bethany beyond the Jordan (Bethabara), in the lower Jordan Valley near Jericho.
  • Luke's genealogy traces from Nazareth (where Jesus lived) back to Adam — spanning the entire geography of human history from Eden to Galilee.

Setting: Luke, a Gentile doctor and Paul's traveling companion, wrote his Gospel for a man named Theophilus so that he might "have certainty" about what he had been taught (1:1–4). Chapters 1–2 told of the births of John and Jesus and ended with Jesus at twelve in the temple, "about my Father's business" (2:49). Now about eighteen years pass in silence. Luke picks up the story around AD 28–29 in the Judean wilderness by the Jordan River, where John, son of the priest Zechariah, begins to preach. Chapter 3 is Luke's version of the beginning of the gospel: John prepares the way, Jesus is baptized and named by the Father as his Son, and a genealogy traces Jesus all the way back to Adam.

John the Baptist Prepares the Way (vv1–22)

  • "In the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar" (v1). Luke dates the event like a Greek historian, by the rulers of the day. Tiberius became sole emperor in AD 14, so the fifteenth year is about AD 28–29. If Luke counted from when Tiberius began to share power with Augustus (around AD 11–12), the date would be a few years earlier. Either way it fits Jesus being "about thirty" (v23).
  • The rulers (v1). Each name is a reminder of who held power. Pontius Pilate governed Judea for Rome (AD 26–36). "Herod" is Herod Antipas, son of Herod the Great, who ruled Galilee and Perea (4 BC–AD 39); he will imprison John. His half-brother Philip ruled the regions northeast of the Sea of Galilee. Lysanias ruled Abilene, northwest of Damascus. Scholars once said Luke was wrong about Lysanias because Josephus mentions an earlier ruler of that name who died in 36 BC. An inscription found at Abila naming "Lysanias the tetrarch" from the time of Tiberius has since supported Luke. A "tetrarch" originally meant ruler of a fourth of a kingdom, and came to mean any minor ruler.
  • "During the high priesthood of Annas and Caiaphas" (v2). There was only supposed to be one high priest. The Romans removed Annas in AD 15, but he remained the power behind the office, and five of his sons and his son-in-law Caiaphas held it after him. Jesus would be taken to Annas first after his arrest (John 18:13). Luke names both because both mattered.
  • "The word of God came to John… in the wilderness" (v2). This is how the Old Testament introduces prophets: "the word of the LORD came to Jeremiah" (Jer 1:2; Hos 1:1). After roughly four centuries in which Israel had no recognized prophet, the word of God comes again. It does not come to the emperor in Rome, the governor or the high priest in the temple. It comes to a man in the desert.
  • "A baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins" (v3). Jews washed ritually for cleansing (Lev 15), and converts to Judaism were later immersed as part of joining Israel. John's baptism was different: he called Jews to be baptized, as if they too needed to come in from outside. "Repentance" (metanoia) means a change of mind that turns a person's whole direction. "Forgiveness" (aphesis) means release, the same word used for freeing captives in 4:18.
  • "The voice of one crying in the wilderness" (vv4–6). Isaiah 40:3–5 promised that God would come to rescue his people from exile in Babylon and lead them home through the desert. In the ancient world, roads were built and leveled before a visiting king. John's ministry is the road crew. The valleys and mountains are people's hearts. Only Luke continues the quotation to "all flesh shall see the salvation of God" (v6). Salvation for all people, Gentiles as well as Jews, is one of Luke's great themes (2:30–32; 24:47; Acts 1:8).
  • "You brood of vipers!" (v7). Snakes fleeing a brush fire in the wilderness was a familiar sight. John says the crowds are like that: running from the fire of judgment, not turning from their sin. Matthew aims the words at Pharisees and Sadducees (Matt 3:7). Luke has John say it to the crowds, a reminder that no group is exempt.
  • "Bear fruits in keeping with repentance" (v8). Repentance is not proved by a ritual but by a changed life.
  • "We have Abraham as our father" (v8). Many believed that being descended from Abraham guaranteed a place in God's people. "God is able from these stones to raise up children for Abraham" is likely a wordplay in Hebrew or Aramaic, where "sons" (banim) and "stones" (abanim) sound alike. God is not dependent on any family tree. Paul makes the same point: "not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel" (Rom 9:6–8; Gal 3:7).
  • "Even now the axe is laid to the root of the trees" (v9). Not pruning branches but cutting down the whole tree. Judgment is near and total for the fruitless.
  • "What then shall we do?" (vv10–14). Only Luke records this exchange. Three groups ask the same question, and John gives three practical answers.
  • The crowds: share (v11). A "tunic" was the shirt worn next to the skin, and most people had little more than one or two. Whoever has a spare should give it to someone who has none.
  • Tax collectors: stop stealing (vv12–13). Jews who collected taxes and tolls for Rome or Herod. They paid for the right and kept whatever they could collect above the set amount, so many grew rich by overcharging. Their own people despised them as traitors and cheats. John does not tell them to quit. He tells them to stop stealing.
  • Soldiers: no extortion (v14). They were probably not Romans but Jewish troops serving Herod Antipas, who may have guarded the tax collectors. "Extort by threats" is a word meaning to shake someone down. "False accusation" uses a word that later gave English "sycophant," originally an informer who brought false charges for gain. "Be content with your wages": soldiers' pay was low, and many made it up by bullying civilians.
  • John's ethic. None of these answers is unusual. They are the ordinary justice of the Law and the Prophets (Mic 6:8). But they strike at the particular sin each person's position made easy. Repentance always reaches into ordinary work and money.
  • "Whether he might be the Christ" (v15). "The people were in expectation." Hope for the Messiah ran high, and John's power made some wonder. John points away from himself at once.
  • "The strap of whose sandals I am not worthy to untie" (v16). Untying sandals was a slave's job. A later rabbinic saying held that a disciple should do for his teacher whatever a slave would do except untie his sandals. John says he is not fit even for that. The most famous prophet in Israel calls himself less than a slave next to the one who is coming.
  • "He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire" (v16). The prophets promised God would pour out his Spirit in the last days (Ezek 36:26–27; Joel 2:28–29). Fire can mean judgment (v9, v17) or purifying, as when metal is refined (Mal 3:2–3). Many take both together: the coming one will cleanse those who receive him and judge those who refuse. Luke's readers would also remember Pentecost, when the Spirit came with "tongues as of fire" (Acts 2:3).
  • "His winnowing fork is in his hand" (v17). On a threshing floor, harvested grain was beaten loose and then tossed into the wind with a wooden fork. The heavy grain fell back to the floor, and the light chaff blew aside to be burned. The farmer is already holding the fork. The harvest has begun.
  • "He preached good news to the people" (v18). Luke calls even John's stern warnings "good news." A coming judge is good news when he also brings rescue.
  • "Herod… locked up John in prison" (vv19–20). Luke finishes John's story here, out of order, though it happened later (compare Mark 1:14; 6:17–29). Herod Antipas had divorced his wife, the daughter of the Nabatean king Aretas, to marry Herodias, who was both his niece and the wife of his half-brother. The Law forbade marrying a brother's wife (Lev 18:16; 20:21). Josephus says Herod imprisoned John at the fortress of Machaerus, east of the Dead Sea, because he feared John's influence might lead to a revolt. By placing John's imprisonment before Jesus' baptism, Luke clears the stage: from here on, the story is about Jesus.
  • "When all the people were baptized, and when Jesus also had been baptized and was praying" (v21). Luke tells the baptism in one clause and does not even mention John. His stress falls on what happened next, while Jesus prayed. Luke often shows Jesus praying at key moments: before choosing the Twelve (6:12), before Peter's confession (9:18), at the transfiguration (9:28–29) and on the cross (23:34, 46). Why the sinless one was baptized at all is answered best by Matthew: "to fulfill all righteousness" (Matt 3:15). He stood with his people in the water, beginning the work of taking their place.
  • "The heavens were opened" (v21). Isaiah had prayed, "Oh that you would rend the heavens and come down" (Isa 64:1). Now heaven opens.
  • "The Holy Spirit descended on him in bodily form, like a dove" (v22). Only Luke says "in bodily form," stressing that it was seen. The dove recalls the Spirit hovering over the waters at creation (Gen 1:2) and the dove Noah sent out over the flood waters (Gen 8:8–12), the sign of a new beginning. Jesus is anointed with the Spirit for his work, as Isaiah foretold (Isa 61:1; Luke 4:18; Acts 10:38).
  • "You are my beloved Son; with you I am well pleased" (v22). The words echo two Old Testament texts. "You are my Son" is from Psalm 2:7, spoken to the king God installs in Zion. "In whom my soul delights" is from Isaiah 42:1, spoken of the servant who will bring justice to the nations and, later in Isaiah, suffer for others (Isa 53). The King and the suffering Servant are one person. Father, Son and Spirit are all present in one scene, one of the clearest pictures of the Trinity in the Gospels.
  • Commentators: JFB opens with a vivid line: here "the curtain of the New Testament is, as it were, drawn up." It notes that Annas, though deposed, kept much of his power, and reads "extort" as intimidating people for money. Henry observes that the Gospels pass over Jesus' years from twelve to thirty and begin the gospel with John's ministry. Guzik stresses that the bold dating places the gospel in real history and that John did not require tax collectors or soldiers to leave their jobs, only to do them honestly. He also links the Father's words to Psalm 2:7 and Isaiah 42:1, as do the Tyndale notes, which add that Luke often shows Jesus praying at turning points.

The Genealogy of Jesus Christ (vv23–38)

  • "About thirty years of age" (v23). The only statement of Jesus' age in the New Testament, and Luke gives it loosely. If Jesus was born around 6–4 BC, he was probably a little over thirty. Thirty was the age priests began full service (Num 4:3) and the age at which Joseph entered Pharaoh's service and David became king (Gen 41:46; 2 Sam 5:4).
  • "Being the son (as was supposed) of Joseph" (v23). Luke has already told of the virgin birth (1:34–35). Jesus was Joseph's legal son, not his natural one. In Jewish law, a man who accepted a child as his own gave him full legal standing, including descent.
  • Why put the genealogy here? Matthew opens his Gospel with a genealogy (Matt 1:1–17). Luke places his after the baptism and right before the temptation. God has just said, "You are my Son." The list ends with Adam, "the son of God" (v38), and the next chapter opens with the devil saying, "If you are the Son of God…" (4:3, 9). Adam, the first son of God, failed when tempted. Jesus, the true Son, will not.
  • Luke goes back to Adam. Matthew starts with Abraham and moves forward, presenting Jesus as heir of the promises to Israel. Luke runs backward from Jesus all the way to Adam, presenting Jesus as connected to the whole human race. That fits a Gospel written by a Gentile for Gentiles, in which "all flesh shall see the salvation of God" (v6).
  • Luke and Matthew differ. From Abraham to David the two lists largely agree. From David to Jesus they are almost completely different. Matthew traces the line through Solomon and the kings of Judah. Luke traces it through Nathan, another son of David (v31; 2 Sam 5:14). Even Joseph's father is different: Jacob in Matthew 1:16, Heli in Luke. Christians have offered several explanations:
  • View one: Mary's line. Luke gives Mary's ancestry, with Heli as her father and Joseph his son-in-law. The Greek of v23 could be read as "the son, as was supposed, of Joseph, [but really] of Heli." Luke's interest in Mary, who was likely one of his sources (2:19, 51), fits this. A weakness is that women were not usually listed in genealogies, and the text names Joseph, not Mary.
  • View two: legal and natural lines. Matthew gives the royal, legal line of succession, and Luke Joseph's natural descent, the two crossing because of levirate marriage, when a man married his dead brother's childless widow to carry on his name (Deut 25:5–6). This view goes back to Julius Africanus around AD 220, who said he had it from Jesus' own relatives.
  • What both agree on. Both writers agree that Jesus is of David's line, as the angel promised Mary (1:32). Even Jesus' opponents never seem to have disputed that claim.
  • Names after the exile (vv23–27). Most of these names are known only from this list. Zerubbabel and Shealtiel (v27) also appear in Matthew. Zerubbabel was the governor who led exiles home from Babylon and rebuilt the temple (Ezra 3:2, 8; Hag 2:23). How both lists can include them is part of the same puzzle, and may involve another levirate marriage or adoption.
  • From David to Abraham (vv31–34). These names match the Old Testament lists in Ruth 4:18–22 and 1 Chronicles 2. Boaz (v32) married Ruth, a Moabite. Judah (v33) was Jacob's son, from whose tribe the king would come (Gen 49:10). Some names differ slightly between manuscripts: "Admin" and "Arni" (v33) appear in older manuscripts, where many later ones and the KJV have only "Aram," as in Ruth 4:19.
  • "The son of Cainan" (v36). This Cainan, between Arphaxad and Shelah, does not appear in the Hebrew text of Genesis 11:12 or 1 Chronicles 1:24. He does appear in the Greek Old Testament, the Septuagint, which Luke usually followed. Luke is using the Bible his readers knew. Ancient genealogies often skipped generations, so the lists were not meant as exact timelines.
  • From Abraham to Adam (vv34–38). Here Luke follows Genesis 5 and 11: Terah, Shem and Noah; Methuselah and Enoch, who "walked with God" (Gen 5:24); and Seth, the son given to Adam and Eve after Abel's death (Gen 4:25).
  • "The son of Adam, the son of God" (v38). Adam had no human father. He was "son of God" because God made him directly (Gen 1:26–27; 2:7). The list ends where human history began, with God, and Jesus stands as the new head of a fallen race. Paul calls him "the last Adam" (1 Cor 15:45; Rom 5:12–19).
  • Commentators: JFB weighs both views of the genealogy and leans toward its being Mary's line, with Joseph named by custom, and notes that Luke runs back to Adam for Gentile readers. It argues that "about thirty" may mean "about entering on His thirtieth year," while granting that "about thirty years of age when He began" is the better Greek. Guzik also favors the view that Luke follows Mary's line while Matthew gives Joseph's legal line, and stresses that tracing to Adam shows Jesus belongs to all humanity. The Tyndale notes point out the link between Adam as son of God and the temptation that follows. JFB's printed notes skip vv31–37, and none of the three comments on Cainan.

Pointing to Christ

Luke sets the beginning of the gospel in the reign of Tiberius, under Pilate and Herod and Caiaphas, and the word of God passes them all by to come to a prophet in the desert. That prophet points to someone mightier: the one who carries the winnowing fork, baptizes with the Spirit and fire, and stands in the Jordan with sinners. Heaven opens over him, the Spirit rests on him, and the Father calls him his beloved Son. The genealogy then runs all the way back to the first man, as if to say that this Son belongs to everyone descended from Adam, and has come for all of them. "All flesh shall see the salvation of God."

Reflection

  • Rooted in Real History (vv.1-2) — Luke pins his account to named emperors, governors, and high priests, grounding the Gospel in verifiable history rather than myth. How does knowing your faith is anchored in real events, not legend, shape your confidence when it's challenged?
  • Fruit Worthy of Repentance (vv.8-14) — John didn't accept vague religious feeling; he gave the crowds, tax collectors, and soldiers specific, practical changes to make. What would concrete, specific fruit of repentance look like in your particular temptations right now, not in general terms?
  • Truth-Telling at a Cost (vv.19-20) — John's confrontation of Herod's sin led directly to his imprisonment. Is there a truth you're avoiding because speaking it would cost you comfort, relationship, or standing?
  • Identity Before Performance (v.22) — The Father declared His pleasure in Jesus before Jesus had preached a sermon or performed a miracle. Do you tend to seek God's approval through what you do, or rest in the approval He's already spoken over you in Christ?
  • Years of Preparation (v.23) — Jesus spent about thirty years in obscurity before three years of public ministry. If you're in a season that feels hidden or unproductive, how might God be using it to prepare you rather than sideline you?
  • One Family Back to Adam (v.38) — Luke's genealogy traces Jesus all the way to Adam, tying Him to every human being, not just Israel. How does the universal reach of the Gospel shape the way you view and treat people who don't look, think, or live like you?
