1Then Jesus spoke to the crowds and to His disciples:

2“The scribes and Pharisees sit in Moses’ seat.

3So practice and observe everything they tell you. But do not do what they do, for they do not practice what they preach.

4They tie up heavy, burdensome loads and lay them on men’s shoulders, but they themselves are not willing to lift a finger to move them.

5All their deeds are done for men to see. They broaden their phylacteries and lengthen their tassels.

6They love the places of honor at banquets, the chief seats in the synagogues,

7the greetings in the marketplaces, and the title of ‘Rabbi’ by which they are addressed.

8But you are not to be called ‘Rabbi,’ for you have one Teacher, and you are all brothers.

9And do not call anyone on earth your father, for you have one Father, who is in heaven.

10Nor are you to be called instructors, for you have one Instructor, the Christ.

11The greatest among you shall be your servant.

12For whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted.

13Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, you hypocrites! You shut the kingdom of heaven in men’s faces. You yourselves do not enter, nor will you let in those who wish to enter.

15Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, you hypocrites! You traverse land and sea to win a single convert, and when he becomes one, you make him twice as much a son of hell as you are.

16Woe to you, blind guides! You say, ‘If anyone swears by the temple, it means nothing; but if anyone swears by the gold of the temple, he is bound by his oath.’

17You blind fools! Which is greater: the gold, or the temple that makes it sacred?

18And you say, ‘If anyone swears by the altar, it means nothing; but if anyone swears by the gift on it, he is bound by his oath.’

19You blind men! Which is greater: the gift, or the altar that makes it sacred?

20So then, he who swears by the altar swears by it and by everything on it.

21And he who swears by the temple swears by it and by the One who dwells in it.

22And he who swears by heaven swears by God’s throne and by the One who sits on it.

24You blind guides! You strain out a gnat but swallow a camel.

25Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, you hypocrites! You clean the outside of the cup and dish, but inside they are full of greed and self-indulgence.

26Blind Pharisee! First clean the inside of the cup and dish, so that the outside may become clean as well.

27Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, you hypocrites! You are like whitewashed tombs, which look beautiful on the outside but on the inside are full of dead men’s bones and every kind of impurity.

28In the same way, on the outside you appear to be righteous, but on the inside you are full of hypocrisy and wickedness.

29Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, you hypocrites! You build tombs for the prophets and decorate the monuments of the righteous.

30And you say, ‘If we had lived in the days of our fathers, we would not have been partners with them in shedding the blood of the prophets.’

31So you testify against yourselves that you are the sons of those who murdered the prophets.

32Fill up, then, the measure of the sin of your fathers.

33You snakes! You brood of vipers! How will you escape the sentence of hell?

35And so upon you will come all the righteous blood shed on earth, from the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zechariah son of Berechiah, whom you murdered between the temple and the altar.

36Truly I tell you, all these things will come upon this generation.

37O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, who kills the prophets and stones those sent to her, how often I have longed to gather your children together, as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, but you were unwilling!

38Look, your house is left to you desolate.

39For I tell you that you will not see Me again until you say, ‘Blessed is He who comes in the name of the Lord.’”

Summary

Jesus warns the crowds and his disciples against the hypocrisy of the scribes and Pharisees, who teach the law but do not practice it and burden others while seeking honor, titles, and the best seats for themselves (vv. 1-12). He then pronounces seven scathing woes against the scribes and Pharisees: for shutting people out of the kingdom (v. 13), for making converts into sons of hell (v. 15), for elevating trivial oaths and neglecting justice, mercy, and faithfulness while meticulously tithing garden herbs (vv. 16-23), for straining out a gnat while swallowing a camel (v. 24), for cleaning the outside of the cup while remaining full of greed and self-indulgence within (vv. 25-26), for resembling whitewashed tombs that look beautiful outside but are full of dead bones and uncleanness within (vv. 27-28), and for building tombs for the prophets while being the spiritual heirs of those who killed them (vv. 29-36). Jesus closes with a heartfelt lament over Jerusalem, longing to have gathered its people together as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, though they were unwilling, and declares that its house is left desolate until it welcomes the one who comes in the name of the Lord (vv. 37-39).

Authorship & Background

Author: Matthew (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 23 contains Jesus' most devastating public denunciation of the religious leaders — the Seven Woes against the scribes and Pharisees. This is not a private rebuke but a public indictment, delivered in the Temple during Passion Week. It is the last public discourse Jesus gives before the Olivet Discourse (chapters 24-25).
The Seven Woes: "Woe" (Greek: ouai) is a pronouncement of grief and judgment — not anger for its own sake but righteous sorrow over corruption that destroys people. Each woe exposes a specific form of religious hypocrisy: (1) Shutting the kingdom (v.13) (2) Devouring widows (v.14) (3) Making converts worse (v.15) (4) Blind oath-making (vv.16-22) (5) Neglecting justice, mercy, faith (vv.23-24) (6) External cleanliness, internal corruption (vv.25-26) (7) Whitewashed tombs (vv.27-28)
Classification: Gospel — Narrative and Discourse
Key Themes: instruction to obey the scribes and Pharisees paired immediately with a warning not to imitate their works, the charge being inconsistency rather than error, three titles forbidden to the disciples, Rabbi, father and master, seven specific woes rather than general abuse, straining a gnat and swallowing a camel while whited sepulchres are named beautiful outward and full of dead men's bones, and a closing lament that shifts entirely from prosecution to grief, how often would I have gathered thy children together, even as a hen gathereth her chickens under her wings, and ye would not
They Say, and Do Not (vv.1-12): The address is to the multitude and the disciples about the scribes and Pharisees, and it opens with an instruction to obey them, whatsoever they bid you observe, that observe and do, but do not ye after their works. The charge is inconsistency rather than error. Then the specifics, and all are about visibility: heavy burdens laid on others, phylacteries made broad, the uppermost rooms at feasts, greetings in the markets, and the title Rabbi. Three titles are then forbidden to the disciples, Rabbi, father and master, and the section closes on the reversal that governs the chapter, he that is greatest among you shall be your servant.
The Seven Woes (vv.13-33): Woe is a lament as much as a curse, and the seven are specific charges rather than general abuse. Shutting the kingdom and not entering it. Compassing sea and land for one convert and making him worse. An elaborate scheme of oath-technicalities, answered by pointing out that the temple sanctifies the gold and the altar the gift. Tithing mint, anise and cummin while omitting judgment, mercy and faith, with the image of straining a gnat and swallowing a camel. Cleaning the outside of the cup. Whited sepulchres, beautiful outward and full of dead men's bones. And building the tombs of the prophets their fathers killed, which is the one that turns into the sentence, fill ye up then the measure of your fathers.
O Jerusalem, Jerusalem (vv.34-39): The tone changes in the last six verses from prosecution to grief. Prophets and wise men and scribes sent and killed, from the blood of righteous Abel to Zechariah, which spans the whole Hebrew canon as it was then ordered. Then the lament, and the image is domestic and maternal, how often would I have gathered thy children together, even as a hen gathereth her chickens under her wings, and ye would not. Behold, your house is left unto you desolate. The last sentence leaves the door ajar, ye shall not see me henceforth, till ye shall say, Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord.

Map & Geography

  • The Temple in Jerusalem: Jesus delivers the seven woes against the Pharisees in the Temple courts — His final public teaching.

  • "Your house is left unto you desolate" (v.38): A reference to the Temple — Jesus departs, and with Him goes God's presence.

Setting: The temple courts in Jerusalem, Tuesday of Passover week, spring of AD 30. All day the leaders have tried to trap Jesus with questions about authority, taxes, the resurrection and the law, and at the end "no one was able to answer him a word" (22:46). Now Jesus turns to the crowds and his disciples and speaks openly about the scribes and Pharisees. Chapter 23 is the last public teaching of Jesus in Matthew. It begins the fifth and final teaching block (chs 23–25), which ends with his teaching about the end of the age. The tone is the sharpest in the Gospels, but the chapter ends with tears. It helps to remember that "scribes" were the experts in the law, many of them Pharisees, and that the Pharisees were a respected lay movement devoted to keeping the law in every detail. Jesus is not attacking the Jewish people. He is warning against a kind of religion that can grow in any people, including the church.

Seven Woes to the Scribes and Pharisees (vv1–36)

  • "The scribes and the Pharisees sit on Moses' seat" (v2). A teacher in that world sat to teach (5:1). To sit on Moses' seat meant to have the role of explaining the law Moses gave. Archaeologists have found stone seats in ancient synagogues, at Chorazin and elsewhere, that may have been called by this name, though they come from somewhat later.
  • "So do and observe whatever they tell you, but not the works they do" (v3). This sounds surprising after Jesus has rejected their traditions (15:1–20) and warned against their "leaven" (16:12). Most read it as respect for the office and for the law they read out, not for every rule they added. Some take it as irony. The main point is the contrast: "they preach, but do not practice."
  • "They tie up heavy burdens… but they themselves are not willing to move them with their finger" (v4). The picture is of loading an animal with a pack too heavy to carry. The rabbis counted 613 commandments, and the Pharisees' traditions built a "fence" of further rules around them to keep people from breaking the law by accident. Jesus offered the opposite: "my yoke is easy, and my burden is light" (11:30). Peter later called the old load "a yoke… that neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear" (Acts 15:10).
  • "They make their phylacteries broad and their fringes long" (v5). Phylacteries (phylaktēria, "safeguards"; Hebrew tefillin) were small leather boxes holding Scripture passages, tied to the forehead and the arm during prayer, a literal reading of Deuteronomy 6:8 ("bind them as a sign on your hand… frontlets between your eyes"). Fringes, or tassels, were worn on the corners of a garment as God commanded (Num 15:38–41). Jesus himself wore them (9:20; 14:36). The commands were good. The fault was enlarging them to be noticed.
  • The best seats and greetings (vv6–7). At feasts the place of honor was beside the host. In the synagogue the best seats were at the front, near the scrolls, facing the people. In the marketplace men of standing expected to be greeted first with titles. "Rabbi" (Hebrew, "my great one," meaning "my master" or "teacher") was becoming a formal title in Jesus' day.
  • "You are not to be called rabbi… call no man your father… neither be called instructors" (vv8–10). "Father" was an honored title for great teachers of the past ("Father Abraham," and the Mishnah's "the fathers of the world"). "Instructor" (kathēgētēs, found only here in the New Testament) means a guide or tutor, a word used in later Greek for a professor. Jesus is not banning the words. Paul calls himself a "father" to the Corinthians (1 Cor 4:15), and the church has always had teachers (Eph 4:11). He is warning against titles that set some believers above others, as if anyone but God were the source of truth. Christians differ on how far this applies to church titles such as "Father" for priests. Catholic and Orthodox Christians use it as a family term of spiritual care, and many Protestants avoid it because of this verse. All agree the point is the heart: "you are all brothers," with one Teacher and one Father.
  • "The greatest among you shall be your servant" (v11). Jesus repeats what he taught on the road (20:26–28). "Whoever exalts himself will be humbled" (v12) is a saying he used often (Luke 14:11; 18:14).
  • The seven woes (vv13–36). "Woe" (ouai) is a cry of grief and a warning of judgment, used by the prophets (Isa 5:8–23 has six woes; Hab 2:6–20 has five). Matthew's seven woes form a dark mirror of the Beatitudes at the start of Jesus' teaching (5:3–12): there "blessed," here "woe." Each woe names a "hypocrite" (hypokritēs), a stage actor playing a role behind a mask.
  • First woe: "You shut the kingdom of heaven in people's faces" (v13). The scribes held the "key of knowledge" (Luke 11:52), the teaching of Scripture, and used it to lock the door. By rejecting Jesus and turning people against him, they kept out themselves and others.
  • Verse 14 is missing. The ESV goes from v13 to v15 and gives v14 in a footnote: "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you devour widows' houses and for a pretense you make long prayers; therefore you will receive the greater condemnation." The earliest manuscripts of Matthew lack it, and most scholars think scribes copied it in from Mark 12:40 or Luke 20:47, where it belongs. Some later manuscripts place it before v13. Without it there are seven woes; with it, eight.
  • Second woe: "You travel across sea and land to make a single proselyte" (v15). A proselyte was a Gentile who fully converted to Judaism, with circumcision, ritual washing and an offering. Many Gentiles admired Judaism without converting fully; Acts calls them "God-fearers" (Acts 10:2). Some Pharisees worked hard to bring such people all the way into their system. "Twice as much a child of hell" (literally "a son of Gehenna") means that converts often became more zealous than their teachers in the same errors.
  • Third woe: the "blind guides" and their oaths (vv16–22). The scribes had worked out which oaths were binding. Swearing "by the temple" did not bind a man; swearing "by the gold of the temple" did. The Mishnah has a whole tractate on vows and oaths (Shevuot) with distinctions like these. Such rules let people make promises sound solemn while keeping a loophole. Jesus says every oath, whatever it names, is made before God, who "dwells" in the temple and sits on the throne of heaven. He had already taught a better way: simply let "yes" mean yes (5:33–37). "Blind guides" is a sad title for men whose whole job was to lead others (15:14; Rom 2:19).
  • Fourth woe: "You tithe mint and dill and cumin" (v23). The law required a tenth of grain, wine, oil and fruit (Lev 27:30; Deut 14:22–23). The Pharisees extended it to every garden herb, down to the seeds. "The weightier matters of the law: justice and mercy and faithfulness." These echo Micah 6:8: "do justice… love kindness… walk humbly with your God." "Weightier" means more important, not more difficult. "These you ought to have done, without neglecting the others." Jesus does not scorn their tithing; he scorns the loss of proportion.
  • "Straining out a gnat and swallowing a camel!" (v24). Careful Jews strained wine through cloth to catch a gnat, the smallest unclean creature (Lev 11:41–43), and so not eat it. The camel was the largest unclean animal (Lev 11:4). In Aramaic the words are close in sound (qalmaʾ and gamlaʾ), which makes the saying a pun. The picture is meant to be funny and devastating.
  • Fifth woe: "You clean the outside of the cup and the plate" (vv25–26). The Pharisees debated in detail when the outside or inside of a vessel became ritually unclean. Jesus turns this into a picture of the heart. They kept their dishes spotless while filling them with what they gained by "greed and self-indulgence." Clean the inside, and the outside will follow (15:18–20).
  • Sixth woe: "Like whitewashed tombs" (vv27–28). Touching a grave made a person unclean for seven days (Num 19:16), which would keep a pilgrim from the Passover. So before the feast, tombs near Jerusalem were painted white with lime to warn travelers. They looked bright and clean in the spring sun, yet they were full of bones and decay. In Luke Jesus uses the reverse image: "unmarked graves" that people walk over without knowing (Luke 11:44). The point is the same. The Pharisees looked holy and made others unclean.
  • Seventh woe: "You build the tombs of the prophets" (vv29–32). Monuments to famous figures of Israel's past were built around Jerusalem in this period; Herod built a marble memorial at the tomb of David. The leaders said they would never have joined their ancestors in killing the prophets. But by calling the murderers "our fathers," they admit they are their sons, and they are about to prove it by killing the greatest Prophet. "Fill up, then, the measure of your fathers" (v32) is a bitter invitation: finish what they started. Scripture speaks of sin reaching its full measure before judgment falls (Gen 15:16; 1 Thess 2:16).
  • "You serpents, you brood of vipers" (v33). John the Baptist used the same words for the same people (3:7). Serpents recall the first deceiver (Gen 3:1). These are strong words, spoken by the one who also weeps for them a few verses later.
  • "I send you prophets and wise men and scribes" (v34). Jesus speaks as the one who sends God's messengers. In Luke the same saying is introduced as "the Wisdom of God said" (Luke 11:49). The "prophets and wise men and scribes" are his own followers, who will be flogged in synagogues and hunted from town to town, as Acts records (Acts 5:40; 7:58; 12:2; 22:19).
  • "From the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zechariah the son of Barachiah" (v35). Abel was the first murder in Scripture (Gen 4:8). Zechariah son of Jehoiada was a priest stoned "in the court of the house of the LORD" for warning the people, and as he died he said, "May the LORD see and avenge!" (2 Chr 24:20–22). In the Hebrew Bible, Chronicles is the last book, so "Abel to Zechariah" means from the first book to the last, like saying "from Genesis to Revelation." The difficulty is the name "son of Barachiah," since Chronicles calls him son of Jehoiada. The prophet Zechariah was "the son of Berechiah" (Zech 1:1), and there is no record of that prophet being murdered. Explanations include that Jehoiada, who lived to 130, was really his grandfather, with Barachiah his father; that he had two names; or that an early copyist confused the two Zechariahs. Luke's version simply says "Zechariah" (Luke 11:51). Many scholars see a copying confusion, while others accept a double name. The point of the saying is not in doubt.
  • "All these things will come upon this generation" (v36). The judgment fell within forty years, when Rome destroyed Jerusalem and the temple in AD 70. This prepares for chapter 24.
  • Commentators: JFB reads Moses' seat as the scribes' role "as interpreters of the law given by Moses," and describes phylacteries as "strips of parchment with Scripture-texts on them." It applies vv8–10 to "the itch for ecclesiastical superiority which has been the bane and the scandal of Christ's ministers in every age," but agrees with Guzik that Jesus is not banning all titles, since Paul calls himself a father and a teacher. On the whitewashed tombs JFB writes, "What a powerful way of conveying the charge, that with all their fair show their hearts were full of corruption!" Henry: "They were very strict and precise in smaller matters of the law, but careless and loose in weightier matters." Guzik follows Carson in reading "weightier" as more central rather than harder, and notes that most early manuscripts lack v14. He sees the harsh words as a warning meant to rescue, not personal anger. On "son of Barachiah" Guzik offers the double-name explanation, while JFB, noting "there is no record of any fresh murder answering to this description," settles on the Zechariah of 2 Chronicles 24.

Lament over Jerusalem (vv37–39)

  • "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem" (v37). The doubled name is the language of grief, like David's "O my son Absalom, my son, my son" (2 Sam 18:33). "The city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to it." Jeremiah was nearly killed there (Jer 26:8–11, 20–23), and Zechariah was stoned there. Jesus will soon be killed outside its walls.
  • "How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings" (v37). The Old Testament pictures God sheltering his people under his wings (Deut 32:11; Ruth 2:12; Ps 91:4). Jesus speaks as that God. "How often" suggests he had come to Jerusalem many times, which fits John's Gospel, where Jesus visits for several feasts (John 2:13; 5:1; 7:10). The picture is tender and humble. A hen is not an eagle or a lion. She protects her chicks by covering them with her own body, even when fire or a hawk comes. "And you were not willing!" The barrier was not his willingness but theirs.
  • "See, your house is left to you desolate" (v38). "Your house" probably means the temple, no longer called "my house" (21:13) but "your house." Ezekiel saw the glory of the LORD leave the temple before Babylon destroyed it (Ezek 10:18–19; 11:22–23). In the next verse of Matthew, Jesus himself leaves the temple and does not return (24:1). Some manuscripts lack "desolate," and the ESV notes this, but the sense is the same.
  • "You will not see me again, until you say, 'Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord'" (v39). These are the words of Psalm 118:26 that the crowds shouted on Sunday (21:9). Interpreters read this in two main ways. Some take it as a hope: a day will come when Israel will welcome its Messiah, as Paul expects (Rom 11:25–27; Zech 12:10). Others take it as a warning: when he returns, all will have to acknowledge him, willingly or not (Phil 2:10–11). Either way the door is not shut forever.
  • Commentators: JFB hears in the lament "the very heart of God pouring itself forth through human flesh and speech," and reads "your house" as the temple, "deserted, that is, of its Divine Inhabitant." It links v39 to Zechariah 12:10, the day Israel looks on "him whom they have pierced." Henry calls the hen gathering her chicks "an apt emblem of the Saviour's tender love." Guzik stresses that the problem was never Jesus' willingness but Jerusalem's refusal, and that the lament shows he loved the very people he had just rebuked. He reads v39 as pointing to a future turning of Israel to the Messiah, as do the Tyndale notes.

Pointing to Christ

This chapter shows two sides of the same Lord. He is the one Teacher and the one Master, who sees through every religious mask and will judge the abuse of God's law and God's people. He is also the mother hen, longing to gather the very city that is about to kill him. The leaders laid heavy burdens on others and would not lift a finger. Jesus would soon carry the heaviest burden of all on his own shoulders, the cross, and bear the judgment he warned of, so that whoever humbles himself may be exalted and gathered under his wings.

Reflection

  • Words Without Action (v.3) — Jesus says the religious leaders teach truth but do not live it themselves. Is there a gap between what you profess and how you actually live?
  • Humility Before Exaltation (v.12) — Jesus warns that whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted. Where is pride quietly working against you right now?
  • Blocking the Way to God (v.13) — The religious leaders make it harder for others to enter the kingdom rather than easier. Is there anything in how you live your faith that makes it harder for someone else to come to Christ?
  • Precise About Small Things, Careless About Weighty Ones (v.23) — The Pharisees tithe garden herbs while neglecting justice, mercy, and faithfulness. What weightier matters are you neglecting while staying careful about lesser things?
  • Clean Outside, Corrupt Within (v.27) — Jesus compares the hypocrites to whitewashed tombs, beautiful on the outside, decayed within. How different is your public life from your private, inner life?
  • A Love That Grieves Rejection (v.37) — Jesus laments over Jerusalem, longing to gather her children even as they refuse Him. Where might you be resisting the gathering, protecting love God keeps extending to you?
