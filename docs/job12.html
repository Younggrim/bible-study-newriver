Summary

Job answers Zophar with biting sarcasm, telling his friends that wisdom will not die with them and that he understands these things just as well as they do, even though he — a righteous man whom God once answered — has become a laughingstock (vv. 1-6). He points out that even the animals, birds, fish, and earth itself know that the hand of the Lord controls all creation and holds the life and breath of every living thing (vv. 7-10). Job insists that true wisdom and power belong to God alone, who tears down what cannot be rebuilt, controls the waters, and strips kings, priests, counselors, and judges of their strength and understanding (vv. 11-21). He describes God as the one who makes nations rise and fall, deprives their leaders of understanding, and leaves them groping in darkness like drunkards with no light (vv. 22-25).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown. Candidates include Job himself, Moses, Solomon, or an anonymous sage. Likely the oldest book in the Bible by setting (patriarchal era, approximately 2000-1800 BC), though the date of composition is debated. The book addresses the problem of innocent suffering and God's sovereignty.
Historical Context: Chapter 12 begins Job's reply to Zophar and extends through chapters 13-14. This is Job's longest speech in the first cycle and marks a turning point: Job moves from defense to counterattack. He mocks his friends' pretensions to wisdom (v.2), affirms his own intellectual equality (v.3), and then delivers a devastating hymn to God's absolute sovereignty (vv.13-25) that actually surpasses anything the friends have said about God's power. The irony is sharp: Job uses the same theology of divine sovereignty that the friends used against him, but draws the opposite conclusion. The friends say "God is sovereign, therefore your suffering is deserved." Job says "God is sovereign, therefore He does whatever He wants — including letting the righteous suffer and the wicked prosper."
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: sarcasm opening the reply, no doubt but ye are the people, and wisdom shall die with you, before Job claims equal standing with his accusers, the tabernacles of robbers prosper named as an observation that directly undercuts the friends' whole theology, creation itself called on as a witness, ask now the beasts, and they shall teach thee, a hymn to divine sovereignty stretching from kings to counselors to nations that actually exceeds anything the friends themselves have offered, and the same theology of God's absolute control turned to precisely the opposite conclusion the friends drew from it
Job's Sarcasm (vv.1-6): "No doubt but ye are the people, and wisdom shall die with you" (v.2) — pure sarcasm. You think you have a monopoly on wisdom? "I am not inferior to you" (v.3) — Job is their intellectual equal or superior. "Who knoweth not such things as these?" — your theology is obvious, not profound. Job then describes his humiliation: he is a "laughingstock" to friends (v.4) — a just and blameless man mocked by the comfortable. The bitter observation: "The tabernacles of robbers prosper, and they that provoke God are secure" (v.6) — the wicked thrive while the righteous suffer. This directly contradicts the friends' theology.
Creation Testifies (vv.7-12): Job points to nature itself: "ask now the beasts, and they shall teach thee" (v.7). Even animals know that "the hand of the LORD hath wrought this" (v.9) — that God is sovereign over all life. Every living thing's soul is in God's hand (v.10). Job's point: you don't need special wisdom to know God is in control — the whole creation declares it. "Doth not the ear try words?" (v.11) — just as the palate tastes food, wisdom discerns truth. "With the ancient is wisdom" (v.12) — Job acknowledges what Bildad said about aged wisdom, but then shows that his wisdom about God's sovereignty exceeds theirs.
The Hymn to Divine Sovereignty (vv.13-25): This is the chapter's centerpiece — a magnificent and terrifying description of God's absolute control over all things. God breaks down and it cannot be rebuilt (v.14). He withholds waters (drought) or sends them (flood) — v.15. The deceived and the deceiver are His (v.16). He leads counselors away "spoiled" (stripped), makes judges fools (v.17), loosens kings' bonds (v.18), leads priests stripped, overthrows the mighty (v.19), removes speech from the trusted, takes understanding from the aged (v.20). He pours contempt on princes (v.21), reveals deep things from darkness (v.22), makes nations great and destroys them (v.23), removes understanding from leaders and makes them wander like drunken men (vv.24-25). This is not comfort — it is raw sovereignty. God controls everything, including chaos and injustice.

Deep Dive

Setting: Zophar has just finished the harshest speech of the first round (ch 11). He called Job "a man full of talk," accused him of claiming to be "clean in God's eyes," told him God was exacting "less than your guilt deserves," lectured him on wisdom "higher than heaven" and "deeper than Sheol," and ended with a sneer about a "stupid man" who will get understanding when a wild donkey is born a man. Now Job answers, and his reply runs three chapters (chs 12–14), his longest speech so far. The Tyndale notes point out that he is answering not just Zophar but the whole first round of speeches. In chapter 12 he speaks to the friends. He begins with sarcasm and a complaint about being mocked (vv1–6), turns to creation as a witness that God is behind everything (vv7–12), and then gives his own hymn to God's wisdom and power (vv13–25). Zophar praised God's greatness to shame Job. Job shows he can praise it just as well, with a darker edge, because he has felt that power turned against him.

Job Replies: The LORD Has Done This (vv1–25)

  • "No doubt you are the people, and wisdom will die with you" (v2). This is open sarcasm, the first time Job answers his friends this way. The "you" is plural, aimed at all three. They act as though they are the only people who matter, and wisdom will die out with them. Zophar had called Job a hollow fool (11:12), and Job throws the insult back.
  • "But I have understanding as well as you; I am not inferior to you" (v3). "Understanding" here is lebab, the ordinary Hebrew word for heart. In Hebrew thought the heart was the seat of the mind and will. That answers to Zophar, who said a "stupid man" would "get understanding" (literally "get a heart") only when a donkey gave birth to a man (11:12). Job has a mind as good as theirs. He will say "I am not inferior to you" again almost word for word in 13:2.
  • "Who does not know such things as these?" (v3). Everything the friends have said about God's greatness is common knowledge, and Job never denied it. Zophar's poem on God's unsearchable wisdom (11:7–9) told Job nothing he had not already said himself (9:4–10). The problem was not their facts about God. It was what they did with those facts.
  • "I am a laughingstock to my friends" (v4). The word is sechoq, laughter or derision. There is a painful irony here. Bildad had promised Job that if he were blameless, God would "fill your mouth with laughter" (8:21). The lexicon confirms it is the same noun. Instead of having laughter in his mouth, Job has become the laughter in theirs. And these mockers are "friends," men who came to "comfort him" (2:11).
  • "I, who called to God and he answered me, a just and blameless man" (v4). He prayed and God answered. "Just and blameless" echoes the opening, where the narrator and then God himself call Job "blameless and upright" (1:1, 8). Job is not boasting. He is stating what the reader already knows. The friends see a ruined man and assume a sinner.
  • "In the thought of one who is at ease there is contempt for misfortune" (v5). Job names the reason for their scorn. The comfortable easily look down on the suffering, as if trouble is "ready for those whose feet slip" and is what they deserve. The friends have lost nothing, and from that safe place they lecture a man who has lost everything.
  • Two readings of v5. The KJV reads this verse very differently: "He that is ready to slip with his feet is as a lamp despised in the thought of him that is at ease." The difference comes down to one Hebrew word. Read as lappid, it means "torch" or "lamp." Read as lappid split into two parts, l- ("for") plus pid ("ruin, disaster"), it means "for misfortune." The BDB lexicon takes the second view and lists Job 12:5 under pid, "ruin, disaster," a rare word that also appears in 30:24 and 31:29. Modern translations (ESV, NIV, CSB) follow this. The older "lamp" reading pictures the suffering man as a torch nobody needs once the sun is up.
  • "The tents of robbers are at peace, and those who provoke God are secure" (v6). This is Job's counterpunch. Zophar promised that a repentant man would "feel secure" and "rest in security" (11:18), while the wicked would find no way of escape (11:20). Job says the facts say otherwise. Robbers live in peace, and people who openly defy God sleep soundly. The friends' system said the righteous always prosper and the wicked always fall, and the world does not fit it. Psalm 73 and Jeremiah 12:1 wrestle with the same problem: "Why does the way of the wicked prosper?"
  • "Who bring their god in their hand" (v6). The line is obscure, and readers take it in two main ways. Some think it describes idolaters who carry a small idol around with them, a god small enough to hold. Others think it means men whose own hand, their strength, is their god. Habakkuk describes the Babylonians this way: men "whose own might is their god" (Hab 1:11). Either way, these are people who answer to nothing above themselves, and they are doing fine.
  • "But ask the beasts, and they will teach you" (v7). Now the "you" becomes singular. The Tyndale notes suggest Job is turning to Zophar in particular. Zophar had compared Job to a wild donkey. Job answers: go ask the animals. Beasts, birds, the "bushes of the earth" (or "the earth" itself, as some translate) and fish all know what Zophar treats as a great secret.
  • What do the animals teach? Readers differ on what lesson Job has in mind. One view, held by many modern commentators and by Guzik, is that creation teaches what the friends keep saying: God is in charge. Their deep theology is so basic that animals know it. A second view, held by JFB, is that the animal world shows the violent and strong living most securely, the hawk thriving and the dove being eaten, which supports Job's point in v6. The Tyndale notes add a third angle: Job thinks the cause of his grief is so obvious that creation itself could tell it. The irony is that God will later take Job on a long tour of the animal kingdom (chs 38–41) to show him how much he does not understand.
  • "That the hand of the LORD has done this" (v9). "The LORD" here is God's personal covenant name, Yahweh. Through the speeches of chapters 3–37, Job and his friends call God El, Eloah or Shaddai, "the Almighty," more general names. The Tyndale notes and JFB both point out that this is the only place in the dialogues where the name Yahweh appears. Some scholars have doubted that the name was original here, and Isaiah 41:20 has exactly the same phrase, "the hand of the LORD has done this." Others think the rarity is the point: in the middle of his anguish, Job reaches for the name of the God he knows personally. The Tyndale notes put Job's position well. He never doubts who caused his troubles. His question is why.
  • "In his hand is the life of every living thing and the breath of all mankind" (v10). Every creature's life and every human's breath are held in God's hand. Daniel tells Belshazzar of "the God in whose hand is your breath" (Dan 5:23), and Paul says the same to the Athenians: God "himself gives to all mankind life and breath and everything" (Acts 17:25). For Job this is both a comfort and a terror. If God holds his breath, then nothing has happened to Job outside of God's hand.
  • "Does not the ear test words as the palate tastes food?" (v11). Job used a taste image in 6:6 and 6:30. Food is tasted and the bad spat out. Words should be tested the same way, not swallowed whole because they sound wise. Elihu will later quote this very line (34:3).
  • "Wisdom is with the aged, and understanding in length of days" (v12). This is a common saying, and it is hard to tell how Job means it. Bildad had told Job to learn from the former generations (8:8–10), and Eliphaz will later boast that "gray-haired and the aged are among us" (15:10). Some readers think Job is agreeing: wisdom does come with age, and he is old enough to have it too. Others, including the Tyndale notes, think Job is quoting the friends' slogan in order to question it. That fits v20, where God "takes away the discernment of the elders." Young Elihu will make the same point (32:9).
  • "With God are wisdom and might; he has counsel and understanding" (v13). Wisdom finally rests not with old men but with God. Notice the four words: wisdom, might, counsel and understanding. Isaiah uses the same set when he describes the Spirit resting on the coming King: "the Spirit of wisdom and understanding, the Spirit of counsel and might" (Isa 11:2). What follows is a hymn to God's power like Zophar's (11:7–11) and Eliphaz's (5:9–16), with one difference. In theirs God's power works in tidy ways. In Job's it mostly tears things down, and no one can say why.
  • "If he tears down, none can rebuild; if he shuts a man in, none can open" (v14). Zophar had said God "imprisons" and no one can turn him back (11:10). Job agrees, and he speaks from experience. He has been torn down, and he is shut in. The cross-references point to Isaiah 22:22, where the one with "the key of the house of David" opens and none shuts, and shuts and none opens, a line Jesus later claims for himself (Rev 3:7). They also point to Genesis 7:16, where "the LORD shut him in," Noah in the ark.
  • "If he withholds the waters, they dry up" (v15). Drought and flood come from the same hand. JFB thinks Noah's flood is in view.
  • "With him are strength and sound wisdom; the deceived and the deceiver are his" (v16). "Sound wisdom" is tushiyyah, the wisdom-book word for practical, effective wisdom that gets results. Zophar used it in 11:6 for God's "manifold" wisdom, and Job used it in 6:13 for the resource driven from him. Now Job gives it back to God: it is all his. "The deceived and the deceiver" are a pair of related Hebrew words. The lexicon gives the first (shogeg) as one who errs or goes astray, and the second (mashgeh) as one who leads others astray. Both are under God's rule. That does not make God the author of lies. It means even deceivers and their victims cannot step outside his control, as in the story of the lying spirit in Ahab's prophets (1 Kgs 22:19–23). JFB points to Ezekiel 14:9.
  • "He leads counselors away stripped" (v17). From here to v25, Job walks through every kind of powerful person and shows God humbling each one: counselors, judges, kings, priests, the mighty, trusted speakers, elders, princes, the strong, nations and chiefs. "Stripped" is sholal, which the lexicon gives as "barefoot." It occurs only here, in v19, and in Micah 1:8, where the prophet goes "stripped and naked" in mourning. The picture is of prisoners of war led away barefoot, which is exactly what Assyria did to the captives of Egypt and Cush (Isa 20:4). The king's advisors become a line of shuffling captives, and judges lose their good sense.
  • "He looses the bonds of kings and binds a waistcloth on their hips" (v18). The Tyndale notes give two ways to read the first half. It may mean God loosens the hold kings have on others, their power to bind and control. Or it may mean God strips away their royal belt or robe, the sign of their office. Either way, the second half finishes the picture. The king who once wore royal clothing now has only a captive's loincloth.
  • "He leads priests away stripped… and takes away the discernment of the elders" (vv19–20). Religious leaders are not safe either; sholal appears again. Trusted speakers suddenly have nothing to say, and the elders lose their judgment. After v12, this stings. The friends lean on the wisdom of age, and Job reminds them God can take it away in a moment.
  • "He pours contempt on princes and loosens the belt of the strong" (v21). The first half is repeated word for word in Psalm 107:40. The psalm uses it to praise God for rescuing the needy. Job uses it in a list of acts that seem to have no pattern. "Loosens the belt" means taking away a warrior's readiness: with his belt undone, his long robe trips him.
  • "He uncovers the deeps out of darkness and brings deep darkness to light" (v22). "Deep darkness" is tsalmaveth, often translated "shadow of death," as in Psalm 23:4. Job used it twice in 10:21–22 for the land of the dead. The Tyndale notes suggest Job may be answering Zophar's talk of what is "deeper than Sheol" (11:8). Nothing is hidden from God, not even the darkest place. JFB points to Daniel 2:22: "he reveals deep and hidden things; he knows what is in the darkness."
  • "He makes nations great, and he destroys them" (v23). The hymn widens from individuals to whole nations, raised up and led off into exile. Daniel 2:21 says the same: "he removes kings and sets up kings."
  • "He takes away understanding from the chiefs" (v24). The KJV has "heart" here, which JFB glosses as "intelligence." Leaders lose their minds and wander in a desert with no road. The Tyndale notes point to Nebuchadnezzar, who lost his reason and lived like an animal until he acknowledged God's rule (Dan 4:32–33). Psalm 107:40 uses the same picture of rulers wandering "in trackless wastes."
  • "They grope in the dark without light, and he makes them stagger like a drunken man" (v25). The most powerful people on earth end up reeling like drunks, which the Tyndale notes tie to God's judgment on proud rulers (Isa 19:14). Deuteronomy 28:29 uses the same image for Israel under the covenant curses: "you shall grope at noonday, as the blind grope in darkness."
  • Why end on such a dark note? Eliphaz's hymn (5:9–16) had God frustrating the crafty and lifting the lowly, a neat picture. Job paints the other side: God tearing down, stripping and scattering, with no explanation. Some readers think Job is mocking the friends' tidy version of providence. Others think he is describing his own experience in grand terms, since he too has been torn down, shut in and left groping in the dark. Both may be true. Job is not denying God's wisdom. He is saying it is far bigger, and far less predictable, than the friends' formula allows.
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry admires the tone of vv12–25: "Here are no passionate complaints, no peevish reflections, but every thing masculine and great." He says of God, "What God says cannot be gainsaid, nor what he does undone," and "Kings are not exempt from God's jurisdiction." On vv6–11 he writes that "Job appeals to facts": robbers and oppressors "often prosper," yet "this is not by fortune or chance; the Lord orders these things." He closes with hope: "with him we look for a kingdom that cannot be shaken." Guzik, like the Tyndale notes, reads v9 as the only use of the name Yahweh in the poetry, and he passes on Andersen's view that the rarity is what makes it stand out. Guzik cites Smick's suggestion that it may be mocking Eliphaz's tidy picture in 5:18–26, and thinks the lost and groping leaders of vv24–25 are partly Job describing himself. JFB calls v2 "Ironical, as if all the wisdom in the world was concentrated in them," and on v9 says "the agency must be referred to Jehovah." On vv7–8 JFB reads the animals as teaching "that the violent live the most securely," a different lesson from Guzik's. On v5 both Henry and JFB follow the KJV's "lamp" reading, and JFB pictures Job as a torch his friends used and then threw aside. The BDB lexicon does not support this. It reads the word as "for misfortune," which is the ESV's sense. JFB has no comment on vv1 and 17, and only cross-references for vv16 and 22.

Pointing to Christ

Job, "a just and blameless man," became "a laughingstock" to the very people who should have stood by him (v4). Jesus walked that road much further. The truly righteous One was mocked by soldiers, priests and passersby, and the comfortable looked on his misfortune with contempt (v5). Job said that God "leads counselors away stripped" and "pours contempt on princes," and at the cross the King himself was stripped, crowned with thorns and mocked, yet there God's wisdom was doing its deepest work. Paul says the rulers of this age did not understand it, "for if they had, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory" (1 Cor 2:8). The four gifts Job gave to God, wisdom, might, counsel and understanding (v13), are the very gifts Isaiah promised would rest on the Messiah (Isa 11:2). Job could only say that God "brings deep darkness to light" (v22). In Jesus the light has come: "the people dwelling in darkness have seen a great light, and for those dwelling in the region and shadow of death, on them a light has dawned" (Matt 4:16). He holds the key that opens what none can shut (Rev 3:7), and through him the "kingdom that cannot be shaken" (Heb 12:28) is given to those who trust him, whatever is torn down around them.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik observes that Job's hymn to sovereignty (vv.13-25) is actually a more exalted description of God's power than anything the friends have offered. Job is not denying God's sovereignty — he is affirming it with terrifying thoroughness. The friends see God's sovereignty as predictable and moral; Job sees it as absolute and sometimes inscrutable. Guzik notes the irony: Job is charged with questioning God, but his description of God's power exceeds his accusers' theology.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Refusing a False Verdict (v.3) — Job insists he is every bit the intellectual and spiritual equal of the friends who are condemning him without evidence. When someone claims spiritual authority to judge you unfairly, do you test their words against truth or simply accept their verdict?
  • Theology That Faces Reality (v.6) — Job points out the uncomfortable fact that the wicked often thrive while the righteous suffer, directly challenging his friends' simple cause-and-effect theology. Can your faith hold steady when what you see in the world doesn't match the tidy explanations you've been taught?
  • Creation Itself Testifies (vv.7-9) — Job says even the animals and the earth itself know that God's hand is behind all things, so wisdom about His sovereignty isn't reserved for the specially educated. Where do you see God's hand in the ordinary world around you, and do you take time to notice it?
  • Sovereignty Over Every Power (vv.13-19) — Job describes God removing wisdom from counselors, stripping judges of authority, and loosening the grip of kings, showing that no human power stands beyond God's reach. Do you trust God's authority over leaders and circumstances you cannot control?
  • Purpose Inside the Chaos (vv.22-25) — Job describes God bringing hidden things to light and leaving leaders stumbling like drunken men, insisting that even confusion and upheaval fall under God's control. When your circumstances feel chaotic, can you still trust that God is sovereign over what you can't make sense of?
