Summary

Isaiah summons all nations and peoples to hear a sweeping oracle of judgment (vv. 1-2), as the LORD's anger falls on their armies and the heavens themselves are rolled up like a scroll while the stars fall (vv. 3-4). The LORD's sword, sated with blood, descends particularly on Edom, whose land becomes a great slaughter and sacrifice at Bozrah (vv. 5-7), for this is the day of vengeance owed to Zion's cause (v. 8). Edom's streams turn to pitch and its soil to burning sulfur, its ruin lasting generation after generation with no one passing through (vv. 9-10). Wild and desert creatures — the porcupine, owl, jackal, and hawk — take permanent possession of the abandoned land, each finding its place exactly as decreed (vv. 11-17).

Authorship & Background

Author: Isaiah son of Amoz (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Apocalyptic Judgment Oracle — Universal Wrath
Key Themes: Universal judgment on all nations, cosmic upheaval (stars falling, heavens rolled up), God's sword of vengeance, Edom as representative of all enemies, total desolation, the Book of the LORD
Historical Context: Chapters 34-35 form a pair: judgment (34) and redemption (35). Chapter 34 addresses all nations (v.1-2) then focuses on Edom as the representative enemy. Edom (descended from Esau) was Israel's perpetual adversary — related by blood yet hostile in spirit. Edom represents all who oppose God's people throughout history. The imagery is apocalyptic: stars falling like figs from a tree (v.4), heavens rolled up like a scroll (v.4), God's sword drunk with blood (v.5). This points beyond any single historical judgment to the final Day of the LORD.
Come Near, Ye Nations (vv.1-4): Come near, ye nations, to hear, and hearken, ye people, let the earth hear, and all that is therein. The summons is universal and the scale is deliberately larger than the chapter's actual subject, which turns out to be Edom. Then the sky, and all the host of heaven shall be dissolved, and the heavens shall be rolled together as a scroll, and all their host shall fall down, as the leaf falleth off from the vine. Revelation 6:13-14 takes up both images, the scroll and the falling fruit, in the same order.
The Sword Against Edom (vv.5-7): My sword shall be bathed in heaven, behold, it shall come down upon Idumea, and upon the people of my curse. The language throughout is sacrificial rather than military, the sword of the LORD is filled with blood, for the LORD hath a sacrifice in Bozrah, and the animals listed are the ones a sacrifice would use, lambs, goats, rams, bullocks and unicorns. Edom is described as an offering rather than as an enemy, which is the same figure Ezekiel 39 uses of Gog's army.
The Day of Vengeance (v.8): One verse, and it states the accounting the whole chapter rests on. For it is the day of the LORD's vengeance, and the year of recompences for the controversy of Zion. The word controversy is a legal term, so what is described is a case being settled rather than a grudge being satisfied, and the party bringing it is named.
Edom's Perpetual Desolation (vv.9-15): And the streams thereof shall be turned into pitch, and the dust thereof into brimstone, which puts Edom in the same category as Sodom. The desolation is then measured, as at 13:20-22, by what moves in: the cormorant and the bittern, the owl and the raven, dragons in the palaces, thorns in the fortresses. The wild goats and the screech owl take over, and the great owl makes her nest and lays and hatches there, which is the detail that makes it permanent. Nesting is not passing through.
Seek It in the Book (vv.16-17): Seek ye out of the book of the LORD, and read, no one of these shall fail, none shall want her mate. The instruction is documentary and it is unusual: the reader is told to check the list against a written record. And the allocation is put in the language of the conquest, they shall possess it for ever, from generation to generation shall they dwell therein, so the birds and beasts of the previous section receive Edom by lot the way the tribes received Canaan in Joshua.

Setting: Chapters 28–33 dealt with the Assyrian crisis of Isaiah's own day. Chapters 34 and 35 now step back and close the first half of the book with a matching pair of pictures: the whole world under God's judgment, with Edom as its chief example (ch 34), and the redeemed returning to Zion through a blossoming desert (ch 35). Many scholars call these two chapters a "little apocalypse," like chapters 24–27. After them come the history chapters 36–39, and then the comfort of chapter 40. Chapter 34 is one of the darkest in the Bible, and it is meant to be. It shows what it means for God to set the world right, so that the joy of chapter 35 is not cheap.

Judgment on the Nations (vv1–17)

  • "Draw near, O nations, to hear… let the earth hear, and all that fills it" (v1). The summons is to the whole world, not just Judah. It is courtroom language, the same kind of call that opened the book: "Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth" (1:2). Henry: "All nations must hear and hearken," because God has a quarrel with them all.
  • "The LORD is enraged against all the nations… he has devoted them to destruction" (v2). "Devoted to destruction" is charam, the word used for the cherem, things or people handed over wholly to God and so removed from human use, as with Jericho (Josh 6:17–21; Lev 27:28–29). It is the most serious word for judgment in the Old Testament. Here God himself carries it out on a world that has set itself against him.
  • "Their slain shall be cast out… the mountains shall flow with their blood" (v3). To lie unburied was the worst disgrace in the ancient world (Jer 22:19). The language is deliberately extreme. It is prophetic poetry, but poetry that tells the truth about how serious sin is.
  • "All the host of heaven shall rot away, and the skies roll up like a scroll" (v4). The judgment reaches beyond earth. Ancient books were scrolls, and rolling one up meant the reading was finished. "All their host shall fall, as leaves fall from the vine." Jesus uses this kind of language for the end of the age: "the stars will fall from heaven, and the powers of the heavens will be shaken" (Matt 24:29). John picks up this exact verse: "the sky vanished like a scroll that is being rolled up," with stars falling "as the fig tree sheds its winter fruit" (Rev 6:13–14). Peter adds that "the heavens will pass away with a roar" (2 Pet 3:10).
  • How literally? Readers differ. Some take vv1–4 as describing literal cosmic events at the end of history, as JFB and Guzik do. Others read it as the standard prophetic way of saying that God's judgment on a nation shakes the whole world order, as with Babylon in 13:10. Both agree that it points forward to the final day of the Lord, which the New Testament says is still to come.
  • "My sword has drunk its fill in the heavens; behold, it descends for judgment upon Edom" (v5). The view narrows from the whole world to one nation. Edom was descended from Esau, Jacob's twin brother (Gen 25:30; 36:1), and lived in the rugged hills south and east of the Dead Sea. The two brother nations were enemies from the start. Edom refused Israel passage on the way out of Egypt (Num 20:14–21), fought with Israel's kings for centuries, and later cheered and helped when Babylon destroyed Jerusalem: "Remember, O LORD, against the Edomites the day of Jerusalem, how they said, 'Lay it bare, lay it bare'" (Ps 137:7; see Obadiah 10–14). The Tyndale notes say Edom here stands for all the nations, just as Moab did in 25:10.
  • "The LORD has a sword; it is sated with blood… the LORD has a sacrifice in Bozrah" (v6). The picture turns to a temple sacrifice. The lambs, goats and rams, with "the fat of the kidneys," are the animals and parts offered on the altar (Lev 3:9–11). Edom's judgment is pictured as a great sacrifice in which Edom itself is the offering. Jeremiah and Ezekiel use the same picture of a sacrificial feast for judgment (Jer 46:10; Ezek 39:17–19). Bozrah was one of Edom's chief cities, at modern Buseirah in southern Jordan. JFB places it in Auranitis, but that confuses it with Bostra, a different city far to the north. Isaiah returns to Bozrah in 63:1: "Who is this who comes from Edom, in crimsoned garments from Bozrah?"
  • "Wild oxen shall fall with them, and young steers with the mighty bulls" (v7). "Wild oxen" is re'em, the KJV's "unicorns." It means the aurochs, a huge and powerful wild ox, now extinct. The animals are probably a picture of Edom's leaders and warriors, since wild oxen were never used for sacrifice.
  • "The LORD has a day of vengeance, a year of recompense for the cause of Zion" (v8). This is the key verse of the chapter. "For the cause of Zion" means God taking up Zion's case in court, as her defender. The judgment is not random rage. It is justice for those who were wronged. "Vengeance" in the Bible belongs to God precisely so that people do not take it into their own hands (Deut 32:35; Rom 12:19). Isaiah pairs this phrase with good news in 61:2: "the year of the LORD's favor, and the day of vengeance of our God."
  • "The streams of Edom shall be turned into pitch, and her soil into sulfur" (vv9–10). Burning pitch and sulfur are the language of Sodom and Gomorrah (Gen 19:24–28), which lay not far from Edom near the south end of the Dead Sea, where pitch and sulfur are still found. "Its smoke shall go up forever" is borrowed in Revelation for Babylon the great (Rev 14:11; 19:3). "None shall pass through it forever and ever." JFB notes the fitting irony: Edom refused passage to Israel, and now no one will pass through Edom.
  • "The hawk and the porcupine shall possess it, the owl and the raven" (v11). The identities of several of these creatures are uncertain, and translations vary. Many of them were unclean birds (Deut 14:12–18). The point is clear: the land of proud kings becomes a home for scavengers and night creatures.
  • "He shall stretch the line of confusion over it, and the plumb line of emptiness" (v11). This is a striking image. A builder stretches a measuring line and drops a plumb line to build straight (28:17). God measures Edom out for demolition. "Confusion" and "emptiness" are tohu and bohu, the very words of Genesis 1:2: "the earth was without form and void." BDB lists only three places where bohu occurs: Genesis 1:2, Jeremiah 4:23 (the earth under judgment) and here. Judgment is creation run backward, a land returned to the chaos before God shaped it.
  • "Its nobles—there is no one there to call it a kingdom" (v12). Edom was once ruled by kings before Israel had any (Gen 36:31). Now there are none left to proclaim a king.
  • "Thorns shall grow over its strongholds… the haunt of jackals, an abode for ostriches" (v13). Edom's mountain fortresses, like Sela (later Petra), seemed impregnable (Obad 3–4: "you who live in the clefts of the rock"). Thorns and wild animals will take them over. Henry: "That which was a court for princes shall now be a court for owls or ostriches."
  • "The wild goat shall cry to his fellow" (v14). "Wild goat" is sa'ir, literally "hairy one," the normal word for a male goat (Lev 4:23). In some passages it is used of goat-demons that Israel worshiped (Lev 17:7), which is why the KJV has "satyr."
  • "There the night bird settles" (v14). This is lilith, found only here in the Bible. BDB defines it as the name of a female night-demon, probably borrowed from the Babylonian lilitu, and says the link with laylah, "night," may only be popular etymology. Later Jewish legend made Lilith into a demon who preyed on children. Translations divide between an animal ("night bird," "night creature," "screech owl") and a demon ("night hag"). Either way Isaiah is not teaching folklore. He is saying the place will be so desolate that it feels haunted, the kind of place people believe demons live. Jesus speaks of unclean spirits that wander "through waterless places" (Matt 12:43), and Revelation calls fallen Babylon "a haunt for every unclean spirit" (Rev 18:2).
  • "There the owl nests… there the hawks are gathered, each one with her mate" (v15). A quiet, odd turn: life goes on in the ruins. The only families left in Edom are the birds.
  • "Seek and read from the book of the LORD: Not one of these shall be missing" (v16). Isaiah tells his readers to check his words later against what happens. It is one of the clearest moments in the prophets where a prophet's words are called "the book of the LORD." The Tyndale notes take it as God's witness to the truth of his word. "His Spirit has gathered them." The same Spirit who hovered over the tohu and bohu at creation (Gen 1:2) now gathers the creatures to their new home.
  • "He has cast the lot for them; his hand has portioned it out to them with the line" (v17). This is the language of Joshua dividing Canaan among the tribes by lot and measuring line (Josh 18:6–10). Edom's land is given away, but to the wild animals. Its inheritance passes to owls and jackals.
  • Was it fulfilled? Edom as a nation did fade from history. The Babylonians conquered it in the 500s BC. Arab peoples, the Nabateans, pushed the Edomites out of their homeland into southern Judah, which came to be called Idumea, and in the 120s BC the Jewish ruler John Hyrcanus forced the Idumeans to be circumcised and absorbed them. Herod the Great was an Idumean. After the Romans destroyed Jerusalem in AD 70, the Edomites vanish from history. Malachi 1:2–4 already looks back on Edom's land as laid waste. But the cosmic language of vv1–4 and the use Revelation makes of this chapter show that Edom is also a type of the final judgment on everything that opposes God.
  • Commentators: JFB treats chapters 34–35 as "one prophecy," judgment on Israel's foes with Edom as their representative, followed by the flourishing of the church. He reads the line and plummet of v11 as an architect's tools used for exact justice, and he takes the night creature of v14 as "the night specter" of Jewish superstition, which the text uses to paint the desolation without affirming such beings exist. Henry sees the slaughter of v6 as a sacrifice to God's justice and presses v16 on every reader: "The book of the prophets is the book of the Lord," to be searched and compared with events. Guzik reads the near context as Assyria and Edom but the larger context as the Great Tribulation. He explains sa'ir as "hairy one," a goat or possibly a goat-demon, and lilith as a word later Jewish folklore turned into a demon. The lexicon supports both points, while doubting that lilith really comes from the word for "night."

Pointing to Christ

Isaiah 34 is hard to read because it takes evil and God's justice completely seriously. The man who comes from Bozrah in 63:1–3, with garments stained red, is the one who "trod the winepress alone," and Revelation 19:13–15 shows him as Christ the Word of God. The judge of Edom is Jesus. But the same Isaiah who shows that sword also shows the Servant who was "pierced for our transgressions" (53:5). At the cross the sword fell on the one who was truly innocent, so that all who shelter in him are spared the day of vengeance and brought into the year of the LORD's favor. That is why chapter 35 follows chapter 34, and why Jesus stopped reading Isaiah 61 just before "the day of vengeance of our God" (Luke 4:18–19): the day of favor is open now.

Reflection

  • Nothing in Creation Is Permanent (v.4) — The heavens themselves are pictured rolling up like a scroll and the stars falling like withered leaves. What are you building your security on that feels permanent but is actually as temporary as the sky?
  • God's Vengeance for Zion's Cause (v.8) — This judgment is described as the LORD's day of vengeance, His recompense for the wrongs done to His people. Do you believe God notices and keeps account of injustice done to you, even when it feels unseen?
  • Edom's Lasting Desolation (vv.9-15) — Edom, representing every nation that opposed God's people, becomes a permanent ruin overtaken by wild creatures. What opposition or enemy in your life can you trust already has an end written by God, even if it doesn't feel that way yet?
  • An Invitation to Check the Record (v.16) — God invites His people to search His book and see that not one of these words has failed. Have you tested God's promises against your own life and history, or just assumed they're true?
