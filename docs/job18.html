Summary

Bildad answers again, irritated that Job dismisses his friends as senseless beasts, and insists the created order will not be rearranged just to accommodate Job's anger (vv. 1-4). He then paints an elaborate portrait of the wicked man's downfall: his lamp is snuffed out, his own schemes trap his feet in nets and snares, and terror dogs him on every side (vv. 5-11). Bildad describes calamity devouring the wicked man's body, dragging him from the safety of his tent to the king of terrors, while fire and burning sulfur consume his dwelling and his roots wither both above and below ground (vv. 12-16). He concludes that such a man's memory perishes from the earth, leaving no name, no children, and no descendants, so that people east and west are horrified and recognize his fate as the just end of one who does not know God (vv. 17-21).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown. Candidates include Job himself, Moses, Solomon, or an anonymous sage. Likely the oldest book in the Bible by setting (patriarchal era, approximately 2000-1800 BC), though the date of composition is debated. The book addresses the problem of innocent suffering and God's sovereignty.
Historical Context: Chapter 18 is Bildad's second speech and demonstrates the deterioration of the dialogue. Where his first speech at least contained an "if... then" conditional (if you are pure, God will restore you — 8:6), his second speech is pure condemnation with no conditions. He describes the fate of the wicked in elaborate, poetic detail (vv.5-21) and the portrait clearly points at Job. The entire speech is a description of judgment without a single word of encouragement or hope. Bildad has moved from "perhaps you have sinned" to "here is what happens to wicked people like you." There is no comfort offered, no path to restoration suggested — only a catalogue of horrors that Bildad believes Job is experiencing because of wickedness.
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: Bildad offended enough by Job's earlier comment about animals to open with open irritation rather than argument, an accusation that Job tears himself in anger and expects the earth to be forsaken for his sake, an elaborate, unbroken poem describing the wicked man's destruction with no conditional and no offer of restoration this time, disease called the firstborn of death devouring his skin, and a portrait so detailed, snares, terrors, a scattered dwelling, a vanished name, that it can only be read as aimed directly at Job
Bildad's Irritation (vv.1-4): Bildad is offended. "How long will it be ere ye make an end of words?" (v.2) — Job talks too much. "Wherefore are we counted as beasts?" (v.3) — Job's earlier comment about animals knowing God's sovereignty (12:7) apparently stung. "He teareth himself in his anger: shall the earth be forsaken for thee?" (v.4) — Bildad accuses Job of destroying himself with anger and expecting the universe to rearrange for his benefit. This is projection: Bildad cannot handle being challenged, so he accuses Job of self-destructive pride.
The Elaborate Fate of the Wicked (vv.5-21): Bildad delivers an extended poem on the wicked man's destruction. His light is extinguished (vv.5-6). His steps are shortened; his own schemes bring him down (vv.7-8). He is caught in nets, snares, and traps on every side (vv.8-10). Terrors chase him (v.11). His strength wastes away; calamity waits (v.12). Disease devours his skin — "the firstborn of death" (v.13). He is torn from his tent and brought to "the king of terrors" (v.14). His dwelling is scattered with brimstone (v.15). His roots dry up, his branches wither (v.16). His memory perishes, his name vanishes (v.17). He is driven from light to darkness, from the world entirely (v.18). He has no offspring, no survivor (v.19). The conclusion: "Surely such are the dwellings of the wicked, and this is the place of him that knoweth not God" (v.21).
Every detail of Bildad's description corresponds to something in Job's experience: loss of children (v.19), disease consuming skin (v.13), loss of home and dwelling (v.15), isolation (v.18). This is not generic theology — it is a deliberate portrait of Job.

Deep Dive

Setting: The second round of speeches is under way. Eliphaz has spoken (ch 15), and Job has answered him over two chapters (chs 16–17). Job called his friends "miserable comforters" (16:2), said that God had torn him in his wrath (16:9), and then reached for hope: "my witness is in heaven" (16:19). But chapter 17 ended in near despair. Job said he could not find "a wise man among you" (17:10), called the pit his father and the worm his mother (17:14), and asked, "where then is my hope?" (17:15). Now Bildad the Shuhite speaks for the second time. In his first speech (ch 8) he at least held out a promise: if Job was pure, God would restore him (8:5–7). This time there is no promise at all. After a short, irritated rebuke (vv2–4), Bildad paints one long picture of the wicked man's ruin (vv5–21): his lamp goes out, he walks into traps, terrors hunt him, disease eats him, death drags him away, and even his name is forgotten. He never says Job's name. He doesn't need to. Every detail of the portrait is taken from Job's own life.

Bildad Speaks: God Punishes the Wicked (vv1–21)

  • "How long will you hunt for words?" (v2). Bildad opens the way he did the first time (8:2), with "How long?" He is tired of Job's speeches. The Hebrew of this line is hard. The word behind "hunt" (qenets) appears only here in the Old Testament. The lexicon gives its meaning as "snare" and renders the line "how long will ye lay snares for words," that is, catch at words and talk without knowledge. It also notes that the ancient translations took the word to mean "end," which is why some English versions read, "How long until you end these speeches?" Either way, Bildad means that Job's talking is getting nowhere.
  • "Consider, and then we will speak" (v2). The verbs in vv2–3 are plural in Hebrew, as if Bildad were addressing a group. The Tyndale notes suggest he is lumping Job together with "people like you." JFB takes the plural differently, as Bildad scolding the other two friends for their empty words. Most readers take it as aimed at Job. Bildad's demand is "Think first, then we can talk," which assumes the problem is that Job hasn't thought it through.
  • "Why are we counted as cattle? Why are we stupid in your sight?" (v3). Job's words have stung. He had told the friends to go and learn from the animals: "ask the beasts, and they will teach you" (12:7), which implied that even cattle knew more than they did. He had also said, "I shall not find a wise man among you" (17:10). Bildad is offended. Both sides have now stopped listening and started trading insults.
  • "You who tear yourself in your anger" (v4). This is a sharp reply to Job's complaint in 16:9, "He has torn me in his wrath." The verb is the same, taraph. The lexicon notes that it is normally used of a wild animal tearing its prey, and it lists both Job's use (16:9) and Bildad's (18:4). Job said God's anger was tearing him. Bildad says no, Job's own anger is tearing himself. God is not the wild beast here; Job is.
  • "Shall the earth be forsaken for you, or the rock be removed out of its place?" (v4). Bildad believes the moral order of the world is as fixed as the rocks and the land. The wicked suffer and the righteous prosper. That rule will not be rewritten so that Job can be both innocent and suffering. JFB notes that the "rock" picks up Job's own words from 14:18, where Job said that even a mountain falls and "the rock is removed from its place." Bildad turns the image around: Job's case will not move this rock. The irony is that the book itself is about to show that the order of the world is bigger than Bildad's rule. God does allow the righteous to suffer, for reasons Bildad never sees (1:8–12).
  • "Indeed, the light of the wicked is put out" (v5). Now the portrait begins. Light, lamp and fire stand for life and well-being. A lamp left burning in a tent all night was a sign that the household was alive and well. Proverbs uses the same picture: "the lamp of the wicked will be put out" (Prov 13:9; 24:20). The Tyndale notes say this may mean a life lived in confusion (Prov 4:19), but more likely it means an early death. JFB suggests that "the flame of his fire" refers to the fire of hospitality, the campfire that welcomed travelers in Arab custom. When it goes out, the household is no more.
  • "The light is dark in his tent, and his lamp above him is put out" (v6). JFB explains that the lamp hung from the roof of the tent, and its going out was "an image of utter desolation." Bildad is surely thinking of Job's household, which had been wiped out in a single day (1:13–19).
  • "His strong steps are shortened, and his own schemes throw him down" (v7). A long, firm stride belongs to a healthy, secure person. The Tyndale notes contrast it with the blessing of Proverbs 4:12: "When you walk, your step will not be hampered." The wicked man's stride is cut short, and what trips him is his own plans. Bildad's point is that the wicked bring ruin on themselves.
  • "For he is cast into a net by his own feet" (vv8–10). Bildad piles up hunting words: a net, its mesh, a trap that seizes the heel, a snare, a rope (or noose) hidden in the ground, a trap in the path. Guzik passes on Francis Andersen's count of six different trapping devices in these verses and notes that we can't always be sure which device each word names. The point is clear even without the details. The wicked man walks along, unaware, and the ground itself is full of traps for him. The Tyndale notes see here the principle of retribution, that a person reaps what he sows (34:11). Psalm 9:15 says the same: "in the net that they hid, their own foot has been caught." Matthew Henry puts it plainly: "The gin shall take him by the heel." JFB has no note on v10.
  • "Terrors frighten him on every side, and chase him at his heels" (v11). The hunted man now becomes a fugitive. "Terrors" is ballahot, a word the lexicon defines as terrors, dreadful events or calamity. Most of its uses are in Job (18:11, 14; 24:17; 27:20; 30:15). "On every side" is the same phrase Jeremiah used when he renamed his persecutor Pashhur "Terror on Every Side" (Jer 20:3), and it became a refrain in his book (Jer 6:25; 20:10). JFB notes that link and says the terrors are personified, rising from an evil conscience. Henry agrees: "The terrors of his own conscience shall haunt him, so that he shall never be easy." Guzik notes that Bildad is taking Job's own complaint of being attacked on every side (16:9–14) and treating it as evidence that Job is wicked.
  • "His strength is famished, and calamity is ready for his stumbling" (v12). The wicked man's strength wastes away from hunger, and disaster stands waiting for the moment he trips. JFB calls the Hebrew "brief and bold."
  • "It consumes the parts of his skin; the firstborn of death consumes his limbs" (v13). This is the cruelest line in the speech. Job is sitting in the ashes with a disease that covers him from head to foot (2:7–8; 7:5). Bildad describes exactly that, a disease eating the wicked man's skin. "The firstborn of death" is a striking picture. In Hebrew, "firstborn" (bekor) can mean the strongest or chief of a group. The lexicon gives the phrase here as "deadly disease," death's eldest and most powerful child. JFB calls it "a personification full of poetical horror," and adds that Arabs called fever the "daughter of death." The Tyndale notes point out that Isaiah later turns this picture upside down. Here death devours, but in Isaiah the LORD "will swallow up death forever" (Isa 25:8), words Paul quotes of the resurrection (1 Cor 15:54).
  • "He is torn from the tent in which he trusted and is brought to the king of terrors" (v14). The wicked man trusted in his household and his home. Now he is dragged from it and marched like a captive before a king. "The king of terrors" is death itself, pictured as a ruler, and "terrors" is the same ballahot as in v11. The terrors that chased him were the king's servants, and now they bring him in. Guzik passes on Elmer Smick's suggestion that the picture borrows from Canaanite stories of Mot, the god of death and the underworld. JFB disagrees and says it is simply death personified, not a pagan ruler of the dead. Both can be held together: Scripture often borrows the language of its neighbors without borrowing their beliefs. Henry adds a Christian note: death is "in a special manner the king of terrors" to the wicked, but for believers Christ has changed its character. The New Testament says the same, that Jesus came to free "all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery" (Heb 2:15).
  • "In his tent dwells that which is none of his" (v15). The first half of the verse is hard Hebrew. The ESV takes it to mean that strangers now live in his tent. Other readings are possible. JFB thinks "it" means the terror of v14, which now lives in the tent instead of its owner. Some translations read "nothing of his remains in his tent." On any reading, the house no longer belongs to the man.
  • "Sulfur is scattered over his habitation" (v15). Sulfur, or brimstone, recalls the fire that fell on Sodom and Gomorrah (Gen 19:24). The Tyndale notes trace the image forward to the judgment of all evil (Ps 11:6; Rev 21:8), and JFB makes the same link to Sodom. Guzik passes on Adam Clarke's alternative, that sulfur was also used to fumigate a house after disease, so the line may mean the place is treated as polluted. Bildad may also be thinking of the "fire of God" that fell from heaven and burned up Job's flocks (1:16).
  • "His roots dry up beneath, and his branches wither above" (v16). The picture changes to a tree. Bildad used plant pictures in his first speech too (8:11–19). JFB says the roots are the man himself and the branches are his children. The whole tree dies from both ends. Job had lost all ten of his children (1:2, 18–19).
  • "His memory perishes from the earth, and he has no name in the street" (v17). In the ancient world a man lived on through his name, remembered by his sons and spoken of in the town. To have one's name forgotten was a curse. The Tyndale notes list the pattern: "the memory of the righteous is a blessing, but the name of the wicked will rot" (Prov 10:7; see Ps 34:16; 109:13). JFB passes on a suggestion that "street" may mean the open field or pasture, fitting the life of herdsmen. Henry draws a sobering point: the wicked man's memory "perishes wholly, for it was never written in heaven."
  • "He is thrust from light into darkness, and driven out of the world" (v18). JFB says light and darkness here stand for existence and nonexistence. He is pushed out of life altogether, like an outcast driven from the camp.
  • "He has no posterity or progeny among his people, and no survivor where he used to live" (v19). No children, no grandchildren, no one left. Guzik and the Tyndale notes both stress how cruel this is when spoken to a man who had just buried all his children. Bildad had already said it outright in his first speech: "If your children have sinned against him, he has delivered them into the hand of their transgression" (8:4). Guzik passes on Andersen's point that in that culture losing all one's descendants was seen as a sign of God's rejection. Bildad is telling Job that his family's death proves the case against him.
  • "They of the west are appalled at his day, and horror seizes them of the east" (v20). The man's "day" is the day of his ruin. JFB reads west and east as "all peoples." The Tyndale notes mention another possibility. The words can also mean "those who come after" and "those who went before," so the line may mean people of every generation, not only of every place. Either way, his fall becomes a byword. Job himself will later say that he has become just that (17:6; 30:9).
  • "Surely such are the dwellings of the unrighteous, such is the place of him who knows not God" (v21). Bildad sums up. This is the end of every wicked man, and by implication, the explanation of Job's ruin. To "know not God" is the deepest charge of all. The Tyndale notes say Bildad keeps implying that Job has rejected God, as Eliphaz had (15:4). Henry treats not knowing God as the root of all wickedness: "They know not God, and then they commit all iniquity." But the reader knows what Bildad does not. God himself called Job "blameless and upright, one who fears God and turns away from evil" (1:1, 8). The man Bildad says does not know God is the man God boasted about. JFB gives only a cross-reference here, back to Bildad's own earlier line (8:22).
  • True, but not the whole truth (vv5–21). Almost everything Bildad says about the wicked can be found elsewhere in Scripture, especially in Proverbs and the Psalms. Wickedness does lead to ruin. What Bildad gets wrong is the timing and the application. He assumes that every sufferer is being punished, and that the punishment always comes fully in this life. Job will answer in chapter 19 that he knows very well how his life looks, and yet "I know that my Redeemer lives" (19:25). Later, in chapter 21, he points out that the wicked often do prosper and die in peace (21:7–13). The book as a whole warns against reading suffering as proof of sin, a warning Jesus repeats (Luke 13:1–5; John 9:2–3).
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry sums up Bildad's argument in one line: "This is the condition of a wicked man; and therefore thou art one." He notes that Bildad has grown "more peevish" since his first speech, and turns v4 into a lesson about anger: "Anger is a sin that is its own punishment." He closes with a warning taken from the passage: "Let us therefore stand in awe and not sin." Guzik agrees with Henry that Bildad's description of the wicked is true in itself but wrongly applied to Job. Following G. Campbell Morgan, he says it is true but not the whole truth. He also passes on the Puritan John Trapp's verdict that Bildad sinned against the law of love, and Oswald Chambers' remark that Bildad describes the worst man he can think of. Guzik adds, through Bible teacher Mike Mason, that God is not exposing Job's wickedness here but proving his faithfulness. JFB is most useful on the images. He explains the hanging lamp (v6), the personified terrors (v11), the "firstborn of death" (v13), and the roots and branches (v16). JFB and Guzik's sources differ on whether "the king of terrors" borrows from Canaanite myth (v14), as noted above. JFB has no comment on v10, and on v21 gives only a cross-reference.

Pointing to Christ

Bildad's portrait of the wicked man reads, in places, like an account of the cross. Jesus was cast out and "thrust from light into darkness," as darkness covered the land at midday (Mark 15:33). He was led out of the city like an outcast and "suffered outside the gate" (Heb 13:12). He died with no children to carry on his name, and Isaiah asked, "as for his generation, who considered that he was cut off out of the land of the living?" (Isa 53:8). By Bildad's logic, the man on the cross must have been the worst of sinners, and many who watched him thought so (Isa 53:4). But like Job, Jesus was innocent, and unlike Job, he was without any sin at all. He went down under the "king of terrors" on behalf of the guilty, and he did not stay there. Isaiah's promise that the LORD "will swallow up death forever" (Isa 25:8) was fulfilled when he rose, and now Paul can ask, "O death, where is your sting?" (1 Cor 15:55). Bildad said the wicked man's name perishes from the earth. God gave the one who was treated as the wicked man "the name that is above every name" (Phil 2:9). And for all who trust him, death is no longer a king of terrors but a defeated enemy, because he has delivered "all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery" (Heb 2:15).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik observes that Bildad's speech is entirely destructive — no hope, no conditions, no path to restoration. He has abandoned even the pretense of counsel. His elaborate description of the wicked man's fate is crafted to mirror Job's experience point by point: skin disease, loss of children, isolation, approaching death. Bildad is essentially saying "this is you, Job" without having the honesty to say it directly. The indirectness makes it more cruel, not less.

Reflection

  • Assuming Arrogance in an Honest Question (v.4) — Bildad implies Job is prideful for expecting the world to bend to his complaint, when Job is really just asking for an explanation. Do you ever mistake someone's honest search for understanding as arrogance instead of genuine need?
  • Accusation Disguised as General Teaching (vv.5-10) — Bildad describes "the wicked man's" fate in terms that line up exactly with Job's own losses, without ever naming Job directly. Have you ever used a general statement to make a pointed accusation without taking responsibility for actually saying it?
  • Vivid Words That Lack Love (v.13) — Bildad's description of disease devouring the wicked man's skin mirrors Job's own affliction with poetic precision but zero compassion. Can you have theological knowledge and still fail to love the person you're speaking to?
  • The King of Terrors Has Been Dethroned (v.14) — Bildad names death itself as the final horror awaiting the wicked, the ultimate and unanswerable threat. Does your hope in the resurrection actually change how you face the fear of death, or does it still hold power over you?
  • Outward Circumstances Don't Reveal Inner Reality (v.21) — Bildad concludes that this grim fate belongs to everyone who doesn't know God, assuming that suffering itself proves spiritual guilt. Do you judge someone's standing with God by their circumstances rather than by what you actually know about their heart?
