Summary

This chapter contrasts the righteous and the wicked across leadership, wealth, and honesty. The wicked flee even when no one pursues them, while the righteous stand bold as a lion (v. 1), and a nation's stability depends on leaders with real understanding rather than oppressive rulers who prey on the poor like a roaring lion (vv. 2, 15-16). An honest poor person is better off than a rich one of crooked character (v. 6), and hiding sin brings no success, while confessing and forsaking it brings mercy (v. 13). Steady reverence for God is called blessed, but a hardened heart leads to ruin (v. 14). Diligent work on one's own land brings plenty, while chasing empty pursuits brings poverty (v. 19), and rushing to get rich only invites trouble (vv. 20, 22). The chapter favors honest rebuke over flattery (v. 23), trust in God over self-reliance (vv. 25-26), and generosity to the poor over turning a blind eye (v. 27).

Authorship & Background

Author: Primarily Solomon (1:1, 10:1, 25:1), with sections by Agur (ch.30) and King Lemuel (ch.31). Compiled over several centuries. Proverbs is wisdom literature — practical instruction for godly living. Hebrew title: 'Mishlei' — 'Proverbs/Comparisons.'
Historical Context: Chapter 28 belongs to the "Hezekiah collection" (chapters 25-29), proverbs of Solomon copied by the scribes of King Hezekiah (25:1) during Judah's spiritual reformation (c. 700 BC). These proverbs deal heavily with governance, justice, wealth, and the contrast between the righteous and the wicked — themes especially relevant during Hezekiah's effort to purge idolatry and restore godly rule after his father Ahaz's corruption. Chapter 28 is structured as antithetical couplets — nearly every verse sets the righteous against the wicked, the wise against the fool, the faithful against the greedy. The chapter's dominant concern is the moral foundation of society: how sin and righteousness affect governance, economics, and communal life.
Themes in This Chapter: Boldness of the Righteous vs. Flight of the Wicked (v.1); Confession vs. Concealment of Sin (v.13); The Danger of Self-Trust (v.26); Faithfulness vs. Haste to Be Rich (vv.20, 22); The Fear That Never Rests (v.14); Righteous and Wicked Governance (vv.2, 12, 15-16, 28); Generosity vs. Greed (vv.8, 22, 25, 27)
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: bearing contrasted rather than outcome from the opening verse, the wicked flee when no man pursueth, but the righteous are bold as a lion, governance and money woven through nearly every antithetical couplet, a condition the rest of the Bible builds a whole doctrine on, he that covereth his sins shall not prosper, but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy, hasty riches condemned as inevitably corrupting, he that maketh haste to be rich shall not be innocent, and a closing verse that repeats the opening image from the opposite direction, when the wicked rise, men hide themselves
Bold as a Lion, and Sin Confessed (vv.1-14): The chapter opens with a contrast in bearing rather than in outcome, the wicked flee when no man pursueth, but the righteous are bold as a lion. Much of the block is about government and money, for the transgression of a land many are the princes, and the substance increased by usury and unjust gain that will be gathered by someone who pities the poor. Twice it prefers the poor man who walks uprightly to the rich man who is perverse. At verse 13 it states the condition the rest of the Bible builds a doctrine on, he that covereth his sins shall not prosper, but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy, and it is immediately balanced by a blessing on the man who is never quite at ease, happy is the man that feareth alway.
Hasty Riches, Self-Trust and the Rising of the Wicked (vv.15-28): The second half is a study in haste and in misplaced confidence. He that maketh haste to be rich shall not be innocent, and the man who hastens after wealth has an evil eye and does not consider that poverty shall come upon him. Against that stands slow work, he that tilleth his land shall have plenty of bread. The two verses at the turn state the choice the chapter has been circling, he that putteth his trust in the LORD shall be made fat, and he that trusteth in his own heart is a fool. The last verse repeats the opening image from the other side, when the wicked rise, men hide themselves, but when they perish, the righteous increase.

Setting: The collection copied by "the men of Hezekiah" (25:1–29:27) moves into its last stretch. Hezekiah reigned in Judah about 715–686 BC. His officials had watched the northern kingdom of Israel collapse under bad kings and fall to Assyria in 722 BC, so sayings about rulers, justice and the fate of a nation had real weight for them. Chapters 28–29 are mostly two-line contrasts: the wicked and the righteous, the rich and the poor, the wise and the fool. Three threads run through chapter 28. The word "law" (torah) appears four times (vv4, 7, 9), more than anywhere else in Proverbs. Rulers and the people under them come up again and again (vv2–3, 12, 15–16, 28). And money, gained honestly or crookedly, kept or given away, fills the second half (vv6, 8, 11, 19–22, 25, 27). Two near-identical sayings, "when the wicked rise, people hide themselves" (vv12, 28), act as markers, and similar lines in 29:2 and 29:16 do the same in the next chapter. The ESV has no headings inside the collection, so the headings below follow its groupings.

Boldness, Rulers and the Law (vv1–5)

  • "The wicked flee when no one pursues, but the righteous are bold as a lion" (v1). A guilty conscience makes a person jumpy. The Tyndale notes connect this to the covenant curses in Leviticus: those who break faith with God will run at "the sound of a driven leaf… and they shall fall when none pursues" (Lev 26:36–37). The righteous person has nothing to hide, so he can stand still. The lion is the classic picture of fearless confidence (30:30). Compare Peter, who denied Jesus to a servant girl (Mark 14:66–72), and the same Peter, forgiven and filled with the Spirit, standing before the council that killed Jesus (Acts 4:13). Henry sums it up: "Sin makes men cowards."
  • "When a land transgresses, it has many rulers" (v2). A nation in rebellion against God gets one ruler after another, through coups, assassinations and short reigns. Hezekiah's men had seen this in the north: Israel went through about nineteen kings and nine ruling families in two centuries, several of them murdered by the next (1 Kgs 15–16; 2 Kgs 15). Judah, by contrast, kept David's line. "But with a man of understanding and knowledge, its stability will long continue." One wise leader steadies a whole country. JFB reads the instability as itself a punishment: bad government is what a sinful nation gets.
  • "A poor man who oppresses the poor is a beating rain that leaves no food" (v3). Rain is a blessing in a dry land, but a driving storm flattens the crop and washes the soil away. A poor man given power over others can be the harshest master of all, because he knows how to squeeze people with little and may be eager to get his share. Jesus' parable of the unforgiving servant fits: a man forgiven a huge debt chokes a fellow servant over a small one (Matt 18:28). Some scholars, noting that "poor man" (rash) and "ruler" (rosh) look alike in Hebrew, read "a ruler who oppresses the poor" (so the NRSV). The ESV keeps the Hebrew text as it stands, and both readings make sense.
  • "Those who forsake the law praise the wicked, but those who keep the law strive against them" (v4). In Proverbs torah often means a parent's or teacher's "instruction" (1:8; 3:1). In chapter 28, linked with prayer (v9) and seeking the LORD (v5), most commentators take it as God's law. When people throw off God's standard, they end up applauding what is evil (Isa 5:20; Rom 1:32). Those who hold to it can't stay neutral. The Tyndale notes: without law, chaos follows and bad people thrive.
  • "Evil men do not understand justice, but those who seek the LORD understand it completely" (v5). Moral blindness is not mainly an intellectual problem. People who do wrong tend to redefine justice as whatever suits them. Those who seek God see clearly, because they are looking at the one whose character defines what is just. "Completely" is literally "all things." John says much the same: "you have been anointed by the Holy One, and you all have knowledge" (1 John 2:20). JFB: moral ignorance comes from being unwilling to know the truth.
  • Commentators: Guzik passes on Kidner's picture of the righteous having God's goodness and mercy as their rear guard (Ps 23:6), so they don't need to look over their shoulder, and Bridges' point that guilt removed brings confidence back. On v2 he notes an old Arabic curse, passed on by Waltke, wishing "many sheikhs" on an enemy. Henry on v4: "Wicked people strengthen one another in wicked ways."

Integrity, Law and the Rich Man (vv6–11)

  • "Better is a poor man who walks in his integrity than a rich man who is crooked in his ways" (v6). This repeats 19:1, with "rich man" where that verse has "fool." "Crooked" is literally "twisted in two ways," a double life: one face in public, another in business. The Tyndale notes: virtue is worth more than wealth. Guzik points out, with Ross, that the proverb does not say all rich people are crooked. Abraham, Job and Joseph of Arimathea were wealthy and upright. The contrast is between integrity and its absence.
  • "The one who keeps the law is a son with understanding, but a companion of gluttons shames his father" (v7). "Gluttons" is the same word as in 23:20–21, "be not among drunkards or among gluttonous eaters of meat." It describes people who live for appetite and excess. Deuteronomy uses it of the rebellious son brought before the elders, "a glutton and a drunkard" (Deut 21:20). The friends a young person chooses show what he values, and his family's name goes with him. The Tyndale notes say the saying applies to mothers as much as fathers.
  • "Whoever multiplies his wealth by interest and profit gathers it for him who is generous to the poor" (v8). "Interest" is neshek, literally "something bitten off," and "profit" (tarbit) is the increase added on top. The law forbade charging interest to a poor fellow Israelite (Exod 22:25; Lev 25:36–37; Deut 23:19–20). Loans in Israel were mostly emergency loans to the needy, not business investment, so interest meant profiting from someone's hardship. Guzik, with Waltke, notes interest rates in the ancient Near East could reach twenty to thirty percent or more. The proverb promises that God has a way of moving such money on: it ends up with someone who will use it kindly (13:22; Eccl 2:26). Zacchaeus, a rich tax collector, gave half his goods to the poor and repaid fourfold anyone he had cheated (Luke 19:8).
  • "If one turns away his ear from hearing the law, even his prayer is an abomination" (v9). "Abomination" is the strong word for what God finds repulsive (15:8). A person who won't listen to God cannot expect God to listen to him. Samuel told Saul, "to obey is better than sacrifice" (1 Sam 15:22). Isaiah: "even though you make many prayers, I will not listen; your hands are full of blood" (Isa 1:15). This is not about a struggling believer who prays imperfectly. It is about someone who deliberately shuts his ears to God's word and then asks God for things. The Tyndale notes: such prayers are insincere, so why should God listen?
  • "Whoever misleads the upright into an evil way will fall into his own pit" (v10). This builds on 26:27, "whoever digs a pit will fall into it." Leading a good person astray is a special kind of evil. Jesus warned that it would be better to have a millstone around the neck than to cause one of his little ones to stumble (Matt 18:6). "But the blameless will have a goodly inheritance." JFB gives only a cross-reference here and no comment.
  • "A rich man is wise in his own eyes, but a poor man who has understanding will find him out" (v11). Money breeds the illusion that you are wise, because people listen to you and your plans seem to work. A poor man with real discernment sees right through it. "Find him out" means search him and expose what he is. Henry: flattery leads the rich to think themselves better than others.
  • Commentators: Henry on v6: an honest poor person has more contentment and does more good than a dishonest rich one. Guzik passes on Clarke's sharp words against those who grow rich by grinding the poor, and Kidner's point that Deuteronomy distinguishes between a loan to a brother in need and ordinary trade. On v9 Guzik, with Ross, observes that such people pray for things, not for God's will.

Concealing and Confessing (vv12–14)

  • "When the righteous triumph, there is great glory, but when the wicked rise, people hide themselves" (v12). When good people come out on top, the whole community celebrates. When the wicked gain power, people go into hiding. "Hide themselves" is literally "a man must be searched for": people have to be hunted out because they have gone to ground. Elijah hid from Ahab and Jezebel, and Obadiah hid a hundred prophets in caves (1 Kgs 18:4). The same saying returns, slightly changed, in v28.
  • "Whoever conceals his transgressions will not prosper, but he who confesses and forsakes them will obtain mercy" (v13). This is one of the best-known verses in Proverbs and the gospel in miniature. "Conceals" is "covers up." "Confesses" (yadah) means to acknowledge openly, and "forsakes" means to let go of. Real repentance does both: it names the sin and walks away from it. David knew both sides. While he hid his sin with Bathsheba, "my bones wasted away… my strength was dried up," and when he said, "I will confess my transgressions to the LORD," God forgave (Ps 32:3–5). John: "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins" (1 John 1:9). The Tyndale notes point to Psalm 32. Achan hid stolen goods in his tent and died for it (Josh 7). The Ephesian believers brought their magic books into the open and burned them (Acts 19:18–19).
  • "Blessed is the one who fears the LORD always" (v14). The Hebrew does not actually say "the LORD." It says, literally, "blessed is the man who is in dread continually." The verb is pachad, "to dread" or "be in awe," not the usual word for the fear of the LORD (yare'). The ESV and most translations supply "the LORD" from the context of the whole book. The BDB lexicon suggests "deeply dreading" sin. Either way the idea is a tender conscience that takes God and sin seriously all the time, not just in a crisis. This is not anxiety. It is the opposite of the next line: "whoever hardens his heart will fall into calamity." Pharaoh hardened his heart again and again until it was too late (Exod 8:15, 32). Hebrews warns, "Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts" (Heb 3:7–8, 15). Coming right after v13, the pairing is plain: a soft heart confesses, a hard heart conceals.
  • Commentators: Guzik spends the most time here. He passes on Spurgeon's list of the ways people try to cover sin (excuses, secrecy, lying, delay, even tears and religious ceremony) and his point that none of them works. Guzik also gives practical counsel: confession should be specific and honest, made to God and to those actually wronged, and not broadcast further than the sin went. He passes on Trapp's image that when the debt is confessed God "crosses the book," striking it from the ledger. Henry: hiding sin brings no peace, but confession with repentance and faith brings mercy, because "the Son of God is our great atonement." JFB on v14: a hardened heart becomes insensible to sin.

Lions, Bears and Bloodguilt (vv15–18)

  • "Like a roaring lion or a charging bear is a wicked ruler over a poor people" (v15). Lions and bears were real dangers in ancient Israel (1 Sam 17:34–36; 2 Kgs 2:24). A roaring lion is hungry, and a bear charging is out of control. A wicked ruler is both: greedy and violent. The poor have no defense. Rehoboam threatened to be just such a ruler, "my father disciplined you with whips, but I will discipline you with scorpions," and lost ten tribes (1 Kgs 12:14–16). Daniel later sees the world's empires as beasts, a lion and a bear among them (Dan 7:4–5). Peter uses the same picture of the devil, "a roaring lion, seeking someone to devour" (1 Pet 5:8). Henry: whatever titles a wicked ruler gives himself, "this scripture calls a roaring lion, and a ranging bear."
  • "A ruler who lacks understanding is a cruel oppressor, but he who hates unjust gain will prolong his days" (v16). The Hebrew is compressed, but the point is clear: oppression is stupid as well as wicked. A tyrant thinks he is strengthening his position, but he is making enemies of the people he depends on. A ruler who refuses to profit unjustly lasts longer. JFB: hating covetousness leads to a long reign.
  • "If one is burdened with the blood of another, he will be a fugitive until death; let no one help him" (v17). "Until death" is literally "to the pit," meaning the grave. A murderer carries the weight of what he did and runs from it all his life. The law offered cities of refuge for accidental killing, but not for deliberate murder, and no ransom could be paid in place of the killer's life (Num 35:30–32). Cain, the first murderer, became "a fugitive and a wanderer on the earth" (Gen 4:12–14). "Let no one help him" means don't shield him from justice. It does not mean withholding the gospel. Guzik passes on Bridges' balance here: uphold justice, and still point the guilty to the one whose blood "speaks a better word than the blood of Abel" (Heb 12:24).
  • "Whoever walks in integrity will be delivered, but he who is crooked in his ways will suddenly fall" (v18). "Crooked" is again the "two-way" word from v6. A double life eventually collapses, often without warning. Integrity is its own protection. Henry: "Those that are honest are always safe."
  • Commentators: Guzik takes "delivered" in v18 mainly as rescue from life's troubles, not a statement about eternal salvation. JFB reads the bear and lion as pictures of "rapacity and cruelty." Henry on v17: "The murderer shall be haunted with terrors," and no one should rescue him from just punishment or pity him into sin.

Getting Rich Quick (vv19–24)

  • "Whoever works his land will have plenty of bread, but he who follows worthless pursuits will have plenty of poverty" (v19). This repeats 12:11 with a new ending. The repeated "plenty" is ironic: the farmer has plenty of bread, and the dreamer has plenty of poverty. "Worthless pursuits" means empty things, schemes and idle company. JFB calls such people "useless drones." The prodigal son spent his inheritance on "reckless living" and ended up feeding pigs (Luke 15:13–16), an example Guzik uses.
  • "A faithful man will abound with blessings, but whoever hastens to be rich will not go unpunished" (v20). "Faithful" ('emunot) means reliable, true to his word, the same root as "Amen." The contrast is not between rich and poor but between steady faithfulness and a rush for money. Haste usually means cutting corners: fraud, gambling, schemes. The Tyndale notes: wealth gained dishonestly causes harm and doesn't last (13:11). Paul: "those who desire to be rich fall into temptation, into a snare" (1 Tim 6:9).
  • "To show partiality is not good, but for a piece of bread a man will do wrong" (v21). "Show partiality" is literally "regard faces," judging by who someone is rather than what is right (24:23). The second line shows how cheap a person's integrity can be. Ezekiel condemns false prophets who profaned God's name "for handfuls of barley and for pieces of bread" (Ezek 13:19). The Tyndale notes connect it with bribery (17:23).
  • "A stingy man hastens after wealth and does not know that poverty will come upon him" (v22). "Stingy" is literally "a man with an evil eye" (also 23:6), a Hebrew idiom for a grudging, greedy person who looks on others' good with envy. Jesus uses the same idiom: in the parable of the vineyard workers, "do you begrudge my generosity?" is literally "is your eye evil because I am good?" (Matt 20:15; compare Matt 6:23). He is so busy chasing money he can't see the loss coming. JFB: poverty comes "by God's providence."
  • "Whoever rebukes a man will afterward find more favor than he who flatters with his tongue" (v23). Honest correction is unwelcome at first, but over time people come to value the friend who told them the truth over the one who only told them what they wanted to hear (27:5–6). The Tyndale notes: wise people want honest feedback so they can improve. Nathan's rebuke wounded David (2 Sam 12:7), yet Nathan stayed a trusted adviser, brought God's name for the baby Solomon (2 Sam 12:25), and later helped secure Solomon's throne (1 Kgs 1).
  • "Whoever robs his father or his mother and says, 'That is no transgression,' is a companion to a man who destroys" (v24). Taking from parents can feel like taking what will be yours anyway. The excuse, "That is no transgression," makes it worse. Jesus condemned a religious version of this: people who declared their money "Corban," dedicated to God, so they would not have to support their parents (Mark 7:10–13). Micah in Judges stole silver from his mother (Judg 17:1–2), an example Guzik passes on from Trapp. JFB ranks such a child with highway robbers. The Tyndale notes connect the fifth commandment (Exod 20:12).
  • Commentators: Guzik passes on Morgan's contrast in v20 between faithfulness and mere accumulation, and Trapp's remark that people will sell their integrity cheaply, for a piece of bread. Kidner adds that even preachers can be bought. Henry on v20: true happiness comes through holiness and honesty, not getting rich quickly by any means. On v22 Guzik, following Waltke, notes that God sees to it that wealth eventually passes to the compassionate (v8).

Trusting the LORD and Opening the Hand (vv25–28)

  • "A greedy man stirs up strife, but the one who trusts in the LORD will be enriched" (v25). "Greedy" is literally "wide of appetite" (rechab nefesh), a person whose desires have no bottom. The KJV reads "proud heart," and Henry and JFB follow that. Either way, such a person grasps and pushes, and conflict follows. The contrast is surprising: the opposite of greed is not generosity but trust. The person who trusts God doesn't need to grab, because his future is in God's hands. "Enriched" is literally "made fat," a picture of health and plenty (11:25). The Tyndale notes: trust keeps the wise calm, and surprisingly they find prosperity.
  • "Whoever trusts in his own mind is a fool, but he who walks in wisdom will be delivered" (v26). "Mind" is literally "heart." Proverbs began with "Trust in the LORD with all your heart, and do not lean on your own understanding" (3:5). Jeremiah: "The heart is deceitful above all things" (Jer 17:9). Self-trust is the root of folly. The Tyndale notes: wise people listen to others' advice. Guzik passes on Bridges' picture: trusting your own heart is like trusting someone who has lied to you many times.
  • "Whoever gives to the poor will not want, but he who hides his eyes will get many a curse" (v27). Generosity doesn't impoverish. God sees to that (19:17; 11:24–25). "Hides his eyes" means deliberately not looking. It is easier not to see need than to see it and do nothing. The priest and Levite in Jesus' parable saw the wounded man and "passed by on the other side" (Luke 10:31–32). Isaiah calls God's people "not to hide yourself from your own flesh" (Isa 58:7). The "curses" are the complaints of the neglected poor, and God hears them (Exod 22:23). JFB: hiding the eyes "denotes inattention."
  • "When the wicked rise, people hide themselves, but when they perish, the righteous increase" (v28). The chapter ends where its middle began (v12). Under wicked rule the righteous go underground. When the wicked fall, the righteous come out and multiply. When Herod Agrippa, who had killed James and jailed Peter, died, "the word of God increased and multiplied" (Acts 12:23–24), an example Guzik passes on from Bridges. Waltke, also cited by Guzik, notes how fitting this was in Hezekiah's day, after the wicked reign of his father Ahaz (2 Kgs 16; 2 Chr 29).
  • Commentators: Henry on v25: those who depend on God and his grace stay at peace. On v26 he says the fool trusts "his own heart, which has often deceived him." Guzik, following Garrett, ties v26 to Jesus' wise and foolish builders (Matt 7:24–27), and passes on Morgan's rebuke of people who say they are too sensitive to look at poverty. Henry on v28: when the wicked rule, wise people step back from public life.

Pointing to Christ

Proverbs 28 sets the bold righteous against the fleeing guilty (v1), and v13 shows how the guilty can become bold: by confessing and forsaking sin and finding mercy. That mercy has a cost. Christ is the "man of understanding and knowledge" whose kingdom stands forever (v2; Isa 9:7), the King who is the opposite of the roaring lion over a poor people (v15), coming instead "humble and mounted on a donkey" (Zech 9:9) and preaching good news to the poor (Luke 4:18). He bore bloodguilt that was not his (v17) so that whoever confesses can be forgiven (1 John 1:9). He is the faithful man who abounds with blessing (v20), who "though he was rich, yet for your sake became poor" (2 Cor 8:9). And the Lion of Judah (Rev 5:5) gives his people his own boldness: "we have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus" (Heb 10:19).

Reflection

  • Guilt Steals Your Boldness (v.1) — The wicked run even when no one is chasing them, while the righteous are as bold as a lion. What unresolved guilt might be draining your confidence?
  • Confess Instead of Cover (v.13) — Hiding sin blocks God's mercy, but confessing and forsaking it opens the door to it. What are you managing quietly instead of bringing honestly before God?
  • The Blessing of Staying Alert to Sin (v.14) — A constant, healthy awareness of sin's danger is called a blessing, while hardening your heart invites disaster. Where have you grown too confident that you'd never fall?
  • Faithful and Steady Beats Fast (v.20) — A faithful person is rewarded abundantly, while one in a hurry to get rich doesn't escape trouble. Where are you tempted to cut corners for a quicker result?
  • Don't Trust Your Own Heart (v.26) — Relying on your own judgment is called foolish, while walking in wisdom keeps you safe. What decision are you facing where you need outside counsel instead of just following your feelings?
