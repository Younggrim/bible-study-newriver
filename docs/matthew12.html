1At that time Jesus went through the grainfields on the Sabbath. His disciples were hungry and began to pick the heads of grain and eat them.

2When the Pharisees saw this, they said to Him, “Look, Your disciples are doing what is unlawful on the Sabbath.”

3Jesus replied, “Have you not read what David did when he and his companions were hungry?

4He entered the house of God, and he and his companions ate the consecrated bread, which was not lawful for them to eat, but only for the priests.

5Or haven’t you read in the Law that on the Sabbath the priests in the temple break the Sabbath and yet are innocent?

6But I tell you that One greater than the temple is here.

7If only you had known the meaning of ‘I desire mercy, not sacrifice,’ you would not have condemned the innocent.

8For the Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath.”

9Moving on from there, Jesus entered their synagogue,

10and a man with a withered hand was there. In order to accuse Jesus, they asked Him, “Is it lawful to heal on the Sabbath?”

11He replied, “If one of you has a sheep and it falls into a pit on the Sabbath, will he not take hold of it and lift it out?

12How much more valuable is a man than a sheep! Therefore it is lawful to do good on the Sabbath.”

13Then Jesus said to the man, “Stretch out your hand.” So he stretched it out, and it was restored to full use, just like the other.

14But the Pharisees went out and plotted how they might kill Jesus.

15Aware of this, Jesus withdrew from that place. Large crowds followed Him, and He healed them all,

16warning them not to make Him known.

17This was to fulfill what was spoken through the prophet Isaiah:

18“Here is My Servant, whom I have chosen, My beloved, in whom My soul delights. I will put My Spirit on Him, and He will proclaim justice to the nations.

19He will not quarrel or cry out; no one will hear His voice in the streets.

20A bruised reed He will not break, and a smoldering wick He will not extinguish, till He leads justice to victory.

21In His name the nations will put their hope.”

22Then a demon-possessed man who was blind and mute was brought to Jesus, and He healed the man so that he could speak and see.

23The crowds were astounded and asked, “Could this be the Son of David?”

24But when the Pharisees heard this, they said, “Only by Beelzebul, the prince of demons, does this man drive out demons.”

25Knowing their thoughts, Jesus said to them, “Every kingdom divided against itself will be laid waste, and every city or household divided against itself will not stand.

26If Satan drives out Satan, he is divided against himself. How then can his kingdom stand?

27And if I drive out demons by Beelzebul, by whom do your sons drive them out? So then, they will be your judges.

28But if I drive out demons by the Spirit of God, then the kingdom of God has come upon you.

29Or again, how can anyone enter a strong man’s house and steal his possessions, unless he first ties up the strong man? Then he can plunder his house.

30He who is not with Me is against Me, and he who does not gather with Me scatters.

31Therefore I tell you, every sin and blasphemy will be forgiven men, but the blasphemy against the Spirit will not be forgiven.

32Whoever speaks a word against the Son of Man will be forgiven, but whoever speaks against the Holy Spirit will not be forgiven, either in this age or in the one to come.

33Make a tree good and its fruit will be good, or make a tree bad and its fruit will be bad, for a tree is known by its fruit.

34You brood of vipers, how can you who are evil say anything good? For out of the overflow of the heart the mouth speaks.

35The good man brings good things out of his good store of treasure, and the evil man brings evil things out of his evil store of treasure.

36But I tell you that men will give an account on the day of judgment for every careless word they have spoken.

37For by your words you will be acquitted, and by your words you will be condemned.”

38Then some of the scribes and Pharisees said to Him, “Teacher, we want to see a sign from You.”

39Jesus replied, “A wicked and adulterous generation demands a sign, but none will be given it except the sign of the prophet Jonah.

40For as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish, so the Son of Man will be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth.

41The men of Nineveh will stand at the judgment with this generation and condemn it; for they repented at the preaching of Jonah, and now One greater than Jonah is here.

42The Queen of the South will rise at the judgment with this generation and condemn it; for she came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon, and now One greater than Solomon is here.

43When an unclean spirit comes out of a man, it passes through arid places seeking rest and does not find it.

44Then it says, ‘I will return to the house I left.’ On its return, it finds the house vacant, swept clean, and put in order.

45Then it goes and brings with it seven other spirits more wicked than itself, and they go in and dwell there. And the final plight of that man is worse than the first. So will it be with this wicked generation.”

46While Jesus was still speaking to the crowds, His mother and brothers stood outside, wanting to speak to Him.

47Someone told Him, “Look, Your mother and brothers are standing outside, wanting to speak to You.”

48But Jesus replied, “Who is My mother, and who are My brothers?”

49Pointing to His disciples, He said, “Here are My mother and My brothers.

50For whoever does the will of My Father in heaven is My brother and sister and mother.”

1At that time Jesus went on the sabbath day through the corn; and his disciples were an hungred, and began to pluck the ears of corn, and to eat.

2But when the Pharisees saw it, they said unto him, Behold, thy disciples do that which is not lawful to do upon the sabbath day.

3But he said unto them, Have ye not read what David did, when he was an hungred, and they that were with him;

4How he entered into the house of God, and did eat the shewbread, which was not lawful for him to eat, neither for them which were with him, but only for the priests?

5Or have ye not read in the law, how that on the sabbath days the priests in the temple profane the sabbath, and are blameless?

6But I say unto you, That in this place is one greater than the temple.

7But if ye had known what this meaneth, I will have mercy, and not sacrifice, ye would not have condemned the guiltless.

8For the Son of man is Lord even of the sabbath day.

9And when he was departed thence, he went into their synagogue:

10And, behold, there was a man which had his hand withered. And they asked him, saying, Is it lawful to heal on the sabbath days? that they might accuse him.

11And he said unto them, What man shall there be among you, that shall have one sheep, and if it fall into a pit on the sabbath day, will he not lay hold on it, and lift it out?

12How much then is a man better than a sheep? Wherefore it is lawful to do well on the sabbath days.

13Then saith he to the man, Stretch forth thine hand. And he stretched it forth; and it was restored whole, like as the other.

14Then the Pharisees went out, and held a council against him, how they might destroy him.

15But when Jesus knew it, he withdrew himself from thence: and great multitudes followed him, and he healed them all;

16And charged them that they should not make him known:

17That it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Esaias the prophet, saying,

18Behold my servant, whom I have chosen; my beloved, in whom my soul is well pleased: I will put my spirit upon him, and he shall shew judgment to the Gentiles.

19He shall not strive, nor cry; neither shall any man hear his voice in the streets.

20A bruised reed shall he not break, and smoking flax shall he not quench, till he send forth judgment unto victory.

21And in his name shall the Gentiles trust.

22Then was brought unto him one possessed with a devil, blind, and dumb: and he healed him, insomuch that the blind and dumb both spake and saw.

23And all the people were amazed, and said, Is not this the son of David?

24But when the Pharisees heard it, they said, This fellow doth not cast out devils, but by Beelzebub the prince of the devils.

25And Jesus knew their thoughts, and said unto them, Every kingdom divided against itself is brought to desolation; and every city or house divided against itself shall not stand:

26And if Satan cast out Satan, he is divided against himself; how shall then his kingdom stand?

27And if I by Beelzebub cast out devils, by whom do your children cast them out? therefore they shall be your judges.

28But if I cast out devils by the Spirit of God, then the kingdom of God is come unto you.

29Or else how can one enter into a strong man’s house, and spoil his goods, except he first bind the strong man? and then he will spoil his house.

30He that is not with me is against me; and he that gathereth not with me scattereth abroad.

31Wherefore I say unto you, All manner of sin and blasphemy shall be forgiven unto men: but the blasphemy against the Holy Ghost shall not be forgiven unto men.

32And whosoever speaketh a word against the Son of man, it shall be forgiven him: but whosoever speaketh against the Holy Ghost, it shall not be forgiven him, neither in this world, neither in the world to come.

33Either make the tree good, and his fruit good; or else make the tree corrupt, and his fruit corrupt: for the tree is known by his fruit.

34O generation of vipers, how can ye, being evil, speak good things? for out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh.

35A good man out of the good treasure of the heart bringeth forth good things: and an evil man out of the evil treasure bringeth forth evil things.

36But I say unto you, That every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account thereof in the day of judgment.

37For by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned.

38Then certain of the scribes and of the Pharisees answered, saying, Master, we would see a sign from thee.

39But he answered and said unto them, An evil and adulterous generation seeketh after a sign; and there shall no sign be given to it, but the sign of the prophet Jonas:

40For as Jonas was three days and three nights in the whale’s belly; so shall the Son of man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth.

41The men of Nineveh shall rise in judgment with this generation, and shall condemn it: because they repented at the preaching of Jonas; and, behold, a greater than Jonas is here.

42The queen of the south shall rise up in the judgment with this generation, and shall condemn it: for she came from the uttermost parts of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon; and, behold, a greater than Solomon is here.

43When the unclean spirit is gone out of a man, he walketh through dry places, seeking rest, and findeth none.

44Then he saith, I will return into my house from whence I came out; and when he is come, he findeth it empty, swept, and garnished.

45Then goeth he, and taketh with himself seven other spirits more wicked than himself, and they enter in and dwell there: and the last state of that man is worse than the first. Even so shall it be also unto this wicked generation.

46While he yet talked to the people, behold, his mother and his brethren stood without, desiring to speak with him.

47Then one said unto him, Behold, thy mother and thy brethren stand without, desiring to speak with thee.

48But he answered and said unto him that told him, Who is my mother? and who are my brethren?

49And he stretched forth his hand toward his disciples, and said, Behold my mother and my brethren!

50For whosoever shall do the will of my Father which is in heaven, the same is my brother, and sister, and mother.

Summary

Conflict over the Sabbath opens the chapter as the Pharisees challenge Jesus over his disciples plucking grain and his healing a man's withered hand in the synagogue, prompting them to plot his death (vv. 1-14). Jesus withdraws and quietly fulfills Isaiah's prophecy of the humble servant (vv. 15-21), then heals a demon-possessed man who was blind and mute, leading the Pharisees to accuse him of casting out demons by the power of Satan (vv. 22-24). He answers that a divided kingdom cannot stand and warns that blasphemy against the Holy Spirit is the one unforgivable sin (vv. 25-32). When the religious leaders demand a miraculous sign, he points instead to the sign of Jonah, foretelling his own death and resurrection, and warns that Nineveh and the queen of the South will condemn this unbelieving generation (vv. 38-42). He closes by describing the danger of a reformed but empty life and declaring that his true family are those who do the will of his Father in heaven (vv. 43-50).

Authorship & Background

Author: Matthew (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 12 records the decisive break between Jesus and the Pharisees. Two Sabbath controversies (vv.1-14) lead to the Pharisees' decision to destroy Him (v.14). The chapter escalates through the Beelzebub accusation (vv.22-37), the sign of Jonah (vv.38-42), and Jesus redefining His true family (vv.46-50). This is the point of no return for the religious establishment.
Sabbath Controversies: The Pharisees had added 39 categories of prohibited work to the Sabbath, with hundreds of sub-rules. Plucking grain was classified as "reaping" and "threshing." Jesus cuts through their legalism with three arguments: David ate the showbread (precedent), priests work on the Sabbath (exception in the Law itself), and "the Son of man is Lord even of the sabbath" (authority).
Classification: Gospel — Narrative
Key Themes: two sabbath disputes both answered from precedent rather than principle, David eating the showbread and priests working in the temple, before two claims are made that are larger than either argument requires, one greater than the temple and Lord even of the sabbath, the longest Old Testament quotation in the whole Gospel placed deliberately between a murder plot and an accusation of demonic power, a warning about the unforgivable sin arising from a permanent, willful hardening rather than a momentary lapse, and family redefined in the chapter's final verse, whosoever shall do the will of my Father which is in heaven, the same is my brother, and sister, and mother
Lord of the Sabbath (vv.1-14): Two sabbath disputes, and both are answered from precedent rather than principle. The disciples pluck corn and are accused, and the reply cites David eating the shewbread and the priests working in the temple, then makes two claims larger than the argument requires: in this place is one greater than the temple, and the Son of man is Lord even of the sabbath day. Hosea is quoted in between, I will have mercy, and not sacrifice. Then the man with the withered hand, and this time the question is put to them first, is it lawful to heal on the sabbath days? with the sheep-in-a-pit argument attached. Matthew notes the result, the Pharisees held a council against him, how they might destroy him.
He Shall Not Strive Nor Cry (vv.15-30): Matthew interrupts the conflict with the longest Old Testament quotation in his Gospel, from Isaiah 42, and it describes a manner rather than a mission: he shall not strive, nor cry, neither shall any man hear his voice in the streets. A bruised reed shall he not break, and smoking flax shall he not quench. It is placed immediately after a plot to kill him and immediately before the Beelzebub accusation, which is the point. The answer to that accusation is the divided kingdom argument, and then the alternative it forces, if I cast out devils by the Spirit of God, then the kingdom of God is come unto you.
The Unforgivable Sin (vv.31-32): The blasphemy against the Holy Spirit — attributing the clear work of the Spirit to Satan. This was not a momentary slip but a deliberate, sustained, willful rejection of the Spirit's testimony about Christ. The Pharisees saw undeniable evidence and called it demonic. This sin is unforgivable not because God's grace is limited but because the person has permanently hardened themselves against the only means of salvation.
By Thy Words Thou Shalt Be Judged (vv.33-42): The tree and its fruit again, and this time applied to speech, for out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh. Then a warning that has no parallel elsewhere, every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account thereof in the day of judgment, with the summary, by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned. The demand for a sign follows and is refused except for one, the sign of the prophet Jonah, with Matthew's own gloss attached, as Jonah was three days and three nights in the whale's belly. The Ninevites and the queen of the south are then produced as Gentiles who responded to less.
The Empty House, and Who Is My Mother (vv.43-50): A parable about a house swept and garnished and left empty, and the returning spirit bringing seven others, so that the last state of that man is worse than the first. Reform without occupancy is described as an invitation. Then the family arriving and asking for him, and the answer given while stretching out his hand toward the disciples, behold my mother and my brethren, with the criterion stated in the last verse, whosoever shall do the will of my Father which is in heaven, the same is my brother, and sister, and mother.

Map & Geography

  • Galilee: The Sabbath controversy and healing take place in or near Capernaum. The grain fields (v.1) were along paths around the Sea of Galilee.
  • The synagogue (v.9): A local synagogue in Galilee, likely Capernaum, where Jesus healed the man with the withered hand.
  • Jesus' ministry remained centered in Galilee during this period, with growing opposition from Pharisees who had come from Jerusalem to observe Him.

Deep Dive

Setting: Galilee, around AD 28–29. Chapter 11 ended with Jesus' invitation: "Come to me… and I will give you rest… my yoke is easy" (11:28–30). Chapter 12 opens "at that time" with two disputes about the Sabbath, the day of rest, and the contrast is deliberate. The Pharisees' Sabbath had become a burden of rules, and Jesus claims to be its Lord. From there the opposition hardens fast. By v14 the Pharisees are plotting to kill him, and by v24 they say his power comes from Satan. Jesus answers with some of his most solemn words: the warning about blasphemy against the Spirit, the sign of Jonah, and the parable of the empty house. The chapter ends with Jesus redefining his family around doing the Father's will.

Jesus Is Lord of the Sabbath (vv1–8)

  • "His disciples were hungry, and they began to pluck heads of grain" (v1). The law allowed a traveler to pick grain by hand from a neighbor's field, as long as he did not use a sickle (Deut 23:25). So the issue is not theft but the day. The law forbade work on the Sabbath, including harvesting (Exod 34:21), and Jewish teachers had worked out long lists of what counted as work. The Mishnah, written down about AD 200, lists thirty-nine kinds, including reaping and threshing. Rubbing grain in the hands (Luke 6:1) could be counted as both.
  • "What David did when he was hungry" (vv3–4). When David fled from Saul, the priest at Nob gave him the "bread of the Presence," twelve loaves set before God each week and then eaten only by priests (1 Sam 21:1–6; Lev 24:5–9). Scripture does not condemn David. Human need outweighed a ceremonial rule. The comparison also hints at something more: David was God's anointed king, not yet on the throne, and so is Jesus, the Son of David.
  • "The priests in the temple profane the Sabbath and are guiltless" (v5). On the Sabbath the priests worked harder than ever, changing the bread and offering double sacrifices (Num 28:9–10). Temple service overrode the Sabbath rest.
  • "Something greater than the temple is here" (v6). "Something" is neuter in Greek, which may point to the kingdom present in Jesus, or simply be a way of speaking about Jesus himself. Either way, the claim is astonishing. The temple was the holiest place on earth, the house of God's presence. Jesus says what he brings is greater.
  • "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" (v7). Jesus quotes Hosea 6:6 a second time (see 9:13). If the Pharisees had understood God's heart, they would not have condemned hungry men.
  • "The Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath" (v8). God gave the Sabbath (Gen 2:3; Exod 20:8–11). Only its giver could claim to be its Lord. Mark adds "the Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27). Jesus does not throw the Sabbath away. He shows its purpose: rest and mercy. Christians differ on how the Sabbath command applies now, with some keeping Sunday as a Christian Sabbath and others seeing the Sabbath fulfilled in the rest Christ gives (Heb 4:9–10; Col 2:16–17).
  • Commentators: Henry says "the works of necessity and mercy are lawful on the sabbath day," and argues that one day in seven is still binding, with Christ's lordship allowing it to become the Lord's Day. JFB sees Jesus' lordship as raising the Sabbath to "the nearest resemblance to the eternal sabbatism," not abolishing it. Guzik notes that by strict rabbinic rules the disciples' snack could count as several violations at once, and reads the claims to be greater than the temple and lord of the Sabbath as direct claims to deity.

A Man with a Withered Hand (vv9–14)

  • "Their synagogue" (v9). Matthew often says "their" synagogues, a sign of the growing gap between Jesus and the leaders.
  • "A man was there with a withered hand" (v10). "Withered" means shriveled or paralyzed. Luke adds that it was his right hand (Luke 6:6), which would make ordinary work hard. An early Christian writing, quoted by Jerome, imagines him as a stonemason begging to be healed so he would not have to beg for food. That is a later story, not Scripture.
  • "Is it lawful to heal on the Sabbath?" (v10). The question was a trap. Jewish teaching allowed treatment on the Sabbath only when life was in danger. A withered hand could wait a day.
  • "Which one of you who has a sheep, if it falls into a pit on the Sabbath" (v11). Many Jews would rescue an animal on the Sabbath, though the stricter Essenes at Qumran forbade even that. Jesus argues from lesser to greater: "Of how much more value is a man than a sheep!"
  • "So it is lawful to do good on the Sabbath" (v12). Jesus moves the question from "what is allowed?" to "what is good?" Not doing good when one can is not neutral (Mark 3:4).
  • "Stretch out your hand" (v13). It was the one thing the man could not do. In obeying, he found he could. Jesus does no "work" here: he does not even touch the man. He only speaks.
  • "The Pharisees went out and conspired against him, how to destroy him" (v14). This is the first mention in Matthew of a plot to kill Jesus. A healing on the Sabbath leads to plans for murder, probably on the same Sabbath. Mark adds that they joined with the Herodians, supporters of Herod's family (Mark 3:6).
  • Commentators: Henry reads the hand as a picture of spiritual inability: "By nature our hands are withered," and the command to stretch it out shows that grace gives power to obey. Guzik makes the same point, and cites the Mishnah's rule allowing treatment only to save life. JFB defers to Mark and Luke for this scene.

God's Chosen Servant (vv15–21)

  • "Jesus, aware of this, withdrew" (v15). He does not run from danger out of fear. His time has not come. He keeps healing "all" who follow him, and tells them not to make him known.
  • "Behold, my servant whom I have chosen" (v18). This is Isaiah 42:1–4, the longest Old Testament quotation in Matthew. It is the first of Isaiah's "Servant Songs," which climax in the suffering servant of Isaiah 53 (already quoted in 8:17). "My beloved with whom my soul is well pleased" and "I will put my Spirit upon him" echo the voice and the Spirit at Jesus' baptism (3:16–17). Matthew's wording does not match either the Hebrew or the Greek Old Testament exactly, and seems to be his own translation, shaped to bring out the fulfilment.
  • "He will proclaim justice to the Gentiles… and in his name the Gentiles will hope" (vv18, 21). "Justice" here means God's right order, his saving rule set right. The quotation looks ahead to the mission to the nations (28:19).
  • "He will not quarrel or cry aloud" (v19). This explains why Jesus tells people to keep quiet (v16). He is not a political agitator rallying crowds in the streets.
  • "A bruised reed he will not break, and a smoldering wick he will not quench" (v20). A reed that is cracked is good for nothing, and a wick that only smokes is about to go out. Most people would throw them away. The servant does not. He is gentle with the weak, the failing and the nearly hopeless. It is the same heart as "gentle and lowly" (11:29), in sharp contrast with the leaders who have just plotted his death.
  • "Until he brings justice to victory" (v20). His gentleness is not weakness. He will finish what he came to do.
  • Commentators: Henry applies v20 pastorally: "Young beginners in religion are weak as a bruised reed," and Christ will support rather than crush them. JFB notes that Matthew "merely seizes the spirit, instead of the letter of the prediction," quoting loosely but faithfully. Guzik says Jesus withdrew because of timing, not fear, and draws from v20 how tenderly God handles weakness.

Blasphemy Against the Holy Spirit (vv22–32)

  • "Blind and mute" (v22). This man could neither see nor speak, and the cause was a demon. Jesus heals him completely.
  • "Can this be the Son of David?" (v23). The Greek question expects a hesitant "no," almost "this couldn't be the Son of David, could it?" The crowds are moving toward faith but unsure.
  • "It is only by Beelzebul, the prince of demons, that this man casts out demons" (v24). The Pharisees cannot deny the miracle, so they attack its source (9:34; 10:25). "Beelzebul" was a name for Satan.
  • "Every kingdom divided against itself is laid waste" (v25). Jesus answers with plain logic. If Satan were casting out his own demons, his kingdom would be tearing itself apart.
  • "By whom do your sons cast them out?" (v27). "Your sons" means the Pharisees' own followers, Jewish exorcists who used prayers and rituals (Acts 19:13). If casting out demons proves a link with Satan, the Pharisees condemn their own people.
  • "If it is by the Spirit of God that I cast out demons, then the kingdom of God has come upon you" (v28). Matthew usually writes "kingdom of heaven." Here he uses "kingdom of God," perhaps to match "Spirit of God." Luke says "finger of God" (Luke 11:20), recalling the plagues in Egypt (Exod 8:19). The kingdom is not just coming. In Jesus it has arrived.
  • "Unless he first binds the strong man" (v29). Satan is the strong man, and the people he holds are his goods. Jesus has come into his house, tied him up and is carrying off his captives. Isaiah asked, "Can the prey be taken from the mighty?" and answered that God himself would do it (Isa 49:24–25). Jesus defeated Satan in the wilderness (4:1–11) and won the decisive victory at the cross (Col 2:15).
  • "Whoever is not with me is against me" (v30). There is no neutral ground in this war. The picture of gathering and scattering is a shepherd's.
  • "Every sin and blasphemy will be forgiven… but the blasphemy against the Spirit will not be forgiven" (v31). "Blasphemy" means slander, speaking evil of someone. In context, the blasphemy against the Spirit is what the Pharisees have just done: looking straight at the Spirit's work in Jesus, which they cannot deny, and calling it Satan's. It is not a careless word or a single sin, but a settled, deliberate rejection of the Spirit's witness to Christ. Mark adds the reason: "for they were saying, 'He has an unclean spirit'" (Mark 3:30).
  • "Whoever speaks a word against the Son of Man will be forgiven" (v32). Many misunderstood Jesus in his humble state, including his own family and later Paul, who persecuted his followers (1 Tim 1:13). That can be forgiven. But to reject the Spirit's clear testimony is to shut the only door through which forgiveness comes. It is unforgivable because it refuses the forgiver.
  • A pastoral word. Christians across traditions agree that someone troubled by fear of having committed this sin shows by that very concern that they have not. Those who committed it here were not grieving. They were hardening.
  • Commentators: JFB stresses that "All manner of sin and blasphemy shall be forgiven unto men," and reads the exception as deliberate rejection of the Spirit's light. Henry also reads it narrowly, tying it to persistent unbelief in the face of clear evidence. Guzik calls it a settled rejection of the Spirit's testimony about Jesus, and says that anyone who still wants Jesus has not committed it. He suggests that Jesus' knowing their thoughts (v25) may have come through the Spirit rather than from his divine nature, a point on which commentators differ.

A Tree Is Known by Its Fruit (vv33–37)

  • "Either make the tree good and its fruit good, or make the tree bad and its fruit bad" (v33). Jesus has used this picture before for false prophets (7:16–20). Here the point is that the Pharisees' words reveal their hearts. Their slander is bad fruit from a bad tree.
  • "You brood of vipers!" (v34). John the Baptist used the same words for the Pharisees (3:7). Snakes produce snakes.
  • "Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks" (v34). In the Bible the heart is the center of the person: thoughts, will and desires. Words overflow from what fills it.
  • "Every careless word" (v36). "Careless" (argos) literally means "not working," idle or useless. If even idle words will be accounted for, how much more slander against the Holy Spirit.
  • "By your words you will be justified, and by your words you will be condemned" (v37). This is not salvation by speech. Words are evidence of what is in the heart, and the heart is what God judges (15:18–19; James 3:2–12).
  • Commentators: Henry says "vain, idle, impertinent talk is displeasing to God" and will be judged. JFB agrees that idle words "however idle they may seem, will be taken account of." Guzik cites Adam Clarke's explanation of argos as speech that gives no grace or instruction, and turns it sharply toward preachers.

The Sign of Jonah (vv38–42)

  • "Teacher, we wish to see a sign from you" (v38). Jesus has just healed a blind and mute man in front of them, and they ask for a sign. They want proof on their terms, perhaps something in the sky (16:1). Paul says "Jews demand signs" (1 Cor 1:22).
  • "An evil and adulterous generation" (v39). "Adulterous" means spiritually unfaithful, the prophets' picture of Israel chasing other gods like an unfaithful wife (Hos 2; Jer 3).
  • "The sign of the prophet Jonah" (v39). Jonah was swallowed by a great fish and came out alive to preach to Nineveh (Jonah 1:17–3:5). The only sign Jesus will give is his own death and resurrection.
  • "Three days and three nights in the heart of the earth" (v40). "Heart of the earth" means the grave. Jesus was buried Friday and rose Sunday, which does not add up to seventy-two hours. Jewish reckoning counted any part of a day as a whole day, and a later rabbinic saying puts it plainly: a day and a night make a full day, and part of one counts as the whole. Elsewhere Jesus says "on the third day" (16:21), which shows the two expressions meant the same thing. Some have argued for a Wednesday or Thursday crucifixion to fit a full seventy-two hours, but most scholars see no need.
  • "The men of Nineveh will rise up at the judgment… and condemn it" (v41). Nineveh was the capital of Assyria, Israel's cruel enemy. Its people repented at one reluctant prophet's message. "Something greater than Jonah is here," and this generation will not repent.
  • "The queen of the South… came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon" (v42). The queen of Sheba, probably from southwest Arabia, traveled perhaps 1,200 miles to hear Solomon (1 Kings 10:1–10). "Something greater than Solomon is here," and the leaders will not cross the street to listen. Two Gentiles will stand as witnesses against God's own people.
  • Greater than… (vv6, 41, 42). Three times in this chapter Jesus says "something greater is here": greater than the temple (priest), Jonah (prophet) and Solomon (king).
  • Commentators: Henry writes, "The grave was to Christ as the belly of the fish was to Jonah," and takes the three days and nights as partial days. JFB agrees that "heart of the earth" "means simply the grave" and that the count follows Jewish reckoning. Guzik sees the request for a sign as insincere, and cites the rabbinic idiom about part of a day counting as a whole.

Return of an Unclean Spirit (vv43–45)

  • "It passes through waterless places seeking rest" (v43). Jews thought of the desert as a home of evil spirits (Isa 13:21; 34:14; Rev 18:2).
  • "It finds the house empty, swept, and put in order" (v44). The house has been cleaned up, but no one lives there. Reform without a new occupant leaves a person open to worse. The spirit comes back with "seven other spirits more evil than itself."
  • "So also will it be with this evil generation" (v45). Jesus applies the picture to the nation. John's preaching and Jesus' ministry had swept the house, and many had repented outwardly. But if they did not receive the Messiah, their last state would be worse. Within about forty years Jerusalem was destroyed (AD 70).
  • The lesson for individuals. Turning from sin is not enough by itself. The emptied heart must be filled by Christ and his Spirit (Eph 3:17; Rom 8:9).
  • Commentators: Henry applies the parable to the Jewish nation, worse off after rejecting Christ, and adds that "the last state of such will be worse than the first." Guzik agrees and draws the personal lesson that moral reform without the Spirit's indwelling leaves a person exposed. JFB defers to its notes on Luke.

Jesus' Mother and Brothers (vv46–50)

  • "His mother and his brothers stood outside" (v46). Mark explains why they came: they had heard he was not even taking time to eat, and said, "He is out of his mind" (Mark 3:21, 31). They wanted to take him home.
  • A missing verse. The ESV and other modern versions put v47 ("Someone told him, 'Your mother and your brothers are standing outside'") in a footnote, since some early manuscripts lack it. It may have been added from Mark 3:32, or dropped by accident because it ends with the same words as v46. The meaning is the same either way.
  • "Who are my brothers?" The New Testament names four brothers, James, Joseph, Simon and Judas, and mentions sisters (13:55–56). Protestants generally take them as children of Mary and Joseph born after Jesus. The Eastern Orthodox have traditionally seen them as Joseph's children from an earlier marriage, and Roman Catholics as cousins, in keeping with belief in Mary's perpetual virginity. The text itself does not settle the question. What is clear is that at this point they did not believe in him (John 7:5). Later James led the Jerusalem church and wrote the letter of James, and Judas (Jude) wrote the letter of Jude.
  • "Here are my mother and my brothers!" (v49). Jesus points to his disciples. He is not rejecting his family. He honors his mother from the cross (John 19:26–27). But he defines a new family, bound not by blood but by doing God's will.
  • "Whoever does the will of my Father in heaven is my brother and sister and mother" (v50). "Whoever" means anyone, Jew or Gentile, man or woman. The leaders who claimed God as Father are outside, and the disciples who follow Jesus are family.
  • Commentators: Henry writes, "All obedient believers are near akin to Jesus Christ." JFB notes that Jesus used the moment to teach rather than to rebuke his family. Guzik thinks the family may have come to hold Jesus back, and takes the brothers to be Mary's later sons, which reflects the Protestant reading.

Pointing to Christ

The chapter asks who Jesus is, and he answers in claims no prophet made: greater than the temple, Lord of the Sabbath, greater than Jonah and Solomon. He is the chosen servant on whom the Spirit rests, who will not break a bruised reed. He is the stronger man who has bound Satan and is carrying off his captives. The only sign he will give is his own death and rising, three days in the heart of the earth. Those who see the Spirit's work and call it evil shut themselves out. But anyone who comes to him, however weak, finds a place in his family, the brothers and sisters of the Son.

Reflection

  • Mercy Over Rule-Keeping (vv.10-13) — The Pharisees care more about Sabbath regulations than about a man with a withered hand. Where might you be prioritizing rules over compassion for people?
  • Gentleness With the Weak (v.20) — Jesus is described as one who will not break a bruised reed or snuff out a smoldering wick. How does knowing His gentleness toward the weak change how you approach Him in your weakest moments?
  • No Neutral Ground (v.30) — Jesus says whoever is not with Him is against Him, leaving no room for passive neutrality. Where in your life are you trying to stay neutral when Jesus is asking for a decision?
  • The Danger of a Hardened Heart (v.31) — Jesus warns of a sin so persistent in rejecting the Spirit's testimony that it becomes unforgivable. Rather than provoking fear, does examining this passage remind you that a concerned conscience is itself evidence of a soft heart?
  • Words That Reveal the Heart (v.34) — Jesus says the mouth speaks from what fills the heart. What do your words this week — especially the unguarded ones — reveal about the state of your heart?
  • A Family Defined by Obedience (v.50) — Jesus redefines His true family as those who do the will of the Father, not those bound to Him by blood. How invested are you in your spiritual family compared to your biological one?
