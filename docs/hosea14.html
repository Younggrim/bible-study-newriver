Summary

The book closes with a call for Israel to return to the LORD, since their sin has caused their downfall, and to bring words of genuine repentance instead of empty ritual (vv. 1-2). They are told to stop trusting Assyria, horses, or the work of their own hands, since only in God does the fatherless find mercy (v. 3). In response, God promises to heal their unfaithfulness and love them freely, no longer angry with them (v. 4). He pictures Israel flourishing again like a lily, taking root like the cedars of Lebanon, spreading out with beauty and fragrance, and living fruitfully in his shade (vv. 5-8). The book ends with a wisdom appeal: whoever is wise should understand these things, for the ways of the LORD are right, and the righteous walk in them while transgressors stumble (v. 9).

Authorship & Background

Author: Hosea son of Beeri. Prophesied to the northern kingdom (Israel) approximately 755-715 BC. Contemporary with Isaiah and Micah. Key theme: God's unfailing love for unfaithful Israel, illustrated through Hosea's marriage to Gomer (a prostitute). Despite Israel's spiritual adultery, God says: "I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely" (14:4).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — The Final Invitation: Return and Flourish
Key Themes: The call to return, words of repentance, God's free love, healing of backsliding, Israel as a flourishing tree, the wisdom of walking in God's ways
Historical Context: Chapter 14 is the conclusion and climax of Hosea — the book that began with heartbreak ends with healing. After 13 chapters of accusation, judgment, lament, and fleeting hope, God gives the final invitation, return. He even provides the words of repentance for them (v.2-3). Then He makes seven "I will" promises of restoration (vv.4-8). The imagery shifts from barrenness to lush growth: lily, Lebanon cedar, olive tree, grain, vine. The fatherless find mercy; the backslidden find healing; the rebellious find free love. The book ends with a wisdom saying (v.9): the wise will understand these things.
The Final Call: Return to the LORD (vv.1-3): O Israel, return unto the LORD thy God, for thou hast fallen by thine iniquity. The book that opened with a marriage to a prostitute closes with an invitation, and it comes with the wording supplied. Take with you words, and say unto him, Take away all iniquity, and receive us graciously. The prayer given includes what has to be renounced to pray it, Asshur shall not save us, we will not ride upon horses, neither will we say any more to the work of our hands, Ye are our gods. Alliance, army and idol, the three things the book has been arguing about, dropped in one sentence, and the ground of appeal is that in thee the fatherless findeth mercy.
God's Seven I Will Promises (vv.4-8): The answer is a run of first-person commitments. I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely, for mine anger is turned away from him. Then the imagery reverses everything the judgment oracles used: he shall grow as the lily, cast forth his roots as Lebanon, his beauty as the olive tree, they shall revive as the corn and grow as the vine. The vine was empty in chapter 10 and the root was dried in chapter 9. God says I am like a green fir tree, taking the fertility language Baal was worshipped for and claiming it, and asks Ephraim what he still has to do with idols.
The Wisdom Conclusion (v.9): Who is wise, and he shall understand these things? prudent, and he shall know them? for the ways of the LORD are right, and the just shall walk in them, but the transgressors shall fall therein. The book ends by stepping out of prophecy into the vocabulary of Proverbs and handing the reader a test. The same road is walked by one and stumbled over by the other, which is the point, nothing changes about the way, and everything depends on who is on it.

Setting: This is the last chapter of Hosea, the final word of a prophet who preached to the northern kingdom of Israel for perhaps forty years, from the prosperous days of Jeroboam II (died about 753 BC) to the eve of Samaria's fall in 722 BC. Chapter 13 ended in the grave and the horrors of siege. Chapter 14 does not deny that judgment. It looks past it. The ESV heads it "A Plea to Return to the LORD." Hosea gives Israel the very words to pray (vv1–3), God answers with a promise of healing and new life (vv4–8), and a closing proverb tells every later reader what to do with the book (v9). The key word is shuv, "return" or "turn," which appears in vv1, 2, 4 and 7. In Hebrew Bibles the verses are numbered 14:2–10.

A Plea to Return to the LORD (vv1–9)

  • "Return, O Israel, to the LORD your God" (v1). Hosea has used the word "return" again and again: Gomer returning to her husband (2:7; 3:5), Israel's shallow "let us return" (6:1), and Israel's refusal to return (7:10; 11:5). Now the call is plain and final. "To the LORD" can be translated "all the way to the LORD," not just in his direction. "For you have stumbled because of your iniquity": their fall was their own doing (5:5; 13:9), and the way back is open.
  • "Take with you words and return to the LORD" (v2). In the law no one was to appear before the LORD empty-handed (Exod 23:15). Hosea says what to bring: not more sacrifices, which had become worthless (6:6; 8:13), but words. Repentance is to be spoken, not only felt. JFB notes that the Hebrew devarim means both "words" and "things," so God wants real things, not empty phrases. Hosea even writes the prayer for them.
  • "Take away all iniquity; accept what is good" (v2). The prayer begins with forgiveness. "Take away" (nasa') is the word for lifting and carrying a load, the same word used of the scapegoat that "shall bear all their iniquities" away (Lev 16:22). "Accept what is good" may mean "receive us graciously" or "accept the good we offer," that is, the confession itself.
  • "We will pay with bulls the vows of our lips" (v2). The Hebrew is literally "we will pay the bulls of our lips": instead of animals, their lips will be the offering. The Greek translation reads "the fruit of our lips," and that is the phrase Hebrews picks up: "let us continually offer up a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that acknowledge his name" (Heb 13:15). With the temple far away in exile, praise and confession would be their sacrifice (compare Ps 51:16–17).
  • "Assyria shall not save us; we will not ride on horses" (v3). The prayer renounces the three things Israel had trusted in place of God. Assyria stands for foreign alliances (5:13; 8:9; 12:1). Horses stand for military power, often bought from Egypt (Deut 17:16; Isa 31:1). "We will say no more, 'Our God,' to the work of our hands" renounces idols (8:6; 13:2). True repentance names its false saviours and lets them go.
  • "In you the orphan finds mercy" (v3). The prayer ends with the reason for hope. "Finds mercy" is the verb racham, the root of Lo-ruhamah, "No Mercy," the name of Hosea's daughter (1:6). The orphan is someone with no one to stand up for them. Israel, having lost its kings and its gods, is that orphan, and God is "father of the fatherless" (Ps 68:5). The child named "No Mercy" will find mercy.
  • "I will heal their apostasy" (v4). Now God speaks. "Apostasy" is meshuvah, "turning away," from the same root as "return." Their turning away will be met by God's turning toward them. Healing has been a thread through the book: Israel ran to Assyria for healing (5:13), hoped God would heal them (6:1), and did not realise he had healed them before (7:1; 11:3). Now he will do it. Sin is pictured as a disease that only God can cure (Ps 103:3; Jer 3:22).
  • "I will love them freely, for my anger has turned from them" (v4). "Freely" is nedavah, the word for a freewill offering, given gladly and not demanded (Exod 35:29). God's love is a gift he chooses to give, not wages owed. Henry: "Whom God loves, he loves freely; not because they deserve it, but of his own good pleasure." And his anger has "turned," shuv again. They turn back, and his anger turns away.
  • "I will be like the dew to Israel" (v5). In Israel's long dry summer, from May to October, there is almost no rain, and the heavy night dew keeps plants alive (Gen 27:28; Deut 33:28). Earlier Israel's love was "like the dew that goes early away" (6:4; 13:3). Now God himself will be the dew, and he does not evaporate.
  • "He shall blossom like the lily; he shall take root like the trees of Lebanon" (v5). The pictures pile up: the lily for beauty, the cedars of Lebanon for deep roots and strength (Ps 92:12). JFB comments that "spiritual growth consists most in the growth of the root which is out of sight." The fruitless, root-dried Ephraim of 9:16 becomes a living garden.
  • "His shoots shall spread out; his beauty shall be like the olive" (v6). The olive tree was evergreen, long-lived and fruitful, the source of oil for food, light and anointing (Ps 52:8; Jer 11:16). "His fragrance like Lebanon": the scent of cedar forests. JFB: "One plant is not enough to express the graces of God's elect people."
  • "They shall return and dwell beneath my shadow" (v7). The Hebrew reads "his shadow." It may mean God's shade, as the ESV takes it (compare v8 and Ps 91:1), or Israel's shade, the restored tree giving shelter to others, as JFB reads it. "They shall flourish like the grain; they shall blossom like the vine; their fame shall be like the wine of Lebanon." Grain, vine and wine: exactly the gifts Israel had credited to Baal (2:5, 8). Now God gives them openly.
  • Lebanon three times (vv5–7). Lebanon, the mountain range north of Israel, stood for what was lofty, strong, fragrant and fine: its cedars, its scent, its wine. Hosea uses it three times, as if to say the restored Israel will be everything Baal promised and could never give.
  • "O Ephraim, what have I to do with idols?" (v8). The Hebrew could be God speaking or Ephraim speaking. Most read it as God: what more has he to do with idols? Or as the restored Ephraim saying it, "What have I to do with idols any more?" (KJV). Either way, the long marriage between Israel and its idols is over.
  • "It is I who answer and look after you" (v8). The idols could not hear or answer (1 Kgs 18:26–29). Some scholars think there is a sly wordplay here: "I answer" ('aniti) and "I look after him" ('ashurennu) sound like Anat and Asherah, two Canaanite goddesses of fertility. If so, God is saying that he is everything those goddesses claimed to be. It is a suggestion, but it fits the chapter.
  • "I am like an evergreen cypress; from me comes your fruit" (v8). This is the only place in the Old Testament where God compares himself to a tree. Canaanite religion worshipped at sacred trees "under every green tree" (Deut 12:2; Hos 4:13). God says the true evergreen is himself. The tree is "evergreen," never losing its leaves, and Ephraim's fruit, the very meaning of the name, comes from him. Jesus says the same: "apart from me you can do nothing" (John 15:5).
  • "Whoever is wise, let him understand these things" (v9). The book closes with a word in the style of Proverbs, probably added to the collected prophecy, addressing every future reader. "The ways of the LORD are right, and the upright walk in them, but transgressors stumble in them." The same road leads one person home and trips another. It ties back to v1, "you have stumbled," and to Psalm 1. JFB notes this is the only use of "upright" (literally "righteous") in Hosea. Henry and JFB both connect it with Christ, "a stone of stumbling, and a rock of offence" to some and a foundation to others (Isa 8:14; 1 Pet 2:6–8).
  • Commentators: All three read the chapter as mercy following judgment. Guzik stresses that repentance must be spoken, "take words with you," and connects it with confessing with the mouth in Romans 10:9–10. He sees in "I will heal" a disease to cure, not only a crime to punish. Henry on v2: "Take away iniquity; lift it off as a burden we are ready to sink under." He compares v4 to the prodigal's father: "The Lord meets penitents with mercy, as the father of the prodigal met his returning son." JFB reads "freely" as "gratuitous, unmerited, and abundant love" and links "calves of our lips" to the New Testament "sacrifice of praise." On v7 JFB takes "his shadow" as Israel's own, while the Tyndale notes take it as God's. Guzik also passes on a description of the chapter as a rainbow after a storm.

Pointing to Christ

Hosea ends where the gospel begins: with words of confession, a God who takes away all iniquity, and love given freely to people who deserve none. The prophet's own family carried the story: a son called "Not My People" and a daughter called "No Mercy" (ch 1). In this chapter the orphan finds mercy. Paul and Peter both say that in Christ the names are reversed for Jew and Gentile alike (Rom 9:25–26; 1 Pet 2:10). Jesus is the evergreen tree from whom all fruit comes and the dew that never dries. He is also the stone on which some stumble and others build. "Whoever is wise, let him understand these things."

Reflection

  • An Invitation to Return (v.1) — After chapters of judgment and exile, Hosea's closing word to Israel is simply an invitation to come back. Where do you assume the door has closed on you, when God is still saying return?
  • Words Provided for the Repentant (vv.2-3) — Israel is given the exact words to bring to God, asking Him to take away their sin and refusing to trust foreign alliances or idols any longer. When you don't know how to pray after failure, do you let Scripture give you the words?
  • Loved Freely, Not Earned (v.4) — God promises to heal Israel's backsliding and love them freely, with no mention of anything they did to deserve it. Do you believe God's love toward you is unconditional, or do you still think you have to earn it back after failure?
  • All Fruit Traced Back to Him (v.8) — God declares that Israel's fruitfulness comes from Him alone, not from their own effort. Where are you striving to produce results in your own strength instead of staying rooted in Him?
  • The Ways of the LORD Are Right (v.9) — The book closes with a call to the wise to walk in God's ways, while transgressors stumble over that same path. Does your life show that you're walking in God's ways, or stumbling over them?
