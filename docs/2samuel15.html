1Some time later, Absalom provided for himself a chariot with horses and fifty men to run ahead of him.

2He would get up early and stand beside the road leading to the city gate. Whenever anyone had a grievance to bring before the king for a decision, Absalom would call out and ask, “What city are you from?” And if he replied, “Your servant is from one of the tribes of Israel,”

3Absalom would say, “Look, your claims are good and right, but the king has no deputy to hear you.”

4And he would add, “If only someone would appoint me judge in the land, then everyone with a grievance or dispute could come to me, and I would give him justice.”

5Also, when anyone approached to bow down to him, Absalom would reach out his hand, take hold of him, and kiss him.

6Absalom did this to all the Israelites who came to the king for justice. In this way he stole the hearts of the men of Israel.

7After four years had passed, Absalom said to the king, “Please let me go to Hebron to fulfill a vow I have made to the LORD.

8For your servant made a vow while dwelling in Geshur of Aram, saying: ‘If indeed the LORD brings me back to Jerusalem, I will worship the LORD in Hebron.’”

9“Go in peace,” said the king. So Absalom got up and went to Hebron.

10Then Absalom sent spies throughout the tribes of Israel with this message: “When you hear the sound of the horn, you are to say, ‘Absalom reigns in Hebron!’”

11Two hundred men from Jerusalem accompanied Absalom. They had been invited as guests and they went along innocently, for they knew nothing about the matter.

12While Absalom was offering the sacrifices, he sent for Ahithophel the Gilonite, David’s counselor, to come from his hometown of Giloh. So the conspiracy gained strength, and Absalom’s following kept increasing.

13Then a messenger came to David and reported, “The hearts of the men of Israel are with Absalom.”

14And David said to all the servants with him in Jerusalem, “Arise and let us flee, or we will not escape from Absalom! We must leave quickly, or he will soon overtake us, heap disaster on us, and put the city to the sword.”

15The king’s servants replied, “Whatever our lord the king decides, we are your servants.”

16Then the king set out, and his entire household followed him. But he left behind ten concubines to take care of the palace.

17So the king set out with all the people following him. He stopped at the last house,

18and all his servants marched past him—all the Cherethites and Pelethites, and six hundred Gittites who had followed him from Gath.

19Then the king said to Ittai the Gittite, “Why should you also go with us? Go back and stay with the new king, since you are both a foreigner and an exile from your homeland.

20In fact, you arrived only yesterday; should I make you wander around with us today while I do not know where I am going? Go back and take your brothers with you. May the LORD show you loving devotion and faithfulness.”

21But Ittai answered the king, “As surely as the LORD lives, and as my lord the king lives, wherever my lord the king may be, whether it means life or death, there will your servant be!”

22“March on then,” said David to Ittai. So Ittai the Gittite marched past with all his men and all the little ones who were with him.

23Everyone in the countryside was weeping loudly as all the people passed by. And as the king crossed the Kidron Valley, all the people also passed toward the way of the wilderness.

24Zadok was also there, and all the Levites with him were carrying the ark of the covenant of God. And they set down the ark of God, and Abiathar offered sacrifices until all the people had passed out of the city.

25Then the king said to Zadok, “Return the ark of God to the city. If I find favor in the eyes of the LORD, He will bring me back and let me see both it and His dwelling place again.

26But if He should say, ‘I do not delight in you,’ then here I am; let Him do to me whatever seems good to Him.”

27The king also said to Zadok the priest, “Are you not a seer? Return to the city in peace—you with your son Ahimaaz, and Abiathar with his son Jonathan.

28See, I will wait at the fords of the wilderness until word comes from you to inform me.”

29So Zadok and Abiathar returned the ark of God to Jerusalem and stayed there.

30But David continued up the Mount of Olives, weeping as he went up. His head was covered, and he was walking barefoot. And all the people with him covered their heads and went up, weeping as they went.

31Now someone told David: “Ahithophel is among the conspirators with Absalom.” So David pleaded, “O LORD, please turn the counsel of Ahithophel into foolishness!”

32When David came to the summit, where he used to worship God, Hushai the Archite was there to meet him with his robe torn and dust on his head.

33David said to him, “If you go on with me, you will be a burden to me.

34But you can thwart the counsel of Ahithophel for me if you return to the city and say to Absalom: ‘I will be your servant, my king; in the past I was your father’s servant, but now I will be your servant.’

35Will not Zadok and Abiathar the priests be there with you? Report to them everything you hear from the king’s palace.

36Indeed, their two sons, Ahimaaz son of Zadok and Jonathan son of Abiathar, are there with them. Send them to me with everything you hear.”

37So David’s friend Hushai arrived in Jerusalem just as Absalom was entering the city.

Summary

Absalom spends four years at the city gate intercepting petitioners and quietly courting Israel's loyalty away from his father (vv. 1-6). He then travels to Hebron under a false pretext and proclaims himself king, drawing conspirators including David's trusted counselor Ahithophel (vv. 7-12). When David learns of the revolt he flees Jerusalem on foot, weeping, leaving ten concubines behind to keep the house (vv. 13-17, 30). He sends the ark back to the city rather than use it as a talisman, trusting God's will over its presence, and sends his friend Hushai back to Absalom's court to undermine Ahithophel's counsel (vv. 24-29, 32-37).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Nathan and Gad (1 Chronicles 29:29). Originally one book with 1 Samuel in the Hebrew Bible. The book covers David's entire reign — from his lament over Saul's death to the purchase of the threshing floor for the future Temple (approximately 1010-970 BC).
Historical Context: Chapter 15 records the most devastating crisis of David's reign — his own son's rebellion and the king's flight from Jerusalem. This is the direct fulfillment of Nathan's prophecy in 12:11: "I will raise up evil against thee out of thine own house." Absalom, restored to David's presence at the end of chapter 14 but never truly reconciled, now executes a carefully planned coup. The chapter divides into two major movements: Absalom's conspiracy (vv.1-12) and David's flight (vv.13-37). What makes this chapter theologically rich is David's response to the crisis — not the warrior-king of his youth but a broken, humble man who submits to God's sovereignty even in judgment. David's ascent of the Mount of Olives, weeping and barefoot (v.30), prefigures Christ's own agony in the same location. The chapter also reveals David's strategic wisdom even in retreat — he sends the ark back, positions Hushai as a spy, and establishes a communication network. Faith and prudence work together.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: A conspiracy that begins with chariots and runners and a seat at the city gate rather than with any act of violence, every petitioner's complaint validated and every fault laid at the king's door before Absalom ever asks to be made judge, a religious vow used as the pretext to reach Hebron, the very city of David's own first anointing, an ark sent back into the city rather than carried along as a talisman, if I find favour he will bring me again, and a king climbing the Mount of Olives weeping, barefoot and covered, in a scene later echoed by a greater Son of David
Absalom Steals Israel's Hearts (vv.1-6): Absalom's conspiracy begins not with violence but with politics. He acquires the trappings of royalty — chariot, horses, fifty runners (v.1) — imitating the style of surrounding kings. He positions himself at the city gate where legal disputes were heard and systematically undermines David's administration. His method is subtle and effective: he validates every petitioner's complaint ("thy matters are good and right," v.3), blames the king for neglect ("there is no man deputed of the king to hear thee," v.3), and positions himself as the solution ("Oh that I were made judge in the land," v.4). He refuses the formal homage due to royalty, instead embracing and kissing each person (v.5) — creating an image of accessibility and humility. The narrator's summary is devastating: "so Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel" (v.6). The verb "stole" (Hebrew "ganav") indicates deception — these hearts were taken by fraud, not earned by faithfulness.
The Conspiracy Launched (vv.7-12): After four years of this political groundwork (ESV; KJV reads "forty years," likely a textual variant), Absalom moves to open rebellion. He uses a religious pretext — a vow to the LORD made in Geshur (vv.7-8) — to get David's permission to go to Hebron. Hebron is strategically chosen: it was David's first capital (2 Samuel 2:1-4), the place where David was anointed king over all Israel (5:3), and Absalom's birthplace (3:3). It carried royal legitimacy. From Hebron, Absalom sends "spies" (KJV) or "secret messengers" (ESV) throughout Israel with a coordinated signal: at the trumpet blast, declare "Absalom reigneth in Hebron" (v.10). Two hundred men from Jerusalem accompany him "in their simplicity" — innocent dupes who lend credibility to the movement (v.11). Most critically, Absalom recruits Ahithophel, David's own counselor (v.12), whose defection signals that the conspiracy has reached the highest levels of government. "The conspiracy was strong; for the people increased continually with Absalom" (v.12).
David's Flight from Jerusalem (vv.13-23): When word reaches David, his response is immediate and decisive: "Arise, and let us flee" (v.14). This is not cowardice but wisdom — David knows that a siege of Jerusalem would destroy the city and its inhabitants. He also recognizes God's hand in this judgment (as he will state explicitly in v.26). David leaves with his household, his servants, his personal guard (the Cherethites and Pelethites), and six hundred Gittites — Philistine mercenaries who had followed him from Gath. The encounter with Ittai the Gittite (vv.19-22) is one of the chapter's most moving moments. David urges this foreigner to stay behind — "thou art a stranger, and also an exile" (v.19). Ittai's response echoes Ruth's famous declaration: "in what place my lord the king shall be, whether in death or life, even there also will thy servant be" (v.21). A Gentile foreigner shows more loyalty than Israel's own tribes. "All the country wept with a loud voice" as the king crossed the brook Kidron toward the wilderness (v.23).
The Ark and David's Submission (vv.24-29): Zadok and the Levites bring the ark of the covenant out of Jerusalem to accompany David. David's response reveals the depth of his spiritual maturity: "Carry back the ark of God into the city: if I shall find favour in the eyes of the LORD, he will bring me again" (v.25). David refuses to use the ark as a talisman or political prop. He will not manipulate God's presence for his own advantage. His submission is complete: "But if he thus say, I have no delight in thee; behold, here am I, let him do to me as seemeth good unto him" (v.26). This is the language of absolute surrender — David places himself entirely in God's hands, accepting whatever verdict God renders. At the same time, David wisely positions Zadok and Abiathar as intelligence assets in Jerusalem, with their sons as messengers (vv.27-29). Faith and wisdom are not opposites.
David on the Mount of Olives (vv.30-37): David ascends the Mount of Olives "weeping as he went up, and had his head covered, and he went barefoot" (v.30) — signs of mourning and humiliation. All the people with him do the same. This scene powerfully prefigures Christ's own agony on the Mount of Olives (Luke 22:39-44) — a greater Son of David, betrayed by one close to Him, weeping in submission to the Father's will. When David learns that Ahithophel has joined the conspiracy, he prays: "O LORD, I pray thee, turn the counsel of Ahithophel into foolishness" (v.31). God answers this prayer through Hushai the Archite, who arrives at the summit with torn coat and dirt on his head (v.32). David sends Hushai back to Jerusalem as a counterintelligence agent — to feign loyalty to Absalom and defeat Ahithophel's counsel from within (vv.33-36). The chapter ends with Hushai entering Jerusalem just as Absalom arrives — the stage is set for the battle of counselors in chapters 16-17.

Setting: Jerusalem, around 976 BC, roughly the twenty-fifth year of David's reign in Jerusalem and some eleven years after Amnon violated Tamar (13:23, 38; 14:28; 15:7). Absalom has been brought home from exile and given a cold kiss of reconciliation (14:33), but nothing has been resolved. He is handsome, popular and probably now the eldest surviving son, but no one has named him heir. David is older and seems to have grown distant from the daily work of judging his people. Nathan had warned, "I will raise up evil against you out of your own house" (12:11). In this chapter that evil takes the throne, and David leaves his city on foot, weeping. Psalm 3 is headed "A Psalm of David, when he fled from Absalom his son."

Absalom's Conspiracy (vv1–12)

  • "Absalom got himself a chariot and horses, and fifty men to run before him" (v1). These were the trappings of a king. Samuel had warned that a king would take sons "to run before his chariots" (1 Sam 8:11). Adonijah, the next son to grab for the throne, does exactly the same (1 Kings 1:5). Horses and chariots were still rare in Israel, so this was a public show.
  • "Absalom used to rise early and stand beside the way of the gate" (v2). The city gate was where courts sat and business was done (Ruth 4:1–2). People from the tribes came to the king's court for justice when local courts failed them. Absalom gets there first, every morning.
  • "Your claims are good and right, but there is no man designated by the king to hear you" (v3). He agrees with everyone before hearing the other side. He does not promise to help. He only plants the idea that the king does not care. There may have been some truth in the complaint. David had failed to do justice for Tamar (13:21), and Absalom knew it.
  • "Oh that I were judge in the land!" (v4). "Judge" here means ruler, the one who sets things right. He is campaigning.
  • "He would put out his hand and take hold of him and kiss him" (v5). When people bowed to him as a prince, he raised them up and embraced them like equals. He used the same kiss David had given him (14:33).
  • "So Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel" (v6). In Hebrew, "to steal someone's heart" means to deceive them. Jacob "stole the heart of Laban" when he slipped away without telling him (Gen 31:20, where the ESV has "tricked"). So the phrase means both that he won their affection and that he did it by deceit.
  • "At the end of four years" (v7). The standard Hebrew text reads "forty years," which cannot be right, since David reigned only forty years in all. The ESV follows the Greek, the Syriac and the historian Josephus in reading "four." Four years, probably counted from Absalom's return or from his reconciliation with David.
  • "Let me go and pay my vow… in Hebron" (v7). Hebron was where Absalom was born (3:2–3) and where David was first crowned, over Judah and then over all Israel (2:4; 5:3). It was a natural place to launch a claim to the throne, and perhaps the men of Judah there resented that David had moved the capital to Jerusalem. The "vow" is a cover story using worship, as David himself had once used a family sacrifice as an excuse to be absent from Saul's table (1 Sam 20:6, 29).
  • "Go in peace" (v9). These are the last words David ever speaks to Absalom. The father blesses the son who is leaving to overthrow him.
  • "As soon as you hear the sound of the trumpet, then say, 'Absalom is king at Hebron!'" (v10). Secret agents were planted in every tribe. The trumpet would sound from hill to hill, and the cry would be taken up all over the country at once, making the revolt seem unstoppable.
  • "Two hundred men… who were invited guests… knew nothing" (v11). Respected men from Jerusalem went along to the "sacrifice." Their presence made it look as if the court supported Absalom. They were used without their knowledge.
  • "He sent for Ahithophel the Gilonite, David's counselor" (v12). This was the biggest blow. Ahithophel's advice was treated "as if one consulted the word of God" (16:23). Giloh was a town in the hill country of Judah near Hebron (Josh 15:51). He was probably Bathsheba's grandfather (11:3; 23:34). The text never gives his motive, but many think he had never forgiven David for what he did to her and to Uriah. Many Christians have heard David's grief over him in Psalm 41:9 and 55:12–14: "my close friend in whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted his heel against me." Jesus applies Psalm 41:9 to Judas (John 13:18).
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry doubts there was any vow at all: "If he said he had made such a vow, nobody could disprove him." JFB thinks the real vow, if there was one, was a thanksgiving promised in exile, and that Hebron was chosen because it was a famous place of worship and his birthplace. JFB also sees genuine discontent behind the revolt: leftover loyalty to Saul, the Bathsheba scandal, Joab's arrogance and slow justice. Guzik grants that Absalom had real grievances, since David had done nothing about Tamar, but calls his deepest sin impatience: he tried to take the throne while his father lived. Guzik also notes that Absalom may well have felt spiritual and sure of God's blessing while he did it. All three agree on "four" rather than "forty" years in v7.

David Flees Jerusalem (vv13–37)

  • "The hearts of the men of Israel have gone after Absalom" (v13). The stolen hearts (v6) have now followed the thief.
  • "Arise, and let us flee… lest he… strike the city with the edge of the sword" (v14). David does not try to hold Jerusalem. He may have feared a siege trapping him with traitors inside, or he wanted to spare the city a massacre. He also knew Absalom would kill without hesitation (13:28–29).
  • "The king left ten concubines to keep the house" (v16). A concubine was a lesser wife, a legal wife without full rights. David expects to return soon. Their fate is the fulfillment of Nathan's word in 12:11, as 16:21–22 will show.
  • "They halted at the last house" (v17). Literally "the far house," perhaps the last building on the edge of the city. David stops there and reviews his loyal troops as they pass.
  • The Cherethites, Pelethites and Gittites (v18). The Cherethites and Pelethites were David's bodyguard, probably Cretans and Philistines (8:18; 20:23). The six hundred Gittites were men from Gath, the Philistine city where David once lived as a refugee (1 Sam 27:2–3). At the darkest hour of his reign, foreigners stand by David while his own son and countrymen turn against him.
  • "Why do you also go with us?… You came only yesterday" (vv19–20). Ittai of Gath was a newcomer and a foreigner. David releases him: go back and serve "the king," meaning Absalom. He prays the LORD will show Ittai "steadfast love and faithfulness" (ḥesed and ʾemet), the covenant loyalty David cannot now promise him himself.
  • "Wherever my lord the king shall be, whether for death or for life, there also will your servant be" (v21). Ittai swears by the LORD, Israel's God. His words echo another foreigner's pledge: Ruth the Moabite's "where you go I will go… where you die I will die" (Ruth 1:16–17). Ruth was David's great-grandmother. Ittai later commands a third of David's army (18:2).
  • "All the land wept aloud… and the king crossed the brook Kidron" (v23). The Kidron is the valley on the east side of Jerusalem, dry in summer and running only after the winter rains. It separates the city from the Mount of Olives. About a thousand years later, on the night he was betrayed, Jesus "went out with his disciples across the brook Kidron" to a garden on that same mountain (John 18:1).
  • The ark brought out (v24). Zadok and Abiathar, David's two priests (8:17), bring the ark to go with the king. In the days of Eli, Israel had carried the ark into battle like a lucky charm, and it was captured (1 Sam 4:3–11).
  • "Carry the ark of God back into the city" (v25). David will not use God's presence as a tool. "If I find favor in the eyes of the LORD, he will bring me back… But if he says, 'I have no pleasure in you,' behold, here I am, let him do to me what seems good to him" (vv25–26). This is the old David, humble before God. He accepts that this may be the LORD's discipline, and he leaves the outcome in God's hands. It echoes Joab's faith in 10:12 and Eli's in 1 Sam 3:18.
  • "Are you not a seer?" (v27). A seer was a prophet (1 Sam 9:9). The Hebrew is a little unclear and some read it as "Do you see?" meaning "Do you understand?" Either way, David is giving Zadok a role. The priests' sons, Ahimaaz and Jonathan, will carry news from the city to the king at "the fords of the wilderness" (v28), the Jordan crossings near Jericho. David trusts God and also makes plans.
  • "David went up the ascent of the Mount of Olives, weeping as he went, barefoot and with his head covered" (v30). These are signs of deep mourning and shame (Esth 6:12; Isa 20:2–4; Jer 14:3–4). The people with him do the same. The king who once danced before the ark (6:14) now walks barefoot up the hill in tears.
  • "O LORD, please turn the counsel of Ahithophel into foolishness" (v31). A short, urgent prayer. "Foolishness" may play on the name: some think Ahithophel means "brother of folly." God answers almost at once.
  • "While David was coming to the summit, where God was worshiped" (v32). At the top of the Mount of Olives was a place of worship. As David arrives there, the answer to his prayer meets him: Hushai the Archite with his coat torn and dirt on his head. The Archites were a clan living near the border of Benjamin and Ephraim (Josh 16:2). Hushai may not have been an Israelite by birth.
  • "If you return to the city… then you will defeat for me the counsel of Ahithophel" (v34). An old man would slow the flight, but as a spy in Absalom's court he can do far more. David tells him to pretend to switch sides. Hushai's reports are to go through the priests and their sons (vv35–36).
  • "So Hushai, David's friend, came into the city, just as Absalom was entering Jerusalem" (v37). "The king's friend" was an official title for a trusted royal adviser (1 Chr 27:33; 1 Kings 4:5). The timing is tense: the rebel enters by one gate, and David's man slips in at the same moment.
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry says of the ark: "David's heart, like Eli's, trembles for the ark of God," and he admires David's submission to God's will. JFB holds that David also sent the ark back so the priests could watch the enemy's movements for him. All three treat the climb up the Mount of Olives as genuine humility, and JFB says David was moved by "contrition for his transgressions." Guzik draws the line to Jesus, who walked the same path from the city across the Kidron, but who suffered for others' sins, while David suffered for his own. On Hushai, Henry admits he cannot see how this deception "can be justified, as a stratagem in war." Guzik points out how quickly God answered David's prayer against Ahithophel. JFB sees in David's prayer and his plan for Hushai "fervent piety… combined… with… far-seeing policy." The Tyndale notes say the same in their own way: David trusted God's guidance and still made strategic moves.

Pointing to Christ

A king is betrayed by his own son and by a trusted friend who ate at his table. He leaves Jerusalem, crosses the Kidron, climbs the Mount of Olives weeping and gives himself into God's hands: "let him do to me what seems good to him." The Gospel writers saw the pattern. On the night he was betrayed, Jesus crossed the same brook to the same mountain (John 18:1), and in Gethsemane prayed, "not as I will, but as you will" (Matt 26:39). He quoted Psalm 41:9 of Judas (John 13:18). The difference is the reason. David was suffering the fruit of his own sin. Jesus, the Son of David, had no sin, and he went up that hill to bear the sins of others. A foreigner, Ittai, swore to follow David "whether for death or for life." The true King calls people from every nation to follow him like that, and promises them a share in his return.

Reflection

  • Stolen Hearts (vv.2-6) — Absalom won people over simply by validating every complaint and blaming David for their problems, without ever actually delivering justice. How do you tell the difference between a leader, or a voice in your own head, that truly cares and one that is just telling you what you want to hear?
  • Wisdom to Retreat (v.14) — David's decision to flee Jerusalem rather than fight looked like weakness but was actually wisdom, sparing the city a siege. Can you recognize when stepping back is faith and strategy rather than defeat?
  • Loyalty Tested by Crisis (v.21) — Ittai the foreigner, who owed David nothing, pledged to stay with him in life or death, while many of David's own people abandoned him. Adversity reveals who is really with you — who has proven that in your life?
  • Surrendering the Outcome to God (v.26) — David refused to use the ark as a good-luck charm, telling Zadok to take it back to the city and trusting God to bring him home if He desired, or not, if He didn't. Can you hand God your outcome that completely, without needing to control it?
  • A One-Sentence Prayer (v.31) — When David learned Ahithophel had joined the rebellion, his prayer was short and desperate, not polished, and God answered it. Do you wait for the right words before you pray, or can you cry out to God exactly as you are?
