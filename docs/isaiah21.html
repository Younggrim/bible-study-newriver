Summary

This chapter gathers three short, urgent oracles. First, Isaiah sees a vision of Babylon's sudden fall under siege from Elam and Media, and the news leaves him physically shaken with dread even as he relays the command to keep watch (vv. 1-5). A watchman set on a tower waits through the night and finally reports that Babylon has fallen and her idols lie shattered on the ground (vv. 6-9). Two brief oracles follow: a cryptic exchange with Edom, called Dumah, about how much longer the night of trouble will last, and a warning that within a year Arabia's glory and its warriors will be swept away (vv. 11-17).

Authorship & Background

Author: Isaiah son of Amoz (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — Burden of the Desert of the Sea / Burden of Dumah / Burden upon Arabia
Key Themes: The fall of Babylon to Medo-Persia, the watchman motif, the emotional toll on the prophet, Edom's uncertain future, Arabia's fleeting glory
Historical Context: This chapter contains one of Isaiah's most dramatic visions. The "desert of the sea" (v.1) refers to Babylon (the marshlands of southern Mesopotamia). Isaiah sees Babylon's fall — "Babylon is fallen, is fallen" (v.9) — a prophecy fulfilled in 539 BC when Cyrus the Persian conquered Babylon. The Dumah oracle (vv.11-12) addresses Edom with an enigmatic watchman exchange. The Arabia oracle (vv.13-17) predicts the decline of the Dedanite and Kedarite Arab tribes within one year. These oracles likely date to different periods of Isaiah's ministry but are grouped thematically as desert-region burdens.
The Desert of the Sea (vv.1-10): The burden of the desert of the sea, and the oracle is deliberately disorienting from its title onward. The prophet reports his own distress at receiving it, therefore are my loins filled with pain, pangs have taken hold upon me, my heart panted, the night of my pleasure hath he turned into fear unto me. Then a watchman is posted, set a watchman, let him declare what he seeth, and what he sees is a chariot with two riders. And the report he brings back is the line the passage is known for, Babylon is fallen, is fallen, which Revelation 18 takes up word for word. Prepare the table, watch in the watchtower, eat, drink, arise, ye princes, anoint the shield, which reads as a feast interrupted.
The Burden of Dumah (vv.11-12): Two verses, and they are the most compressed exchange in the book. He calleth to me out of Seir, Watchman, what of the night, watchman, what of the night. The watchman said, The morning cometh, and also the night, if ye will enquire, enquire ye, return, come. The question is asked twice and the answer refuses to be either good news or bad, morning and night together, followed by an invitation to come back and ask again. It is the least resolved oracle in the collection and it is left that way.
The Burden upon Arabia (vv.13-17): The burden upon Arabia, and it opens with an instruction to the local population to do something concrete, the inhabitants of the land of Tema brought water to him that was thirsty, they prevented with their bread him that fled. Caravan traders sheltering refugees from a war. Then the same hireling's contract that dated the Moab oracle, within a year, according to the years of an hireling, and all the glory of Kedar shall fail. And the survivors are counted as a small number, the residue of the number of archers shall be diminished.

Deep Dive

Setting: Chapter 21 holds three short oracles, each with a puzzling title: "the wilderness of the sea" (Babylon), "Dumah" (Edom), and "Arabia." All three concern lands east and south of Judah, along the desert trade routes. The first returns to Babylon, the subject of chapters 13–14, and is the source of the cry "Fallen, fallen is Babylon," which Revelation takes up (Rev 14:8; 18:2). In Isaiah's day (about 740–685 BC) Babylon kept rebelling against Assyria, with support from its neighbor Elam, and Judah watched these revolts with interest, since a weaker Assyria meant less pressure on Jerusalem (ch 39). This oracle shows Babylon's fall from the inside, through the prophet's own shaken eyes. The oracles about Edom and Arabia picture peoples caught in the long night of empire, asking how much longer it will last.

Fallen, Fallen Is Babylon (vv1–17)

  • "The oracle concerning the wilderness of the sea" (v1). This is a cryptic title for Babylon, as v9 makes clear. Southern Mesopotamia was a flat plain of marshes and lagoons near the Persian Gulf, and Assyrian texts actually called the region "the Sealand." The Euphrates also regularly flooded the plain like a sea. JFB explains it this way, and Guzik describes the plain broken up by lakes and marshes. The Tyndale notes add a possible irony: a land physically lush but spiritually a desert. Babylon, the "glory of kingdoms" (13:19), gets a riddle for a name.
  • "As whirlwinds in the Negeb sweep on, it comes from the wilderness, from a terrible land" (v1). The Negeb is the dry south of Judah, known for sudden violent windstorms off the desert. Isaiah's hearers knew those storms. The attack on Babylon will come just as suddenly.
  • "A stern vision is told to me; the traitor betrays, and the destroyer destroys" (v2). The Hebrew repeats sounds: habboged boged… hashoded shoded, as the Tyndale notes point out. It sounds like hammer blows. Isaiah uses the same phrase in 33:1. Babylon, which betrayed and destroyed, now meets betrayal and destruction.
  • "Go up, O Elam; lay siege, O Media" (v2). Elam lay east of Babylon in what is now southwest Iran. Media lay to the north and east (13:17). When Babylon fell in 539 BC, Cyrus led a Persian empire that included both. Cyrus's family had ruled Anshan, a region of Elam, and the Medes made up a large part of his forces. There is an irony for Isaiah's own day: Elam was then Babylon's ally against Assyria. The friend becomes the attacker. Some scholars connect this oracle with Sennacherib's destruction of Babylon in 689 BC instead, which the Tyndale notes mention as an earlier "fall" before the final one in 539 BC.
  • "All the sighing she has caused I bring to an end" (v2). This is God's purpose. Babylon caused groaning across the nations, including Judah's exiles (Ps 137:1). God ends it.
  • "My loins are filled with anguish… like the pangs of a woman in labor" (v3). Isaiah is not cold. The vision overwhelms him physically: he cannot hear, cannot see, his heart staggers (v4). The Tyndale notes compare Daniel, who was sick for days after a vision (Dan 8:27). Even a judgment that frees God's people is terrible to see.
  • "The twilight I longed for has been turned for me into trembling" (v4). Evening was the time of rest, when the day's heat ended. For Isaiah, the cool twilight brought only horror. It may also anticipate the night Babylon fell.
  • "They prepare the table, they spread the rugs, they eat, they drink. Arise, O princes; oil the shield!" (v5). This scene matches the night of Babylon's fall described in Daniel 5. King Belshazzar held a great feast with a thousand nobles, drinking from the vessels taken from Jerusalem's temple, while the Persian army was outside. "That very night Belshazzar the Chaldean king was killed" (Dan 5:30). The Greek historians Herodotus and Xenophon also say Babylon fell during a festival. Shields were made of leather on a frame and were oiled before battle to keep the leather supple and make blows slide off (2 Sam 1:21). The call to arms comes in the middle of dinner. Henry: they "gird on the harness with as much joy as if they were putting it off."
  • "Go, set a watchman; let him announce what he sees" (v6). God tells Isaiah to post a lookout, perhaps Isaiah himself in the vision. Prophets are often called watchmen (Ezek 3:17; Hab 2:1).
  • "Riders, horsemen in pairs, riders on donkeys, riders on camels" (v7). "Horsemen in pairs" may be chariot teams or riders in formation. Persian armies used donkeys and camels for transport and even in battle. Herodotus says Cyrus used camels to frighten the Lydian cavalry. JFB notes that the Persians used both animals in war. The watchman must listen "very diligently" for the news they bring.
  • "Upon a watchtower I stand, O Lord, continually by day, and at my post I am stationed whole nights" (v8). The Hebrew begins with "a lion," and some versions read "he cried like a lion." The ESV and many scholars follow the Dead Sea Isaiah Scroll, which reads "the one who saw" (ha-ro'eh, a word for a seer) instead of "lion" ('aryeh), since the two look alike in Hebrew. JFB keeps "lion" and takes it of the watchman's tireless watchfulness. Either way, the point is patient, day-and-night watching for God's word.
  • "Fallen, fallen is Babylon; and all the carved images of her gods he has shattered to the ground" (v9). The doubled word signals certainty, as JFB notes: so sure it is spoken as already done. Babylon's gods are named later: "Bel bows down; Nebo stoops" (46:1). Jeremiah repeats the cry (Jer 51:8), and Revelation applies it to the final "Babylon the great," the whole world system opposed to God (Rev 14:8; 18:2). Historically Cyrus did not smash Babylon's idols; he honored Marduk to win the city's priests. The fall of the gods is the reality God sees. The idols could not save their city, and they never rose again as living faiths.
  • "O my threshed and winnowed one" (v10). Literally "my threshing, son of my threshing floor." This is Judah. On a threshing floor, grain was beaten to separate it from the stalk, then thrown into the wind so the chaff blew away. Judah would be threshed by Babylon, but this is God's threshing, and its purpose is to separate the grain. Henry: "The corn of God's floor must expect to be threshed by afflictions and persecutions." Isaiah tells them what he has heard so they can hold on.
  • "The oracle concerning Dumah" (v11). Dumah was an oasis in northern Arabia named after a son of Ishmael (Gen 25:14). But the speaker calls "from Seir," the mountain country of Edom, south of the Dead Sea. The word dumah also means "silence" (Ps 94:17; 115:17). So the name is probably a pun: a word for Edom spoken under a cloud of silence. The Greek Septuagint simply reads "Idumea," Edom.
  • "Watchman, what time of the night? Watchman, what time of the night?" (v11). The question is repeated, anxious. Edom, under Assyrian pressure, is asking how long the darkness of oppression will last. When will morning come? Henry: "The question is serious."
  • "Morning comes, and also the night" (v12). A sober answer. Relief will come, and then more darkness. The Tyndale notes read it this way: the end of Assyrian rule will be followed by Babylon's. Other readers think the morning is for God's people and the night is for Edom, as JFB suggests.
  • "If you will inquire, inquire; come back again" (v12). The watchman leaves the door open. "Come back" can also mean "turn" or "repent" (shuv). Henry turns it into an invitation: "Enquire, return, come. Be inquisitive, be penitent, be willing and obedient." Edom is not shut out from God's word if it is willing to ask.
  • "The oracle concerning Arabia" (v13). Arabia here means the desert peoples east and southeast of Judah who ran the camel caravans carrying incense, spices and gold.
  • "In the thickets in Arabia you will lodge, O caravans of Dedanites" (v13). Dedan was a trading people in northwest Arabia (Ezek 27:20). Normally caravans traveled the main routes between oases. Now, fleeing war, they hide in the scrub. JFB clarifies that the "thickets" are "a region of thick underwood," not a real forest.
  • "To the thirsty bring water; meet the fugitive with bread, O inhabitants of the land of Tema" (v14). Tema (modern Tayma in Saudi Arabia) was a large oasis on the trade road from Babylon. Later, Babylon's last king Nabonidus lived there for about ten years, leaving Belshazzar in charge back home. Desert hospitality required giving water and bread to anyone in need. The refugees are fleeing "the drawn sword… the bent bow… the press of battle" (v15). Assyria and later Babylon both campaigned against the Arab tribes.
  • "Within a year, according to the years of a hired worker, all the glory of Kedar will come to an end" (v16). As in 16:14, the "years of a hired worker" means an exactly counted term. Kedar was the most powerful of the Arab tribes, descended from Ishmael (Gen 25:13), known for its black goat-hair tents (Ps 120:5; Song 1:5) and its flocks (60:7). Its famous archers will be "few" (v17). Ishmael himself had been "an expert with the bow" (Gen 21:20). Assyrian records show Sargon and Sennacherib fighting Arab tribes and queens, but which campaign Isaiah meant is not known.
  • "For the LORD, the God of Israel, has spoken" (v17). The chapter closes on the authority of Israel's God over all these distant peoples. Kedar's glory, like Babylon's, depends on his word.
  • Commentators: Henry calls the chapter "a prophecy of sad times coming, and heavy burdens," and notes that these were nations the people of Israel had "so much to do with," so news from abroad mattered as much as news at home. All three see Belshazzar's feast in v5. Guzik also notes that God often uses one tyrant to punish another. JFB explains "the desert of the sea" from the Euphrates floods and says oiling the shield kept the leather from becoming "hard and liable to crack." On Dumah, JFB and Guzik both find the watchman's answer deliberately veiled. Guzik reads the "night" as successive periods of oppression, while JFB sees a brief dawn for Judah and night for Edom. Henry alone presses the invitation in v12 as a call to repent. Guzik ties v9 to Revelation 18, where the world mourns Babylon's fall and God's people rejoice.

Pointing to Christ

"Watchman, what time of the night?" is the cry of every people living under the dark rule of empires and of sin. The watchman's honest answer, "Morning comes, and also the night," describes history. Each dawn of relief gives way to another night. But Scripture promises a morning with no night after it. Jesus is "the bright morning star" (Rev 22:16), and in the city he brings "there will be no night there" (Rev 21:25; 22:5). Babylon's fall, announced here, is finally told in Revelation, and just after it comes the marriage supper of the Lamb (Rev 18–19). The invitation still stands: "If you will inquire, inquire; come back again."

Reflection

  • The Emotional Weight of Insight (vv.3-4) — Isaiah describes physical anguish, pain like childbirth, and dismay just from seeing what's coming. Have you noticed that real spiritual clarity often comes with emotional cost rather than detached comfort?
  • Babylon Always Falls (v.9) — The announcement that Babylon has fallen represents a pattern repeated throughout history for every power that sets itself against God. What seemingly permanent system or power in your world might actually be far more fragile than it looks?
  • Watchman, What of the Night? (v.11) — This raw question about how much longer the darkness will last captures something deeply human. When you're in a season of waiting, can you hold onto the reality that morning is coming even while the night continues?
  • A Precise Countdown (v.16) — God specifies exactly one year before Arabia's glory fades, showing His judgments run on a real timetable, not vague guesswork. Does it strengthen your trust in God's promises to see how exactly His word plays out on schedule?
