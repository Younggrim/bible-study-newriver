Summary

Jotham becomes king at twenty-five and reigns sixteen years in Jerusalem, doing what is right before the Lord as his father Uzziah had done — but unlike Uzziah, he never enters the temple to burn incense, even though the people continue their corrupt ways (vv. 1-2). He builds the upper gate of the temple, does extensive work on the wall of Ophel, and constructs cities, forts, and towers throughout Judah's hill country and forests (vv. 3-4). He defeats the Ammonites in battle, and they pay him heavy tribute in silver, wheat, and barley for three years (v. 5). Jotham grows powerful because he orders his life before the Lord his God (v. 6), and the chapter closes noting his death and the succession of his son Ahaz (vv. 7-9).

Authorship & Background

Author: Traditionally attributed to Ezra the scribe. Originally one book with 1 Chronicles. Written post-exile (approximately 450-400 BC). 2 Chronicles covers Solomon's reign through the Babylonian exile and Cyrus's decree of return (approximately 970-538 BC). The Chronicler focuses exclusively on Judah (ignoring the northern kingdom) and emphasizes Temple worship, reform movements, and the principle of 2 Chronicles 7:14: "If my people... shall humble themselves, and pray... then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and will heal their land."
Historical Context: Chapter 27 is one of the shortest royal accounts in Chronicles — just nine verses covering Jotham's sixteen-year reign (approximately 750-735 BC). Yet its brevity is itself theologically significant. Unlike his father Uzziah (whose dramatic sin required extensive narration) or his son Ahaz (whose comprehensive wickedness demands a full chapter of documentation), Jotham receives a brief, entirely positive record. He represents the quiet faithfulness that needs no dramatic correction — a king who simply did right and prospered, without the sensational highs or catastrophic lows that fill other royal accounts. The Chronicler's summary verdict is verse 6: "So Jotham became mighty, because he prepared his ways before the LORD his God." This single verse encapsulates the Chronicler's entire theology of prosperity — might comes from prepared, deliberate faithfulness before God. The word "prepared" (Hebrew "hekin" — to establish, fix, direct) suggests not spontaneous emotion but settled, ordered life-direction. Jotham's greatness was the fruit of consistent, intentional obedience.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: One clause carrying an entire comparison to his father, right according to all that Uzziah did, howbeit he entered not into the temple of the LORD, a standing observation slipped in even during praise, the people did yet corruptly, Ammonite tribute specified for three years running as the chapter's one dramatic achievement, a verdict with nothing subtracted from it anywhere in this run of chapters, and nine verses given to the cleanest record in the whole book while collapse and recovery elsewhere claim entire chapters
Jotham's Accession, and the Line He Did Not Cross (vv.1-4): Jotham's summary is written against his father's, and one clause carries the whole comparison, he did that which was right in the sight of the LORD, according to all that his father Uzziah did, howbeit he entered not into the temple of the LORD. He built where his father had built, the high gate of the house of the LORD, a great deal on the wall of Ophel, cities in the mountains of Judah, castles and towers in the forests. The one qualification in the section is not about him, and the Chronicler puts it in the same breath as the praise, the people did yet corruptly, which is his standing observation that a good king does not by himself make a faithful nation.
The Ammonite Tribute, and a Verdict Without Qualification (vv.5-9): He fights the king of the Ammonites and wins, and the tribute is specified for three years running, a hundred talents of silver, ten thousand measures of wheat and ten thousand of barley. Then the verdict, and it is the only one in this whole block of chapters with nothing subtracted from it, so Jotham became mighty, because he prepared his ways before the LORD his God. Sixteen years, buried in the city of David, and Ahaz his son reigns. The brevity is itself worth noticing: this book spends its length on collapse and recovery, so the king with the cleanest record gets nine verses while the king who fails in the next chapter gets twenty-seven.

Map & Geography

  • Parallels 1-2 Kings but focuses exclusively on Judah (the southern kingdom). Jerusalem and the Temple are the theological center throughout.
  • Key locations include various battle sites, reform locations, and high places destroyed or rebuilt by successive kings.
  • The book ends with exile to Babylon (586 BC) and Cyrus's decree permitting return — the geographic arc moves from Jerusalem to Babylon and back.

Deep Dive

Setting: Judah, about 750–735 BC. The Chronicler, writing for the Jews back from exile in Babylon, has just told how King Uzziah's fifty-two prosperous years ended in pride: he forced his way into the Holy Place to burn incense and came out a leper (ch 26). For the last decade of his life he lived in a separate house while his son Jotham ran the government as regent (26:21). Chapter 27 is Jotham's whole reign in nine verses, and it is one of the few reigns in Chronicles that receives no criticism at all. Outside Judah the skies are darkening. Assyria is on the move again under Tiglath-pileser III (from 745 BC), and the northern kingdom of Israel is collapsing into assassinations and coups (2 Kgs 15). Isaiah, Hosea and Micah all began preaching in these years, and all three name Jotham in their opening lines (Isa 1:1; Hos 1:1; Mic 1:1). The parallel account is 2 Kings 15:32–38. The chapter has a single ESV heading.

Jotham Reigns in Judah (vv1–9)

  • "Jotham was twenty-five years old when he began to reign, and he reigned sixteen years" (v1). His name, Yotham, means "the LORD is perfect" or "the LORD is complete." The dates overlap, as they often do in this period. The Tyndale notes count about ten or eleven years as co-regent while Uzziah was a leper (roughly 750–740 BC) and five more alone, with his son Ahaz already sharing the throne before the end. That is why 2 Kings 15:30 can speak of "the twentieth year of Jotham": he lived on for a few years after his official sixteen were complete. The numbers are a puzzle of overlapping reigns, not a contradiction.
  • "His mother's name was Jerushah the daughter of Zadok" (v1). Chronicles names the queen mother for nearly every king of Judah, because she held real influence at court. Zadok was a common priestly name, and JFB suggests she may have come from the high priest's family. If so, Jotham grew up with a priest's daughter for a mother and a leper-king for a father, and he learned exactly where a king's limits lay.
  • "He did what was right in the eyes of the LORD according to all that his father Uzziah had done" (v2). This is the same verdict given to his father, and it covers the good years of Uzziah's reign: seeking God, building, farming, defending the land (26:4–15).
  • "Except he did not enter the temple of the LORD" (v2). This is the Chronicler's own addition, not found in Kings. It does not mean Jotham stayed away from worship. It means he did not do what Uzziah did: barge into the sanctuary to play the priest (26:16). He followed his father's good example and took warning from his father's sin. Few sons manage both.
  • "But the people still followed corrupt practices" (v2). "Still" means the trouble was already there. The Hebrew verb (shachat) means to ruin or act corruptly. Kings is more specific: "the high places were not removed. The people still sacrificed and made offerings on the high places" (2 Kgs 15:35). The high places were local hilltop shrines, sometimes used for the LORD, often mixed with Canaanite practice. Isaiah, preaching in these very years, describes the people beneath the prosperity: "Ah, sinful nation, a people laden with iniquity" (Isa 1:4). Micah says the same about Jerusalem's leaders (Mic 3:9–11). A good king could not by himself change hearts.
  • "He built the upper gate of the house of the LORD" (v3). This is probably the gate on the north side of the temple courts, later called the "upper Benjamin Gate" (Jer 20:2) and the "upper gate, which faces north" (Ezek 9:2). Jotham did not push into the temple. He worked to make it more accessible and secure from the outside. That is the right posture for a king toward God's house: serve it, don't seize it.
  • "And did much building on the wall of Ophel" (v3). Ophel (meaning "swelling" or "mound") was the hill between the old City of David and the Temple Mount, on Jerusalem's southeastern ridge. Its wall protected the vulnerable stretch between palace and temple. Hezekiah and Manasseh would strengthen it again (32:5; 33:14). Jotham continued what his father had begun, rebuilding Jerusalem's defenses after they had been broken down under Amaziah (25:23; 26:9).
  • "He built cities in the hill country of Judah, and forts and towers on the wooded hills" (v4). Where a walled town could not be placed, in the wooded high ground, he put a fort or watchtower. The Tyndale notes explain these as lookouts and road defenses within the kingdom and along its frontier. They also protected farmland and flocks, continuing Uzziah's love of the land (26:10).
  • "He fought with the king of the Ammonites and prevailed against them" (v5). Ammon lay east of the Jordan, around modern Amman, and had paid tribute to Uzziah (26:8). Jotham kept them under Judah's control.
  • "100 talents of silver, and 10,000 cors of wheat and 10,000 of barley" (v5). A talent weighed about 75 pounds (34 kg), so the silver was over three tons. A cor was a large dry measure, perhaps 6 bushels (about 220 litres), so the grain amounted to millions of litres. Selman, cited by Guzik, pictures the barley alone as around ten thousand donkey loads.
  • "The Ammonites paid him the same amount in the second and the third years" (v5). The text names three years of tribute and then stops. The Tyndale notes and JFB both suggest the reason: Judah was soon distracted by war on its northern border. Kings adds that "in those days the LORD began to send Rezin the king of Syria and Pekah the son of Remaliah against Judah" (2 Kgs 15:37). Ammon likely seized the moment to stop paying.
  • "So Jotham became mighty, because he ordered his ways before the LORD his God" (v6). This is the key verse. "Became mighty" (chazaq, grew strong) is the same verb used of Uzziah, whose strength turned to pride (26:8, 15–16). Jotham's strength did not ruin him. "Ordered" is kun, to set firm, establish, prepare. The same verb is used of a throne being made secure (2 Sam 7:16). The picture is a life deliberately set straight and kept steady, and "before the LORD" means it was done with God watching and God in view. For the Chronicler this is the whole lesson of the monarchy: seek the LORD, keep his ways, and he establishes you (2 Chr 7:14; 15:2).
  • What Chronicles leaves out. Kings mentions that Rezin and Pekah began attacking in Jotham's days (2 Kgs 15:37). Chronicles saves that story for Ahaz, whose faithlessness turns the threat into disaster (28:5–8). The Chronicler is not hiding anything. He is placing the defeat where its cause lies.
  • "Now the rest of the acts of Jotham, and all his wars and his ways" (v7). "His wars" hints at campaigns we are not told about, perhaps against the Philistines or Edom, or against the northern alliance. "His ways" echoes v6: the record of his conduct before God. The "Book of the Kings of Israel and Judah" was a court history the Chronicler used that has not survived. It is not our books of Kings.
  • "He was twenty-five years old when he began to reign, and he reigned sixteen years" (v8). Verse 8 repeats v1 word for word. Some scholars think a copyist repeated the line by accident. Others see it as a deliberate frame, bracketing the whole reign between the same two facts. Either way, nothing is lost.
  • "Jotham slept with his fathers, and they buried him in the city of David" (v9). "Slept with his fathers" is the normal phrase for a peaceful death. He was buried in the royal tombs, unlike his leper father, who was buried in a field beside them (26:23). A clean record and an honoured grave.
  • "And Ahaz his son reigned in his place" (v9). The chapter ends on an ominous name. Ahaz will be one of the worst kings Judah ever had: Baal images, child sacrifice, the temple doors shut (ch 28). Four short verses earlier the Chronicler said Jotham ordered his ways before the LORD, and yet his son did not. A godly father is a gift, but not a guarantee.
  • Commentators: Henry makes the most of the contrast with Uzziah: Jotham copied what was good in his father and steered clear of his presumption, and the lesson is to imitate the good in others while taking warning from their faults. On the corrupt people, Henry lays the blame on them rather than on their king, who gave them every advantage. JFB reads v2 the same way: "the widespread and inveterate wickedness of the people frustrated all his laudable efforts." JFB thinks the Ammonites rebelled once Rezin and Pekah invaded, and that Jotham was too busy to bring them back under control. Guzik draws on Spurgeon, who said Uzziah's fall was for Jotham a beacon warning him off the rocks. He also cites Knapp, who links the upper gate to keeping a way open between palace and temple. On v6 he quotes Spurgeon's view that so steady a life implies a man of prayer, and he notes with relief that Jotham, unlike the three kings before him, did not start well and end badly. Guzik passes on Knapp's gloss of the name as "Jehovah-perfect." The lexicon gives "the LORD is perfect," so the point stands, though it describes God, not Jotham.

Pointing to Christ

Jotham is a rare thing in Chronicles: a king with nothing negative recorded against him. Yet even his steady faithfulness could not cleanse the people's hearts or guarantee a godly son. The best of David's sons could set their own ways in order, but they could not set anyone else's. The prophets who began preaching in Jotham's day looked past every human king to one who would do what Jotham could not: a son of David on whose shoulders the government would rest, of whose peace there would be no end (Isa 9:6–7), born in little Bethlehem to shepherd his people "in the strength of the LORD" (Mic 5:2–4). Jesus is that King. He not only ordered his own ways perfectly before his Father (John 8:29), he also gives his people new hearts (Ezek 36:26) and makes them his true children (John 1:12–13).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik notes that Jotham's brief chapter demonstrates that faithfulness does not always produce dramatic narrative. The quiet, steady king receives less text than the dramatic sinner — yet his life was arguably more successful before God. Guzik emphasizes "he prepared his ways" as the key to Jotham's power — deliberate, ordered, consistent faithfulness rather than sporadic spiritual intensity.

Reflection

  • Learning From Another's Fall (v.2) — Jotham refuses to enter the temple to burn incense, having seen his father Uzziah struck with leprosy for that very presumption. What mistake have you watched someone else make that you're determined not to repeat?
  • Serving According to Your Role (v.3) — Jotham can't perform priestly duties, but he builds and fortifies what supports worship, contributing in the way that's actually his to give. Are you trying to serve God in a role that isn't yours, instead of fully using the one that is?
  • Quiet, Prepared Faithfulness (v.6) — Jotham becomes mighty not through some dramatic event but because he deliberately ordered his life before the Lord. Is your walk with God marked by intentional, steady preparation, or by drifting from one moment to the next?
