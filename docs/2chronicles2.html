1Now Solomon purposed to build a house for the Name of the LORD and a royal palace for himself.

2So he conscripted 70,000 porters, 80,000 stonecutters in the mountains, and 3,600 supervisors.

3Then Solomon sent word to Hiram king of Tyre: “Do for me as you did for my father David when you sent him cedars to build himself a house to live in.

4Behold, I am about to build a house for the Name of the LORD my God to dedicate to Him for burning fragrant incense before Him, for displaying the showbread continuously, and for making burnt offerings every morning and evening as well as on the Sabbaths, New Moons, and appointed feasts of the LORD our God. This is ordained for Israel forever.

5The house that I am building will be great, for our God is greater than all gods.

6But who is able to build a house for Him, since the heavens, even the highest heavens, cannot contain Him? Who then am I, that I should build a house for Him, except as a place to burn sacrifices before Him?

7Send me, therefore, a craftsman skilled in engraving to work with gold and silver, with bronze and iron, and with purple, crimson, and blue yarn. He will work with my craftsmen in Judah and Jerusalem, whom my father David provided.

8Send me also cedar, cypress, and algum logs from Lebanon, for I know that your servants have skill to cut timber there. And indeed, my servants will work with yours

9to prepare for me timber in abundance, because the temple I am building will be great and wonderful.

10I will pay your servants, the woodcutters, 20,000 cors of ground wheat, 20,000 cors of barley, 20,000 baths of wine, and 20,000 baths of olive oil.”

11Then Hiram king of Tyre wrote a letter in reply to Solomon: “Because the LORD loves His people, He has set you over them as king.”

12And Hiram added: “Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, who made the heavens and the earth! He has given King David a wise son with insight and understanding, who will build a temple for the LORD and a royal palace for himself.

13So now I am sending you Huram-abi, a skillful man endowed with creativity.

14He is the son of a woman from the daughters of Dan, and his father is a man of Tyre. He is skilled in work with gold and silver, bronze and iron, stone and wood, purple, blue, and crimson yarn, and fine linen. He is experienced in every kind of engraving and can execute any design that is given him. He will work with your craftsmen and with those of my lord, your father David.

15Now let my lord send to his servants the wheat, barley, olive oil, and wine he promised.

16We will cut logs from Lebanon, as many as you need, and we will float them to you as rafts by sea down to Joppa. Then you can take them up to Jerusalem.”

17Solomon numbered all the foreign men in the land of Israel following the census his father David had conducted, and there were found to be 153,600 in all.

18Solomon made 70,000 of them porters, 80,000 stonecutters in the mountains, and 3,600 supervisors.

Summary

Solomon resolves to build a temple for the LORD's name and a palace for his own kingdom, conscripting a large workforce of burden-bearers, stonecutters, and overseers (vv. 1-2). He writes to Huram, king of Tyre, asking for the same help Huram once gave David, explaining that the temple must be great because Israel's God is greater than all gods, though no house can truly contain him (vv. 3-6). He requests a craftsman skilled in gold, silver, bronze, and dyed fabrics, plus cedar and other timber from Lebanon, offering generous payment in wheat, barley, wine, and oil (vv. 7-10). Huram replies with praise for Israel's God and sends the skilled craftsman Huram-abi, along with a plan to raft timber to Joppa (vv. 11-16). Solomon then numbers the foreign residents in the land, 153,600 in all, and assigns them as laborers, stonecutters, and overseers (vv. 17-18).

Authorship & Background

Author: Traditionally attributed to Ezra the scribe. Originally one book with 1 Chronicles. Written post-exile (approximately 450-400 BC). 2 Chronicles covers Solomon's reign through the Babylonian exile and Cyrus's decree of return (approximately 970-538 BC). The Chronicler focuses exclusively on Judah (ignoring the northern kingdom) and emphasizes Temple worship, reform movements, and the principle of 2 Chronicles 7:14: "If my people... shall humble themselves, and pray... then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and will heal their land."
Historical Context: Chapter 2 records Solomon's preparations for Temple construction, focusing on his alliance with Huram (Hiram) of Tyre. This international partnership — a pagan king supplying materials and skilled labor for God's house — raises theological questions the Chronicler addresses through Solomon's own words. Solomon's letter to Huram contains one of Scripture's most profound theological statements about God's transcendence: "who is able to build him an house, seeing the heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain him?" (v.6). Solomon understands the paradox: the infinite God cannot be contained by any structure, yet He condescends to dwell among His people. The Temple is not God's home but humanity's meeting place with the divine.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: Two houses determined at once, one for the LORD and one for the king's own kingdom, with the labor counted before a single stone is cut, a letter to Huram built around a disclaimer, who is able to build him an house, seeing the heaven of heavens cannot contain him, a Gentile king's reply opening with his own confession of Israel's God as creator, a craftsman's tribal ancestry given differently here than in Kings without either book explaining the discrepancy, and a census of resident foreigners numbering over a hundred fifty thousand put to the work
The Levy and the Letter to Huram (vv.1-10): Solomon determines to build two houses, one for the name of the LORD and one for his own kingdom, and the chapter is the procurement that follows. The labor is counted first, seventy thousand to bear burdens, eighty thousand to hew in the mountain, three thousand six hundred to oversee them. Then the letter to Huram of Tyre, which trades on an existing relationship, as thou didst deal with David my father, and even as I send. Its theological center is a disclaimer built into the request: the house must be great because our God is great above all gods, and yet who is able to build him an house, seeing the heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain him. Solomon asks for cedar, fir and algum, for a craftsman skilled in metal and dyed cloth, and offers payment in wheat, barley, wine and oil.
Huram's Answer and the Foreign Workforce (vv.11-18): Huram replies in writing, and his first sentence is a Gentile king confessing Israel's God as creator, blessed be the LORD God of Israel, that made heaven and earth. He sends the craftsman asked for, a man whose mother was of the daughters of Dan and whose father was a man of Tyre, able to work in gold, silver, brass, iron, stone, timber, purple, blue, fine linen and crimson. 1 Kings 7:14 gives his mother's tribe as Naphtali, one of the small differences between the two accounts that neither book explains. The chapter ends with a census of the strangers in the land, a hundred and fifty-three thousand six hundred, set to the carrying and the hewing. The Chronicler states plainly that the temple was built by a conscripted foreign workforce.

Map & Geography

  • Parallels 1-2 Kings but focuses exclusively on Judah (the southern kingdom). Jerusalem and the Temple are the theological center throughout.
  • Key locations include various battle sites, reform locations, and high places destroyed or rebuilt by successive kings.

Setting: The early years of Solomon's reign, before the temple work began in his fourth year, about 966 BC (3:2). Chapter 1 showed Solomon seeking God at Gibeon and receiving wisdom and wealth. Now the Chronicler moves straight to the work Solomon was born for: the temple David had planned and stocked (1 Chr 22; 28–29). This chapter covers Solomon's labor force and his treaty with Hiram, king of Tyre, the great Phoenician port city on the coast north of Israel, for timber and a master craftsman. First Kings 5 tells the same story with different emphases. Chronicles adds Solomon's statement of what the temple is for and a fuller description of the craftsman. Writing for the returned exiles around 400 BC, who were themselves rebuilding the temple with Phoenician cedar shipped to Joppa (Ezra 3:7), the Chronicler shows how the first temple was begun. (In Hebrew Bibles the chapter numbering is one verse off: English 2:1 is Hebrew 1:18, so English 2:2–18 is Hebrew 2:1–17.)

Preparing to Build the Temple (vv1–18)

  • "Solomon purposed to build a temple for the name of the LORD, and a royal palace for himself" (v1). The order matters. The temple comes first, and the palace is mentioned only in passing (compare v12). In Kings, the palace gets its own section and took nearly twice as long (1 Kgs 7:1). Chronicles never describes it. "For the name of the LORD" is the phrase from Deuteronomy for the place where God chooses "to make his name dwell" (Deut 12:11). It means the temple is where God makes himself known and is called upon, not a house that contains him.
  • "70,000 men to bear burdens and 80,000 to quarry in the hill country, and 3,600 to oversee them" (v2). These numbers are repeated in v18, framing the chapter. They are explained there: the workers were resident aliens. Quarrying in "the hill country" means cutting the fine limestone of the Judean hills, the same white stone used in Jerusalem today.
  • "As you dealt with David my father… so deal with me" (v3). Hiram had sent David cedar and craftsmen for his palace (2 Sam 5:11; 1 Chr 14:1). Solomon appeals to that friendship. Tyre had what Israel lacked: access to the cedar forests of Lebanon and centuries of skill in building and metalwork. Israel had farmland and grain. The two kingdoms needed each other.
  • What the temple is for (v4). Kings records no such statement. In Chronicles Solomon spells out the temple's purpose: "the burning of incense of sweet spices… the regular arrangement of the showbread… burnt offerings morning and evening, on the Sabbaths and the new moons and the appointed feasts." This is a summary of the daily, weekly, monthly and yearly worship laid down in the law (Exod 30:7–8; Lev 24:5–9; Num 28–29). The temple is a house of worship, not a monument. For the returned exiles, restoring exactly this pattern of worship was the point of rebuilding (Ezra 3:1–6; Neh 10:32–33).
  • "The house that I am to build will be great, for our God is greater than all gods" (v5). Solomon tells a pagan king plainly that Israel's God is supreme. The building's greatness is meant to reflect the greatness of the God it honors. Psalm 135:5 uses the same words: "I know that the LORD is great, and that our Lord is above all gods."
  • "But who is able to build him a house, since heaven, even highest heaven, cannot contain him?" (v6). Solomon will say the same thing at the dedication (6:18; 1 Kgs 8:27). Here he says it before a stone is laid. "Who am I to build a house for him, except as a place to make offerings before him?" He echoes his father's humility: "Who am I, and what is my people, that we should be able thus to offer willingly?" (1 Chr 29:14). The temple is not a home for God. It is a place for people to come before him. Isaiah 66:1 and Stephen in Acts 7:48–50 press the same point.
  • "Send me a man skilled to work in gold, silver, bronze, and iron, and in purple, crimson, and blue fabrics" (v7). Purple dye, made from murex sea snails, was Tyre's most famous product, so costly that "purple" came to mean royalty. Blue and crimson fabrics were used for the temple's veil (3:14), as in the tabernacle (Exod 26:31). Solomon wants a craftsman who can oversee every kind of work, like Bezalel in the wilderness (Exod 31:1–5; 35:30–35). "Whom David my father provided" recalls the craftsmen David gathered (1 Chr 22:15–16).
  • "Cedar, cypress, and algum timber from Lebanon" (v8). Cedar of Lebanon was the prestige timber of the ancient world. Egyptian, Assyrian and Babylonian kings all boast of cutting it for their temples and palaces. Algum (spelled "almug" in Kings) is unidentified. In 1 Kings 10:11 it comes from Ophir, so its appearance here among Lebanon's woods may mean Hiram was to supply it through his trade, or that the list is general. "I know that your servants know how to cut timber": Solomon honestly admits the Phoenicians' superior skill (1 Kgs 5:6).
  • "Great and wonderful" (v9). "Wonderful" (hapleʾ) is related to the word for God's marvelous deeds. Solomon wants a building that makes people marvel.
  • "20,000 cors of crushed wheat, 20,000 cors of barley, 20,000 baths of wine, and 20,000 baths of oil" (v10). A cor was about 6 bushels (220 liters), and a bath about 6 gallons (22 liters). This was food for the woodsmen in Lebanon. First Kings 5:11 lists a yearly payment of 20,000 cors of wheat and 20 cors of fine oil for Hiram's household. The two passages probably describe two different arrangements: provisions for the work crews here, and payment for the royal court in Kings.
  • "Because the LORD loves his people, he has made you king over them" (v11). A foreign king speaks Israel's theology back to Solomon. The queen of Sheba will say almost the same thing (9:8). Hiram replies "in a letter," which reminds readers that kings of this era corresponded in writing. Many such diplomatic letters from the centuries before survive, such as the Amarna letters from Canaanite kings to Pharaoh.
  • "Blessed be the LORD God of Israel, who made heaven and earth" (v12). Did Hiram worship the LORD? Probably not exclusively. Ancient diplomacy often honored a partner's god, and Tyre's chief god was Melqart. But his words are true, and the Chronicler is glad to record a Gentile king confessing the Creator. "A wise son, who has discretion and understanding" echoes David's prayer for Solomon (1 Chr 22:12).
  • "Huram-abi" (v13). The name means "Huram is my father" or "Huram, my father." Many think ʾabi here is a title of respect, like "master" or "counselor," as Joseph was "a father to Pharaoh" (Gen 45:8). In 2 Chronicles 4:16 he is called "Huram-abi" again. In 1 Kings 7:13 he is simply "Hiram," the same name as the king. "Huram" is the Chronicler's usual spelling of Hiram.
  • "The son of a woman of the daughters of Dan" (v14). First Kings 7:14 says "the son of a widow of the tribe of Naphtali." She may have been from the town of Dan, in Naphtali's territory, or Danite by descent and married into Naphtali, or her two husbands may have come from different tribes. The Chronicler may also mean readers to notice a pattern: Oholiab, Bezalel's partner in making the tabernacle, was from the tribe of Dan (Exod 31:6). Solomon has his Bezalel and Oholiab too.
  • "Trained to work in gold, silver, bronze, iron, stone, and wood… and fine linen" (v14). Kings describes Hiram only as a bronze worker (1 Kgs 7:14). Chronicles gives him a much wider range, matching Solomon's request in v7 and the range of Bezalel and Oholiab (Exod 35:35).
  • "Let him send to his servants" (v15). Hiram is happy to take the deal. Calling Solomon "my lord" is diplomatic courtesy, not a sign of submission.
  • "In rafts by sea to Joppa" (v16). The logs were dragged to the coast, tied into rafts and floated down the coast about 100 miles to Joppa (modern Jaffa, beside Tel Aviv), then hauled about 35 miles uphill to Jerusalem. Kings says Hiram would deliver them to a place Solomon named (1 Kgs 5:9). Joppa was the port where Jonah later boarded a ship to flee (Jonah 1:3), and the returned exiles brought cedar there for the second temple "according to the grant that they had from Cyrus" (Ezra 3:7).
  • "Solomon counted all the resident aliens who were in the land" (v17). "Resident aliens" (gerim) were non-Israelites living among Israel. David had already counted them and set them to cutting stone (1 Chr 22:2). First Kings 9:20–22 identifies them as descendants of the Canaanite peoples and stresses that no Israelites were made slaves, though 1 Kings 5:13–14 does describe a rotating draft of 30,000 Israelites for work in Lebanon. Chronicles mentions only the foreigners.
  • "153,600… Seventy thousand… 80,000… and 3,600 as overseers" (vv17–18). The total is exactly the sum of the three groups. First Kings 5:16 gives 3,300 overseers. Counting the 550 chief officers of 1 Kings 9:23 and the 250 of 2 Chronicles 8:10 gives 3,850 in both books, so the two accounts probably group the same supervisors differently.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Great is our God above all gods, above all idols, above all princes," and Solomon "did not feed his workmen with bread and water, but with plenty." Henry also notes Solomon wanted "the best workmen that can be got," and applies it to the honor God's work deserves. JFB says there is "no discrepancy" between v10 and 1 Kings 5:11: one payment fed the workmen and the other supplied Hiram's court. On Hiram's praise of the LORD, JFB says it "creates a presumption" he knew the true religion, but that the presumption "may be delusive." Guzik observes that Solomon used Gentile skill and materials where they were better, and quotes the scholar Martin Selman's point that the temple's later role as a house of prayer for all nations was no accident, since the nations helped build it. Guzik also mentions an old Jewish legend that no worker died or fell sick in all the years of building, which is legend, not text.

Pointing to Christ

Solomon's best words in this chapter are his question, "Who is able to build him a house?" No one is. Yet God chose to build his own house among his people, first in the temple, then in the flesh of his Son, "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), and now in a people made of Jews and Gentiles together: "you also, like living stones, are being built up as a spiritual house" (1 Pet 2:5; Eph 2:19–22). Solomon's temple was built with the help of foreign kings and foreign workers. The house Christ builds is made from them, welcomed in as members of God's household.

He also notes the significance of Huram's blessing of Israel's God as evidence of God's reputation extending among the nations.

Reflection

  • God Uses Unlikely Partners (vv.3-16) — Solomon draws on a pagan king's resources and skilled labor to build God's house. Where might God want to use an unexpected partnership or unlikely person to accomplish His purposes through you?
  • Building Greatly Because God Is Great (v.5) — Solomon's stated reason for building an extraordinary Temple was the greatness of the God it was for. Does the effort you put into worship and service reflect how great you actually believe God to be?
  • Ambition Held Together With Humility (v.6) — Solomon acknowledges that no house, however magnificent, can contain the God of heaven and heaven's heaven. How do you hold together giving your best effort to God while remembering it could never be enough on its own?
  • Leadership Exists for Others (v.12) — Huram recognizes that God gave Solomon wisdom because God loved His people, not because Solomon deserved special favor. Whatever authority or responsibility you carry, do you see it as existing for the benefit of those under your care?
