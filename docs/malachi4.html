Summary

Malachi closes his prophecy with a warning that the coming day will burn like a furnace, consuming the arrogant and evildoers completely, leaving them neither root nor branch (v. 1). For those who fear God's name, however, the sun of righteousness will rise with healing, and they will go out rejoicing like calves released from the stall, trampling down the wicked (vv. 2-3). The people are told to remember the law given to Moses at Horeb (v. 4), and the book ends with God's promise to send the prophet Elijah before the great and dreadful Day of the Lord, so that he may turn the hearts of fathers and children toward each other and prevent the land from being struck with judgment (vv. 5-6).

Authorship & Background

Author: Malachi ("my messenger"). The last OT prophet, approximately 450-430 BC. Key theme: post-exilic spiritual lethargy — the people go through religious motions without heart. Structured as disputations (God accuses, people challenge, God responds). Famous: "I have loved you" (1:2), "Will a man rob God?" (3:8-10), "the Sun of righteousness arise with healing in his wings" (4:2), and Elijah's return (4:5-6). The OT ends with a promise and a warning.
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — The Day of the LORD: Sun of Righteousness, Elijah's Return, and the Last Word of the OT
Key Themes: The coming day burning like an oven, judgment on the arrogant, the Sun of righteousness with healing, the faithful leaping like calves, remembering the law of Moses, Elijah's return, the turning of hearts, the OT's final word
Historical Context: Malachi 4 is the final chapter of the Old Testament. After these six verses, the prophetic voice falls silent for approximately 400 years — until John the Baptist breaks the silence in the wilderness. This chapter carries the weight of finality: God's last word before the long silence.
The Day Burning Like an Oven (v.1): The final chapter is six verses and opens on a furnace. The proud and the wicked are stubble, and what is left is neither root nor branch — an agricultural way of saying nothing remains to grow back. The same day that closes the chapter with healing begins it with burning, and the difference between the two outcomes is not the day but who is standing in it.
The Sun of Righteousness, with Healing (vv.2-3): For those who fear His name the same dawn brings the Sun of righteousness rising with healing in his wings, and the image that follows is oddly domestic: they go out and grow up like calves let out of a stall. It is a picture of release rather than triumph. Verse 3 does describe the wicked trodden as ashes underfoot, so the chapter holds both without softening either.
Remember the Law of Moses (v.4): The instruction looks backwards: remember the law of Moses my servant, commanded in Horeb. Nothing new is added. At the close of the Old Testament the community is pointed to what it already has, which fits a book whose complaint throughout has been neglect rather than ignorance.
Elijah Is Coming, and the Last Word (vv.5-6): Then the book looks forward: Elijah will be sent before the great and dreadful day, to turn the hearts of fathers to children and children to fathers. So the Old Testament ends suspended between memory and expectation, law and promise. Its final word is “curse”, and it functions as a warning of what remains without that turning of hearts rather than as a threat — the closing note is of something unfinished. Jesus identifies John the Baptist with this Elijah, some four centuries later.

Deep Dive

Setting: Jerusalem in the fifth century BC, in the days of Ezra and Nehemiah, about a hundred years after the first exiles came home from Babylon. The temple stands again, but the people are tired and cynical. Malachi has answered six of their complaints, and chapter 3 ended with a small group who feared the LORD, a book of remembrance written for them, and a promise that one day everyone would see "the distinction between the righteous and the wicked" (3:18). Chapter 4 shows that day. In Hebrew Bibles these six verses are not a separate chapter at all but the end of chapter 3 (3:19–24), so the thought runs straight on. These are also the last words of the Old Testament in the order English Bibles use. After them, no prophet speaks in Israel for about four hundred years, until a man in camel's hair appears by the Jordan.

The Great Day of the Lord (vv1–6)

  • "For behold, the day is coming, burning like an oven" (v1). "The day" is the day of the LORD, the prophets' phrase for the time when God steps in to judge evil and set things right (Joel 2:31; Zeph 1:14–18). The "oven" is a clay baking oven, fired until it glowed. The picture answers the people's sneer in 3:15 that "evildoers not only prosper but they put God to the test and they escape." They will not escape.
  • "All the arrogant and all evildoers will be stubble" (v1). "Arrogant" picks up the very word the cynics used in 3:15 ("we call the arrogant blessed"). Stubble is the dry stalk left in the field after harvest. It has no value, and it burns in seconds. The same fire that refines God's people like silver (3:2–3) consumes those who refuse him.
  • "It will leave them neither root nor branch" (v1). A tree with roots left can sprout again (Job 14:7–9). This is total: nothing above ground and nothing below. Elsewhere the prophets use root and branch for a family line and its future (Isa 11:1; Amos 2:9).
  • "But for you who fear my name" (v2). The turn is sharp. These are the people of 3:16 who "feared the LORD and esteemed his name." To fear God's name is to take him seriously: to honor who he has shown himself to be. The priests of chapter 1 had despised that name (1:6).
  • "The sun of righteousness shall rise with healing in its wings" (v2). For the wicked the day is an oven. For the God-fearing it is a sunrise. The same heat that burns stubble warms and heals the living. "Righteousness" here is more than a quality of the sun. It is what the sun brings: God putting things right, vindicating his people and ending the wrong they have suffered. The Tyndale notes point out that the winged sun disk was a common image in the art of Egypt, Assyria and Persia, a picture of divine protection. Malachi takes a familiar symbol and gives it to the LORD. Wings in Scripture often mean shelter: "hide me in the shadow of your wings" (Ps 17:8; Exod 19:4).
  • Is the sun a person? In the Hebrew both "rise" and "its wings" are feminine, agreeing with "sun" (shemesh), so the grammar describes the sun itself, not a man. Even so, Christians from Justin Martyr onward read the Sun of Righteousness as Christ. Zechariah, John the Baptist's father, seems to echo the verse when he praises God for "the sunrise from on high" that will "visit us… to give light to those who sit in darkness" (Luke 1:78–79). Some scholars think Malachi meant the dawning of a new age of righteousness rather than a personal figure. The two readings meet in Jesus, who said "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12).
  • "Healing in its wings." The word for "wings" is kanaph, which also means the corner or edge of a garment. It is the word for the corners of the robe where Israelites wore tassels (Num 15:38). Some readers connect this with the woman who touched "the fringe of his garment" and was healed (Matt 9:20). That is a lovely link for devotion, but it is not what Malachi's sun picture means. Here the wings are the sun's rays spreading out at dawn. "Healing" in the prophets is often more than the body: it is forgiveness and restoration of a wounded people (Jer 33:6–8; Isa 53:5).
  • "You shall go out leaping like calves from the stall" (v2). The verb is push, which the lexicon defines as "spring about" or "gambol," used of frisking calves (Jer 50:11 uses it the same way). A calf shut in a stall through winter, let out on the first warm morning, kicks and jumps for the sheer joy of it. The KJV's "grow up" misses this. The God-fearing remnant, who had been mocked and pressed down, will be set loose.
  • "You shall tread down the wicked, for they will be ashes under the soles of your feet" (v3). The fire of v1 has done its work. All that is left of the arrogant is ash. "On the day when I act" repeats 3:17. God is the one who acts. His people do not take vengeance into their own hands. They walk out into a world where he has already dealt with evil (Rom 12:19; Rev 19:11–16).
  • "Remember the law of my servant Moses" (v4). The last three verses step back from the vision and give two instructions for the waiting time. The first looks back. "Remember" in Hebrew is not only recalling a fact but acting on it. Horeb is another name for Mount Sinai, where God gave the covenant (Deut 4:10; 5:2). Malachi's whole book has been about people who drifted from that covenant: in sacrifices, marriage, tithes and speech. The answer is not a new law but the old one, taken to heart.
  • "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet" (v5). The second instruction looks forward. In 3:1 God promised "my messenger" to prepare his way. Now the messenger has a name. Elijah fits: he stood alone against a nation sliding into idolatry, called them to choose between the LORD and Baal (1 Kings 18:21), and was taken to heaven without dying (2 Kings 2:11). The Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint) reads "Elijah the Tishbite," making plain that the famous prophet is meant.
  • "Before the great and awesome day of the LORD comes" (v5). The wording follows Joel 2:31 almost exactly. Before God's day of judgment, he sends a final call to repent. Mercy comes first.
  • Was John the Baptist Elijah? The angel told Zechariah that John would go "in the spirit and power of Elijah, to turn the hearts of the fathers to the children" (Luke 1:17), quoting this verse. Jesus said plainly, "if you are willing to accept it, he is Elijah who is to come" (Matt 11:14), and after the Transfiguration, "Elijah has already come" (Matt 17:12–13). Yet John himself, asked "Are you Elijah?", said "I am not" (John 1:21). The best way to hold these together: John was not Elijah come back in person, which is what his questioners meant, but he was the Elijah Malachi promised, in his role and spirit. He even dressed like Elijah (2 Kings 1:8; Matt 3:4). Many Christians also expect a further fulfilment before Christ's return, sometimes linked with the two witnesses of Revelation 11, who act much like Elijah and Moses (Rev 11:6). Jewish tradition still waits for Elijah: a cup is poured for him at the Passover meal and the door is opened for him.
  • "He will turn the hearts of fathers to their children and the hearts of children to their fathers" (v6). "Turn" (shuv) is the Old Testament's main word for repentance, used four times in 3:7 ("Return to me, and I will return to you"). Two readings stand side by side, and both are true to the book. First, family healing: Malachi has rebuked men who broke faith with the wives of their youth, and God seeks "godly offspring" (2:14–16). Second, turning the present generation back to the faith of their fathers Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. Luke 1:17 adds "and the disobedient to the wisdom of the just," which suggests both: real reconciliation in homes, rooted in a shared return to God.
  • "Lest I come and strike the land with a decree of utter destruction" (v6). The Hebrew is one word, cherem, the "ban" or "devoted thing." The lexicon gives its strongest use as something hostile to God that must be destroyed completely, as Jericho was (Josh 6:17). It is a shocking last word. Israel, if it refuses to turn, would be treated like the Canaanites it replaced. The warning is conditional: "lest." God sends Elijah precisely so that the ban need not fall.
  • The Old Testament ends with a curse. The English Old Testament's final word is a threat of destruction. Jewish readers felt this so keenly that, when Malachi is read in the synagogue, v5 is read again after v6 so the reading ends with the promise of Elijah. (Isaiah, Lamentations and Ecclesiastes are treated the same way.) The Greek Septuagint moved v4 to the end. The Hebrew canon itself ends differently, with Chronicles and Cyrus's call to go up and build (2 Chron 36:23). In the Christian order, the curse is the last word until the New Testament opens with a genealogy and a birth. The New Testament ends: "The grace of the Lord Jesus be with all" (Rev 22:21), and before that, "no longer will there be anything accursed" (Rev 22:3).
  • Moses and Elijah together. The chapter closes by setting the Law (Moses) and the Prophets (Elijah) side by side. The Tyndale notes suggest these two verses may have served as closing notes for the whole scroll of the Twelve Minor Prophets, tying them to the Law and to the prophets before them. Both men met God at Horeb (Exod 3; 1 Kings 19:8–18). Both appear again on the mountain with Jesus, talking with him about the departure he would accomplish at Jerusalem (Luke 9:30–31).
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry outlines the chapter as "the misery of the wicked and the happiness of the righteous," and reads v1 first as "a prophecy of the destruction of Jerusalem, and the unbelieving Jews with it," fulfilled in AD 70, and finally in the last judgment. JFB agrees and calls this "successive fulfilment," not a double meaning. On v2 JFB says simply, "The beams of the Sun are His 'wings,'" and translates "grow up" as "leap as frisking calves," which the lexicon supports. Guzik reads the Sun as Christ and notes the progression in vv2–3: freedom, flourishing and sharing in the Lord's victory. He compares trying to add our own righteousness to Christ's work with lighting a candle at noon. On Elijah all three hold the same line from different angles. Henry says the people must "keep to the law of Moses" and "expect a further discovery of God's will by Elijah the prophet, that is, by John Baptist, the harbinger of the Messiah." JFB says Elijah comes in "his official, not his personal capacity," and that John "did not exhaustively fulfil" the prophecy. Guzik also expects a fuller fulfilment before the Second Coming. JFB names the last word as cherem, the term used for "the extermination of the Canaanites," and Guzik notes both the synagogue habit of repeating v5 and the Septuagint's reordering.

Pointing to Christ

The Old Testament closes with fire and a sunrise, a law to remember and a prophet to wait for. Four centuries later the prophet came, preaching repentance by the Jordan, and pointed to one greater than himself: "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29). The curse Malachi threatened did not simply disappear. Christ took it: he "redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Gal 3:13). So for all who fear his name, the day that burns like an oven has become a dawn, and the Sun of Righteousness has risen with healing.

Reflection

  • The Day That Burns (v.1) — For the arrogant and wicked, the coming day is pictured as an inescapable fire leaving no root or branch. Are you living now in a way that prepares you for that day, or ignoring it?
  • Healing in His Wings (v.2) — For those who fear God's name, that same day brings healing and joyful freedom, like calves let loose from the stall. Do you experience your faith as joyful freedom, or as grim duty?
  • Remembering the Law of Moses (v.4) — Before pointing forward to what's next, God tells His people to remember what He has already revealed. What foundational truths of Scripture do you need to return to rather than chase something new?
  • The Last Word: Turning Hearts (vv.5-6) — The Old Testament closes with the promise of Elijah, whose mission is to turn the hearts of parents and children toward each other before judgment falls. Where do you need to pursue reconciliation with family before it's too late?
