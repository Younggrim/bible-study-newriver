What the Bible Says About Unforgiveness & Bitterness

"Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you."

— Ephesians 4:32 (ESV)

Understanding Unforgiveness and Bitterness

Bitterness begins as a natural response to real injury — someone has genuinely wronged us, and the hurt is legitimate. But left unaddressed, that legitimate pain can calcify into something else entirely: a root that Hebrews warns "springs up and causes trouble, and by it many become defiled" (Hebrews 12:15). The image of a root is deliberate — bitterness does not stay contained to the original wound; it spreads underground, affecting other relationships, other decisions, other areas of the heart that had nothing to do with the original offense.

Unforgiveness is often described as drinking poison and expecting the other person to die. The offender frequently remains unaffected, sometimes entirely unaware of the ongoing resentment, while the person withholding forgiveness carries the weight — replaying the offense, rehearsing the injustice, allowing one wrong to continue causing damage indefinitely. Bitterness does not punish the offender; it imprisons the one who holds it, keeping them tethered to a past moment that continues to control the present.

Jesus' teaching connects our willingness to forgive with our own experience of being forgiven in a way that is startling in its directness: "if you do not forgive others their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses" (Matthew 6:15). This is not a transactional bargain but a description of spiritual reality — a heart that has truly grasped the magnitude of its own forgiveness by God finds it increasingly difficult to withhold a much smaller forgiveness from others. Unforgiveness reveals a heart that has not yet fully absorbed the scale of grace it has received.

What Scripture Teaches

Forgiveness, in Scripture's vision, is not primarily an emotion that arrives naturally over time but a decision made in advance of feeling ready — a choice to release someone from the debt they owe rather than continuing to collect on it through resentment, distance, or rehearsed grievance. This is why forgiveness can be commanded ("forgive... as the Lord has forgiven you," Colossians 3:13) in a way emotions cannot be commanded; it is an act of the will that emotions eventually follow, not a feeling that must arrive before the choice can be made.

Crucially, forgiveness does not require pretending the offense did not happen, minimizing its severity, or automatically restoring trust as though nothing occurred. Reconciliation and restored trust are separate processes that may take significant time, wisdom, and, in some cases, evidence of genuine change — and in some situations, full relational restoration may never be appropriate or safe. Forgiveness releases the debt before God; it does not obligate the forgiver to reenter a relationship without wisdom or appropriate boundaries.

Jesus' answer to Peter's question about the limits of forgiveness — "seventy-seven times" (Matthew 18:21-22) — was not a literal count to stop at but a deliberate rejection of counting altogether. The parable that follows, of the servant forgiven an impossible debt who then refuses to forgive a much smaller one, makes the math explicit: whatever has been done to us is almost always smaller than what we have already been forgiven by God, and remembering that scale reorients the willingness to extend forgiveness to others.

Releasing the Debt

Releasing bitterness practically begins with entrusting justice to God rather than pursuing it personally. Romans 12:19's instruction — "never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God" — is not a denial that real wrong occurred or that justice matters; it is a redirection of who is responsible for administering it. God sees what happened with perfect clarity and will deal with it perfectly; the forgiver is released from the exhausting, ultimately futile task of trying to extract justice or satisfaction on their own.

Mark 11:25 ties forgiveness directly to prayer — "whenever you stand praying, forgive, if you have anything against anyone" — suggesting that regular, honest prayer is itself the setting where forgiveness is most naturally worked out, brought repeatedly before God rather than resolved in a single decisive moment. For deep or repeated wounds, forgiveness is rarely one clean transaction; it is often a decision renewed each time the memory resurfaces, until the root Hebrews warns against no longer has soil to grow in.

Key Scriptures

Matthew 6:14-15

"For if you forgive others their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you, but if you do not forgive others their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses."

Colossians 3:13

"Bearing with one another and, if one has a complaint against another, forgiving each other; as the Lord has forgiven you, so you also must forgive."

Hebrews 12:15

"See to it that no one fails to obtain the grace of God; that no root of bitterness springs up and causes trouble, and by it many become defiled."

Romans 12:19

"Beloved, never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God, for it is written, Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord."

Matthew 18:21-22

"Lord, how often will my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? As many as seven times? Jesus said to him, I do not say to you seven times, but seventy-seven times."

Mark 11:25

"And whenever you stand praying, forgive, if you have anything against anyone, so that your Father also who is in heaven may forgive you your trespasses."

Ephesians 4:32

"Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you."
