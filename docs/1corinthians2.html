1When I came to you, brothers, I did not come with eloquence or wisdom as I proclaimed to you the testimony about God.

2For I resolved to know nothing while I was with you except Jesus Christ and Him crucified.

3I came to you in weakness and fear, and with much trembling.

4My message and my preaching were not with persuasive words of wisdom, but with a demonstration of the Spirit’s power,

5so that your faith would not rest on men’s wisdom, but on God’s power.

6Among the mature, however, we speak a message of wisdom—but not the wisdom of this age or of the rulers of this age, who are coming to nothing.

7No, we speak of the mysterious and hidden wisdom of God, which He destined for our glory before time began.

8None of the rulers of this age understood it. For if they had, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory.

9Rather, as it is written: “No eye has seen, no ear has heard, no heart has imagined, what God has prepared for those who love Him.”

10But God has revealed it to us by the Spirit. The Spirit searches all things, even the deep things of God.

11For who among men knows the thoughts of man except his own spirit within him? So too, no one knows the thoughts of God except the Spirit of God.

12We have not received the spirit of the world, but the Spirit who is from God, that we may understand what God has freely given us.

13And this is what we speak, not in words taught us by human wisdom, but in words taught by the Spirit, expressing spiritual truths in spiritual words.

14The natural man does not accept the things that come from the Spirit of God. For they are foolishness to him, and he cannot understand them, because they are spiritually discerned.

15The spiritual man judges all things, but he himself is not subject to anyone’s judgment.

16“For who has known the mind of the Lord, so as to instruct Him?” But we have the mind of Christ.

Summary

Paul recalls that when he first came to Corinth, he deliberately set aside eloquence and clever argument, resolving to know nothing among them except Jesus Christ crucified, preaching in weakness and fear so their faith would rest on God's power rather than human wisdom (vv. 1-5). Yet he does speak wisdom to the mature — not the wisdom of this age's rulers, but God's hidden wisdom, planned before the ages, which those rulers failed to recognize when they crucified the Lord of glory (vv. 6-8). No eye or ear has grasped what God has prepared for those who love him, but God has revealed it through the Spirit, who searches even the depths of God (vv. 9-11). Because believers have received God's Spirit rather than the world's spirit, they can understand what God has freely given them, expressing spiritual truths in Spirit-taught words (vv. 12-13). The natural person cannot accept these things, since they are spiritually discerned, but the spiritual person evaluates all things while being subject to no one's judgment — for believers possess the mind of Christ (vv. 14-16).

Authorship & Background

Author: Paul (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 2 continues Paul's argument against worldly wisdom. He describes his own approach to Corinth (vv.1-5) — deliberately weak and simple — and then explains the true wisdom of God revealed by the Spirit (vv.6-16). The chapter contrasts human wisdom (which cannot know God) with divine revelation (which the Spirit gives to believers).
Classification: Epistle — Pauline
Key Themes: Paul's own arrival described in weakness and fear so that faith would rest in power rather than eloquence, a hidden wisdom eye has not seen and ear has not heard, the Spirit named as the only means by which the things of God can be received, a natural man unable to know what is spiritually discerned, and a closing claim to the mind of Christ resting on the Spirit rather than on ability
Paul's Approach (vv.1-5): Paul came to Corinth "in weakness, and in fear, and in much trembling" (v.3). He deliberately avoided impressive rhetoric. Why? "That your faith should not stand in the wisdom of men, but in the power of God" (v.5). If people are converted by eloquence, their faith rests on eloquence. If converted by the Spirit's power, their faith rests on God.
God's Hidden Wisdom (vv.6-10): God has a wisdom the world cannot discover — "the hidden wisdom, which God ordained before the world unto our glory" (v.7). This wisdom is revealed only by the Spirit (v.10). "Eye hath not seen, nor ear heard" (v.9) — human senses cannot discover it; only the Spirit reveals it.
The Natural and the Spiritual Man (vv.11-16): The argument moves from what God has revealed to who can receive it, and the analogy is drawn from ordinary privacy: what man knoweth the things of a man, save the spirit of man which is in him? even so the things of God knoweth no man, but the Spirit of God. Then the distinction that has been argued over ever since, the natural man receiveth not the things of the Spirit of God, for they are foolishness unto him, neither can he know them, for they are spiritually discerned. The chapter closes on a claim that sounds arrogant until you notice what it rests on, we have the mind of Christ, which is the gift described in verse 12 rather than an achievement.

Deep Dive

Setting: Paul writes from Ephesus in about AD 53–55 (16:8), a few years after founding the Corinthian church on his second journey (Acts 18:1–17). Corinth was a rich Roman colony and port, proud of its orators and teachers. Public speakers competed for audiences, and polished rhetoric was a mark of status. The church had split into fan clubs around Paul, Apollos and Peter (1:12), judging their teachers the way the city judged speakers. Chapter 1 answered this with the cross: God chose what the world calls foolish and weak to shame the wise and strong (1:18–31). Chapter 2 continues the argument, first with Paul's own preaching in Corinth, then with the wisdom only God's Spirit can reveal.

Proclaiming Christ Crucified (vv1–5)

  • "And I, when I came to you" (v1). "And I" ties this to 1:26–31. The Corinthians themselves were not much by worldly standards, and neither was the way Paul came to them. Both showed that the power was God's.
  • "Lofty speech or wisdom" (v1). Literally "excellence of word or of wisdom." Paul refuses the two things Corinth admired most: impressive delivery and clever philosophy. He came as a witness bringing "the testimony of God," not a performer selling an idea.
  • "Testimony" or "mystery"? (v1). The ESV footnotes that some manuscripts read "mystery" (mystērion) instead of "testimony" (martyrion). The two Greek words look alike, and both readings have early, strong support. Many modern editions now prefer "mystery," which would anticipate v7. Either way the sense is the same: Paul announced what God had made known, not his own insight.
  • "I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified" (v2). This is a deliberate decision, not a lack of learning. Paul could quote Greek poets (Acts 17:28). He chose to keep the cross at the center because it was the very thing Greeks found foolish and Jews found offensive (1:23). Crucifixion was a slave's death, unmentionable in polite company. Paul built his whole message on it.
  • "In weakness and in fear and much trembling" (v3). Paul arrived in Corinth after being run out of Thessalonica and Berea and after a modest response in Athens (Acts 17). In Corinth the Lord told him in a vision, "Do not be afraid… for I am with you" (Acts 18:9–10), which suggests he was afraid. His critics later said his "bodily presence is weak, and his speech of no account" (2 Cor 10:10). Paul does not deny it. He makes it part of his point (2 Cor 12:9–10).
  • "Not in plausible words of wisdom" (v4). "Plausible" renders a rare word related to peithō, "persuade." The manuscripts vary in the exact wording here, but the meaning is clear: the technique of persuasion was not what won the Corinthians.
  • "In demonstration of the Spirit and of power" (v4). "Demonstration" is apodeixis, used by Greek philosophers for a conclusive proof. Paul takes their technical word and gives it a new content: the proof was not a syllogism but the Spirit at work, in changed lives and, Acts and 2 Corinthians 12:12 suggest, in signs as well.
  • "So that your faith might not rest in the wisdom of men" (v5). Faith that rests on a clever argument can be argued away by a cleverer one. Faith that rests on God's power stands on something that does not move.
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry calls Christ, "in his person and offices," "the sum and substance of the gospel," and so the main subject of every faithful preacher. JFB reads the decision of v2 as aimed at the Corinthians who had toned down the cross to avoid offending learned Greeks and Jews. JFB takes the trembling as not personal fear but "trembling anxiety to perform a duty." Guzik allows that the weakness may have been illness or the threat of persecution. He also says Paul was not against persuasion as such, since he reasoned and persuaded often in Acts (18:4; 26:28). What he rejected was trusting technique, because whatever talks people into faith can talk them out of it.

Wisdom from the Spirit (vv6–16)

  • "Yet among the mature we do impart wisdom" (v6). After two chapters against "wisdom," Paul insists the gospel is not anti-intellectual. It has its own wisdom, deeper than the world's. "Mature" is teleios, full-grown, the opposite of the "infants" of 3:1 (nēpios, as in 14:20 and Eph 4:13). Readers differ on who the mature are. Some think Paul means all true believers, set against unbelievers, since vv14–15 contrast the natural and the spiritual person. Others think he means growing believers in contrast to spiritual babies, which fits 3:1–3. Both readings fit the passage, and Paul may be pressing the Corinthians, who thought themselves advanced, to see they were not.
  • "The rulers of this age, who are doomed to pass away" (v6). "Doomed to pass away" is a present passive of katargeō, to make ineffective or abolish. They are being brought to nothing even now. Paul uses the same verb of Christ destroying "every rule and every authority" (15:24).
  • "A secret and hidden wisdom of God" (v7). "Secret" is mystērion. In Greek religion a "mystery" was a rite revealed only to initiates. In Paul it means something nearly the opposite: God's plan, hidden for ages and now openly announced in the gospel (Rom 16:25–26; Col 1:26). It is not hard to understand. It simply could not be known until God revealed it.
  • "Which God decreed before the ages for our glory" (v7). The cross was not God's back-up plan. Before creation he purposed it, and its goal is the believer's future glory (Rom 8:30).
  • "None of the rulers of this age understood this" (v8). Who are the rulers? Some see the human authorities who crucified Jesus: Caiaphas, Herod, Pilate (Acts 4:25–28). Others see spiritual powers behind them, as in Ephesians 6:12 and Colossians 2:15. Many see both, human rulers acting with dark powers behind them. The Tyndale notes lean toward political rulers. On any reading the irony is the same: those who crucified Jesus thought they were ending him, and in fact they carried out God's plan for their own defeat.
  • "They would not have crucified the Lord of glory" (v8). "The Lord of glory" is one of the highest titles Paul gives Jesus. In the Old Testament glory belongs to God alone (Ps 24:7–10), and here the one who holds it is the one crucified. The phrase joins the most exalted title to the most shameful death.
  • "What no eye has seen, nor ear heard" (v9). Paul loosely quotes Isaiah 64:4, perhaps blended with Isaiah 65:17 ("the former things shall not be remembered or come into mind"). Isaiah 64 is a prayer for God to "rend the heavens and come down," and Paul says this has happened. Where Isaiah has "those who wait for him," Paul writes "those who love him." The verse is often read at funerals as a picture of heaven. That is not wrong, since full glory is still future, but v10 shows Paul's main point: these things God "has revealed to us" already in the gospel.
  • "The Spirit searches everything, even the depths of God" (v10). The Spirit is not a force but a person who searches and knows. "Searches" here does not mean he is looking for something he lacks. It means nothing in God is hidden from him.
  • "Who knows a person's thoughts except the spirit of that person" (v11). An everyday analogy: no one knows what is going on inside another person unless they tell them. In the same way, no one can know God's mind unless his own Spirit discloses it. Human wisdom cannot climb up to God. God must come down and speak.
  • "Not the spirit of the world, but the Spirit who is from God" (v12). Every believer has received the Spirit (12:13; Rom 8:9). His purpose is "that we might understand the things freely given us by God," the gifts of grace that cost the receiver nothing.
  • "Interpreting spiritual truths to those who are spiritual" (v13). The Greek is compact and debated. The verb synkrinō can mean "combine," "compare" (2 Cor 10:12) or "interpret" (as of dreams in the Greek Old Testament, Gen 40:8). And "spiritual" could mean spiritual people or spiritual words. So the ESV text has "to those who are spiritual," while its footnote offers "interpreting spiritual truths in spiritual language." Either way, God's truth must be taught in a way that fits it, by the Spirit's words and not borrowed rhetorical tricks.
  • "The natural person" (v14). "Natural" is psychikos, from psychē, "soul" or natural life. It describes a person living only by ordinary human life, without the Spirit (Jude 19 uses it of people "devoid of the Spirit"). It does not mean someone crude or immoral. A brilliant, cultured philosopher can be psychikos. Such a person finds the gospel "folly" and "is not able to understand" it. The problem is not intelligence but sight.
  • "Spiritually discerned" (v14) and "judges all things" (v15). Both verbs are anakrinō, to examine or investigate, the word for the Bereans "examining the Scriptures" (Acts 17:11). The spiritual person can examine everything in the light of God's truth, but the natural person is not equipped to examine him in return. This is not a claim that Christians are above correction. Paul spends the rest of the letter correcting spiritual people.
  • "Who has understood the mind of the Lord?" (v16). Paul quotes Isaiah 40:13 from the Greek Old Testament, where "mind" (nous) stands for the Hebrew "Spirit of the LORD." The expected answer is "no one." "But we have the mind of Christ." What no one could reach, Christ has given. And Paul puts "Christ" where Isaiah had "the LORD," quietly identifying Jesus with Israel's God. "We" is plural: the mind of Christ is the shared possession of his people, not a private elite.
  • Commentators: All three see vv6–16 as the gospel's true wisdom, hidden from the world and opened by the Spirit. Henry calls "Lord of glory" a title "much too great for any creature to bear," and links the rulers' ignorance to Jesus' prayer, "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). JFB reads the rulers as "the great and learned men of this world" and rejects any idea of a hidden inner teaching for an elite. The mystery is truth once hidden in God's counsels and now made known. JFB also argues that v15 gives practical spiritual insight, not infallibility on every question. Guzik lays out the human and demonic readings of the rulers without forcing a choice. He rejects reading v9 only of heaven, because v10 says it is already revealed. He also warns that Christians can think like "natural" people when they judge everything by what works and the bottom line. Guzik, Henry and JFB all take v10 as a proof of the Spirit's deity, and Guzik and JFB both read v16 as Paul treating Christ as the LORD of Isaiah 40.

Pointing to Christ

The chapter turns on one strange pairing: "the Lord of glory" was "crucified." The world's wisest and strongest saw only a condemned man, and in that blindness they fulfilled God's plan from before the ages. Paul staked everything on that message, not on his skill. The same Spirit who searches the depths of God now opens those depths to ordinary believers, so that a church in a city of orators could say, "we have the mind of Christ."

Reflection

  • Christ Crucified as the Whole Message (v.2) — Paul determined to know nothing among the Corinthians except Jesus Christ and Him crucified. Is Christ crucified still the center of your faith, or have other things quietly displaced Him?
  • Weakness That Displays God's Power (vv.3-5) — Paul came in weakness and trembling, deliberately avoiding persuasive rhetoric, so the Corinthians' faith would rest on God's power rather than human eloquence. What is your own faith resting on — a compelling voice, or the power of God?
  • A Wisdom Beyond Human Imagination (v.9) — Paul describes a hidden wisdom that no eye has seen and no mind has conceived, prepared by God for those who love Him. How does trusting that God's plan exceeds your imagination shape the way you face uncertainty?
  • The Limits of Natural Understanding (v.14) — Paul explains that a person without the Spirit cannot receive or understand spiritual truth, no matter how intelligent they are. How does this shape the way you pray for and talk with someone who isn't yet a believer?
  • Access to the Mind of Christ (v.16) — Through the Spirit, believers are given the mind of Christ, able to grasp spiritual truth on His terms rather than the world's. Are you drawing on that access through Scripture and prayer, or defaulting to worldly reasoning?
