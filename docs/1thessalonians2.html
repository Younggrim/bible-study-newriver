1You yourselves know, brothers, that our visit to you was not in vain.

2As you are aware, we had already endured suffering and shameful treatment in Philippi. But in the face of strong opposition, we were bold in our God to speak to you the gospel of God.

3For our appeal does not arise from deceit or ulterior motives or trickery.

4Instead, we speak as those approved by God to be entrusted with the gospel, not in order to please men but God, who examines our hearts.

5As you know, we never used words of flattery or any pretext for greed. God is our witness!

6Nor did we seek praise from you or from anyone else, although as apostles of Christ we had authority to demand it.

7On the contrary, we were gentle among you, like a nursing mother caring for her children.

8We cared so deeply that we were delighted to share with you not only the gospel of God, but our own lives as well. That is how beloved you have become to us.

9Surely you recall, brothers, our labor and toil. We worked night and day so that we would not be a burden to anyone while we proclaimed to you the gospel of God.

10You are witnesses, and so is God, of how holy, righteous, and blameless our conduct was among you who believed.

11For you know that we treated each of you as a father treats his own children—

12encouraging you, comforting you, and urging you to walk in a manner worthy of God, who calls you into His own kingdom and glory.

13And we continually thank God because, when you received the word of God that you heard from us, you accepted it not as the word of men, but as it truly is, the word of God, which is also now at work in you who believe.

14For you, brothers, became imitators of the churches of God in Judea that are in Christ Jesus. You suffered from your own countrymen the very things they suffered from the Jews,

15who killed both the Lord Jesus and their own prophets and drove us out as well. They are displeasing to God and hostile to all men,

16hindering us from telling the Gentiles how they may be saved. As a result, they continue to heap up their sins to full capacity; the utmost wrath has come upon them.

17Brothers, although we were torn away from you for a short time (in person, not in heart), our desire to see you face to face was even more intense.

18For we wanted to come to you—indeed I, Paul, tried again and again—but Satan obstructed us.

19After all, who is our hope, our joy, our crown of boasting, if it is not you yourselves in the presence of our Lord Jesus at His coming?

20You are indeed our glory and our joy.

Summary

Paul reminds the Thessalonians that his visit was not without purpose, and that despite earlier suffering and opposition in Philippi, he and his companions spoke the gospel boldly amid much conflict (vv. 1-2). He insists his appeal came from pure motives, not error, impurity, or trickery, and that he sought to please God rather than people, avoiding flattery and any pretext for greed (vv. 3-5). Though he had the right to be supported as an apostle, Paul was gentle among them like a nursing mother caring for her children, and worked night and day so as not to be a financial burden while he preached (vv. 6-9). He describes exhorting, comforting, and urging each of them individually the way a father deals with his own children, so they would live lives worthy of God (vv. 10-12). The chapter closes with Paul recalling their joyful reception of the gospel as God's word, their suffering at the hands of their own countrymen much like the churches in Judea, and his deep longing — twice thwarted by Satan — to see them again (vv. 13-20).

Authorship & Background

Author: Paul the Apostle (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 2 is Paul's defense of his ministry among the Thessalonians. After his forced departure (Acts 17:5-10), opponents apparently slandered Paul — accusing him of being a charlatan, a coward who fled, and a self-serving manipulator. Paul responds by reminding them of his conduct: he was bold despite suffering (v.2), honest in motive (vv.3-6), gentle as a nursing mother (v.7), hardworking and self-supporting (v.9), and fatherly in his care (v.11).
Classification: Epistle — Pauline
Key Themes: A ministry defended point by point against a slander it never states outright, boldness claimed despite suffering already endured at Philippi, gentleness compared to a nursing mother and burden compared to a father, a word received as God's own rather than as merely human, and Paul calling himself orphaned by a separation he did not choose
Paul's Ministry Character (vv.1-12): Paul's ministry was: not in vain (v.1), bold despite persecution (v.2), free from deceit and impure motives (v.3), God-pleasing not man-pleasing (v.4), without flattery or greed (v.5), without seeking glory (v.6), gentle as a nursing mother (v.7), self-giving (v.8), self-supporting (v.9), holy and blameless (v.10), fatherly (v.11).
The Word's Power (v.13): The Thessalonians received Paul's message not as human words but as God's word — which "effectually worketh" in believers.
Persecution and Longing (vv.14-20): The Thessalonians suffered from their own countrymen as the Judean churches suffered from Jews (v.14). Paul was "taken from" them (v.17) — Greek "orphaned" — and longed to return but was hindered by Satan (v.18). They are his hope, joy, and crown (v.19).

Deep Dive

Setting: Paul writes from Corinth around AD 50–51, a few months after a mob forced him out of Thessalonica (Acts 17:5–10). He had stayed only a short time: three Sabbaths in the synagogue, and probably some weeks more working among the Gentiles, long enough for the Philippians to send him help twice (Phil 4:16). After he slipped away by night, his opponents seem to have told the new believers that Paul was just another traveling speaker who talked people out of their money and then ran when trouble came. The Greek world was full of such men: wandering philosophers and religious teachers who lived off their followers. Chapter 1 thanked God for the church. Chapter 2 answers the slander. Paul does not argue from his credentials but from what the Thessalonians saw with their own eyes, then thanks God again for how they received the word, and finally explains why he has not come back.

Paul's Ministry to the Thessalonians (vv1–16)

  • "For you yourselves know" (v1). Paul keeps saying it: "you know" (vv1, 2, 5, 11) and "you remember" (v9), with "God is witness" (vv5, 10). He calls two witnesses, the church and God, as the law required (Deut 19:15). He has nothing to hide, so he asks them to check his record against their memory.
  • "Our coming to you was not in vain" (v1). "In vain" (kenē) means empty or hollow. Paul's point is that the visit had real substance. The Tyndale notes add that the word for "coming" was used for a speaker's arrival in a city, and orators were famous for grand entrances. Paul's arrival had no show, but it had results.
  • "Shamefully treated at Philippi" (v2). Just before Thessalonica, Paul and Silas had been stripped, beaten with rods and put in the stocks, all without trial, though they were Roman citizens (Acts 16:22–24, 37). "Shamefully treated" (hybrizō) means insulted and outraged, an attack on their dignity as well as their bodies. A con man would have taken the hint and found an easier line of work.
  • "Boldness in our God… in the midst of much conflict" (v2). "Boldness" is the freedom to speak openly. Its source was "in our God," not in Paul's temperament. "Conflict" is agōn, the word for an athletic contest or struggle (Phil 1:30; Col 2:1). Preaching in Thessalonica was a fight from the start.
  • "Not from error or impurity or any attempt to deceive" (v3). These were exactly the charges made against wandering teachers: false ideas, impure motives (greed or sexual misconduct, since some religious cults of the day mixed the two) and trickery. Paul denies all three.
  • "Approved by God to be entrusted with the gospel" (v4). The verb dokimazō means to test and approve, like metal tested for purity. In Greek cities, officials were examined before they took office. God had tested Paul and handed him the gospel as a trust, so Paul answers to the one "who tests our hearts," and the same verb ends the verse. A steward does not change the message to suit the audience (Gal 1:10).
  • "Words of flattery… a pretext for greed" (v5). Flattery tells people what they want to hear to get something from them. "Pretext" is a cloak or mask: religion worn as a cover for making money. Paul says the Thessalonians can judge whether he flattered them, but only God can see motives, so he calls God as witness to those.
  • "Nor did we seek glory from people" (v6). Paul did not want honor, applause or followers of his own. "Though we could have made demands as apostles" is literally "could have been a weight." The word can mean "throw our weight around" or "be a financial burden," and Paul likely means both. As an apostle he had the right to be supported (1 Cor 9:4–14), but he chose not to use it here.
  • "We were gentle among you" (v7). Here is the chapter's best-known textual question. The Greek words for "gentle" (ēpioi) and "little children" (nēpioi) differ by one letter, and the word before ends in that same letter n, so a scribe could easily add or drop it. Many of the oldest manuscripts read "we became little children," and the latest standard Greek text prints that. The ESV, with most English versions, reads "gentle" and footnotes "infants." Those who prefer "children" say it is the harder reading and Paul often uses it. Those who prefer "gentle" say it fits the next phrase better, since Paul would not call himself an infant and a nursing mother in one breath. Either way the point is the same: the apostles did not act like big men.
  • "Like a nursing mother taking care of her own children" (v7). Trophos is a nursing woman, often a hired wet nurse in wealthy homes. Paul specifies her own children, the tenderest picture possible. "Taking care" (thalpō) means to keep warm, the word used of a bird warming her chicks (Deut 22:6 in the Greek Old Testament). Paul, the former persecutor, describes himself as a mother.
  • "Being affectionately desirous of you" (v8). The verb homeiromai is very rare and appears only here in the Bible. It means to long for someone. One ancient grave inscription uses it for grieving parents longing for their dead child. It is the language of family love, not of a teacher and his clients.
  • "Not only the gospel of God but also our own selves" (v8). "Selves" is psychas, literally "lives" or "souls." They shared their lives, not just their message. Real ministry cannot be done at arm's length.
  • "Our labor and toil: we worked night and day" (v9). Paul was a tentmaker or leatherworker (Acts 18:3). Workshops opened before dawn, and he likely worked early and late and preached in between, perhaps while he worked, as customers and fellow craftsmen came through. Greek culture looked down on manual labor, and a teacher who did it lost status. Paul chose that loss so no one could say he preached for money, and to set an example of honest work for a church with some idle members (4:11; 2 Thess 3:7–10).
  • "Holy and righteous and blameless" (v10). The Tyndale notes say these words were used in Greek writing for faithfulness to both divine and human laws. JFB's division is useful: holy toward God, righteous toward people, blameless in their own lives.
  • "Like a father with his children" (v11). Paul moves from mother (v7) to father. In the ancient world, a father's role was to train his children in how to live. Paul did this "each one of you," personally and not only from a platform. Three verbs (v12) describe his teaching: exhorted (urged), encouraged (comforted, cheered on), and charged (solemnly insisted, as a witness testifies). The goal: "to walk in a manner worthy of God." "Walk" is the Bible's word for the whole of a person's daily life.
  • "Who calls you into his own kingdom and glory" (v12). "Calls" is present tense: God keeps calling. The kingdom is both now and coming. Its fullness arrives when Jesus returns, as v19 says. Being called to share God's own glory is the reason to live worthy of him.
  • "You accepted it… as what it really is, the word of God" (v13). Paul uses two verbs. They "received" it (heard it) and "accepted" it (welcomed it). The message came "from us," through human preachers, but it was God's own word, and it was still "at work in you believers." The verb is energeō, the root of "energy." God's word is active: it convicts, comforts and changes people over time (Heb 4:12; Isa 55:11).
  • "Imitators of the churches of God… in Judea" (v14). In chapter 1 they imitated Paul and the Lord. Here they share the sufferings of the first churches. The believers in Judea had been persecuted from the beginning (Acts 8:1; 12:1–3), and Paul himself had led part of it (Gal 1:13, 22–23). In Thessalonica the pressure came from "your own countrymen," fellow Gentiles, though Acts 17:5 says Jews stirred them up.
  • "Who killed both the Lord Jesus and the prophets" (v15). This is one of the hardest passages in Paul's letters, and it needs careful reading. First, Paul is a Jew writing about some of his own people, not about Jews as a race. The Christians in Judea he has just praised were themselves Jews. Second, he names the specific Judean leaders who opposed Jesus and the gospel, in line with Jesus' own words about Jerusalem killing the prophets (Matt 23:29–37) and Stephen's speech (Acts 7:52). Third, the Romans carried out the crucifixion, and the New Testament holds all humanity responsible (Acts 4:27). Romans 9–11 shows Paul's heart for Israel: "my heart's desire and prayer to God for them is that they may be saved" (Rom 10:1). Christians who have used this verse to justify hatred of Jews have badly misused it.
  • "Hindering us from speaking to the Gentiles that they might be saved" (v16). This is the core of Paul's charge. The sin he names is trying to keep the gospel from Gentiles. That had just happened in Thessalonica and Berea (Acts 17:5, 13), and soon would in Corinth (Acts 18:6, 12).
  • "Always to fill up the measure of their sins" (v16). The picture is a cup filling to the brim. God told Abraham the Amorites' iniquity was "not yet complete" (Gen 15:16), and Jesus told the leaders, "Fill up, then, the measure of your fathers" (Matt 23:32). Judgment waits until the cup is full.
  • "But wrath has come upon them at last!" (v16). The Greek tense looks back on wrath as already arrived. What Paul meant is debated. Some see a recent disaster: around AD 49 Emperor Claudius expelled Jews from Rome (Acts 18:2), and Josephus describes a riot at Passover in Jerusalem in those years in which thousands were killed. Others read it as prophetic: the coming judgment is so certain that Paul speaks of it as done, pointing to Jerusalem's destruction in AD 70. Still others take "at last" as "fully" or "to the end." A few scholars have argued these verses were added later, but there is no manuscript evidence for that, and most reject it.
  • Commentators: All three read vv1–12 as Paul answering slanders against his motives. Guzik passes on William Barclay's list of the charges probably behind the chapter, among them deception, impure motives, people-pleasing, greed and seeking glory. On v7, JFB admits the oldest manuscripts read "little children" but prefers "gentle," as the ESV does, and Guzik says either reading shows Paul putting himself at their service. Henry: a nursing mother "condescends to mean offices for its good," and Paul charged them "with a father's affection rather than a father's authority." Henry also cautions that "night and day" does not mean Paul spent every hour in labor. Guzik makes a similar point from the dawn start of workshops. On v13 Henry writes, "We should receive the word of God as the word of God." On v16, Henry and JFB both link the wrath to Jerusalem's destruction. Henry notes, "It was not many years after this that Jerusalem was destroyed," while JFB also mentions the Passover tumult. Guzik stresses that Paul's anger comes from grief for his people, and that Christians must never answer persecution with persecution.

Paul's Longing to See Them Again (vv17–20)

  • "Since we were torn away from you" (v17). The verb aporphanizō, used only here, means "to be orphaned, bereaved." Paul has called himself their mother (v7) and father (v11), and now he says that being separated from them left him feeling orphaned. He did not leave by choice. The believers sent him out by night to protect him and Jason (Acts 17:10).
  • "For a short time, in person not in heart" (v17). Literally "for the time of an hour." Paul thought he would be back soon. He was not, and that delay gave his critics material.
  • "Face to face" (v17). A letter was second best. Paul wanted to see their faces again, and the theme continues into chapter 3 (3:6, 10).
  • "I, Paul, again and again" (v18). Paul breaks out of the "we" for a moment to make it personal: he himself tried more than once.
  • "But Satan hindered us" (v18). "Hindered" (enkoptō) means to cut in, block or obstruct. Some see a military picture, breaking up a road to stop an army. Paul does not say how Satan did it. Suggestions include the city officials' bond on Jason that kept Paul away (Acts 17:9), continuing danger, or illness. Elsewhere the Holy Spirit stopped Paul from going places (Acts 16:6–7). Paul could tell the difference. Here he sees an enemy at work, but he trusts God over it, and Paul did eventually return to Macedonia (Acts 20:1–2). Meanwhile, God used the delay: because Paul couldn't come, he wrote, and the church still has the letter.
  • "Our hope or joy or crown of boasting before our Lord Jesus at his coming" (v19). "Coming" is parousia, the first use of this word in Paul's letters for Christ's return. In ordinary Greek it described the official visit of a king or emperor to a city, when citizens went out to meet him and presented crowns and honors. The "crown" (stephanos) is the victor's wreath, not a king's diadem. Paul pictures himself standing before the arriving King, and his wreath of honor will be the people he led to Christ.
  • "You are our glory and joy" (v20). Paul ends with emphasis: "you are." His joy is not a reputation or a successful program but people. Compare Philippians 4:1: "my joy and crown."
  • Commentators: Henry notes Paul was "unwillingly sent away by night to Berea," and draws the general lesson that "Satan is a constant enemy to the work of God, and does all he can to obstruct it." JFB contrasts this hindrance with the Spirit's in Acts 16:6–7, suggests Satan may have worked through wicked men or Paul's "thorn in the flesh," and stresses that God overruled it. JFB also points out that Paul's hope for them is "in a lower sense," since his chief hope is Christ himself. Guzik reads the hindrance as spiritual discernment, not superstition, and sees the letters that resulted as part of God's purpose. He also calls the people one disciples a crown that will be shown at Jesus' coming. The Tyndale notes add that the crown was given for civic honor or athletic victory.

Pointing to Christ

Paul's ministry in this chapter is shaped like the gospel it carried. He refuses to use his rights, gives his own life with his message, and works like a servant so others will not carry the burden. That is the pattern of Jesus, "who, though he was in the form of God… emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant" (Phil 2:6–7). The word Paul preached was God's word, and it was still working in them. And everything points to one day: "our Lord Jesus at his coming" (v19), when the King arrives and his servants find their joy in the people he saved through them.

Reflection

  • Living for an Audience of One (v.4) — Paul says he spoke to please God, who examines the heart, not to please people. Whose approval drives your choices day to day, God's or the people around you?
  • Ministry as Self-Giving (v.8) — Paul was glad to share not only the gospel but his own life with the Thessalonians because they had become dear to him. Are you investing your actual life in the people you're discipling, or just passing along information?
  • Receiving Scripture as God's Word (v.13) — The Thessalonians accepted Paul's message not as human opinion but as the word of God actually at work in believers. Do you approach the Bible expecting to hear from God, or treat it as a routine to complete?
  • Facing Spiritual Opposition (v.18) — Paul names Satan directly as the reason his plans to return were repeatedly blocked. When your good intentions keep meeting resistance, do you consider that there may be spiritual opposition at work, and respond with persistence rather than giving up?
  • People as Our True Reward (v.20) — Paul calls the Thessalonians his glory and joy — not any achievement or possession. What actually brings you the deepest joy, and are you investing in people or things?
