1“At that time the kingdom of heaven will be like ten virgins who took their lamps and went out to meet the bridegroom.

2Five of them were foolish, and five were wise.

3The foolish ones took their lamps but did not take along any extra oil.

4But the wise ones took oil in flasks along with their lamps.

5When the bridegroom was delayed, they all became drowsy and fell asleep.

6At midnight the cry rang out: ‘Here is the bridegroom! Come out to meet him!’

7Then all the virgins woke up and trimmed their lamps.

8The foolish ones said to the wise, ‘Give us some of your oil; our lamps are going out.’

9‘No,’ said the wise ones, ‘or there may not be enough for both us and you. Instead, go to those who sell oil and buy some for yourselves.’

10But while they were on their way to buy it, the bridegroom arrived. Those who were ready went in with him to the wedding banquet, and the door was shut.

11Later the other virgins arrived and said, ‘Lord, lord, open the door for us!’

12But he replied, ‘Truly I tell you, I do not know you.’

13Therefore keep watch, because you do not know the day or the hour.

14For it is just like a man going on a journey, who called his servants and entrusted them with his possessions.

15To one he gave five talents, to another two talents, and to another one talent—each according to his own ability. And he went on his journey.

16The servant who had received the five talents went at once and put them to work and gained five more.

17Likewise, the one with the two talents gained two more.

18But the servant who had received the one talent went off, dug a hole in the ground, and hid his master’s money.

19After a long time the master of those servants returned and settled accounts with them.

20The servant who had received the five talents came and presented five more. ‘Master,’ he said, ‘you entrusted me with five talents. See, I have gained five more.’

21His master replied, ‘Well done, good and faithful servant! You have been faithful with a few things; I will put you in charge of many things. Enter into the joy of your master!’

22The servant who had received the two talents also came and said, ‘Master, you entrusted me with two talents. See, I have gained two more.’

23His master replied, ‘Well done, good and faithful servant! You have been faithful with a few things; I will put you in charge of many things. Enter into the joy of your master!’

24Finally, the servant who had received the one talent came and said, ‘Master, I knew that you are a hard man, reaping where you have not sown and gathering where you have not scattered seed.

25So I was afraid and went out and hid your talent in the ground. See, you have what belongs to you.’

26‘You wicked, lazy servant!’ replied his master. ‘You knew that I reap where I have not sown and gather where I have not scattered seed.

27Then you should have deposited my money with the bankers, and on my return I would have received it back with interest.

28Therefore take the talent from him and give it to the one who has ten talents.

29For everyone who has will be given more, and he will have an abundance. But the one who does not have, even what he has will be taken away from him.

30And throw that worthless servant into the outer darkness, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.’

31When the Son of Man comes in His glory, and all the angels with Him, He will sit on His glorious throne.

32All the nations will be gathered before Him, and He will separate the people one from another, as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats.

33He will place the sheep on His right and the goats on His left.

34Then the King will say to those on His right, ‘Come, you who are blessed by My Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world.

35For I was hungry and you gave Me something to eat, I was thirsty and you gave Me something to drink, I was a stranger and you took Me in,

36I was naked and you clothed Me, I was sick and you looked after Me, I was in prison and you visited Me.’

37Then the righteous will answer Him, ‘Lord, when did we see You hungry and feed You, or thirsty and give You something to drink?

38When did we see You a stranger and take You in, or naked and clothe You?

39When did we see You sick or in prison and visit You?’

40And the King will reply, ‘Truly I tell you, whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers of Mine, you did for Me.’

41Then He will say to those on His left, ‘Depart from Me, you who are cursed, into the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels.

42For I was hungry and you gave Me nothing to eat, I was thirsty and you gave Me nothing to drink,

43I was a stranger and you did not take Me in, I was naked and you did not clothe Me, I was sick and in prison and you did not look after Me.’

44And they too will reply, ‘Lord, when did we see You hungry or thirsty or a stranger or naked or sick or in prison, and did not minister to You?’

45Then the King will answer, ‘Truly I tell you, whatever you did not do for one of the least of these, you did not do for Me.’

46And they will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life.”

1Then shall the kingdom of heaven be likened unto ten virgins, which took their lamps, and went forth to meet the bridegroom.

2And five of them were wise, and five were foolish.

3They that were foolish took their lamps, and took no oil with them:

4But the wise took oil in their vessels with their lamps.

5While the bridegroom tarried, they all slumbered and slept.

6And at midnight there was a cry made, Behold, the bridegroom cometh; go ye out to meet him.

7Then all those virgins arose, and trimmed their lamps.

8And the foolish said unto the wise, Give us of your oil; for our lamps are gone out.

9But the wise answered, saying, Not so; lest there be not enough for us and you: but go ye rather to them that sell, and buy for yourselves.

10And while they went to buy, the bridegroom came; and they that were ready went in with him to the marriage: and the door was shut.

11Afterward came also the other virgins, saying, Lord, Lord, open to us.

12But he answered and said, Verily I say unto you, I know you not.

13Watch therefore, for ye know neither the day nor the hour wherein the Son of man cometh.

14For the kingdom of heaven is as a man travelling into a far country, who called his own servants, and delivered unto them his goods.

15And unto one he gave five talents, to another two, and to another one; to every man according to his several ability; and straightway took his journey.

16Then he that had received the five talents went and traded with the same, and made them other five talents.

17And likewise he that had received two, he also gained other two.

18But he that had received one went and digged in the earth, and hid his lord’s money.

19After a long time the lord of those servants cometh, and reckoneth with them.

20And so he that had received five talents came and brought other five talents, saying, Lord, thou deliveredst unto me five talents: behold, I have gained beside them five talents more.

21His lord said unto him, Well done, thou good and faithful servant: thou hast been faithful over a few things, I will make thee ruler over many things: enter thou into the joy of thy lord.

22He also that had received two talents came and said, Lord, thou deliveredst unto me two talents: behold, I have gained two other talents beside them.

23His lord said unto him, Well done, good and faithful servant; thou hast been faithful over a few things, I will make thee ruler over many things: enter thou into the joy of thy lord.

24Then he which had received the one talent came and said, Lord, I knew thee that thou art an hard man, reaping where thou hast not sown, and gathering where thou hast not strawed:

25And I was afraid, and went and hid thy talent in the earth: lo, there thou hast that is thine.

26His lord answered and said unto him, Thou wicked and slothful servant, thou knewest that I reap where I sowed not, and gather where I have not strawed:

27Thou oughtest therefore to have put my money to the exchangers, and then at my coming I should have received mine own with usury.

28Take therefore the talent from him, and give it unto him which hath ten talents.

29For unto every one that hath shall be given, and he shall have abundance: but from him that hath not shall be taken away even that which he hath.

30And cast ye the unprofitable servant into outer darkness: there shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth.

32And before him shall be gathered all nations: and he shall separate them one from another, as a shepherd divideth his sheep from the goats:

33And he shall set the sheep on his right hand, but the goats on the left.

35For I was an hungred, and ye gave me meat: I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink: I was a stranger, and ye took me in:

36Naked, and ye clothed me: I was sick, and ye visited me: I was in prison, and ye came unto me.

37Then shall the righteous answer him, saying, Lord, when saw we thee an hungred, and fed thee? or thirsty, and gave thee drink?

38When saw we thee a stranger, and took thee in? or naked, and clothed thee?

39Or when saw we thee sick, or in prison, and came unto thee?

Summary

Jesus tells the parable of the ten virgins awaiting a bridegroom, five wise and prepared with extra oil and five foolish who run out, teaching the disciples to watch, since they do not know the day or hour of his return (vv. 1-13). In the parable of the talents, a master entrusts his servants with different sums of money before departing, and rewards the two who invested and multiplied what they were given while condemning the one who buried his talent out of fear as wicked and lazy (vv. 14-30). Jesus then describes the final judgment, when the Son of Man will come in glory and separate all nations as a shepherd separates sheep from goats, welcoming the righteous into the kingdom because they fed, clothed, welcomed, and visited him in the person of the least of his brothers, and sending away those who failed to do so into eternal punishment (vv. 31-46).

Authorship & Background

Author: Matthew (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 25 continues the Olivet Discourse with three parables about readiness for Christ's return: the Ten Virgins (vv.1-13), the Talents (vv.14-30), and the Sheep and Goats (vv.31-46). Together they answer the question: "What does it look like to be ready when Jesus returns?"
The Ten Virgins: Readiness through spiritual preparedness (you cannot borrow someone else's relationship with God).
Classification: Gospel — Narrative and Discourse
Key Themes: five wise and five foolish virgins differing only in oil kept in reserve, all ten having slept, the failure being unpreparedness for delay rather than wakefulness itself, three servants given unequal sums and judged identically for what they do with them rather than how much they receive, a buried talent justified by an accusation about the master that is turned back against the servant who made it, and a final judgment in which both sheep and goats ask the identical surprised question, when did we see thee, because neither group did anything for credit, inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me
The Ten Virgins (vv.1-13): Five wise and five foolish, and the only difference between them is oil in reserve. All ten sleep, which is worth noticing: the failure is not staying awake but being unprepared for a delay. The refusal to share is practical rather than unkind, lest there be not enough for us and you, and by the time the others return the door is shut. The exchange at the door is the coldest sentence in the chapter, verily I say unto you, I know you not. The moral given is the same word chapter 24 ended on, watch therefore, for ye know neither the day nor the hour.
The Talents (vv.14-30): Three servants and unequal sums, to every man according to his several ability, and the distribution is not the point of the parable. The first two double what they were given and are commended in identical words. The third buries his and explains himself with an accusation, I knew thee that thou art an hard man, and the answer uses his own premise against him, thou knewest that I reap where I sowed not, wherefore then hast thou not put my money to the exchangers? The sin is inactivity justified by a theory about the master. The talent is taken and given to the man who already had ten.
The Talents: Readiness through faithful stewardship (use what God has given you).
The Sheep and Goats: Readiness through compassionate action (serve "the least of these" as if serving Christ Himself). These three parables form a complete picture: genuine faith is prepared (virgins), productive (talents), and compassionate (sheep/goats). All three are necessary.
The Parable of the Talents: A talent was an enormous sum — approximately 20 years' wages. The master entrusted his servants with staggering resources. The issue was not the amount given but what was done with it. The five-talent and two-talent servants both received the same commendation: "Well done, good and faithful servant."
The Sheep and the Goats (vv.31-46): The last teaching in Matthew before the passion, and the criterion is unexpected. Both groups are surprised, and both ask the same question, when did we see thee? Neither recognized him at the time, which means the deeds were not done for credit. Six actions are listed twice, and they are all mundane: food, drink, shelter, clothing, visiting the sick and the prisoner. The identification is the sentence the passage turns on, inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me. The judgment separates on the basis of what was done to people nobody was watching.

Map & Geography

  • The Mount of Olives: Jesus continues His Olivet Discourse (begun in ch.24), still seated on the Mount of Olives overlooking Jerusalem and the Temple.
  • "All nations" (v.32): The final judgment encompasses all peoples from every geographic location — the scope is universal.

Setting: The Mount of Olives, Tuesday evening of Passover week, spring of AD 30, two days before the crucifixion (26:2). Jesus is still answering the disciples' question about his coming and the end of the age (24:3). Chapter 24 ended with a warning that "no one knows" the day or hour and with a parable of two servants, one faithful and one wicked (24:45–51). Chapter 25 finishes the Olivet Discourse with three more pictures, all unique to Matthew. Each one answers the question of how to wait for a Lord who seems delayed: the ten virgins (be ready), the talents (be faithful with what is entrusted), and the sheep and the goats (show mercy to Christ's brothers). The last is not a parable in the strict sense but a picture of the final judgment, and it closes Jesus' public teaching in Matthew.

The Parable of the Ten Virgins (vv1–13)

  • "Then the kingdom of heaven will be like" (v1). "Then" ties this to the coming of the Son of Man in chapter 24. The story is about the waiting time before he comes.
  • "Ten virgins who took their lamps and went to meet the bridegroom" (v1). These are young unmarried women, bridesmaids in the wedding party. Ten was a customary number for a group at a formal occasion. Jewish weddings in that day had two stages: a binding betrothal, and months later the wedding itself, when the groom came to bring his bride from her father's house to his own, followed by a feast that could last a week. The exact customs are not fully known. The bridesmaids' task was to wait and then light the procession to the feast. The bride is not mentioned; the story's focus is the bridegroom's coming.
  • Lamps and oil (vv3–4). The word lampas can mean a small clay oil lamp or a torch, a stick wrapped with rags soaked in olive oil, which needed fresh oil every so often. Either way, without a supply of oil the light would soon die. The wise brought extra oil in flasks.
  • "As the bridegroom was delayed, they all became drowsy and slept" (v5). All ten slept, wise and foolish alike. The parable does not blame them for sleeping. The difference is not who stayed awake, but who was prepared. The delay is a key word, as in 24:48. Christ's return will take longer than many expect, and the waiting tests readiness.
  • "At midnight there was a cry" (v6). Midnight is the most unexpected hour. Weddings sometimes ran late while the families finished negotiating gifts. "Come out to meet him." The word for "meet" (apantēsis) was used for a formal welcoming party going out to greet a visiting king and escort him back to the city. Paul uses it for believers going out to "meet the Lord in the air" (1 Thess 4:17).
  • "Our lamps are going out" (v8). The foolish virgins' torches flare briefly and fade. They had a lamp, which looked the same as the others' lamps, but nothing to keep it burning.
  • "There will not be enough for us and for you, go rather to the dealers" (v9). This sounds unkind, but the point is that readiness cannot be borrowed. No one can be prepared for Christ on someone else's behalf. Buying oil at midnight would be nearly impossible.
  • "Those who were ready went in with him to the marriage feast, and the door was shut" (v10). The shut door is final. It recalls the door of the ark that "the LORD shut" (Gen 7:16), which Jesus had just mentioned (24:37–39).
  • "Lord, lord, open to us." "Truly, I say to you, I do not know you" (vv11–12). These words echo the end of the Sermon on the Mount, where people say "Lord, Lord" and he answers, "I never knew you" (7:21–23). To be known by Christ means a real relationship with him (Gal 4:9; 2 Tim 2:19). Outward association is not enough.
  • "Watch therefore, for you know neither the day nor the hour" (v13). Since all ten slept, "watch" here cannot mean never sleeping. It means being ready, whenever he comes.
  • What is the oil? Many interpreters through history, including Guzik, see the oil as a picture of the Holy Spirit, since oil and the Spirit are linked in Scripture (1 Sam 16:13; Zech 4:1–6). Others see it as genuine faith, grace, or good works. The Tyndale notes caution that the parable may not intend a precise symbol, and that the point is simply readiness. All agree that it represents something inward and real that cannot be faked or borrowed.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Sincere Christians are the wise virgins, and hypocrites the foolish ones," and "Those that take up short of true grace, will certainly find the want of it one time or other." JFB takes the lamps as outward Christian profession and the oil as "that inward reality of grace which alone will stand." It insists that "I do not know you" means the same as "I never knew you" in 7:23 and rejects softer readings. Guzik, in line with Henry and JFB, sees the dividing line as between real and false believers, not between more and less spiritual Christians, though he reads the oil more specifically as the Holy Spirit.

The Parable of the Talents (vv14–30)

  • "Like a man going on a journey, who called his servants and entrusted to them his property" (v14). Wealthy men in the ancient world often left slaves in charge of their business while they traveled. Such slaves could trade, lend and invest on their master's behalf. Luke has a similar but different parable of the minas (Luke 19:11–27), told at Jericho a few days before. The details differ enough that most think Jesus told two separate stories.
  • "To one he gave five talents, to another two, to another one" (v15). A talent (talanton) was originally a weight, about 75 pounds, and then a large sum of money, about 6,000 denarii, or twenty years' wages for a laborer. Even one talent was a fortune. The English word "talent" meaning a natural gift comes from this parable. But in the story the talents are money, and they stand for everything Christ entrusts to his people: gifts, opportunities, time, possessions, and above all the message of the kingdom (13:11–12).
  • "To each according to his ability" (v15). The master knows his servants. He does not give anyone more than he can handle, and he does not expect the same results from each.
  • "Went at once and traded with them" (v16). The faithful servants act promptly and take risks. Both double what they were given.
  • "Dug in the ground and hid his master's money" (v18). Burying money was considered the safest way to protect it. The rabbis taught that a man who buried money entrusted to him was not liable if it was stolen. So by ordinary standards the third servant was being careful.
  • "After a long time the master… came and settled accounts" (v19). Again a delay. The servants will be called to account when he returns (Rom 14:12; 2 Cor 5:10).
  • "Well done, good and faithful servant" (v21). The servant with two talents hears exactly the same words as the servant with five (v23). The master praises faithfulness, not the size of the result. "You have been faithful over a little." Five talents was a fortune, yet the master calls it "a little" compared with what is coming. "Enter into the joy of your master." The reward is not only more responsibility but sharing the master's own joy, likely a picture of the messianic feast.
  • "I knew you to be a hard man, reaping where you did not sow… so I was afraid" (vv24–25). The third servant blames his master. His picture of the master is false, since the master has just been generous. But his words reveal the real problem: he did not know or trust his master, and he did nothing.
  • "You wicked and slothful servant!" (v26). "Slothful" (oknēros) means lazy or hesitant. The master does not accept the man's picture of him but argues from it. If he really thought the master demanded profit, he should at least have put the money "with the bankers" (v27), literally "the money-changers" or "the table men," who paid interest on deposits. That would have cost him no effort and no risk.
  • "Take the talent from him and give it to him who has the ten talents" (v28). The unused gift is taken away. "For to everyone who has will more be given" (v29) repeats what Jesus said about understanding the kingdom (13:12). Those who use what God gives receive more. Those who neglect it lose even that.
  • "Cast the worthless servant into the outer darkness" (v30). "Worthless" (achreios) means useless or unprofitable. His sin was not stealing or wasting, but doing nothing. Sins of omission are real sins.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Our receiving from Christ is in order to our working for him." JFB observes that "the same fidelity is required of all, and equally rewarded," and that the third servant's fault "consisted, it seems, not in his doing anything against, but simply nothing for his master." On the delay of v19, JFB says that expecting the Lord's return within the apostles' lifetime would be a misreading. Guzik explains that a talent was money and not natural ability, though it pictures all that God entrusts. He stresses that the two faithful servants received the same praise for different amounts, and that the third servant's excuse exposed indifference toward his master. The Tyndale notes add that the delay of Christ's return tempts some to evil (24:48–49), some to carelessness (25:3) and some to fearful passivity (25:18).

The Final Judgment (vv31–46)

  • "When the Son of Man comes in his glory, and all the angels with him" (v31). This brings the Olivet Discourse to its climax (24:30–31; 16:27). The humble king who rode a donkey into Jerusalem two days earlier now sits on "his glorious throne." Daniel saw thrones set and the court sitting in judgment (Dan 7:9–10, 13–14). Jesus takes the place of the judge, which belongs to God.
  • "Before him will be gathered all the nations" (v32). "Nations" (ethnē) usually means Gentiles, but here likely means all people, Jew and Gentile. A few interpreters, especially in the dispensational tradition, see a judgment of nations living at Christ's return, separate from the final judgment of Revelation 20. Most see the one final judgment of all people.
  • "As a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats" (v32). In Israel, sheep and goats grazed together in mixed flocks by day. At evening the shepherd often separated them, since goats needed shelter from the cold at night more than sheep did. In the fields they looked much alike. Ezekiel pictured God judging "between sheep and sheep, between rams and male goats" (Ezek 34:17). The right side was the place of honor (Ps 110:1).
  • "Then the King will say" (v34). The Son of Man is now called "the King," and he speaks of God as "my Father." "Inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world." The kingdom is an inheritance, a gift for children, not wages for workers. It was prepared before the world began (Eph 1:4).
  • "I was hungry and you gave me food" (vv35–36). Six needs are named: hunger, thirst, being a stranger, nakedness, sickness and prison. These were familiar Jewish "works of mercy" (Isa 58:6–7; Job 31:16–23). Visiting prisoners mattered because ancient prisons did not feed inmates. Prisoners depended on family and friends to bring food and clothing.
  • "Lord, when did we see you hungry…?" (vv37–39). The righteous are surprised. They did not serve the needy to earn a reward or to be seen (6:1–4). They simply loved, without calculating.
  • "As you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me" (v40). Christ identifies himself with "the least of these my brothers." Who are the brothers? Two main views are held. (1) Many interpreters through history take the brothers to be Jesus' disciples, especially those who suffer or are sent out with the gospel. In Matthew "my brothers" always means his followers (12:48–50; 28:10), and Jesus has said that whoever receives his disciples receives him, even with a cup of cold water (10:40–42). On this view the nations are judged by how they treated Christ's people. (2) Many others take the brothers as anyone in need, with Christ present in every hungry or suffering person (Prov 19:17). The first view fits Matthew's language better, but it does not shrink the duty of mercy: Jesus elsewhere commands love even for enemies (5:44) and tells the story of the good Samaritan (Luke 10:30–37).
  • Is this salvation by works? The passage does not say that the sheep earn the kingdom by their deeds. The kingdom was "prepared for you" before they did anything (v34). Their deeds are the evidence of who they are, the fruit that shows the tree (7:17–20; Jas 2:14–17). The sheep act as sheep because they belong to the Shepherd. This matches Paul's teaching that believers are saved by grace "for good works, which God prepared beforehand" (Eph 2:8–10).
  • "Depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels" (v41). Notice the contrast. The kingdom was prepared "for you" (v34), but the fire was "prepared for the devil and his angels," not for people. Hell was not made for humans. Those who go there go to a place never intended for them. Notice also that the righteous are "blessed by my Father," while the others are simply "cursed," not "cursed by my Father." Their curse is their own.
  • "Lord, when did we see you hungry…?" (v44). The goats are also surprised. They would gladly have helped Jesus, they think, if they had known it was him. Their failure was not cruelty but neglect. They simply did nothing, like the servant who buried the talent.
  • "These will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life" (v46). The same Greek word, aiōnios ("eternal"), describes both destinies. The word literally means "belonging to the age," that is, the age to come, and in that context it means without end. Christians have debated the nature of final punishment. Most, through history, have held to conscious, everlasting punishment. Some (called annihilationists or conditionalists) hold that the punishment is eternal in its result, ending in destruction. A smaller number hope for final restoration of all. The parallel with "eternal life" in the same sentence is the main reason most interpreters have taken the punishment to be lasting.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Jesus Christ is the great Shepherd; he will shortly distinguish between those that are his, and those that are not," and he adds a careful note, "We are not to suppose that acts of bounty will entitle to eternal happiness." JFB says the two classes have been "mingled all along up to this awful moment," and reads the brothers as Christ's people, since they did it "to these My brethren, now beside you, when cast upon your love." On v46 JFB notes that the same Greek word is used for both destinies, so the English should match. Guzik also points to the same word in both halves of v46 and holds that it means everlasting, rejecting both annihilation and universal restoration. He takes "the least of these" first as needy and suffering Christians, but extends it to all who suffer, and possibly to the Jewish people. The Tyndale notes give both readings of "my brothers" and lean toward Jesus' disciples.

Pointing to Christ

The three pictures in this chapter all center on one person. He is the bridegroom whose coming is delayed but certain, and the only question is whether he "knows" those waiting for him. He is the master who entrusts his wealth to his servants and comes back to share his joy with the faithful. He is the King on the glorious throne, who will judge all nations. Yet the same King says that he is found in the hungry, the stranger and the prisoner, because within days he himself will be thirsty, stripped, arrested and condemned. The Judge of all the earth first became one of "the least of these," so that those who belong to him might hear, "Come, you who are blessed by my Father."

Reflection

  • Faith That Cannot Be Borrowed (v.4) — The wise virgins carry their own oil, and when the foolish ask to borrow some, there isn't enough to share. Is your faith genuinely your own, or are you coasting on someone else's relationship with God?
  • Faithful, Not Just Successful (v.21) — The servants who doubled five talents and two talents receive the identical commendation, since faithfulness is what's rewarded, not the amount produced. Are you being faithful with what God has actually given you, rather than comparing it to what others have?
  • Fear That Leads to Burying the Gift (v.25) — The one-talent servant's failure wasn't incompetence but fear that produced inaction. What is fear keeping you from doing with what God has entrusted to you?
  • Serving Christ in "the Least of These" (v.40) — Jesus says whatever is done for the hungry, the stranger, or the imprisoned is done for Him personally. Who are "the least of these" in your life, and how are you actually treating them?
  • Two Destinies, One Choice (v.46) — The chapter ends with a stark division between eternal punishment and eternal life. How does the weight of that reality shape the urgency with which you live and share your faith?
