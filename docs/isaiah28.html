1Woe to the majestic crown of Ephraim’s drunkards, to the fading flower of his glorious splendor, set on the summit above the fertile valley, the pride of those overcome by wine.

2Behold, the Lord has one who is strong and mighty. Like a hailstorm or destructive tempest, like a driving rain or flooding downpour, he will smash that crown to the ground.

3The majestic crown of Ephraim’s drunkards will be trampled underfoot.

4The fading flower of his beautiful splendor, set on the summit above the fertile valley, will be like a ripe fig before the summer harvest: Whoever sees it will take it in his hand and swallow it.

5On that day the LORD of Hosts will be a crown of glory, a diadem of splendor to the remnant of His people,

6a spirit of justice to him who sits in judgment, and a strength to those who repel the onslaught at the gate.

7These also stagger from wine and stumble from strong drink: Priests and prophets reel from strong drink and are befuddled by wine. They stumble because of strong drink, muddled in their visions and stumbling in their judgments.

8For all their tables are covered with vomit; there is not a place without filth.

9Whom is He trying to teach? To whom is He explaining His message? To infants just weaned from milk? To babies removed from the breast?

10For they hear: “Order on order, order on order, line on line, line on line; a little here, a little there.”

11Indeed, with mocking lips and foreign tongues, He will speak to this people

12to whom He has said: “This is the place of rest, let the weary rest; this is the place of repose.” But they would not listen.

13Then the word of the LORD to them will become: “Order on order, order on order, line on line, line on line; a little here, a little there,” so that they will go stumbling backward and will be injured, ensnared, and captured.

14Therefore hear the word of the LORD, O scoffers who rule this people in Jerusalem.

15For you said, “We have made a covenant with death; we have fashioned an agreement with Sheol. When the overwhelming scourge passes through it will not touch us, because we have made lies our refuge and falsehood our hiding place.”

16So this is what the Lord GOD says: “See, I lay a stone in Zion, a tested stone, a precious cornerstone, a sure foundation; the one who believes will never be shaken.

17I will make justice the measuring line and righteousness the level. Hail will sweep away your refuge of lies, and water will flood your hiding place.

18Your covenant with death will be dissolved, and your agreement with Sheol will not stand. When the overwhelming scourge passes through, you will be trampled by it.

19As often as it passes through, it will carry you away; it will sweep through morning after morning, by day and by night.” The understanding of this message will bring sheer terror.

20Indeed, the bed is too short to stretch out on, and the blanket too small to wrap around you.

21For the LORD will rise up as at Mount Perazim. He will rouse Himself as in the Valley of Gibeon, to do His work, His strange work, and to perform His task, His disturbing task.

22So now, do not mock, or your shackles will become heavier. Indeed, I have heard from the Lord GOD of Hosts a decree of destruction against the whole land.

23Listen and hear my voice. Pay attention and hear what I say.

24Does the plowman plow for planting every day? Does he continuously loosen and harrow the soil?

25When he has leveled its surface, does he not sow caraway and scatter cumin? He plants wheat in rows and barley in plots, and rye within its border.

26For his God instructs and teaches him properly.

27Surely caraway is not threshed with a sledge, and the wheel of a cart is not rolled over the cumin. But caraway is beaten out with a stick, and cumin with a rod.

28Grain for bread must be ground, but it is not endlessly threshed. Though the wheels of the cart roll over it, the horses do not crush it.

29This also comes from the LORD of Hosts, who is wonderful in counsel and excellent in wisdom.

Summary

Isaiah opens with a warning to the drunken leaders of Ephraim, the northern kingdom, whose proud, fading crown of splendor is about to be trampled underfoot like a wilted flower (vv. 1-4). He then turns to Judah's own leaders, priests and prophets who are just as drunk and stumble over their own teaching, mocking Isaiah's message as fit only for babies (vv. 7-13). Because they have struck what amounts to a covenant with death, trusting in lies and a false shelter, God announces he is laying a tested, precious cornerstone in Zion, promising that whoever trusts in it will not be shaken (vv. 14-16). Their false refuge will be swept away by hail and flood, but Isaiah closes with a farming parable: a wise farmer does not plow forever or thresh forever, and God's dealings with his people follow the same purposeful, measured wisdom (vv. 23-29).

Authorship & Background

Author: Isaiah son of Amoz (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Woe Oracle — First of Six Woes (chs. 28-33)
Key Themes: Drunkenness (literal and spiritual), the rejected word of God, the "covenant with death," the precious cornerstone in Zion, God's strange work, the farmer parable
Historical Context: This begins a new section (chs. 28-33) — six woe oracles directed primarily at Judah's leaders during the period when they were seeking Egyptian alliance against Assyria (likely 705-701 BC, during Hezekiah's reign). The chapter opens with Ephraim/Samaria (whose fall in 722 BC serves as a warning to Judah) then pivots to Jerusalem. The "covenant with death" (v.15) refers to Judah's alliance with Egypt — they thought it guaranteed safety, but Isaiah calls it a pact with death itself. The cornerstone (v.16) is one of Scripture's great messianic texts.
The Fading Crown of Ephraim (vv.1-6): Woe to the crown of pride, to the drunkards of Ephraim, whose glorious beauty is a fading flower, which are on the head of the fat valleys. Samaria sat on a hill above a fertile valley, so the crown is a piece of topography as well as an insult. The city is described as a garland on the head of men already drunk, and what happens to it is stated in one image, it shall be as the hasty fruit before the summer, which the eater seeth, and while it is yet in his hand he eateth it up. Then the contrast held over for the remnant, in that day shall the LORD of hosts be for a crown of glory, and for a diadem of beauty, unto the residue of his people.
They Err in Vision (vv.7-13): But they also have erred through wine, and the list of who is included is what makes this section sting, the priest and the prophet have erred through strong drink, they err in vision, they stumble in judgment. Then the mockery, and this is the strangest passage in the prophets: the prophet is quoted being jeered at in what sounds like baby talk, precept upon precept, line upon line, here a little, and there a little, which in Hebrew is a string of repeated syllables, tsav latsav, qav laqav. They are imitating him as though he were teaching toddlers. And his answer takes the same syllables and gives them back, for with stammering lips and another tongue will he speak to this people. If they will not hear plain Hebrew they will hear Assyrian, which is the same sound and not a joke. Paul quotes this passage in 1 Corinthians 14 about tongues.
The Covenant with Death (vv.14-15): Hear the word of the LORD, ye scornful men, that rule this people which is in Jerusalem, and the charge is quoted in their own words, we have made a covenant with death, and with hell are we at agreement. What is being described is almost certainly a treaty, since the surrounding chapters are about the Egyptian alliance, and the rest of their sentence gives away what they think of it, we have made lies our refuge, and under falsehood have we hid ourselves. They know the arrangement is a fiction and are relying on it anyway.
The Tried Stone in Zion (v.16): One verse, and it is the answer to the previous one: against a refuge of lies, a foundation. Therefore thus saith the Lord GOD, Behold, I lay in Zion for a foundation a stone, a tried stone, a precious corner stone, a sure foundation, he that believeth shall not make haste. The last clause means shall not panic, which is exactly the fault of the men who signed the treaty. Paul quotes the verse in Romans 9 and 10 and Peter in 1 Peter 2, both applying it to Christ, and both keeping the contrast with haste.
The Refuge of Lies Swept Away (vv.17-22): Judgment also will I lay to the line, and righteousness to the plummet, so the builder's tools of the previous verse are turned into instruments of inspection. Then the treaty fails in its own terms, your covenant with death shall be disannulled, and your agreement with hell shall not stand. The image of a bed too short is the most domestic in the chapter, for the bed is shorter than that a man can stretch himself on it, and the covering narrower than that he can wrap himself in it, which is inadequate provision rather than active harm. And the closing note is about God's own reluctance, that he may do his work, his strange work, and bring to pass his act, his strange act.
The Farmer's Methods (vv.23-29): The chapter ends with a parable drawn from agriculture, and its subject is the fitting of method to material. Doth the plowman plow all day to sow, doth he not cast in the fitches, and scatter the cummin. Then the threshing, and the detail is technical: the fitches are not threshed with a threshing instrument, nor is the cummin bruised with a wheel, but the bread corn takes the cart wheel and the horsemen. Different crops take different force, and nobody grinds a delicate seed under a cart. Applied to the chapter above it, this says that the varying severity of what God does is expertise rather than inconsistency, and the closing line credits it as such, this also cometh forth from the LORD of hosts, who is wonderful in counsel, and excellent in working.

Setting: Chapter 28 begins a new section of Isaiah (chs 28–33) built around a series of six "woes" (28:1; 29:1, 15; 30:1; 31:1; 33:1). After the worldwide vision of chapters 24–27, Isaiah returns to the politics of his own day. The opening oracle is aimed at Ephraim, the northern kingdom of Israel, and its capital Samaria, shortly before Assyria destroyed it in 722 BC. Then, without a break, Isaiah turns on Jerusalem's own leaders, who are just as drunk on their own cleverness. The background for the rest of the section is Judah's temptation, in the years around 705–701 BC, to make a treaty with Egypt as protection against Assyria (30:1–2; 31:1). Isaiah's message is that the only real security is faith in God. In the middle of the warnings stands one of the great promises of the Old Testament, the tested cornerstone laid in Zion, which the New Testament applies to Jesus three times.

Judgment on Ephraim and Jerusalem (vv1–13)

  • "Ah, the proud crown of the drunkards of Ephraim" (v1). "Ah" is the Hebrew hoy, "woe," the cry of a funeral lament. Samaria sat on a rounded hill above a rich valley, and its circle of walls looked like a garland on a reveler's head. At ancient banquets guests wore wreaths of flowers. By morning the wreaths had wilted. "The fading flower of its glorious beauty" is that wilted wreath. Amos, a generation earlier, described Samaria's elite lying on ivory beds drinking wine from bowls (Amos 6:4–6).
  • "The Lord has one who is mighty and strong; like a storm of hail" (v2). This is Assyria, whom God would use as his instrument (10:5). Shalmaneser V besieged Samaria for three years, and it fell in 722 BC (2 Kings 17:5–6). Hail, flood and a hand that throws down: the drunken crown is knocked to the ground and "trodden underfoot" (v3).
  • "Like a first-ripe fig before the summer… he swallows it as soon as it is in his hand" (v4). The first figs ripen in June, before the main crop in August, and were a delicacy. Whoever spotted one would eat it immediately. Samaria would be gobbled up just as quickly. JFB sees in it "the eagerness of the Assyrian Shalmaneser."
  • "The LORD of hosts will be a crown of glory, and a diadem of beauty, to the remnant of his people" (v5). Against the fading wreath stands a crown that never fades: God himself. The remnant will not glory in a city but in the LORD. He will also be "a spirit of justice to him who sits in judgment, and strength to those who turn back the battle at the gate" (v6). Good judges and brave defenders both draw their strength from him (11:2).
  • "These also reel with wine… the priest and the prophet reel with strong drink" (v7). "These also" turns the spotlight to Judah. Jerusalem's religious leaders are no better than Samaria's. Priests were forbidden wine while serving (Lev 10:9), but these men "reel in vision, they stumble in giving judgment." The very people meant to see clearly and judge rightly are staggering. "All tables are full of filthy vomit, with no space left" (v8) is deliberately disgusting. JFB has no note here.
  • "To whom will he teach knowledge?… Those who are weaned from the milk?" (v9). Most interpreters, including JFB and Guzik, hear the drunken leaders mocking Isaiah. Who does he think he is, treating us like toddlers just off the breast?
  • "Precept upon precept… line upon line, here a little, there a little" (v10). In Hebrew this is tsav latsav, tsav latsav, qav laqav, qav laqav, ze'eir sham, ze'eir sham. It sounds like baby talk or a nursery chant, "blah-blah, blah-blah." The scoffers are mimicking the prophet's message as childish. The Tyndale notes suggest the words may be deliberate nonsense. Some take tsav and qav as letters of the alphabet being drilled in school. English Bibles have long made this a positive principle of step-by-step teaching, which is true in itself, but in context it is a sneer.
  • "By people of strange lips and with a foreign tongue the LORD will speak to this people" (v11). Isaiah turns their mockery back on them. If they will not hear God's word in plain Hebrew, they will hear it in Assyrian, a language that sounds to them like stammering (33:19). Moses had warned that a disobedient Israel would face "a nation whose language you do not understand" (Deut 28:49). Paul quotes this verse to say that tongues are a sign for unbelievers, not for believers (1 Cor 14:21–22). Guzik and the Tyndale notes both make the link.
  • "This is rest; give rest to the weary; and this is repose" (v12). This is what God had actually been saying, and it was simple: trust me and rest. "Yet they would not hear." Isaiah later puts it plainly: "In returning and rest you shall be saved; in quietness and in trust shall be your strength. But you were unwilling" (30:15). Jesus offers the same rest: "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28).
  • "The word of the LORD will be to them precept upon precept… that they may go, and fall backward, and be broken" (v13). God gives them back their own mocking words. Because they called his message baby talk, it will become a meaningless jumble to them, and they will stumble over it. The five verbs at the end, "go… fall backward… be broken… snared… taken," repeat almost exactly 8:15, where the LORD himself is "a stone of offense" to both houses of Israel.
  • Commentators: Henry divides the opening as a rebuke of Ephraim's "pride, drunkenness, complacency," with comfort for the faithful remnant in vv5–6, and then a rebuke of their dullness in learning. Guzik argues that the scoffers' sneer was really a compliment: plain, patient, repeated teaching is what both new believers and mature ones need. JFB dates the oracle just before Samaria's fall and reads v10 as the drunkards mocking Isaiah's "repetitions," then v11 as God answering in the "stammering" speech of foreign invaders.

A Cornerstone in Zion (vv14–29)

  • "Hear the word of the LORD, you scoffers, who rule this people in Jerusalem!" (v14). The scoffers in Jerusalem are its rulers, the political class making foreign policy.
  • "We have made a covenant with death, and with Sheol we have an agreement" (v15). This is probably Isaiah's sarcastic name for their treaty with Egypt. They thought they had signed a deal that would keep death away. The Tyndale notes add another possibility: some may have literally sought protection from Mot, the Canaanite god of death, or from Egypt's gods of the dead. "When the overwhelming whip passes through it will not come to us": the "whip" is Assyria, pictured as a flood and a lash at once. "We have made lies our refuge" is Isaiah putting the truth into their mouths. They would never have said it this way, but this is what their policy amounted to.
  • "Behold, I am the one who has laid as a foundation in Zion, a stone" (v16). God's answer to their false refuge is a true foundation. He is the builder. The stone is described four ways: "a tested stone" (BDB: "a tried stone, approved for use as a foundation-stone"), "a precious cornerstone," and "a sure foundation," literally "a founded foundation," doubly firm. A cornerstone was the large, carefully cut stone set at a corner to bind two walls and fix the line of the whole building.
  • "Whoever believes will not be in haste" (v16). "Believe" is 'aman, the root of "amen": to lean on as firm (7:9). "Be in haste" is chush, to hurry or rush. BDB suggests "hasten away (flee)" or "hasten about distractedly." The person who trusts this stone does not panic, run for Egypt, or scramble for a new deal. Henry: "He that believes will not make more haste than good speed, but be satisfied that God's time is the best time." The Greek Old Testament translated it "will not be put to shame," and the New Testament quotes it that way.
  • Who is the stone? Some have suggested Hezekiah, the temple, or God's promise to David. JFB rejects these and says the Spirit "plainly contemplated its fulfilment in Christ alone." The New Testament agrees. Paul combines this verse with 8:14 to say that Jesus is both the stone that saves those who trust him and the stone over which unbelievers stumble (Rom 9:33; 10:11). Peter calls Jesus "a living stone rejected by men but in the sight of God chosen and precious," quoting this verse alongside Psalm 118:22 (1 Pet 2:4–7). Paul also says Christ Jesus is the cornerstone of the church (Eph 2:20). Henry: "Jesus Christ is a foundation of God's laying."
  • "I will make justice the line, and righteousness the plumb line" (v17). Builders used a measuring line and a plumb line, a weight on a string, to check that walls were straight (Amos 7:7–8). God builds his house true. Every wall that is not built on the cornerstone will be measured and found crooked. "Hail will sweep away the refuge of lies" picks up the hail of v2.
  • "Your covenant with death will be annulled" (v18). Literally "covered over" or "wiped out." Their deal is void. When the scourge passes through, "you will be beaten down by it." "Morning by morning it will pass through, by day and by night" (v19). The flood will keep coming. "It will be sheer terror to understand the message." They mocked the message as childish (v9). When they finally grasp it, it will terrify them.
  • "The bed is too short to stretch oneself on, and the covering too narrow to wrap oneself in" (v20). This sounds like a proverb of the time, and it still works. Imagine trying to sleep in a bed too short with a blanket too small: legs hanging out, shoulders cold, no rest. Their treaties and schemes cannot cover them. Henry simply restates the proverb, and JFB says the point is that every source of their confidence will fail. Guzik contrasts it with the true rest offered in v12.
  • "The LORD will rise up as on Mount Perazim; as in the Valley of Gibeon he will be roused" (v21). At Baal-perazim God "broke through" the Philistines for David like a flood (2 Sam 5:20). In the Valley of Gibeon he hurled hailstones on the Amorites for Joshua and made the sun stand still (Josh 10:10–14). JFB thinks instead of David's second victory near Gibeon (1 Chr 14:16), while the Tyndale notes point to Joshua's. Either way, those were times God fought for Israel.
  • "Strange is his deed!… alien is his work!" (v21). Now he will fight with the same power against his own people. That is what makes it strange. God does not delight in judgment (Ezek 33:11; Lam 3:33). JFB: judgment "is not what God delights in." Henry: "It is a work that all the neighbours will stand amazed at."
  • "Do not scoff, lest your bonds be made strong" (v22). The more they mock, the tighter their chains get. The "decree of destruction" is already fixed.
  • "Give ear, and hear my voice" (v23). The tone shifts to that of a wisdom teacher. Isaiah closes with a parable from farming, the kind of thing every hearer knew well.
  • "Does he who plows for sowing plow continually?" (v24). No farmer plows forever. Plowing breaks the ground in preparation, and then he stops and plants. Dill, cumin, wheat, barley and emmer (v25) each go in their proper place. Dill and cumin were spice seeds. Wheat was sown "in rows." Emmer, an older hardy wheat, was planted around the edges. "His God teaches him" (v26): even ordinary farming know-how is God's gift.
  • "Dill is not threshed with a threshing sledge… but dill is beaten out with a stick" (v27). A threshing sledge was a heavy wooden board studded with sharp stones, dragged over grain. A cart wheel was rolled over grain to loosen it. Tiny seeds like dill and cumin would be crushed or lost that way, so farmers tapped them out with a stick. "Does one crush grain for bread? No, he does not thresh it forever" (v28). Even wheat is threshed only long enough to free the kernel, not to pulverize it.
  • The point of the parable. God knows what each of his people needs. His judgment has a purpose and a limit. He plows, but not forever. He threshes, but not to destroy. The aim is a harvest. JFB puts it this way: threshing is meant "to sever the moral chaff from the wheat, not to crush utterly." The Tyndale notes see degrees of judgment, and Guzik encourages believers not to despair in hard seasons, because God uses the right tool for each task.
  • "He is wonderful in counsel and excellent in wisdom" (v29). "Wonderful in counsel" uses the same two words as the name "Wonderful Counselor" (9:6). The scoffers thought they were the wise ones, cutting clever deals. The truly wise one is God.
  • Commentators: Henry calls v16 "a promise concerning Christ and his grace" set in the middle of warnings, and reads the farmer's parable widely: "If Christ is the true vine, his Father is the husbandman." Guzik identifies the cornerstone as the Messiah and draws comfort from the farmer: God's hard seasons are measured and purposeful. JFB insists the stone is Christ alone, and that the "strange work" is judgment God does not enjoy. All three agree that the scoffers' "covenant with death" could not stand, because no human arrangement can replace trust in God.

Pointing to Christ

The leaders of Jerusalem built their security on a treaty with Egypt and called it safety. It was a bed too short and a blanket too narrow. God's answer was a stone, tested, precious and sure, laid by his own hand. Peter says plainly who it is: Jesus, "rejected by men but in the sight of God chosen and precious" (1 Pet 2:4). Whoever trusts him does not need to scramble or panic, and will never be put to shame. The rest the scoffers refused, "This is rest; give rest to the weary," is the rest Jesus still offers.

Reflection

  • A Crown That Fades Fast (vv.1-4) — Ephraim's pride is compared to a beautiful flower that fades almost as soon as it blooms. What are you currently proud of that is, in reality, far more fragile and temporary than it feels?
  • A Covenant with Death (v.15) — Judah's leaders had made an alliance they believed would protect them, but Isaiah calls it a covenant with death and a pact with the grave. What agreement, relationship, or plan are you leaning on for security that can't actually save you?
  • A Sure and Unshaken Foundation (v.16) — God promises a tested, precious cornerstone in Zion, and says whoever builds on it will not panic or rush in fear. Is your life built on something that steadies you, or something that leaves you scrambling when pressure hits?
  • God's Strange, Reluctant Work (v.21) — Isaiah calls judgment God's "strange work," something unusual and not His preferred activity. Does it change how you view discipline in your life to know it costs God something to bring it?
  • Different Crops, Different Methods (vv.23-29) — The farmer parable shows a skilled farmer using different tools for different crops, all guided by God-given wisdom. Are you comparing your trials to someone else's instead of trusting that God is working with you according to what you specifically need?
