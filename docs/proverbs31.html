Summary

King Lemuel recounts the wisdom his mother taught him about the dangers of wine, injustice, and misused strength for a king, and the responsibility to defend the poor and needy (vv. 1-9). The chapter closes with an acrostic poem, each verse beginning with a successive Hebrew letter, praising a capable, God-fearing wife — valued far above jewels, industrious in trade and household management, generous to the poor, wise in speech, and honored by her husband and children (vv. 10-31). Verse 30 sets the poem's true standard: charm and beauty are fleeting, but a woman who fears the LORD is to be praised.

Authorship & Background

Author: Primarily Solomon (1:1, 10:1, 25:1), with sections by Agur (ch.30) and King Lemuel (ch.31). Compiled over several centuries. Proverbs is wisdom literature — practical instruction for godly living. Hebrew title: 'Mishlei' — 'Proverbs/Comparisons.'
Historical Context: Chapter 31 has two distinct sections by a unique author. It opens with "the words of king Lemuel, the prophecy that his mother taught him" (v.1). King Lemuel is otherwise unidentified — some traditions equate him with Solomon (his mother being Bathsheba), others with an Arabian or North Arabian king. The term "massa" (oracle/prophecy) in v.1 may also indicate he was king of Massa, an Ishmaelite territory (cf. Genesis 25:14). What is remarkable is that this is a mother's instruction to a king — a queen mother shaping her son's character and reign. Verses 1-9 contain her warnings against women, wine, and neglect of justice. Verses 10-31 form the famous "Virtuous Woman" acrostic poem — "eshet chayil" (woman of valor). This is an alphabetic acrostic: each of the 22 verses (10-31) begins with a successive letter of the Hebrew alphabet, from aleph to tav. The acrostic form indicates this is a carefully composed literary masterpiece — a comprehensive, A-to-Z portrait of feminine excellence. It is not a checklist of duties but a celebration of character. The poem crowns the entire book of Proverbs: wisdom, personified as a woman throughout chapters 1-9, now takes flesh as a real woman who embodies everything the book has taught.
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: the book's final chapter attributed entirely to a mother instructing her son the king, three times naming their relationship before a single piece of advice is given, what, my son, and what, the son of my womb, wine withheld specifically from a ruler who might forget the law and pervert judgment for the afflicted, an oracle that closes by reducing itself to advocacy, open thy mouth for the dumb, plead the cause of the poor and needy, and an acrostic poem describing a wife whose strength is explicitly commercial, she considereth a field, and buyeth it, closing the whole book on the very motto it opened with, but a woman that feareth the LORD, she shall be praised
A Mother's Oracle to a King (vv.1-2): The last chapter is attributed to a woman, the words of king Lemuel, the prophecy that his mother taught him, and it opens with the sound of somebody who has been waiting to speak: what, my son? and what, the son of my womb? and what, the son of my vows? Three times the relationship is named before any instruction is given, and the third names a vow, which suggests the child was asked for.
Give Not Thy Strength Unto Women (v.3): One verse, and the warning is put in terms of expenditure rather than morality, give not thy strength unto women, nor thy ways to that which destroyeth kings. The phrase that which destroyeth kings is doing the work: this is advice to a ruler about what has historically ended reigns, not general counsel about appetite.
It Is Not for Kings to Drink Wine (vv.4-7): The prohibition is occupational rather than absolute, and the reason is given plainly, lest they drink, and forget the law, and pervert the judgment of any of the afflicted. A judge who has been drinking is the specific danger. Then the verses that have made this passage awkward for temperance readers ever since: give strong drink unto him that is ready to perish, and wine unto those that be of heavy hearts, let him drink, and forget his poverty, and remember his misery no more. Wine is taken away from the man with power and offered to the man with nothing.
Plead the Cause of the Dumb (vv.8-9): The oracle ends in two verses of instruction that read as the point of the whole speech: open thy mouth for the dumb, in the cause of all such as are appointed to destruction, open thy mouth, judge righteously, and plead the cause of the poor and needy. The mouth that was told to refuse wine in the previous verses is told what to do instead. A mother's advice to a king reduces to advocacy for people who cannot speak for themselves.
Her Worth and Her Husband's Trust (vv.10-12): The poem that closes Proverbs is an acrostic, one verse for each of the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet, which is why it moves between topics without transitions. It opens with a question about scarcity, who can find a virtuous woman? for her price is far above rubies, and the phrase used of her is eshet chayil, where chayil is a military word for strength or capability. The husband's position is described in terms of confidence rather than authority, the heart of her husband doth safely trust in her, so that he shall have no need of spoil.
Her Industry and Enterprise (vv.13-19,24): The longest strand of the poem, and what it describes is a business. She seeketh wool and flax, she is like the merchants' ships, she riseth while it is yet night and giveth meat to her household. Then a transaction in her own name, she considereth a field, and buyeth it, with the profits of her hands she planteth a vineyard. Her arms are described as strong, she layeth her hands to the spindle, and the later verse in the same strand records her selling, she maketh fine linen and girdles, and delivereth them to the merchant. Nothing here is domestic in the sense of being confined to a house.
Her Generosity (v.20): One verse placed in the middle of the account of her trading, and the placing is the argument. She stretcheth out her hand to the poor, yea, she reacheth forth her hands to the needy. The same hands that hold the spindle and take the profit are described reaching outward, and the poem does not treat the two as separate virtues.
Her Preparation and Dignity (vv.21-23,25): Provision described as foresight rather than wealth, she is not afraid of the snow for her household, for all her household are clothed with scarlet. Then her own clothing, coverings of tapestry, silk and purple, which the poem states without apology. Her husband's standing in the gate is credited to her in the same strand. And the summary verse, strength and honour are her clothing, and she shall rejoice in time to come, which describes her wardrobe twice, once in fabric and once in character.
Her Wisdom and Kindness (vv.26-27): Two verses about speech and oversight. She openeth her mouth with wisdom, and in her tongue is the law of kindness, which is the only place in the poem she is described as teaching. Then she looketh well to the ways of her household, and eateth not the bread of idleness. The book that opened with a father's instruction ends by describing a woman whose speech carries law.
Her Praise and the Fear of the LORD (vv.28-31): The praise comes from inside the house first, her children arise up, and call her blessed, her husband also, and he praiseth her, and his words are quoted, many daughters have done virtuously, but thou excellest them all. Then the verse that reaches back to the motto of chapter 1 and closes the book on it, favour is deceitful, and beauty is vain, but a woman that feareth the LORD, she shall be praised. The last instruction is public rather than private, give her of the fruit of her hands, and let her own works praise her in the gates, which is the same city gate where wisdom was crying in chapter 1.

Setting: The last chapter of Proverbs has two parts. The first (vv1–9) is "the words of King Lemuel," advice his mother taught him about how to rule. Lemuel appears nowhere else, and no king of Israel or Judah had that name. His name means "belonging to God" or "devoted to God." Jewish tradition sometimes made him Solomon, with Bathsheba as the mother, but there is no evidence for that, and Guzik and the Tyndale notes both treat him as unknown. If "oracle" in v1 is read as the place name "Massa," as in 30:1, he was a king of that Arab tribe who knew the God of Israel. The second part (vv10–31) is a poem in praise of "an excellent wife." It is an acrostic: each of its twenty-two verses begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, from aleph to tav, a form that helped memory and signaled completeness, the whole subject "from A to Z" (compare Ps 119). The book that opened with Lady Wisdom calling in the streets (1:20–33) and building her house (9:1) closes with a woman whose home is built by wisdom, and the book's motto, "the fear of the LORD," returns in its second-to-last verse (v30; 1:7). The ESV sets the two parts apart, and the headings below follow its groupings.

A Mother's Counsel to a King (vv1–9)

  • "An oracle that his mother taught him" (v1). This is the only section of Proverbs credited to a woman's teaching. The book has urged sons to heed "your mother's teaching" (1:8; 6:20), and here is one. In the courts of the ancient Near East the queen mother often had real influence (1 Kgs 2:19; 15:13). Timothy's faith also came through his mother and grandmother (2 Tim 1:5), as Guzik notes. Henry: "When children are under the mother's eye, she has an opportunity of fashioning their minds aright."
  • "What are you doing, my son? What are you doing, son of my womb? What are you doing, son of my vows?" (v2). The Hebrew is just "What, my son? What, son of my womb? What, son of my vows?" It is the cry of a mother searching for words. JFB: "Repetitions denote earnestness." "Son of my vows" suggests she prayed for him and dedicated him to God, as Hannah vowed Samuel to the LORD before he was born (1 Sam 1:11). Her son is not just hers. He belongs to God.
  • "Do not give your strength to women, your ways to those who destroy kings" (v3). The first danger is sexual excess. Ancient kings collected wives and concubines as a sign of power, and the law warned Israel's king not to (Deut 17:17). David's adultery with Bathsheba brought disaster on his house (2 Sam 11–12). Solomon's many wives "turned away his heart after other gods" (1 Kgs 11:3–4). Guzik, with Waltke, uses both kings. "Strength" here means a man's vigor and resources, and "ways" his whole direction of life. The Tyndale notes: this repeats the earlier warnings in Proverbs 5–7.
  • "It is not for kings, O Lemuel, to drink wine… lest they drink and forget what has been decreed and pervert the rights of all the afflicted" (vv4–5). The second danger is drink. A drunk judge forgets the law and rules badly, and the poor pay for it. Isaiah condemned leaders "who are heroes at drinking wine… who acquit the guilty for a bribe" (Isa 5:22–23). Priests on duty were forbidden wine (Lev 10:9). JFB: stimulants "enfeeble reason, pervert the heart, and do not suit rulers." Henry: "All in authority should be more carefully temperate even than other men."
  • "Give strong drink to the one who is perishing, and wine to those in bitter distress" (vv6–7). The contrast is pointed. Wine is not for the king who needs a clear head, but for the dying and the crushed, to dull pain they can't escape. In the ancient world wine was a painkiller and sedative. Jewish tradition records that women of Jerusalem offered drugged wine to men being executed, which may be the "wine mixed with myrrh" offered to Jesus on the cross, which he refused (Mark 15:23). JFB: the proper use of such drinks is "to restore tone to feeble bodies and depressed minds." Guzik, passing on Garrett, insists this is not a free-drink program for the poor: the point is that the king's job is to keep his head and help those in misery, not to join them. Christians have held different views on alcohol. Scripture both enjoys wine as God's gift (Ps 104:15; John 2:1–11) and warns sharply against drunkenness (20:1; 23:29–35; Eph 5:18). Leaders, like Lemuel, are held to a higher standard (1 Tim 3:3).
  • "Open your mouth for the mute, for the rights of all who are destitute" (v8). Instead of drinking, speak up. "The mute" are those who can't speak for themselves, whether too poor, too weak or too frightened to be heard in court. "Destitute" is literally "sons of passing away," people on the way to ruin. "Open your mouth, judge righteously, defend the rights of the poor and needy" (v9). This is the heart of a good king's job (29:14; Ps 72:4). The Tyndale notes: instead of drinking, the king should defend those who can't protect themselves. Guzik, with Garrett, sees this as the mother's main political concern. JFB: "Plead for those who cannot plead for themselves."
  • Commentators: Guzik notes Lemuel is in no list of kings and sees no basis for making him Solomon. He takes "give your strength to women" as an obsession that drains a man's strength and self-respect, and says it applies to women as well. Henry: "Wine is to be used for want or medicine." He adds that rulers must be stricter with themselves than with others.

The Woman Who Fears the LORD (vv10–31)

  • "An excellent wife who can find?" (v10). "Excellent wife" is 'eshet chayil. Chayil means strength, ability, valor, and wealth. It is used of armies and of "mighty men of valor" (Judg 6:12). JFB: literally "of strength," that is, moral courage. The KJV's "virtuous woman" captures her goodness but loses her strength. The only other woman called 'eshet chayil in Scripture is Ruth, "a worthy woman" (Ruth 3:11). In the Hebrew Bible the book of Ruth comes right after Proverbs, so the poem's ideal is followed by a real woman. "Who can find?" means she is rare and precious, not that she doesn't exist. Finding her is God's gift: "He who finds a wife finds a good thing and obtains favor from the LORD" (18:22). "She is far more precious than jewels." The same is said of wisdom: "She is more precious than jewels" (3:15). The Tyndale notes draw the same link.
  • "The heart of her husband trusts in her, and he will have no lack of gain" (v11). Trust is the first thing said about the marriage. "Gain" is the word for spoil or plunder taken in battle, keeping up the military language of chayil. Under her care he comes out ahead. Henry: "She can be trusted, and he will leave such a wife to manage for him."
  • "She does him good, and not harm, all the days of her life" (v12). Not only at the start, when love is new, but over a lifetime. Compare the quarrelsome wife of 21:9 and 27:15.
  • "She seeks wool and flax, and works with willing hands" (v13). Wool for warm clothes, flax for linen. Making cloth was the great household industry of the ancient world. "Willing hands" means she delights in the work. JFB: "Ancient women of rank thus wrought with their hands."
  • "She is like the ships of the merchant; she brings her food from afar" (v14). She shops widely and well, like a trading ship bringing goods from distant ports (1 Kgs 10:22).
  • "She rises while it is yet night and provides food for her household and portions for her maidens" (v15). Before dawn she is up, making sure everyone, servants included, has food and a day's work. "Portions" may mean their share of food or their assigned tasks.
  • "She considers a field and buys it; with the fruit of her hands she plants a vineyard" (v16). She weighs the purchase carefully, then buys it from her own earnings and develops it. A vineyard took years to produce, so this is long-term thinking. The Tyndale notes: she takes part in business outside the home.
  • "She dresses herself with strength and makes her arms strong" (v17). Literally "she girds her loins with strength," tucking up her robe for hard work, the phrase used of a man readying himself for action (1 Kgs 18:46).
  • "She perceives that her merchandise is profitable. Her lamp does not go out at night" (v18). She knows the quality of her goods and works late. A lamp burning through the night was also a sign of a living, well-supplied household (compare Jer 25:10).
  • "She puts her hands to the distaff, and her hands hold the spindle" (v19). The distaff held raw fiber, and the spindle twisted it into thread. "She opens her hand to the poor and reaches out her hands to the needy" (v20). The two verses mirror each other in Hebrew: the same hands that spin thread reach out to the poor. Her industry funds her generosity. JFB: "Industry enables her to be charitable." The Tyndale notes: generosity is a mark of the wise (11:24–25; 22:9).
  • "She is not afraid of snow for her household, for all her household are clothed in scarlet" (v21). Snow does fall in the hill country of Israel. "Scarlet" means rich, warm, well-made clothing. The Greek and Latin translations read the same letters with different vowels as "double," meaning doubled garments, and Guzik passes on both. Either way, her family is ready for winter.
  • "She makes bed coverings for herself; her clothing is fine linen and purple" (v22). Fine Egyptian linen and purple dye, made from sea snails at great cost, were the dress of the wealthy. She is not careless about beauty. She simply puts it in its place (v30).
  • "Her husband is known in the gates when he sits among the elders of the land" (v23). The city gate was where elders heard cases and settled business (Ruth 4:1–11). Her husband is respected in public, partly because his home is well ordered. JFB: "His domestic comfort promotes his advancement in public dignity." The Tyndale notes say the same.
  • "She makes linen garments and sells them; she delivers sashes to the merchant" (v24). "Merchant" is literally "Canaanite," a word that had come to mean trader, since the Phoenicians, Canaanites of the coast, were the great traders of the age. She sells to the international market.
  • "Strength and dignity are her clothing, and she laughs at the time to come" (v25). Her real wardrobe is her character. "Laughs at the time to come" means she faces the future without fear, because she has prepared well and trusts God (v30). The Tyndale notes: she stays calm facing the unknown. Compare 3:25–26.
  • "She opens her mouth with wisdom, and the teaching of kindness is on her tongue" (v26). "Teaching" is torah, the same word as "your mother's teaching" (1:8). "Kindness" is chesed, loyal, steadfast love, the word for God's covenant faithfulness. Her instruction is wise and her tone is loyal love. JFB: her speech is "wise and gentle."
  • "She looks well to the ways of her household and does not eat the bread of idleness" (v27). "Looks well" is the word for a watchman on a wall. She keeps an eye on everything. Some scholars notice that this Hebrew word (tsophiyyah) sounds like the Greek sophia, "wisdom," and suggest a deliberate echo, but that is a guess. "The bread of idleness" is food not worked for (compare 2 Thess 3:10).
  • "Her children rise up and call her blessed; her husband also, and he praises her: 'Many women have done excellently, but you surpass them all'" (vv28–29). Those who know her best praise her most. "Done excellently" is again chayil. Guzik notes that "you surpass them all" can be literally true of only one woman, but every husband who loves his wife can say it. The Tyndale notes: the people closest to her speak of her character and work.
  • "Charm is deceitful, and beauty is vain, but a woman who fears the LORD is to be praised" (v30). Here is the key to the whole poem. "Charm" (chen) is outward grace and attractiveness. It can deceive. "Beauty" is "vain" (hevel), the word that runs through Ecclesiastes: breath, vapor, gone quickly. What lasts is the fear of the LORD, and this is where all her strength, skill and kindness come from. Proverbs began, "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (1:7), and it ends with a woman who embodies it. Peter makes the same point: not outward adornment but "the hidden person of the heart," precious to God (1 Pet 3:3–4). JFB: "True piety alone commands permanent respect and affection." Henry: "the fear of God reigning in the heart, is the beauty of the soul; it lasts for ever."
  • "Give her of the fruit of her hands, and let her works praise her in the gates" (v31). Let her share in what she has earned, and let her work be honored publicly in the same gates where her husband sits (v23). JFB: "The result of her labor is her best eulogy."
  • How to read the poem. Faithful Christians read it in more than one way, and the readings fit together. Many read it as a portrait of an ideal wife, with real women like Ruth showing it can be lived. Guzik calls it more a search list for a man than a checklist for a woman, and warns against using it to make women feel they fall short. It describes a lifetime's work, not one day's schedule. Others, noticing how closely she matches Lady Wisdom (3:15; 9:1–6), read her as wisdom pictured in a household: the book's last word is what life looks like when wisdom moves in. Henry also reads her as a picture of the church, made beautiful by grace. All agree she is strong, capable, generous and wise, and that the root of it all is the fear of the LORD.
  • Commentators: Guzik, with Ross and Waltke, highlights the military vocabulary, strength, valor, plunder, to show her virtues as hard-won. JFB notes the acrostic and that "who can find" implies such women are rare, "though not entirely wanting." Henry on v20: she is "intent upon giving as upon getting, and does it freely and cheerfully." On v25 Henry says she can look back without regret and "shall rejoice in a world to come." Guzik passes on Clarke's praise of Susanna Wesley, mother of John and Charles, as a living example.

Pointing to Christ

Lemuel's mother describes a king who keeps a clear head, refuses to waste himself, and opens his mouth for the voiceless and the poor (vv8–9). Jesus is that King. He refused the drugged wine on the cross (Mark 15:23) and bore the full suffering with a clear mind. He spoke for those who had no voice and "judged the poor with righteousness" (Isa 11:4). The excellent wife points to him as well. The book has pictured wisdom as a woman, and the New Testament shows that wisdom fulfilled in Christ, "the wisdom of God" (1 Cor 1:24). And the poem's picture of a bride, more precious than jewels, trusted by her husband, clothed in fine linen, praised in the gates, finds its last echo in the church, the bride Christ loved and gave himself for, "that he might present the church to himself in splendor" (Eph 5:25–27), clothed in "fine linen, bright and pure" (Rev 19:7–8).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes that the Proverbs 31 woman is not meant to produce guilt but to celebrate what godly womanhood looks like when the fear of the LORD is its foundation. He notes: "This is not a daily to-do list but a lifetime portrait. No woman does all these things in a single day — this is the accumulated testimony of a life lived in wisdom and faithfulness." On v.30, Guzik connects the acrostic to the book's central theme: "The entire book of Proverbs began with 'the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom' (1:7). It ends with a woman who fears the LORD and receives praise. The book's theology is embodied in a person — wisdom incarnate in a real life." On the queen mother's instruction (vv.1-9), he notes the influence of a godly mother on a future king: "The most powerful man in the nation was shaped by his mother's words."
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • A Mother's Warning to a King (vv.1-3) — Lemuel's mother warns him against giving his strength to women who ruin kings and against habits that cloud his judgment. What counsel from someone who loves you have you been tempted to brush aside because it wasn't what you wanted to hear?
  • Speaking Up for Those Who Can't (vv.8-9) — The king is told to open his mouth for the mute and defend the rights of the poor and needy. Where do you have influence you're not using on behalf of someone who has none?
  • A Trust That Doesn't Need Watching (vv.10-12) — This woman's worth is beyond jewels because her husband can trust her completely and she does him good, not harm, all her life. Are you building the kind of trustworthiness that lets the people who depend on you rest easy?
  • Generosity That Costs Something (v.20) — She opens her hand to the poor and reaches out to the needy as a regular part of her life, not an occasional afterthought. Is generosity something you plan for, or only something you do when it's convenient?
  • Laughing at the Future (v.25) — Clothed in strength and dignity, she can face what's coming without fear because of how she has lived and prepared. What would it take for you to face your own uncertain future with that kind of confidence instead of anxiety?
  • What Actually Endures (v.30) — Charm and beauty are called deceptive and fleeting, but a woman who fears the LORD is the one who is truly praised. Are you investing in what fades or in what will still matter decades from now?
