1Now there was a man of the Pharisees named Nicodemus, a leader of the Jews.

2He came to Jesus at night and said, “Rabbi, we know that You are a teacher who has come from God. For no one could perform the signs You are doing if God were not with him.”

3Jesus replied, “Truly, truly, I tell you, no one can see the kingdom of God unless he is born again.”

4“How can a man be born when he is old?” Nicodemus asked. “Can he enter his mother’s womb a second time to be born?”

5Jesus answered, “Truly, truly, I tell you, no one can enter the kingdom of God unless he is born of water and the Spirit.

6Flesh is born of flesh, but spirit is born of the Spirit.

7Do not be amazed that I said, ‘You must be born again.’

8The wind blows where it wishes. You hear its sound, but you do not know where it comes from or where it is going. So it is with everyone born of the Spirit.”

9“How can this be?” Nicodemus asked.

10“You are Israel’s teacher,” said Jesus, “and you do not understand these things?

11Truly, truly, I tell you, we speak of what we know, and we testify to what we have seen, and yet you people do not accept our testimony.

12If I have told you about earthly things and you do not believe, how will you believe if I tell you about heavenly things?

13No one has ascended into heaven except the One who descended from heaven—the Son of Man.

14Just as Moses lifted up the snake in the wilderness, so the Son of Man must be lifted up,

15that everyone who believes in Him may have eternal life.

16For God so loved the world that He gave His one and only Son, that everyone who believes in Him shall not perish but have eternal life.

17For God did not send His Son into the world to condemn the world, but to save the world through Him.

18Whoever believes in Him is not condemned, but whoever does not believe has already been condemned, because he has not believed in the name of God’s one and only Son.

19And this is the verdict: The Light has come into the world, but men loved the darkness rather than the Light because their deeds were evil.

20Everyone who does evil hates the Light, and does not come into the Light for fear that his deeds will be exposed.

21But whoever practices the truth comes into the Light, so that it may be seen clearly that what he has done has been accomplished in God.”

22After this, Jesus and His disciples went into the Judean countryside, where He spent some time with them and baptized.

23Now John was also baptizing at Aenon near Salim, because the water was plentiful there, and people kept coming to be baptized.

24(For John had not yet been thrown into prison.)

25Then a dispute arose between John’s disciples and a certain Jew over the issue of ceremonial washing.

26So John’s disciples came to him and said, “Look, Rabbi, the One who was with you beyond the Jordan, the One you testified about—He is baptizing, and everyone is going to Him.”

27John replied, “A man can receive only what is given him from heaven.

28You yourselves can testify that I said, ‘I am not the Christ, but am sent ahead of Him.’

29The bride belongs to the bridegroom. The friend of the bridegroom stands and listens for him, and is overjoyed to hear the bridegroom’s voice. That joy is mine, and it is now complete.

30He must increase; I must decrease.

31The One who comes from above is above all. The one who is from the earth belongs to the earth and speaks as one from the earth. The One who comes from heaven is above all.

32He testifies to what He has seen and heard, yet no one accepts His testimony.

33Whoever accepts His testimony has certified that God is truthful.

34For the One whom God has sent speaks the words of God, for God gives the Spirit without limit.

35The Father loves the Son and has placed all things in His hands.

36Whoever believes in the Son has eternal life. Whoever rejects the Son will not see life. Instead, the wrath of God remains on him.”

Summary

Jesus tells the Pharisee Nicodemus that no one can see or enter the kingdom of God without being born again — born of water and the Spirit, not merely of the flesh (vv. 3-7). He compares the Spirit's work to the wind, unseen but unmistakable in its effects (v. 8), then explains that just as Moses lifted up the bronze serpent in the wilderness, the Son of Man must be lifted up so that everyone who believes in Him receives eternal life (vv. 14-15). Verses 16-17 summarize the gospel: God sent His Son not to condemn the world but to save it, out of love for the world. The chapter closes with John the Baptist's disciples growing jealous of Jesus' rising popularity, and John responding that his own role must now diminish as Jesus' increases (vv. 25-30).

Authorship & Background

Author: The Apostle John (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Classification: Gospel — Narrative and Discourse
Key Themes: A ruler who comes at night, birth described as something that happens to a person, a bronze serpent read as a foreshadowing, the most quoted verse in Scripture, and a forerunner content to decrease
Historical Context: Chapter 3 contains one of the most famous conversations in Scripture — Jesus and Nicodemus — and one of the most quoted verses in the Bible (John 3:16). Nicodemus was a Pharisee and a member of the Sanhedrin, the Jewish ruling council of 70 men. He came to Jesus at night, likely to avoid public scrutiny from his peers. The chapter also records John the Baptist's final testimony about Jesus, including his famous statement "He must increase, but I must decrease" (v.30) — a model of humility and God-centered purpose.
Nicodemus Comes by Night (vv.1-8): A Pharisee and a member of the Sanhedrin, the council of seventy, comes at night, which the Gospel mentions without explaining and most readers take as caution. He opens with a compliment and gets an answer to a question he did not ask: except a man be born again he cannot see the kingdom of God. The Greek can mean again or from above and probably means both. Nicodemus's literal objection about entering the womb is answered with wind — you hear it and cannot tell where it comes from.
The Serpent Lifted Up (vv.9-15): How can these things be, asks a man whose profession was knowing such things, and the reply is pointed: art thou a master of Israel and knowest not these things? Then the image from Numbers 21 — as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of man be lifted up. Israel looked at a bronze snake on a pole and lived. Lifted up becomes this Gospel's word for the crucifixion.
For God So Loved the World (vv.16-21): The best-known verse in Scripture sits in a paragraph most people do not read past. God's motive is stated as love and the scope as the world, in a Gospel written to a community inclined to draw boundaries. Verse 17 rules out condemnation as the purpose; v.18 states that judgment is already settled by response. Then the reason given for unbelief is moral rather than intellectual: men loved darkness because their deeds were evil, and hate the light lest their deeds be reproved.
John's Final Testimony (vv.22-30): John's disciples come to him with a complaint that amounts to losing market share, and his answer refuses the premise. He calls himself the friend of the bridegroom who rejoices to hear his voice, which is a role with no ambition attached to it. Verse 30 is his last recorded word in this Gospel and the most quoted line about humility in it: he must increase, but I must decrease.
He That Cometh from Above (vv.31-36): Whether these verses are John the Baptist still speaking or the writer's own commentary is genuinely unclear, and translations differ on where the quotation ends. Either way the content escalates — he that cometh from above is above all, and God giveth not the Spirit by measure. The chapter closes with the two outcomes stated plainly, everlasting life for the believer and the wrath of God abiding on the one who does not.

Setting: Jesus is still in Jerusalem at his first Passover after beginning his public ministry, about AD 27/28. He has just cleared the temple courts and done signs that drew a crowd of shallow believers, and John has said that Jesus "knew what was in man" (2:25). Chapter 3 shows that knowledge at work with one man, a respected teacher who comes at night. The conversation with Nicodemus is the first long teaching passage in John, and it contains the best-known verse in the Bible. The chapter then moves to the Jordan valley, where John the Baptist gives his last recorded testimony to Jesus before his arrest.

You Must Be Born Again (vv1–15)

  • "A man of the Pharisees named Nicodemus" (v1). "Man" (anthrōpos) picks up the last word of chapter 2: Jesus "knew what was in man," and now here is a man. The Pharisees were a lay movement devoted to keeping God's law in detail, including the oral traditions around it. They were respected by ordinary people. Nicodemus is a Greek name, common among Jews of the time. "A ruler of the Jews" means he sat on the Sanhedrin, the seventy-one-member council in Jerusalem (compare 7:50). He was learned, devout and influential, the best Israel's religion could produce.
  • "Came to Jesus by night" (v2). He may have wanted a quiet conversation away from the crowds, or he may have feared being seen with Jesus. Rabbis did sometimes study late into the night. But in John, night is never just a time of day. It stands for darkness and unbelief (9:4; 11:10; 13:30: "And it was night"). Nicodemus comes out of the dark toward the light (3:19–21). John mentions "by night" again when he appears later (19:39), and by then he is honoring Jesus' body in public.
  • "Rabbi, we know that you are a teacher come from God" (v2). He speaks politely, teacher to teacher, and perhaps for a group ("we"). His reasoning is that signs prove God is with someone. That is a good start but not enough: John has just shown that faith built on signs alone does not go deep (2:23–25).
  • "Unless one is born again he cannot see the kingdom of God" (v3). Jesus skips the compliments and goes to the heart. "Again" is anōthen, which can mean "from above" or "again, anew." The lexicon gives both senses and notes that most read "anew" here, though John uses the same word for "from above" in 3:31 and 19:11. John probably means both at once: a second birth that comes from God above. "Truly, truly" (amēn amēn) is Jesus' solemn formula, used only in John and always doubled there, putting his own authority behind what follows.
  • "The kingdom of God" (v3). This is one of only two places John uses the phrase (vv3, 5), so common in the other Gospels. Every Jew expected the kingdom when the Messiah came. Pharisees assumed that faithful Israelites, and especially careful law-keepers, would enter it. Jesus tells a leading Pharisee he cannot even "see" it without a new birth.
  • "Can he enter a second time into his mother's womb?" (v4). Nicodemus takes it literally, maybe deliberately to show how impossible it sounds. Misunderstanding is a pattern in John: the Jews and the temple (2:20), the Samaritan woman and the water (4:11), the crowd and the bread (6:52). Each time, the misunderstanding opens the way to deeper teaching. His question also carries real weight: how can a grown man, set in his ways, start over?
  • "Born of water and the Spirit" (v5). This is the most debated phrase in the chapter. The main views: (1) Christian baptism, the reading of most of the early church and of Catholic, Orthodox and Lutheran traditions; (2) physical birth ("water" as the waters of the womb) followed by spiritual birth; (3) a single cleansing birth, where water and Spirit go together, drawn from Ezekiel 36:25–27: "I will sprinkle clean water on you… And I will put my Spirit within you." Since Jesus expects Nicodemus to know this already (v10), the third view has strong support: a teacher of Israel should have known Ezekiel's promise of a new heart and a cleansing that only God can give. In Greek, one preposition governs both nouns ("of water and Spirit"), which suggests one event with two sides. John the Baptist's water baptism, which Nicodemus would have known about, pointed toward the same thing (1:33).
  • "That which is born of the flesh is flesh" (v6). "Flesh" here is not sinful flesh in Paul's sense but human nature on its own. Like produces like. Human effort, ancestry or religious training can only produce more human life. Only the Spirit gives spiritual life. Being born a Jew, or into any religious family, does not make anyone a child of God (1:12–13).
  • "The wind blows where it wishes" (v8). The same Greek word, pneuma, means both "wind" and "spirit," as does Hebrew ruach. Ezekiel's valley of dry bones uses the same play (Ezek 37:9–14). The wind can't be controlled, scheduled or seen, but its effects are unmistakable. So it is with the Spirit's work in a person. Picture the two men sitting on a Jerusalem rooftop at night, hearing the wind move through the streets.
  • "How can these things be?" (v9). These are Nicodemus's last words in the conversation. He fades out, and Jesus does the talking.
  • "Are you the teacher of Israel?" (v10). The article is there in Greek: "the teacher." Nicodemus may have been a well-known teacher. Jesus' surprise is that someone who knew the Scriptures so well missed what the prophets said about a new heart and God's Spirit (Ezek 36:26–27; Jer 31:33; Deut 30:6).
  • "We speak of what we know" (v11). Jesus answers Nicodemus's "we know" (v2) with his own "we." He may be including his disciples, or using a plural of solemn testimony, or echoing John's own community of witnesses. The point is that Jesus speaks as an eyewitness of heaven, not as a scholar repeating others.
  • "Earthly things… heavenly things" (v12). The new birth happens on earth, in real people, and it is illustrated by things like birth and wind. If Nicodemus stumbles at that, he is not ready for the deeper realities of heaven: who the Son is, and what he came to do.
  • "No one has ascended into heaven except he who descended from heaven, the Son of Man" (v13). Jewish writings told stories of great men, Moses or Enoch, going up to heaven to receive secrets. Jesus says no one has gone up to bring heaven's knowledge down. Instead, the one from heaven has come down. "Son of Man" is Jesus' favorite title for himself, drawn from Daniel 7:13–14, where "one like a son of man" comes before God and receives everlasting rule. Some later manuscripts add "who is in heaven," which the KJV includes. The ESV leaves it out, following the earliest manuscripts.
  • "As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness" (v14). In Numbers 21:4–9, Israel grumbled against God and was bitten by venomous snakes. God told Moses to make a bronze serpent and set it on a pole, and anyone who looked at it lived. The comparison is precise. The cure looked like the curse. The people were saved by looking, not working. And it was lifted up where all could see. Centuries later, Israel turned the serpent itself into an idol and King Hezekiah had to destroy it (2 Kings 18:4).
  • "So must the Son of Man be lifted up" (v14). "Lifted up" (hypsoō) is a double meaning that John repeats (8:28; 12:32–34). It means raised physically on the cross and also exalted in glory. John 12:33 makes it explicit: "He said this to show by what kind of death he was going to die." The cross, which looked like Jesus' lowest moment, is the moment of his glory. "Must" shows divine necessity: this is the plan, not an accident.
  • "Eternal life" (v15). This is John's first use of the phrase, which appears seventeen times in his Gospel. It means more than living forever. It is the life of the age to come, God's own life, which begins now for those who believe (17:3; 5:24).
  • Commentators: Guzik, Henry and JFB all read Nicodemus as sincere but hesitant. JFB says that in him "sincerity and timidity are seen struggling together," and Henry allows both prudence and fear in the night visit, noting that Christ still "bade him welcome." On anōthen, Guzik and Henry take both senses as intended: born again, and born from above. On "water and the Spirit," Guzik surveys five readings and leans toward Ezekiel 36. JFB also points to Ezekiel 36:25–27 and calls it "a thorough spiritual purification by the operation of the Holy Ghost." Henry reads water as cleansing and says the outward sign of baptism points to this inward renewal, though baptism by itself does not save. On v10, JFB says regeneration is "so far disclosed in the Old Testament that Nicodemus was culpable in being ignorant of it." On v14, JFB notes that in both cases "the remedy is conspicuously displayed; in the one case on a pole, in the other on the cross." Guzik adds that bronze in the Old Testament is often linked with judgment, so the serpent pictures sin judged. That is a common reading, though the text itself does not draw it out.

For God So Loved the World (vv16–21)

  • Who is speaking? Greek manuscripts had no quotation marks, so translators must decide where Jesus' words to Nicodemus stop. The ESV and many others keep vv16–21 as Jesus' words. Others, including the Tyndale notes as one option, read them as John reflecting on the conversation, as he seems to do again in vv31–36. Either way, the verses carry the same authority and summarize the chapter.
  • "For God so loved the world" (v16). "So" (houtōs) means "in this way" more than "so much." God loved the world in this manner: he gave. "The world" (kosmos) in John is usually humanity in rebellion against God (1:10; 7:7; 15:18). That is what makes the verse astonishing. God loves not a deserving world but a hostile one, and not Israel only but people from every nation. Many Jews of the time saw the Gentile world as headed for judgment, so this would have stunned Nicodemus.
  • "He gave his only Son" (v16). "Only" is monogenēs. The KJV's "only begotten" comes from an older view that the word is built from gennaō, "beget." The lexicon gives "only" and "unique," and most scholars now trace it to genos, "kind." The idea is the one-of-a-kind Son. In the Greek Old Testament it translates Hebrew yachid, "only one," the word used of Isaac in Genesis 22:2 ("your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love"). Hebrews 11:17 uses monogenēs of Isaac too. The echo is hard to miss: the Father gives what Abraham was spared from giving.
  • "Whoever believes in him should not perish" (v16). "Believes in" (pisteuō eis) is John's characteristic phrase, literally "believes into": trust that goes toward and rests on a person, not just agreement that something is true. "Perish" is the opposite of eternal life, final ruin. John never uses the noun "faith" in his Gospel. He always uses the verb. Faith is something one does.
  • "Not… to condemn the world, but… that the world might be saved" (v17). Jews expected the Messiah to come in judgment on the nations. Jesus' first coming is a rescue. Judgment is real, but it is not the purpose of his coming (12:47).
  • "Condemned already" (v18). The world is not neutral, waiting to be sorted out. It already stands under judgment. Belief moves a person out from under it: "Whoever hears my word and believes… does not come into judgment, but has passed from death to life" (5:24). Unbelief leaves a person where they already are. "The name" means the person himself, as he has revealed himself.
  • "People loved the darkness rather than the light" (v19). Here is the judgment, and it is self-chosen. Light came into the world (1:4–9), and people preferred darkness. The reason given is moral, not intellectual: "because their works were evil." Unbelief is often less about lack of evidence than a refusal to be exposed.
  • "Lest his works should be exposed" (v20). "Exposed" (elenchō) means to bring to light and convict. It is the word Jesus later uses for the Spirit convicting the world of sin (16:8).
  • "Whoever does what is true comes to the light" (v21). "Does what is true" is a Hebrew way of speaking: to live faithfully and honestly, as in Nehemiah 9:33. Such a person isn't afraid of the light, because what they do has been "carried out in God," done in dependence on him. This may describe Nicodemus himself, who has at least come toward the light.
  • Commentators: Henry calls God's love here "so really, so richly" and stresses that it reaches the whole world, Gentiles included, with an offer of pardon open to all who believe. JFB calls v16 the most famous summary of the gospel and draws out "the immense 'Love of God' to that perishing world." Guzik notes how revolutionary it was to say God loved the world and not only Israel, and he defines believing as trusting in, relying on and clinging to Christ, not bare agreement. On v19, Henry says "Wilful ignorance is so far from excusing sin" that it adds to guilt, and Guzik traces unbelief to the same moral root. On v21, JFB describes the one who "doeth truth" as someone whose "only object in life is to be and do what will bear the light."

John the Baptist Exalts Christ (vv22–36)

  • "Into the Judean countryside… and was baptizing" (v22). Jesus leaves the city for the rural parts of Judea. John 4:2 clarifies that his disciples did the baptizing. This was probably still a baptism of repentance like John's, before Christian baptism into Jesus' name was given after the resurrection (Matt 28:19).
  • "Aenon near Salim, because water was plentiful there" (v23). Aenon comes from the word for "springs." Its location is uncertain. One tradition places it in the Jordan valley about eight miles south of Beth-shan, and another near Shechem in Samaria. "Plentiful water" (literally "many waters") suggests springs deep enough for immersion.
  • "For John had not yet been put in prison" (v24). This aside shows that John assumes his readers know the other Gospels, which start Jesus' Galilean ministry after John's arrest (Mark 1:14). John fills in an earlier period when the two ministries overlapped.
  • "A discussion arose… over purification" (v25). "Purification" links back to the stone jars at Cana (2:6). Jews had many washings, and here were two teachers baptizing at once. Which washing counted? The best manuscripts read "a Jew" (singular). Some read "the Jews."
  • "He… to whom you bore witness… all are going to him" (v26). John's disciples are jealous for their master. They don't even use Jesus' name. "All" is an exaggeration born of anxiety.
  • "A person cannot receive even one thing unless it is given him from heaven" (v27). John's answer is a principle that ends all rivalry. Every role and every success is a gift. If people go to Jesus, it is because heaven has given them to him.
  • "I am not the Christ" (v28). John repeats what he said at the start (1:20). He never claimed more than he was.
  • "The friend of the bridegroom… rejoices greatly at the bridegroom's voice" (v29). In a Jewish wedding, the groom's friend, something like a best man, helped arrange the marriage and the feast. His joy was to bring the bride and groom together and then step back. The Old Testament pictures God as Israel's husband (Isa 54:5; 62:4–5; Hos 2:16–20). By calling Jesus the bridegroom, John is quietly placing him in God's role. Jesus uses the same picture of himself (Mark 2:19–20), and Scripture ends with the wedding feast of the Lamb (Rev 19:7–9). "This joy of mine is now complete" is John's answer to his disciples' worry: losing followers to Jesus is not a loss but the fulfillment of his joy.
  • "He must increase, but I must decrease" (v30). These are John the Baptist's last words in this Gospel. "Must" (dei) is the same word used of the Son of Man being lifted up (v14). The same divine plan that sends Jesus to the cross calls John to step aside. It is one of the clearest pictures of humility in Scripture.
  • Who speaks in vv31–36? As with vv16–21, it is not certain whether John the Baptist keeps speaking or the Gospel writer reflects on his testimony. The language sounds much like the Gospel writer's own (compare vv11–13), and many modern scholars read it as his commentary. Others, including Matthew Henry and JFB, take it as the Baptist's words.
  • "He who comes from above is above all" (v31). "From above" is anōthen, the same word as "born again" in v3. The one who comes from above gives the birth from above. John the Baptist, great as he was, was "of the earth" and spoke as a man. Jesus speaks from firsthand knowledge of heaven.
  • "No one receives his testimony" (v32). This is a broad statement, softened at once by v33: some do receive it. It echoes 1:11–12. The general response is rejection, but not every person's.
  • "Sets his seal to this, that God is true" (v33). A seal was a mark pressed into wax that certified a document. Believing Jesus is a person's own stamp of agreement that God tells the truth. To reject Jesus is to call God a liar (1 John 5:10).
  • "He gives the Spirit without measure" (v34). The prophets received the Spirit for particular tasks. Jesus has the Spirit fully and permanently (1:32–33; Isa 11:2). Grammatically, "he" could be God giving the Spirit to the Son, or the Son giving the Spirit to others. Both are true in John (7:39; 20:22), but the context of v35 suggests the Father giving to the Son.
  • "The Father loves the Son and has given all things into his hand" (v35). "Loves" here is agapaō. In 5:20 John uses phileō for the same love. The two verbs overlap in John and should not be pressed apart. The Father's love is the reason for the Son's authority (13:3; 17:2).
  • "Whoever believes in the Son has eternal life" (v36). "Has" is present tense: eternal life is a present possession. The opposite of believing is not just "not believing" but "does not obey" (apeitheō). In John, unbelief is not neutral doubt but a refusal.
  • "The wrath of God remains on him" (v36). This is the only place John's Gospel uses the word "wrath." It is God's settled opposition to sin. "Remains" (menei) is present tense: wrath is not a new punishment added later but a condition already in place, which stays unless it is removed. The chapter that began with new birth ends with the alternative.
  • Commentators: Henry calls the disciples' complaint a sign of a recurring problem: "Aiming at the monopoly of honour and respect has been in all ages the bane of the church." On v22 he says the disciples' baptizing "was his baptizing." JFB captures John's attitude: "Enough for me to be the Bridegroom's friend." Guzik notes that the bridegroom picture, used of God in the Old Testament, implies Jesus' deity, and he points out four "musts" in the chapter: the new birth (v7), the cross (v14), Christ's increase and John's decrease (v30). On v34 Henry writes, "The Spirit was not in Christ as in a vessel, but as in a fountain," and JFB says it means "the entire fulness of divine life and divine power." On v36 JFB says God's wrath "was on Him before," and not being removed "in the only possible way," it "necessarily remaineth on him." Guzik also cites the difference between agapaō here and phileō in 5:20, though in John the two words largely overlap.

Pointing to Christ

This chapter is all about where life comes from. Religion, learning and ancestry cannot produce it, and neither can a great prophet like John. Life comes "from above," and so does the one who gives it. The Son who descended from heaven was lifted up on the cross, like the bronze serpent on its pole, so that anyone who looks to him in faith may live. God did not love a world that deserved it. He loved a world in the dark and gave his one and only Son for it. John the Baptist shows the right response: to rejoice at the bridegroom's voice and gladly step aside, so that Christ increases. Nicodemus's own story, from coming by night here to standing at the tomb in 19:39, shows that the Spirit's wind does blow, quietly, in surprising places.

Reflection

  • Born Again (v.3) — Jesus told a religious expert that his knowledge and moral effort couldn't get him into the kingdom — he needed an entirely new birth. Have you experienced that kind of spiritual transformation, or are you relying on your own goodness?
  • The Spirit's Unseen Work (v.8) — Jesus compares the new birth to wind: invisible, but unmistakable in its effects. What evidence of the Spirit's work would someone else see in your life right now?
  • Lifted Up to Heal (v.14) — Just as those bitten by snakes in the wilderness were healed by looking at the bronze serpent Moses raised, Jesus says He too must be lifted up so people can be saved by looking to Him. Is looking to Christ a one-time decision for you, or an ongoing posture?
  • The Motive Behind the Cross (v.16) — This may be the most familiar verse in the Bible, but its claim is anything but ordinary: God so loved that He gave His only Son. What does it mean to you personally that this gift was for you by name?
  • Loving Darkness Over Light (v.19) — People stay in darkness not from ignorance but because they don't want their deeds exposed. Is there an area of your life you're keeping hidden because you don't want it brought into the light?
  • He Must Increase (v.30) — John the Baptist's response to losing followers to Jesus was joy, not jealousy, because he knew his purpose was to point to Christ, not build his own name. Where in your life — career, family, reputation — are you being asked to decrease so He can increase?
