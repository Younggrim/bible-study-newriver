Summary

Nahum opens with a hymn describing the LORD as jealous and avenging toward his enemies, yet slow to anger and immense in power, marching through the earth in whirlwind and storm (vv. 2-3). Creation itself reacts to his presence — seas dry up, mountains quake, and no one can withstand his fierce anger (vv. 4-6). In contrast, the LORD is good, a refuge in times of trouble for those who trust him (v. 7), even as he vows complete destruction on his adversaries so they will never trouble Judah again (vv. 8-10). The chapter turns to address Nineveh directly, denouncing the wicked plotter who schemes against the LORD and pronouncing an end to that nation's name, idols, and power (vv. 11-14). It closes by announcing good news of peace for Judah, calling the people to keep their festivals now that the wicked oppressor is cut off (v. 15).

Authorship & Background

Author: Nahum the Elkoshite. Prophesied approximately 663-612 BC. Key theme: God's judgment on Nineveh/Assyria — the city that repented under Jonah has returned to wickedness. God is both 'slow to anger' and one who 'will not at all acquit the wicked' (1:3).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — The Character of the Avenging God
Key Themes: God's jealous wrath against oppressors, theophanic power over nature, the goodness of God as refuge, the utter destruction of those who oppose the LORD
Historical Context: Nahum prophesied after the fall of Thebes (No-Amon) in 663 BC (referenced in 3:8) and before Nineveh's destruction in 612 BC. Assyria had been the dominant world power for over a century, terrorizing nations with unprecedented cruelty — impaling captives, skinning enemies alive, and deporting entire populations. They had destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel in 722 BC and besieged Jerusalem under Sennacherib in 701 BC. About 150 years earlier, Nineveh had repented under Jonah's preaching — but that repentance was short-lived. The city returned to violence, idolatry, and oppression of God's people. Nahum's prophecy announces that God's patience has reached its end. The book is "comfort" (Nahum means "comfort/consolation") for Judah: the oppressor will fall. Chapter 1 functions as the theological foundation for the entire book. Before describing how Nineveh will fall (chs. 2-3), Nahum establishes who is bringing the judgment — a God who is both terrifyingly powerful and tenderly good to those who trust Him.
The Burden Against Nineveh (v.1): The book is titled twice over, “the burden of Nineveh” and “the book of the vision of Nahum the Elkoshite”. “Burden” is the prophetic term for an oracle of weight, usually against a foreign power. That it is also called a book suggests something composed to be read rather than only delivered, which suits the tight poetic construction of what follows.
A Hymn to the Avenging God (vv.2-6): The book opens not with Nineveh but with God's character, and the first word of the poem is jealous. Slow to anger is set inside the same sentence as great in power and one who will not acquit the guilty, so patience is presented as restraint rather than tolerance. The imagery is storm and earthquake, the sea dried, Bashan and Carmel withering, and it ends on a question — who can stand? — which the rest of the book answers with a name.
Good to Those Who Trust Him, an End to the Rest (vv.7-11): Verse 7 is the hinge of the chapter and the reason it is not merely fierce: “The LORD is good, a strong hold in the day of trouble; and he knoweth them that trust in him.” The same power that dries the sea is a refuge. What follows turns to Nineveh with an overrunning flood and pursuit into darkness, and v.11 names the offense as counsel devised against the LORD — the city's policy, not merely its armies.
A Decree Against the Dynasty (vv.12-14): Judah is addressed directly with a promise of relief: though Assyria is many, it will be cut down, and “I will break his yoke from off thee”. Assyria had taken tribute from Judah for generations, so the yoke was literal. The decree in v.14 is aimed at the Assyrian king's line, cutting off name and posterity and cutting the idols out of the temple, which strikes at the two things such a king relied on to outlast his own death.
Good Tidings on the Mountains (v.15): The chapter closes with a runner on the hills carrying good news and publishing peace, the same line Isaiah 52:7 applies to the return from exile and Paul later applies to the gospel. Here the good news is specific and grim in its cause: the wicked one shall no more pass through. Judah is told to keep its feasts and pay its vows, ordinary religious life resumed, which for an occupied people is the practical shape of deliverance.

Setting: Nahum prophesied in Judah sometime between 663 BC and 612 BC. The earlier date is fixed by 3:8–10, which looks back on the fall of Thebes in Egypt to the Assyrians in 663 BC. The later is the fall of Nineveh itself, which Nahum describes as still to come. Nineveh, on the Tigris River opposite modern Mosul in northern Iraq, was the capital of Assyria, the most feared empire the world had yet seen. Assyria had destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel in 722 BC, ravaged Judah under Sennacherib in 701 BC (2 Kgs 18–19), and kept Judah as a tribute-paying vassal for much of the following century. Its kings boasted in their inscriptions of skinning rebels and piling up heads. About a century before Nahum, Jonah had preached in Nineveh and the city repented (Jonah 3). Nahum is the sequel. The repentance did not last, and now the God who spared Nineveh announces its end. For Judah, living under the Assyrian boot, the book is exactly what the prophet's name means: Nahum, "comfort."

God's Wrath Against Nineveh (vv1–15)

  • "An oracle concerning Nineveh" (v1). The Hebrew word massa' comes from the verb "to lift" or "to carry." It can mean a "burden," as the KJV has it, a heavy message the prophet must carry, or an "oracle," something lifted up and proclaimed. The ESV takes the second. It is the usual heading for prophecies against nations (Isa 13:1; 15:1; Hab 1:1).
  • "The book of the vision of Nahum of Elkosh" (v1). This is the only prophetic book that calls itself a "book" in its opening line, which suggests it was written down from the start rather than collected from sermons. "Vision" means what the prophet was given to see, not only to hear. Where Elkosh was is unknown. One tradition places it at Alqosh near Mosul, where a tomb is still shown as Nahum's. Jerome, around AD 400, placed it in Galilee. Others place it in southern Judah. Some have connected Capernaum, which means "village of Nahum," with the prophet, but that is a guess.
  • The shape of the poem (vv2–8). These verses form a partial acrostic, a poem in which each line begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, running from aleph to about kaph, the first half of the alphabet, with some letters out of place. Scholars debate how deliberate it is. If it is intentional, it gives a sense of completeness, like saying "God's wrath from A to Z."
  • "The LORD is a jealous and avenging God" (v2). The word for "jealous," qanno', is used in the Old Testament only of God (Josh 24:19; compare the closely related qanna', Exod 20:5; 34:14). It is not petty envy. It is the zeal of a husband for his marriage, or of a king for his people. "Avenging" comes three times in this one verse, an emphasis English cannot easily carry. The Hebrew word naqam does not mean personal spite. It is the legal act of setting wrongs right, which the law forbids individuals to take into their own hands because it belongs to God (Deut 32:35; Rom 12:19). The Tyndale notes stress that God's vengeance flows from his holiness, justice and faithfulness to his covenant, never from random anger.
  • "The LORD is slow to anger and great in power, and the LORD will by no means clear the guilty" (v3). This is a deliberate echo of God's self-description to Moses at Sinai: "merciful and gracious, slow to anger… but who will by no means clear the guilty" (Exod 34:6–7). Jonah quoted the first half of that same text when he complained that God was too kind to Nineveh (Jonah 4:2). Nahum quotes the second half. The two books hold the same God's character from two sides: patience long enough to spare a repentant city, and justice that does not leave guilt unanswered forever. "Slow to anger" is not weakness, Nahum adds at once. It sits beside "great in power."
  • "His way is in whirlwind and storm, and the clouds are the dust of his feet" (v3). The God of Israel walks on storm clouds the way a traveler kicks up dust on a road. Canaanite poems gave Baal the title "rider of the clouds." The Old Testament gives that place to the LORD (Ps 68:4; 104:3).
  • "He rebukes the sea and makes it dry; he dries up all the rivers" (v4). This recalls the Red Sea and the Jordan (Exod 14:21; Josh 3:16; Ps 114:3–5). The Tyndale notes add that Canaanite myth told of a sea-god, Yam, as a chaotic power. Here the sea is simply rebuked and obeys. Jesus did the same on the Sea of Galilee, and his disciples asked, "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (Mark 4:39–41).
  • "Bashan and Carmel wither; the bloom of Lebanon withers" (v4). These were the lushest places the reader knew. Bashan, east of the Sea of Galilee, was famous for its pastures and cattle (Amos 4:1). Carmel, on the coast, for its woodland and fields. Lebanon, for its cedars. If they wither before him, nothing on earth is beyond his reach.
  • "The mountains quake before him; the hills melt" (v5). Earthquake language goes back to Sinai, where "the whole mountain trembled greatly" (Exod 19:18; compare Ps 18:7; Mic 1:4).
  • "Who can stand before his indignation?" (v6). The answer expected is "no one." Malachi asks the same question (Mal 3:2), and Revelation 6:17 puts it on the lips of the kings of the earth: "the great day of their wrath has come, and who can stand?" "Wrath" here repeats the word from v2, which closes this first half of the poem. The rocks, the hardest things in creation, "are broken into pieces by him."
  • "The LORD is good, a stronghold in the day of trouble" (v7). Right in the middle of the storm the tone changes completely. The same power that breaks rocks is a fortress for those who hide in him. A "stronghold" (ma'oz) is a hilltop fort where people ran when raiders came (Ps 27:1; 37:39). The "day of trouble" is real, and the believer is not promised it will not come, only a safe place within it.
  • "He knows those who take refuge in him" (v7). "Knows" in Hebrew is relational, not just informational. It means he recognizes them as his own and cares for them (Ps 1:6; Amos 3:2). Jesus uses the same idea: "I know my own and my own know me" (John 10:14), and Paul: "The Lord knows those who are his" (2 Tim 2:19).
  • "With an overflowing flood he will make a complete end of the adversaries" (v8). The flood is a picture of judgment that sweeps over every barrier. It may also look ahead to how Nineveh actually fell. Nahum 2:6 says "the river gates are opened," and the Greek historian Diodorus, writing centuries later, says a flooding river broke down part of Nineveh's wall during the final siege. How reliable his account is, scholars debate, but the prophecy and the tradition line up strikingly.
  • "What do you plot against the LORD?" (v9). The prophet turns to address the Assyrians directly. Plotting against God's people is plotting against God himself (Ps 2:1–4). "Trouble will not rise up a second time" means the blow will be so complete that it will not need repeating. Readers differ on whose "trouble" it is. Most take it as Assyria's destruction, which needs only one stroke. JFB takes it as Judah's affliction, which will never come from Assyria again. Either way, the result is the same.
  • "Like entangled thorns, like drunkards as they drink" (v10). Two vivid pictures. Thorns tangled together go up in flames all at once. Drunkards are helpless and stumble without being pushed. Assyrian kings were famous for lavish banquets, and Greek tradition later told that Nineveh's last defenders were feasting when the city fell. Whether or not that story is true, the picture fits a city drunk on its own power.
  • "From you came one who plotted evil against the LORD, a worthless counselor" (v11). "Worthless" is beliyya'al, literally "without worth," the word behind "Belial," which by New Testament times had become a name for Satan (2 Cor 6:15). Many think the "one" is Sennacherib, who sent his spokesman to mock the LORD at Jerusalem's walls (2 Kgs 18:28–35; 19:22–23). Others think it refers to Assyria's kings in general. The Tyndale notes draw a sharp contrast with the Messiah, whose name is "Wonderful Counselor" (Isa 9:6).
  • "Though they are at full strength and many, they will be cut down and pass away" (v12). "Full strength" is shalem, whole or complete. Assyria looked untouchable. The verb "cut down" is the word for shearing, like a fleece or a beard. Isaiah had used the same picture of Assyria as God's razor against Israel (Isa 7:20). Now the razor is turned on the barber.
  • "Though I have afflicted you, I will afflict you no more" (v12). The address switches to Judah. God takes responsibility for Judah's suffering under Assyria: "I have afflicted you." Assyria was his rod of discipline (Isa 10:5), but the discipline has a limit. This is the comfort Nahum's name promised.
  • "I will break his yoke from off you and will burst your bonds apart" (v13). The yoke is the tribute and control Assyria imposed. Hezekiah had stripped the temple's gold to pay it (2 Kgs 18:14–16), and Manasseh was dragged to Babylon in hooks by an Assyrian king (2 Chr 33:11). The same promise of a broken yoke runs through the prophets (Isa 10:27; Jer 30:8).
  • "No more shall your name be perpetuated" (v14). Literally "no more of your name shall be sown," meaning no descendants to carry it on. Within a few years of Nineveh's fall, Assyria vanished from history so completely that when the Greek soldier Xenophon marched past the ruins about two hundred years later, he did not even know what city they had been. Nineveh was rediscovered only in the 1840s, when archaeologists dug into the mounds across from Mosul.
  • "From the house of your gods I will cut off the carved image" (v14). Assyria's gods could not save it. "I will make your grave." JFB connects this to Sennacherib, who was murdered by his own sons while worshiping in the temple of his god Nisroch (2 Kgs 19:37). The house of his god became his grave. "For you are vile" means literally "you are light," weightless, of no account, the opposite of the Hebrew word for glory, which means "heavy."
  • "Behold, upon the mountains, the feet of him who brings good news, who publishes peace!" (v15). In Hebrew Bibles this verse is 2:1. The picture is a runner coming over the hills toward Jerusalem with news from the front, the way a messenger brought word to David after a battle (2 Sam 18:24–27). Isaiah uses nearly the same words for the herald announcing Zion's restoration: "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news… who says to Zion, 'Your God reigns'" (Isa 52:7). Paul applies Isaiah's verse to everyone sent to preach the gospel (Rom 10:15).
  • "Keep your feasts, O Judah; fulfill your vows" (v15). Under Assyrian pressure, worship had been disrupted. Now Judah is told to worship freely and keep the promises made to God in the time of trouble (Ps 66:13–14). Freedom is not for carelessness but for faithfulness. "The worthless" here is again beliyya'al. It will "never again… pass through you." Assyria never did invade Judah again. Babylon later would, but that is another story, told by Habakkuk.
  • Commentators: Henry sees the whole chapter as "a mixture of wrath and justice against the wicked, and mercy and grace towards his people." On v3 he says God is "not easily provoked, but ready to show mercy," and on v7, "he knows them, he owns them for his, he takes cognizance of their case." Like JFB, Henry reads vv9–15 mainly against Sennacherib's siege of Jerusalem and the death of his army. JFB explains God's jealousy as arising "because He loves them" and quotes the medieval Jewish scholar Kimchi on v10: drunkards "fall down without any one pushing them." On v12 JFB glosses "full strength" as "without fear, and tranquilly secure." Guzik calls God's jealousy love in action, a jealousy for his people, not of them, and reads "by no means clear the guilty" as proof that every sin is paid for, either by the sinner or at the cross. On v15 Guzik notes the parallel with Revelation 18, where heaven rejoices at Babylon's fall, and stresses that the rejoicing is about God being shown right, not about gloating.

Pointing to Christ

Nahum holds together two things that are hard to hold at once: a God who will by no means clear the guilty, and a God who is a stronghold for those who take refuge in him. The cross is where they meet. There God did not clear guilt by ignoring it but by bearing it, as the Son took the wrath described in vv2–6 (Rom 3:25–26). And the runner on the mountains with good news of peace becomes, in the New Testament, every messenger of Jesus. The yoke he breaks is heavier than Assyria's, and the peace he announces is with God himself (Eph 2:14–17).

Reflection

  • Patience That Is Not Weakness (v.3) — Nahum says the LORD is slow to anger yet will never leave the guilty unpunished. When evil seems to prosper unchecked in the world around you, do you mistake God's patience for indifference?
  • A Stronghold for Those Who Trust Him (v.7) — the same God whose wrath topples nations is described as good, a refuge for anyone who takes shelter in Him. Are you currently relating to God more as someone to fear or as the stronghold you can run to?
  • God's Judgment Is Thorough (v.9) — Nahum declares that when God finally acts, the affliction will not need to rise a second time. Is there an injustice you're still carrying anger or anxiety over that you need to fully entrust to God's complete judgment?
  • Someone's Judgment Is Another's Good News (v.15) — the fall of a cruel oppressor is announced as good tidings and peace for those who suffered under it. Where do you need to trust that God settling accounts with someone else's wrongdoing is actually good news for you?
