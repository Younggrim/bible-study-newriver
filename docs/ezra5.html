Summary

Encouraged by the preaching of the prophets Haggai and Zechariah, Zerubbabel and Jeshua resume construction on the temple in Jerusalem without a fresh royal permit (vv. 1-2). The regional governor Tattenai and his colleague Shethar-bozenai arrive, question the elders about who authorized the rebuilding, and ask for the names of those in charge (vv. 3-4). Because God is watching over the elders, the officials do not stop the work outright but instead send King Darius a letter describing the project and recounting the Jews' explanation, that Cyrus originally decreed the temple be rebuilt and had its sacred vessels returned (vv. 5-16). The letter closes by asking Darius to search the royal archives and confirm whether Cyrus really issued that decree (v. 17).

Authorship & Background

Author: Ezra the priest and scribe (likely author/compiler). Written approximately 450-400 BC. Ezra covers two returns from Babylonian exile: (1) Zerubbabel's return and Temple rebuilding (538-516 BC, chapters 1-6); (2) Ezra's return and spiritual reform (458 BC, chapters 7-10).
Classification: Historical Narrative — Post-Exilic
Key Themes: Two prophets restarting what politics had stopped, a governor's enquiry that turns into vindication, an unusually fair official report, and elders who name their fathers' sin as part of their defense
Historical Context: Chapter 5 marks the restart of Temple construction in 520 BC — the second year of Darius I — after 16 years of inactivity. The catalyst was not political change but prophetic ministry: Haggai and Zechariah spoke God's word to the discouraged community, and the leaders responded with renewed obedience. Immediately, opposition resurfaced — Tattenai (the Persian governor of the Trans-Euphrates province) investigated the building project. But unlike chapter 4, the outcome here is different. The text reveals why: "the eye of their God was upon the elders of the Jews" (v.5). God's watchful providence ensured that Tattenai's investigation became a path to vindication rather than obstruction. Tattenai's letter to Darius (vv.6-17) is remarkably fair — he reports facts without the malicious spin of chapter 4's accusers. He even notes that the work "goeth fast on, and prospereth" (v.8). The Jewish elders' response (vv.11-16) is a model of faithful witness: they identify themselves as "servants of the God of heaven and earth," acknowledge their fathers' sin that caused the exile, and cite Cyrus's decree as their legal authority. They request that the Persian archives be searched for confirmation. The chapter demonstrates that when God's people obey His prophetic word, even official inquiries become instruments of divine vindication.
Haggai and Zechariah Prophesied (vv.1-2): The restart has no political cause. Two prophets speak, and Zerubbabel and Jeshua rise up and begin to build, with the prophets of God helping them. Haggai's own book dates this to 520 BC and records what he said — that the people were paneling their own houses while God's lay waste. Sixteen years of stoppage ends with preaching rather than permission.
Who Commanded You to Build? (vv.3-5): Tattenai, the governor of the province beyond the river, arrives with the obvious question, and asks for the names of the builders. Verse 5 gives the chapter's explanation of what follows: the eye of their God was upon the elders of the Jews, so they could not cause them to cease. The work continues during the investigation rather than pausing for it.
Tattenai's Letter (vv.6-10): The contrast with chapter 4 is the point of quoting this letter at length. Tattenai reports what he found without spin, notes that the work goeth fast on and prospereth, and asks the king to check the record. An honest official is as consequential here as a dishonest one was there, and the difference is not the law but the man applying it.
We Are the Servants of the God of Heaven (vv.11-17): The elders' answer is quoted inside the letter and it is a model of its kind. They identify themselves as servants of the God of heaven and earth, state that the temple was built by a great king of Israel, and then concede the hard part without being asked: our fathers provoked God to wrath, and he gave them into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar. Then the legal ground, Cyrus's decree, with a request that the archives be searched. Honesty about the exile is offered as part of the case rather than hidden from it.

Map & Geography

  • The geographic arc: Babylon → Jerusalem (~900-mile journey through the Fertile Crescent). Two returns: 538 BC under Zerubbabel and 458 BC under Ezra.
  • Jerusalem is the destination — the Temple is rebuilt (516 BC) and worship restored.
  • The journey followed major trade routes along the Euphrates River, through Syria, then south into Judah — a 4-month trek (Ezra 7:9).

Setting: Ezra 4 ended with the temple work stopped "until the second year of the reign of Darius king of Persia" (4:24). The foundation had been laid around 536 BC, and then for about sixteen years nothing more was built. Neighbors had intimidated the builders and bribed officials, and the people themselves had lost heart and turned to their own houses and fields. Meanwhile the Persian Empire had gone through upheaval. Cyrus died in 530 BC, his son Cambyses died in 522, and a new king, Darius I, fought his way to the throne through a year of revolts across the empire before he took firm control. In 520 BC, the second year of Darius, God sent two prophets, and the work began again. Ezra 5 tells the story from the outside, mostly through an official letter of inquiry from the Persian governor to the king. The chapter is in Aramaic, the language of Persian government, like the rest of 4:8–6:18. The books of Haggai and Zechariah tell the same months from the inside, and they belong beside this chapter.

Rebuilding Begins Anew (vv1–5)

  • "Now the prophets, Haggai and Zechariah the son of Iddo, prophesied" (v1). Haggai's first message is dated precisely to the first day of the sixth month of Darius's second year, August 29, 520 BC (Hag 1:1). Zechariah began two months later (Zech 1:1). Haggai's message was blunt. The people were saying, "The time has not yet come to rebuild the house of the LORD," while they lived in "paneled houses" (Hag 1:2–4). Their crops failed and their wages leaked away "into a bag with holes" (Hag 1:6), because they had put their own houses first. Zechariah's message was full of comfort and vision: God was returning to Jerusalem with mercy, and the temple would be finished "not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit" (Zech 4:6–9).
  • "Zechariah the son of Iddo" (v1). Zechariah 1:1 calls him "the son of Berechiah, the son of Iddo," so Iddo was his grandfather. Hebrew and Aramaic "son of" can mean any descendant. Iddo was the head of a priestly family that came back with Zerubbabel (Neh 12:4, 16), so Zechariah was both priest and prophet. Haggai's family is never given.
  • "In the name of the God of Israel who was over them" (v1). The phrase can also be read "who was upon them," that is, in whose name they were called. Either way the point is authority. The prophets spoke not as advisers but as messengers of the God who ruled his people. Persia had given permission. God gave the command.
  • "Then Zerubbabel… and Jeshua… arose and began to rebuild" (v2). The governor and the high priest responded at once. Haggai records that within about three weeks of the first message, "the LORD stirred up the spirit of Zerubbabel… and the spirit of Joshua… and the spirit of all the remnant of the people," and they started work (Hag 1:12–15). "Stirred up" is the same verb used of Cyrus and the first returnees in Ezra 1:1, 5. The work begins and begins again by God's stirring.
  • "The prophets of God were with them, supporting them" (v2). The prophets did not preach and then leave. They stayed alongside, encouraging. Ezra 6:14 credits the finished temple to "the prophesying of Haggai the prophet and Zechariah," as if their words were part of the building materials. Nothing had changed politically. The Persian ban from Cyrus's later years had not been lifted. The people went back to work on God's word alone, before they knew what the authorities would do.
  • "Tattenai the governor of the province Beyond the River" (v3). Tattenai governed the huge Persian province west of the Euphrates, which included Syria, Phoenicia and the land of Israel. A Babylonian business document dated 502 BC mentions a "Tattannu, governor of Across-the-River," and most scholars think it is the same man. Shethar-bozenai was probably his secretary or a fellow official. Judah was a small district inside his province, under its own governor, Zerubbabel (Hag 1:1).
  • "Who gave you a decree to build this house?" (v3). This is a fair question for a governor in a year of rebellions. Darius had just put down revolts in Babylon and elsewhere. A people rebuilding a large structure in a city with a rebellious history was worth checking. Unlike Rehum's letter in chapter 4, there is no sign of malice here. Tattenai inspects in person.
  • "What are the names of the men who are building?" (v4). The Aramaic is compressed, and v10 shows the meaning: the officials asked for the names of the leaders so they could report them. That was a little ominous. If the work turned out to be unauthorized, the named men would be held responsible.
  • "But the eye of their God was on the elders of the Jews" (v5). This is the theological center of the chapter. The phrase "the eye of the LORD" is a familiar Old Testament picture of God's watchful care (Ps 33:18, "the eye of the LORD is on those who fear him"; 2 Chr 16:9). Later in the book Ezra uses a parallel phrase, "the hand of my God" was on him (7:6, 9, 28). The writer gives no credit to the elders' diplomacy or the governor's fairness. God was watching over them.
  • "They did not stop them until the report should reach Darius" (v5). Tattenai could have ordered a halt while he checked. Instead he let the work continue while the letter went to the king, a journey of months each way. The building went on during the inquiry.
  • Commentators: Henry says the work, "hindered by might and power," was "set a-going again by the Spirit of the Lord of hosts," a clear echo of Zechariah 4:6. He notes that God both warmed his people and made their opponents "more mild towards the builders." JFB blames the long stop on the people's own "sloth, negligence, and worldly selfishness," which the prophets rebuked. JFB also points out that "son of Iddo" means grandson. Guzik sees Tattenai as a reasonable administrator rather than an enemy, and he draws the lesson that the delay gave the builders time to keep working and to pray. He also notes that Zechariah's name means "the LORD remembers," which fits his message of God's care for his people.

Tattenai's Letter to King Darius (vv6–17)

  • A copy of the letter (v6). Ezra had access to Persian official correspondence, probably copies kept in Jerusalem or in the provincial archive. The letter is quoted in its official form. Compared with the letter in 4:11–16, the tone is level. It reports, asks a question and suggests a way to check the answer.
  • "To the house of the great God" (v8). "Great God" is not a confession of faith. Persian officials referred respectfully to the high gods of the peoples they ruled. Still, it is a striking phrase to find in a pagan governor's report.
  • "Huge stones, and timber is laid in the walls" (v8). The Aramaic is literally "stones of rolling," blocks so large they had to be rolled into place rather than carried. Timber beams were set between courses of stone, the same building method used in Solomon's temple: "three courses of cut stone and one course of cedar beams" (1 Kgs 6:36; compare Ezra 6:4). The timber may have helped the walls flex in earthquakes. To a governor, heavy stonework could look like fortification, which may be why he wrote at all.
  • "This work goes on diligently and prospers in their hands" (v8). Without meaning to, a Persian official confirms what Haggai promised. Once the people obeyed, God blessed the work (Hag 2:15–19).
  • "We are the servants of the God of heaven and earth" (v11). The elders begin with whose they are, not with their legal position. "God of heaven" was a title the Persians understood and used for the highest god (Cyrus uses it in 1:2), and the elders widen it to "heaven and earth," the Creator of all.
  • "The house that was built many years ago, which a great king of Israel built" (v11). That king was Solomon, about 960 BC, some 440 years earlier (1 Kgs 6). The elders present their work as restoring an ancient and legitimate shrine, not starting something new and suspicious. Persian policy respected old temples.
  • "Because our fathers had angered the God of heaven" (v12). This is a remarkable thing to say to a foreign government. The elders do not blame Nebuchadnezzar's power or Judah's bad luck. They say plainly that the exile was God's judgment on their own nation's sin (2 Kgs 24:20; 2 Chr 36:15–17). It also answers the charge of chapter 4 that Jerusalem was a rebellious city: yes, and God himself had punished it. Nebuchadnezzar is "the Chaldean," the name for the dynasty that ruled Babylon from 626 to 539 BC.
  • "In the first year of Cyrus king of Babylon" (v13). Cyrus took Babylon in 539 BC and issued his decree in his first year as its king (1:1). Calling him "king of Babylon" fits Persian usage, since Cyrus took that ancient title for himself.
  • The vessels and Sheshbazzar (vv14–15). The elders give details that could be checked: the temple vessels Nebuchadnezzar took (2 Kgs 25:13–15; Dan 5:2–3) and the official who received them. Sheshbazzar was the "prince of Judah" to whom Cyrus's treasurer counted out the vessels (1:8, 11). Here he is called "governor."
  • "This Sheshbazzar came and laid the foundations" (v16). Ezra 3:8–10 credits the foundation to Zerubbabel and Jeshua. There are two main views. Some think Sheshbazzar and Zerubbabel are the same man, one a Babylonian name and one a court name. Others, including the Tyndale notes, think Sheshbazzar was the first official governor who began the work, and Zerubbabel, his nephew or successor, led it in practice and later finished it. Both views fit the text. Before a Persian official, the elders naturally name the man Cyrus appointed.
  • "From that time until now it has been in building, and it is not yet finished" (v16). Strictly, the work had stopped for years. But in legal terms the project authorized by Cyrus had never been cancelled, so the elders could say it had been "in building" all along. JFB suggests this line may be Tattenai's own summary rather than the elders' words.
  • "Let search be made in the royal archives there in Babylon" (v17). Literally "the house of treasures," where royal records were stored. Tattenai proposes to settle the matter by evidence. As it turned out, the scroll was not found at Babylon but at Ecbatana, Cyrus's summer capital (6:2). Persian practice held that a king's decree remained in force unless a king revoked it, so if the decree existed, Darius would be bound to honor it.
  • Commentators: JFB praises Tattenai's fairness and contrasts him with Rehum. JFB explains "huge stones" as "stones of rolling," too large to carry, and notes that under Persian custom a decree stood until it was revoked, citing Daniel 6. Henry says God's people, while "employed in God's work," are under his special protection, and he notes the elders' humble confession of their fathers' sin. Guzik mentions both views of Sheshbazzar and Zerubbabel without settling the question.

Pointing to Christ

The temple project in Ezra 5 has no new political protection, only a word from God through two prophets and the watchful "eye of their God." The builders begin before they know how the king will respond. This is the shape of faith: obeying God's word and trusting him with the outcome. The elders' answer also models honest faith. They confess their fathers' sin openly and rest their case on the promise of a king. The gospel works the same way. Believers do not stand before God by hiding their failure but by confessing it and pointing to a King's decree that cannot be revoked, the finished work of Christ. Zerubbabel, the governor rebuilding God's house here, was of David's royal line and appears in the genealogy of Jesus (Matt 1:12–13). Through Haggai God called him "my signet ring" (Hag 2:23), a pledge that David's line would continue. That promise was kept in the Son of David who said, "I will build my church, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it" (Matt 16:18).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik highlights the essential role of the prophets in restarting the work, noting that God's people needed more than permission — they needed prophetic encouragement to overcome 16 years of complacency. He emphasizes that "the eye of their God" (v.5) made all the difference between the failed opposition of chapter 5 and the successful opposition of chapter 4.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • God's Word Breaks Through Paralysis (v.1) — Sixteen years of inaction ended not through a political shift but because God sent prophets to speak fresh words to a discouraged people. What stalled area of your life needs a renewed word from God rather than just better circumstances?
  • Vision and Action Working Together (v.2) — The prophets spoke and the leaders immediately responded by picking the work back up. Are you someone who receives encouragement but never acts on it, or who acts without ever pausing to listen for direction?
  • The Difference God's Watchful Eye Makes (v.5) — The same kind of official scrutiny that shut down the work in chapter 4 couldn't stop it here, because the text says God's eye was on the elders. Does knowing God is watching over your obedience change how you face scrutiny or opposition?
  • Honest Confession Strengthens Credibility (v.12) — Rather than hiding it, the elders openly told the Persian governor that their nation's sin had caused the exile in the first place. Are you willing to be honest about your own past failures instead of hiding them, even when it might seem to weaken your case?
