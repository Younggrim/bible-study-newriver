1These are the words of Nehemiah son of Hacaliah: In the month of Chislev, in the twentieth year, while I was in the citadel of Susa,

2Hanani, one of my brothers, arrived with men from Judah. So I questioned them about the remnant of the Jews who had survived the exile, and also about Jerusalem.

3And they told me, “The remnant who survived the exile are there in the province, in great trouble and disgrace. The wall of Jerusalem is broken down, and its gates have been burned with fire.”

4When I heard these words, I sat down and wept. I mourned for days, fasting and praying before the God of heaven.

5Then I said: “O LORD, God of heaven, the great and awesome God who keeps His covenant of loving devotion with those who love Him and keep His commandments,

6let Your eyes be open and Your ears attentive to hear the prayer that I, Your servant, now pray before You day and night for Your servants, the Israelites. I confess the sins that we Israelites have committed against You. Both I and my father’s house have sinned.

7We have behaved corruptly against You and have not kept the commandments, statutes, and ordinances that You gave Your servant Moses.

8Remember, I pray, the word that You commanded Your servant Moses when You said, ‘If you are unfaithful, I will scatter you among the nations,

9but if you return to Me and keep and practice My commandments, then even if your exiles have been banished to the farthest horizon, I will gather them from there and bring them to the place I have chosen as a dwelling for My Name.’

10They are Your servants and Your people. You redeemed them by Your great power and mighty hand.

11O Lord, may Your ear be attentive to my prayer and to the prayers of Your servants who delight to revere Your name. Give Your servant success this day, I pray, and grant him mercy in the sight of this man.” (At that time I was the cupbearer to the king.)

Summary

Nehemiah, serving as cupbearer to King Artaxerxes in the Persian capital of Susa, learns from his brother Hanani that Jerusalem's wall lies in ruins and its gates have been burned, leaving the returned exiles in disgrace (vv. 1-3). The news drives him to days of weeping, fasting, and prayer (v. 4). His prayer opens by praising God's covenant faithfulness, then moves into confession of Israel's sin and a plea for God to remember His promise to restore a repentant people (vv. 5-9). He closes by asking God to grant him success and favor with the king, since he serves as royal cupbearer (vv. 10-11).

Authorship & Background

Author: Nehemiah, with possible editorial additions by Ezra. Written approximately 445-400 BC. Nehemiah covers the rebuilding of Jerusalem's walls (445 BC) and the spiritual renewal of the community.
Classification: Historical Narrative — Post-Exilic Memoir
Key Themes: A cupbearer with daily access to a king, news that arrives four months before anything is said about it, mourning before petition, and a prayer that quotes God's own promise back to him
Historical Context: Chapter 1 opens in the month of Chislev (November-December), in the twentieth year of Artaxerxes I (445 BC) — approximately 13 years after Ezra's arrival in Jerusalem. Nehemiah served as cupbearer to the Persian king at Susa (the winter palace), a position of extraordinary trust and influence. Cupbearers tasted the king's wine to guard against poisoning — they had daily personal access to the monarch. News arrived from Jerusalem through Nehemiah's brother Hanani: the walls remained broken, the gates burned, and the people were in "great affliction and reproach." This likely refers to the forced stoppage described in Ezra 4:23, when enemies demolished an attempted reconstruction. Nehemiah's response reveals his character in compressed form: he wept, mourned, fasted, and prayed for "certain days" (likely four months, until the king noticed in Nisan — chapter 2:1). His prayer (vv.5-11) is a masterpiece of biblical intercession: it begins with God's character (great, awesome, covenant-keeping), moves to corporate confession (identifying with the sin), appeals to God's own promises (Deuteronomy 30:1-5), and concludes with a specific request for favor "in the sight of this man" — the king. Nehemiah's prayer is bookended by action: he wept before praying, and he planned before acting. This integration of heartfelt prayer with strategic thinking characterizes his entire ministry.
News from Jerusalem (vv.1-3): The month is Chislev in the twentieth year of Artaxerxes, 445 BC, some thirteen years after Ezra reached Jerusalem. Nehemiah is cupbearer at Susa, which meant tasting the king's wine against poison and therefore daily personal access to the monarch — a position of unusual trust for a Jew in exile. His brother Hanani brings the report: the wall broken down and the gates burned, the survivors in great affliction and reproach. This is probably the forced demolition of Ezra 4:23 rather than the destruction of 586 BC.
Four Months of Prayer (vv.4-7): The response is not a plan but a collapse: he sat down and wept and mourned certain days, and fasted and prayed. Comparing 1:1 with 2:1 shows the interval was about four months, so nothing is said to the king for a third of a year. The prayer confesses “I and my father's house have sinned”, putting himself inside the failure rather than reporting on it.
Remember the Word Thou Commandedst (vv.8-11): The petition's argument is God's own word quoted back — the promise from Deuteronomy that if they return, he will gather them from the uttermost parts. He asks for mercy in the sight of “this man”, and only in the last clause of the chapter does the reader learn who he is: for I was the king's cupbearer. The detail is withheld until the prayer is finished, which is a deliberate order.

Map & Geography

  • Susa (Shushan) — the Persian winter capital in modern Iran, where Nehemiah serves as cupbearer to King Artaxerxes.

Deep Dive

Setting: The book of Nehemiah picks up about thirteen years after Ezra arrived in Jerusalem (Ezra 7). In the Hebrew Bible, Ezra and Nehemiah were counted as one book, and they tell one story: the temple rebuilt (Ezra 1–6), the people taught and reformed (Ezra 7–10), and now the city walls rebuilt and the community renewed (Nehemiah). The date is late 446 BC, in the twentieth year of Artaxerxes I. Nehemiah is a Jew born in exile who holds a trusted post in the Persian palace at Susa. Most of the book is his own first-person memoir, one of the most personal accounts in the Old Testament. It opens not with a building plan but with a man on his knees.

Report from Jerusalem (vv1–3)

  • "The words of Nehemiah the son of Hacaliah" (v1). Nehemiah means "the LORD comforts." Nothing else is known of his family, though some scholars think he was of the tribe of Judah, possibly even of royal descent, since his ancestors' graves were in Jerusalem (2:3, 5). He is not the Nehemiah of Ezra 2:2 or Nehemiah 3:16.
  • "In the month of Chislev, in the twentieth year" (v1). Chislev is the ninth month, November–December. The twentieth year of Artaxerxes I was 446/445 BC. Chapter 2 opens in Nisan (March–April) of the same "twentieth year," which seems out of order if the year began in spring. The likely answer is that Nehemiah counted the king's years from the autumn, as some Jewish calendars did, so Chislev came before Nisan in the same regnal year. Between the two dates lie about four months, and Nehemiah spent them praying.
  • "Susa the citadel" (v1). Susa, in what is now southwest Iran, was one of the Persian capitals and the kings' winter residence. It is the setting of the book of Esther (Esth 1:2), and Daniel saw a vision there (Dan 8:2). French archaeologists have excavated its great palace. "The citadel" was the fortified royal quarter. Nehemiah lived and worked at the center of the most powerful empire on earth, nearly 800 miles from Jerusalem as the crow flies, and much farther by road.
  • "Hanani, one of my brothers" (v2). "Brother" can mean a fellow Jew or relative, but in 7:2 Nehemiah calls him "my brother Hanani" and puts him in charge of Jerusalem, so he was probably a real brother. He had come from Judah with a group of men, possibly on an official errand to the court.
  • "I asked them concerning the Jews who escaped… and concerning Jerusalem" (v2). Nehemiah had a comfortable post, but his heart was with his people. He asked. He wanted to know. The story begins with a question from someone who cared (compare Ps 137:5–6).
  • "The remnant… is in great trouble and shame" (v3). "Shame" is cherpah, reproach or disgrace. In the ancient world a city's walls were its defense and its dignity. Neighbors could mock a ruined city and its God as weak (Joel 2:17; Ps 79:4). The people who had returned with such hope were now poor, vulnerable and ridiculed.
  • "The wall of Jerusalem is broken down, and its gates are destroyed by fire" (v3). Jerusalem's walls had lain in ruins since the Babylonians destroyed them in 586 BC (2 Kgs 25:10), 140 years earlier. That would hardly be news. Many scholars therefore think Hanani was reporting a more recent disaster. Ezra 4:7–23 describes an attempt to rebuild the walls under this same Artaxerxes, which the king halted, and officials "by force and power made them cease." Their enemies may have torn down what had been rebuilt and burned the new gates. That would explain why the news hit Nehemiah so hard.
  • Commentators: Henry says of Nehemiah that "Every Jerusalem on this side the heavenly one will have some defect, which will require the help and services of its friends." He notes that Nehemiah "lived at ease, and in honour, but does not forget that he is an Israelite." In his longer commentary, Henry calls this "the rise of this great man, by piety, not by policy." JFB passes on Josephus's story that Nehemiah overheard men speaking Hebrew near the palace and asked them about Judea, and he gives the month as late November and most of December. Guzik draws attention to the link between the people's distress and the broken wall: an unwalled city lived in constant insecurity.

Nehemiah's Prayer (vv4–11)

  • "I sat down and wept and mourned for days" (v4). Nehemiah's first response is grief, not a plan. Sitting down, weeping, mourning and fasting are the signs of deep sorrow (Ezra 9:3; 10:6). "For days" stretches into months. Nehemiah 2:1 shows that four months passed before he spoke to the king.
  • "Fasting and praying before the God of heaven" (v4). "The God of heaven" was the title Persian-era Jews used for the LORD (Ezra 1:2; Dan 2:18). In a palace full of Persian gods and royal power, Nehemiah prays to the one who rules over every throne.
  • "O LORD God of heaven, the great and awesome God who keeps covenant and steadfast love" (v5). The prayer opens with who God is. The words come almost directly from Deuteronomy 7:9, 21 and are nearly the same as Daniel's prayer (Dan 9:4). "Steadfast love" is chesed, God's covenant loyalty. Nehemiah begins with God's character before he mentions any need.
  • "Let your ear be attentive and your eyes open" (v6). He uses the language of Solomon's prayer at the temple's dedication (1 Kgs 8:29, 52; 2 Chr 6:40). Solomon asked God to hear prayers made toward this house, even from exile. Nehemiah, in exile, is doing exactly that.
  • "That I now pray before you day and night" (v6). This was not a single prayer, but a sustained one, over months. The prayer written here is likely a summary of what he prayed again and again.
  • "Confessing the sins of the people of Israel… Even I and my father's house have sinned" (v6). Like Ezra (Ezra 9:6) and Daniel (Dan 9:5), Nehemiah includes himself. He does not blame earlier generations or the people in Jerusalem. "We have acted very corruptly" (v7). The word for "acted corruptly" means to act ruinously, to destroy. Sin is self-destruction.
  • "The commandments, the statutes, and the rules that you commanded your servant Moses" (v7). Three words for God's law, in the style of Deuteronomy (Deut 5:31; 6:1). Nehemiah knows his Bible. The whole prayer is built from Scripture.
  • "Remember the word that you commanded your servant Moses" (v8). This is the heart of the prayer. Nehemiah holds God to his own promise. "If you are unfaithful, I will scatter you among the peoples" (v8) sums up Leviticus 26:33 and Deuteronomy 4:27. "But if you return to me… from there I will gather them" (v9) sums up Deuteronomy 30:1–4. The scattering had happened just as God warned. So the gathering could be counted on just as surely.
  • "Though your outcasts are in the uttermost parts of heaven" (v9). Even if they were scattered to the horizon, "from there" God would gather them. Deuteronomy 30:4 uses the same picture.
  • "The place that I have chosen, to make my name dwell there" (v9). This is the language of Deuteronomy 12:5, 11 for the place of God's worship, which turned out to be Jerusalem. Nehemiah's concern for the walls is not civic pride. It is concern for the city that bears God's name.
  • "Whom you have redeemed by your great power and by your strong hand" (v10). This is exodus language (Deut 9:29). God redeemed Israel from Egypt and made them his. Nehemiah reminds God, and himself, that these struggling people are still God's own.
  • "Your servants who delight to fear your name" (v11). Nehemiah was not alone. Others were praying too. "Fear" here means reverent awe and trust, not terror, and these people "delight" in it.
  • "Give success to your servant today, and grant him mercy in the sight of this man" (v11). The prayer that began with God's greatness ends with a specific request. "Today" suggests Nehemiah was praying this on the day he would finally speak. "This man" is the king of Persia, the most powerful man in the world, and in God's sight just a man. "Mercy" means compassion or favor (compare Gen 43:14; 1 Kgs 8:50). Nehemiah planned to act, but he knew everything depended on God moving the king's heart (Prov 21:1).
  • "Now I was cupbearer to the king" (v11). The chapter's last line explains everything. A cupbearer served the king's wine, tasting it to make sure it was not poisoned. In the Persian court this was a post of great trust and influence, held by a man the king saw daily and relied on. The Greek historian Herodotus describes the honor of the office, and in the Jewish book of Tobit, Ahikar is cupbearer and chief adviser to the king of Assyria. Some Greek manuscripts read "eunuch" instead of "cupbearer," and some scholars think Nehemiah may have been one, since court officials near the queen often were (2:6), but the Hebrew text does not say so. God had placed his man exactly where he was needed, as he had placed Joseph, Daniel and Esther.
  • Commentators: Guzik observes that four months of prayer lay behind a wall that took only fifty-two days to build (6:15), and that the prayer is that of a man ready to act, not one who wants someone else to do it. Henry writes, "Our best pleas in prayer are taken from the promise of God, the word on which he has caused us to hope," and "Communion with God will best prepare us for our dealings with men." JFB says the cupbearer "was always a person of rank and importance" with "frequent access to the royal presence." The Tyndale notes trace the shape of the prayer: praise, confession, remembering God's promises and then a request. That order is a model for anyone who prays.

Pointing to Christ

Nehemiah leaves a palace to rebuild the walls of a broken city, and he does it first by praying day and night, confessing the sins of his people as his own and pleading God's promises. Jesus left far greater glory (Phil 2:6–7). He wept over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41), and he prays continually for his people (Heb 7:25). Nehemiah could only ask for mercy "in the sight of this man," the king. Jesus stands before the King of kings himself, and because of him the promise Nehemiah pleaded is kept. God has gathered his scattered people "from the uttermost parts of heaven" into one, and his name now dwells not in a walled city but in a people (Eph 2:19–22).

Reflection

  • Letting Grief Turn Into Sustained Prayer (v.4) — On hearing devastating news about Jerusalem, Nehemiah wept and then carried that grief into months of mourning, fasting, and prayer rather than a single quick petition. When bad news hits you, does it drive you into brief venting or into real, sustained seeking of God?
  • Starting With Who God Is (v.5) — Before making any request, Nehemiah addressed God as great, awesome, and covenant-keeping. Does your own prayer life begin by remembering who God is, or does it jump straight to your list of needs?
  • Owning the Community's Failure (v.6) — Nehemiah prayed "we have sinned," including himself in the confession of his people's collective unfaithfulness. Are you willing to identify with the failures of your family, church, or community before God, rather than distancing yourself as an innocent bystander?
  • Praying and Planning Together (v.11) — Nehemiah's prayer closed with a specific, practical request for favor with the king — he had already been thinking through what he'd ask for. Does your faith show up as prayer instead of planning, or can you hold both together the way Nehemiah did?
