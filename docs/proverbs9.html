Summary

Wisdom builds a grand house with seven pillars, prepares a feast, and sends her servants to invite the simple to eat and gain understanding (vv. 1-6). Correcting a mocker only brings scorn, but a wise person welcomes correction and grows from it (vv. 7-9). True wisdom begins with reverence for the LORD and brings a longer, fuller life, while scorning it brings consequences the scorner alone must bear (vv. 10-12). Folly is pictured as a loud, undisciplined woman who sits at her door offering the same invitation, tempting the simple with stolen water and secret bread that seem sweet in the moment (vv. 13-17). Her guests never realize her house leads to death, and everyone who enters ends up among the dead (v. 18).

Authorship & Background

Author: Primarily Solomon (1:1, 10:1, 25:1), with sections by Agur (ch.30) and King Lemuel (ch.31). Compiled over several centuries. Proverbs is wisdom literature — practical instruction for godly living. Hebrew title: 'Mishlei' — 'Proverbs/Comparisons.'
Historical Context: Chapter 9 is the climax and conclusion of the discourse section (chs.1-9). It presents the final choice between two women — Lady Wisdom and Lady Folly — each hosting a banquet, each calling out to the simple. The symmetry is deliberate: both call from the high places (vv.3, 14), both address the simple (vv.4, 16), both offer food and drink (vv.5, 17). But the outcomes could not be more different: Wisdom's table leads to life; Folly's banquet seats the dead. The "seven pillars" (v.1) represent perfect completeness — Wisdom's house is fully established and secure. The central pivot (vv.7-12) distinguishes the wise from the scoffer: the wise love correction; scorners hate it. Verse 10 repeats the motto of 1:7 — framing the entire discourse section with "the fear of the LORD."
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: two women, Wisdom and Folly, each building a house and each calling out from the highest places of the city using nearly identical words, whoso is simple, let him turn in hither, a banquet fully prepared, animals killed, wine mingled, servants sent, set against a banquet built on nothing but theft, stolen waters are sweet, an interlude on correction distinguishing not intelligence but response, reprove not a scorner, lest he hate thee, rebuke a wise man, and he will love thee, the book's motto restated a second time, the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom, and a closing detail that separates the two invitations completely, he knoweth not that the dead are there
Lady Wisdom's Banquet (vv.1-6): Wisdom builds a house with seven pillars, kills her beasts, mingles her wine, furnishes her table, and then sends out invitations by servants, which is a considerable amount of preparation before anyone is asked. The invitation itself is shouted from the highest places of the city and is addressed to the least promising audience available, whoso is simple, let him turn in hither. The terms are two imperatives, come, eat of my bread, and drink of the wine which I have mingled, and one condition, forsake the foolish, and live.
The Wise and the Scoffer (vv.7-12): Six verses on who can be corrected, and the advice is unexpectedly practical, reprove not a scorner, lest he hate thee, rebuke a wise man, and he will love thee. The distinction is not intelligence but response, give instruction to a wise man, and he will be yet wiser. Then the book's motto restated with an addition, the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom, and the knowledge of the holy is understanding. And a closing sentence that puts the whole matter back on the reader, if thou be wise, thou shalt be wise for thyself, but if thou scornest, thou alone shalt bear it.
Lady Folly's Banquet (vv.13-18): The mirror image, and the parallels are exact enough to be deliberate. Folly also has a house, also sits at the high places of the city, also calls to passengers, and uses the same words, whoso is simple, let him turn in hither. The differences are two. Her fare is stolen rather than prepared, stolen waters are sweet, and bread eaten in secret is pleasant. And she does not mention what the guests already are, but he knoweth not that the dead are there, and that her guests are in the depths of hell. The first nine chapters end with two invitations that sound identical and one detail the diner is not told.

Setting: The close of Proverbs 1–9 (see Proverbs 1 on authorship), the father's long introduction before the collections of short sayings that begin in chapter 10. Chapter 7 told of the forbidden woman luring a young man home. Chapter 8 gave Wisdom's great speech, ending with life for those who find her and death for those who hate her. Chapter 9 brings the whole introduction to a decision. Two women, Wisdom and Folly, each throw open the door of her house and call out the same invitation to the same people. One table leads to life, the other to the grave. In between sits a short collection of sayings on who can be taught and who cannot.

The Way of Wisdom (vv1–12)

  • "Wisdom has built her house" (v1). The verb tenses through v3 are all completed actions: she has built, hewn, slaughtered, mixed and set. Everything is ready before anyone is invited. "Wisdom" here is the plural form chokmot, as in 1:20, often understood as a plural of fullness: Wisdom in all her greatness.
  • "She has hewn her seven pillars" (v1). "Hewn" means cut from stone, a costly and permanent house, not a tent. Seven is the number of completeness. The Tyndale notes take it as a sign of the house's greatness, and JFB reads it as "many, or a sufficiency." Large houses in the ancient world sometimes had a courtyard ringed with pillars. Interpreters over the centuries have tried to name the seven (seven gifts of the Spirit, seven churches, seven days of creation), but the text does not say, and it is wise not to press it.
  • "She has slaughtered her beasts; she has mixed her wine; she has also set her table" (v2). This is a full feast with meat, a luxury most people ate only at festivals. "Mixed" wine was blended with spices to enhance its flavor (JFB) or with water, as was common for drinking. The Tyndale notes point out that banquets marked a marriage, a victory or a treaty, the beginning of a committed relationship. Compare the forbidden woman's feast of leftover sacrifice meat (7:14). Both women offer a meal, but only one is honest about the cost.
  • "She has sent out her young women to call from the highest places in the town" (v3). A wealthy hostess sent servants to bring in the guests, as in Jesus' parable of the wedding feast (Matt 22:3–4; Luke 14:16–17). The "highest places" are the most visible spots in town. The Tyndale notes add that temples were often built on high ground, so the call is also a call to worship. JFB reads "the ridges of heights, conspicuous places."
  • "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!" (v4). The invitation is for the peti, the naïve and undecided, and for "him who lacks sense," literally "lacking heart," the exact description of the young man in 7:7. Wisdom does not wait for people to become wise before she invites them. The whole point of the feast is to make them wise.
  • "Come, eat of my bread and drink of the wine I have mixed" (v5). Eating and drinking are pictures of taking something in so deeply it becomes part of you (Isa 55:1–3; Jer 15:16). Isaiah's invitation is close: "Come, buy and eat… wine and milk without money."
  • "Leave your simple ways, and live, and walk in the way of insight" (v6). Accepting the invitation means leaving something behind. "Simple ways" could also be read "the simple ones," leaving foolish company. Life is not a reward added later. It is what walking with Wisdom is.
  • "Whoever corrects a scoffer gets himself abuse" (v7). The scene changes suddenly to a cluster of sayings. The "scoffer" (lets) is the hardest kind of fool in Proverbs, the person who mocks instruction itself (1:22; 21:24). The Tyndale notes suggest the sayings are a warning placed between the two invitations: by now some people have already chosen, and correcting them only brings insult. "Abuse" is literally "shame." JFB explains the "blot" as a "stain on character" that the mocker throws back at the one who corrected him.
  • "Do not reprove a scoffer, or he will hate you; reprove a wise man, and he will love you" (v8). The difference between a wise person and a fool is not whether they make mistakes. It is how they respond when someone points one out (12:1; 15:12). Jesus gave similar counsel: "Do not throw your pearls before pigs" (Matt 7:6). This is not permission to give up on people easily. It is realism about where to spend words.
  • "Give instruction to a wise man, and he will be still wiser" (v9). The wise keep growing. Teaching builds on teaching, and the more a person learns, the more teachable they become (1:5). JFB: "The more a wise man learns, the more he loves wisdom."
  • "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" (v10). The introduction to Proverbs ends where it began (1:7). These two verses frame chapters 1–9, and the motto is the key to everything inside. The "beginning" (techillah) is the starting point, the first step on the road. Reverent trust in God is not where wisdom ends up but where it starts.
  • "And the knowledge of the Holy One is insight" (v10). "Holy One" is plural in Hebrew, qedoshim, as again in 30:3. Most scholars read it as a plural of majesty, as with the common word for God, ʾElohim. Some Christian readers have seen a hint of the Trinity, but the grammar does not require it. What matters is the parallel: fearing the LORD and knowing the Holy One are the same thing, and wisdom is relational. It means knowing a person, not just knowing facts.
  • "For by me your days will be multiplied" (v11). Wisdom is speaking again, picking up the promise of 3:2 and 3:16. Proverbs states general patterns, not guarantees for every case: a wise life tends to be a longer, healthier one.
  • "If you are wise, you are wise for yourself; if you scoff, you alone will bear it" (v12). Each person carries the result of their own choice. Wisdom does not need us. God gains nothing he lacks when we become wise (Job 22:2–3; 35:6–8). JFB: "you are mainly concerned in your own conduct." The Greek translation adds several extra lines after this verse about a man who leans on lies and chases the wind. They are not in the Hebrew, and modern translations omit them.
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry reads Wisdom as Christ inviting sinners. The house is his church, held up by God's power and promises as by seven pillars. The feast is the gospel, with the slaughtered beasts pointing to his atoning sacrifice and the meal to the Lord's Supper. On v4 he stresses that it is the simple who are invited, so that they may be made wise, and on vv7–8 he reads "Reprove not a scorner" alongside "cast not your pearls before swine." Guzik treats much of that as reading in. He calls the interpretations of the pillars and of the bread and wine as communion speculation, and he takes the house simply as spacious, beautiful and secure. On v12 he sees a strong statement of personal responsibility. JFB stays close to the words throughout, and gives v11 nothing but cross-references.

The Way of Folly (vv13–18)

  • "The woman Folly is loud" (v13). "Folly" is kesilut, the quality of the kesil, the dull and self-confident fool. She is "loud," the same word as the forbidden woman in 7:11: noisy, restless, turbulent. JFB has "clamorous." The two women of chapters 7 and 9 are clearly meant to be seen together. The adulteress is what Folly looks like in one person's life.
  • "She is seductive and knows nothing" (v13). "Seductive" (petayyut) comes from the same root as "simple." She is simpleness itself, and she spreads it. "Knows nothing" means she has nothing to teach. Wisdom spent all of chapter 8 explaining what she knows. Folly has no content, only appeal.
  • "She sits at the door of her house; she takes a seat on the highest places of the town" (v14). Wisdom worked hard: she built, hewed, slaughtered, mixed and sent out her servants. Folly just sits. "Seat" is the word for a throne (JFB: she takes her place "impudently and haughtily"). She occupies "the highest places of the town," exactly where Wisdom's servants called from (v3). The Tyndale notes see the high places as hints of pagan shrines. Folly sets herself up as a rival god.
  • "Calling to those who pass by, who are going straight on their way" (v15). Her targets are people minding their own business, heading in the right direction. JFB says she aims "to allure those who are right-minded." Temptation does not wait for people already on the wrong road. It calls to those who are doing fine.
  • "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!" (v16). These are Wisdom's very words from v4, copied exactly. Folly is not original. She imitates the true invitation, which is why the simple find it hard to tell the two apart. The difference shows only in what each offers and where each house leads.
  • "Stolen water is sweet, and bread eaten in secret is pleasant" (v17). Wisdom offered meat and wine. Folly offers water and bread, plain fare made exciting only because it is stolen and secret. Water is a picture of sexual pleasure in 5:15–18, where a husband is told to drink from his own well, so the main reference is to adultery. But the saying sums up every kind of sin: the thrill comes from its being forbidden. JFB: "forbidden delights are sweet and pleasant, as fruits of risk and danger." It is the oldest lie in the book, as old as the fruit in the garden that was "a delight to the eyes" (Gen 3:6).
  • "But he does not know that the dead are there" (v18). "The dead" are the repha'im, the shades of the departed. The guest sees a party. The narrator sees a tomb. This is the same blindness as the ox who "does not know that it will cost him his life" (7:23).
  • "That her guests are in the depths of Sheol" (v18). Sheol is the realm of the dead, pictured as deep below the earth. The book's introduction ends on this word, the same ending as chapter 7 (7:27). There is no closing exhortation. The two houses are simply left standing side by side for the reader to choose between.
  • Commentators: Guzik notes how Folly copies Wisdom's words, and he grants that forbidden pleasure really does have an appeal, but it is short-lived. A feast that hides its ending becomes a funeral. JFB adds the detail that her "seat" is a word for a throne, and gives v18 only cross-references (2:18–19; 7:27).

Pointing to Christ

Two houses, two tables, one invitation repeated word for word. Jesus took up this picture more than once: a king's wedding feast with everything ready and servants sent out to call the guests (Matt 22:1–14), and a great banquet where the host fills his house with "the poor and crippled and blind and lame" (Luke 14:21). He stands in Wisdom's place: "I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger" (John 6:35). And unlike Wisdom's slaughtered beasts, the sacrifice at his table is himself: "my flesh is true food, and my blood is true drink" (John 6:55). Folly offers stolen bread that ends in Sheol. Christ offers his own body, given freely, and his guests do not end in the depths of the grave, because he went there first and came out again (Acts 2:27, 31). The final invitation of the Bible sounds like Proverbs 9: "Let the one who is thirsty come; let the one who desires take the water of life without price" (Rev 22:17).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes the parallel structure: both Wisdom and Folly call from the same high places to the same audience. The simple person must choose which invitation to accept. He notes that Folly's only selling point is transgression itself — "stolen waters are sweet." She has no genuine product, only the thrill of the forbidden. On v.8, Guzik states the practical test: "If you can't receive correction, you're not wise — regardless of how much you know."
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Two Voices Are Always Calling (vv.3-4,14-16) — both Wisdom and Folly call out from the high places of the city to the same simple passersby. Which voice have you been listening to more this week — the one that calls openly with truth, or the one that lures quietly with what's forbidden?
  • Your Response to Correction Reveals Your Heart (v.8) — a wise person loves the one who corrects him, while a mocker only hates him for it. When someone points out a fault in you, is your first instinct gratitude or resentment?
  • Wisdom's Benefits and Folly's Costs Are Deeply Personal (v.12) — being wise benefits you, and mocking brings its consequences on you alone, with no one else bearing it for you. Are you taking ownership of your own growth in wisdom rather than assuming someone else will handle the outcome?
  • Sin's Appeal Is Often Just the Thrill of the Forbidden (v.17) — Folly's whole pitch is that stolen water tastes sweeter simply because it's stolen. When you strip away the illicit thrill of a temptation, what's actually left worth wanting?
  • Check Who Else Took the Invitation Before You Do (v.18) — those who accept Folly's invitation don't realize her past guests are already dead. Before saying yes to a tempting path, are you willing to honestly ask where it has led everyone else who's walked it?
