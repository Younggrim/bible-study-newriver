Summary

Paul urges the Thessalonians not to be quickly shaken or alarmed by any claim, even one supposedly from him, that the Day of the Lord has already come (vv. 1-2). He explains that day cannot come until the rebellion occurs and the man of lawlessness is revealed, who exalts himself above every so-called god and sets himself up in God's temple, proclaiming himself to be God (vv. 3-4). Paul notes that a restraining force currently holds this figure back, but once removed, the lawless one will be revealed, empowered by Satan with counterfeit signs and wonders that deceive those who are perishing because they refused to love the truth (vv. 5-10). Because of this, God will send a powerful delusion so they believe the lie and are condemned (vv. 11-12). Paul thanks God for choosing the Thessalonians for salvation and urges them to stand firm in the teaching they were given (vv. 13-17).

Authorship & Background

Author: Paul the Apostle (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 2 addresses the primary reason Paul wrote this letter: someone had told the Thessalonians (falsely claiming Paul's authority) that "the day of Christ is at hand" — that it had already come (v.2). This caused panic and confusion. Paul corrects this by explaining that two things must happen first: (1) the great apostasy/falling away, and (2) the revelation of the "man of sin" (Antichrist). This is one of the most important and debated eschatological passages in the NT.
Classification: Epistle — Pauline
Key Themes: A forged letter or claimed prophecy spreading panic that the Day had already come, a falling away and a man of sin required before it can arrive, something currently restraining what is not yet revealed, a strong delusion sent on those who refused to love the truth, and a people called and chosen set against those who perish
Don't Be Shaken (vv.1-2): Paul urges them not to be disturbed by false claims — whether by "spirit" (prophecy), "word" (teaching), or "letter" (forged correspondence) — that the Day of the Lord has already arrived.
The Man of Sin (vv.3-12): Before that Day comes, there must be: (1) "a falling away" (apostasia — the great apostasy, v.3), and (2) the revelation of "the man of sin, the son of perdition" (v.3) — who exalts himself above God and sits in God's temple claiming to be God (v.4). Something/someone currently "restrains" him (vv.6-7). When the restrainer is removed, the lawless one will be revealed — and Christ will destroy him at His coming (v.8).
God's Chosen (vv.13-17): In contrast to those who perish, the Thessalonians are "chosen to salvation" (v.13). Paul urges them to "stand fast" and hold to the traditions they were taught (v.15).

Deep Dive

Setting: Paul writes from Corinth around AD 51, a few months after 1 Thessalonians. Chapter 1 comforted a persecuted church with the promise that Jesus will come in fire to judge their persecutors and be glorified in his people. But someone had told the Thessalonians that "the day of the Lord has come" already, perhaps claiming a prophecy, a report of Paul's teaching, or even a forged letter in his name. A church under fire would hear that as terrible news: had they missed the gathering of 1 Thessalonians 4? Were their sufferings the day of wrath itself? This chapter corrects the error. It is one of the hardest passages in the New Testament, partly because Paul is reminding readers of oral teaching we no longer have (v5). Augustine admitted he did not know what Paul meant by part of it. A wise reader holds the details loosely and the main points firmly: the day has not come, certain things must happen first, and Christ will crush the lawless one by a word.

The Man of Lawlessness (vv1–12)

  • "Concerning the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ and our being gathered together to him" (v1). The "coming" (parousia) and the "gathering" (episynagōgē) are joined under one article in Greek. They belong together, and they recall 1 Thessalonians 4:15–17. Jesus used the related verb for the angels gathering his elect (Matt 24:31). Some read the "gathering" as a rapture that comes before "the day of the Lord," and others read both as parts of one event at the end. Whatever the sequence, the Thessalonians had been told they had missed it.
  • "Not to be quickly shaken in mind or alarmed" (v2). "Shaken" (saleuō) is used of things tossed by wind or waves (Luke 7:24; Acts 4:31). "Alarmed" (throeō) is the word Jesus used: "See that you are not alarmed" (Matt 24:6). The false teaching had unsettled them in both mind and emotions.
  • "Either by a spirit or a spoken word, or a letter seeming to be from us" (v2). "Spirit" means a supposed prophecy (1 Thess 5:19–21). "Spoken word" could be a sermon or a rumor of what Paul taught. "A letter seeming to be from us" may mean a forgery, or a misreading of 1 Thessalonians. This is why Paul later signs the letter in his own hand as a mark of genuineness (3:17).
  • "To the effect that the day of the Lord has come" (v2). The verb (enestēken) means "is present" or "has arrived," not "is near" as the KJV's "at hand" suggests. Paul himself taught that the day was near. What he denies is that it had already begun. The error was something like Hymenaeus and Philetus's claim that "the resurrection has already happened" (2 Tim 2:18).
  • "The rebellion comes first" (v3). "Rebellion" (apostasia) gives us "apostasy." In the Greek Old Testament it means rebellion against God (Josh 22:22; Jer 2:19), and in the New Testament it is used for abandoning the law of Moses (Acts 21:21). With the article it is "the rebellion," a known event Paul had taught them about. Most read it as a great turning from God at the end, whether inside the visible church or across the world, as Jesus foretold (Matt 24:10–12) and Paul later describes (1 Tim 4:1; 2 Tim 3:1–5). A minority of pre-tribulation readers argue that apostasia here means "departure" and refers to the rapture. The word can mean departure in classical Greek, but the lexicon gives "revolt" or "apostasy" as its meaning in the Bible, and most interpreters across traditions reject the rapture reading.
  • "The man of lawlessness is revealed, the son of destruction" (v3). Some manuscripts read "man of sin," as in the KJV. The oldest read "lawlessness," and the ESV follows them. "Son of destruction" is a Hebrew way of saying "the one destined for destruction." Jesus used the same phrase of Judas (John 17:12). John calls this figure "antichrist" (1 John 2:18), but Paul never uses that word.
  • "Opposes and exalts himself against every so-called god or object of worship" (v4). The picture draws on Daniel 11:36: a king who "shall exalt himself and magnify himself above every god." That prophecy was first fulfilled in Antiochus IV Epiphanes, the Greek king who in 167 BC set up an altar to Zeus in the Jerusalem temple, the "abomination that makes desolate" (Dan 11:31). Jesus said such an abomination would come again (Mark 13:14). And only ten years before this letter, the emperor Caligula had ordered his statue placed in the Jerusalem temple. Only his death in AD 41 stopped it. Thessalonica itself honored the emperor with temples and divine titles. The Thessalonians did not need to imagine what a man claiming to be God would look like.
  • "He takes his seat in the temple of God, proclaiming himself to be God" (v4). "Temple" (naos) is the inner sanctuary. Christians read this in several ways. Some take it literally: a rebuilt temple in Jerusalem, which requires a future temple since this one was destroyed in AD 70. Others read it as the church, which Paul often calls God's temple (1 Cor 3:16; 2 Cor 6:16), meaning the man of lawlessness rises inside the visible church. Others take it as a picture: he seizes the place that belongs to God alone, whatever building he uses. Ezekiel 28:2 gives the pattern, the king of Tyre saying "I am a god, I sit in the seat of the gods."
  • "Do you not remember that when I was still with you I told you these things?" (v5). In a stay of only weeks, Paul taught brand-new converts about the end times. He thought it mattered for ordinary believers. The verb tense means "I kept telling you." This verse also explains why the passage is hard for later readers. Paul is speaking in shorthand to people who had heard the full lecture.
  • "You know what is restraining him now" (v6). "Restraining" (katechō) means "to hold back" or "to hold down." In v6 it is neuter, "what restrains," and in v7 it is masculine, "he who restrains." So it is both a thing and a person, or a power with a person behind it. The main views are listed below.
  • Views on the restrainer. The Roman Empire and its emperor, the oldest recorded view (Tertullian, about AD 200), now usually widened to the rule of law and government, which God uses to hold back chaos (Rom 13:1–4). This fits the neuter and masculine together and explains why Paul might speak cautiously. The Holy Spirit, or the Spirit working through the church, the common pre-tribulation view, which takes "out of the way" as the church's removal at the rapture. The preaching of the gospel and Paul himself, since the gospel must go to all nations before the end (Mark 13:10). An angel, such as Michael, who restrains evil powers in Daniel 10–12. God himself. The Tyndale notes also mention a minority reading of the word as "hold sway" rather than "restrain," making it a hostile power. Augustine's honesty is worth repeating: the Thessalonians knew, and later readers can only reason toward it. What is plain is that the man of lawlessness cannot appear until God allows it, "in his time."
  • "The mystery of lawlessness is already at work" (v7). A "mystery" in Paul is something hidden that will be revealed. Lawlessness is not just a future problem. It is at work now, under the surface, as John says: "now many antichrists have come" (1 John 2:18). The restraint holds it down. It does not abolish it.
  • "Until he is out of the way" (v7). Literally "until he comes out of the midst." The KJV's "he who now letteth will let" uses old English "let," meaning "hinder."
  • "Whom the Lord Jesus will kill with the breath of his mouth" (v8). This is Isaiah 11:4, said of the Messiah from Jesse's line: "with the breath of his lips he shall kill the wicked." After all the buildup, the great enemy goes down without a battle. Jesus only has to breathe.
  • "Bring to nothing by the appearance of his coming" (v8). "Appearance" is epiphaneia, a word used for a god showing himself in visible power, and for an emperor's state visit. Paul uses it for Jesus' return (1 Tim 6:14; Titus 2:13). The phrase "the appearance of his coming" doubles up the language of royal arrival. The lawless one is nullified, put out of action, by the first shining of Christ's presence.
  • "The coming of the lawless one is by the activity of Satan" (v9). Paul uses parousia for the lawless one too. He is a parody of Christ: he has a "coming" and a "revelation," and he does miracles. "Power and false signs and wonders" is the same trio of words used for Jesus' miracles (Acts 2:22). Jesus warned that false christs would do "great signs and wonders, so as to lead astray, if possible, even the elect" (Matt 24:24). Miracles alone do not prove that a message is from God (Deut 13:1–3).
  • "Because they refused to love the truth and so be saved" (v10). They are not deceived innocently. They did not want the truth. "Love of the truth" is a striking phrase. Truth is not just something to know but something to love.
  • "God sends them a strong delusion, so that they may believe what is false" (v11). This is hard, and Paul does not soften it. Scripture often shows God judging sin by handing people over to it. He hardened Pharaoh's heart after Pharaoh hardened his own (Exod 8:15; 9:12). He let a lying spirit deceive Ahab, who wanted to be deceived (1 Kings 22:19–23). In Romans 1 he "gave them up" three times (Rom 1:24, 26, 28). God does not deceive the innocent. He gives those who refuse the truth what they have chosen. "What is false" is literally "the lie," with the article: perhaps the great lie that a man can be God (v4).
  • "All may be condemned who did not believe the truth but had pleasure in unrighteousness" (v12). The root is not intellectual but moral. They did not believe because they enjoyed sin.
  • Commentators: On the restrainer, Henry and JFB both lean toward the power of the Roman Empire and civil order. Henry: "It is supposed to be the power of the Roman empire." JFB, citing Alford and others, calls it the "coercive power" of law, and also allows that the Spirit working through the church may be involved. Guzik takes the restrainer to be the Holy Spirit, whose restraining role ends when the church is removed. On the man of lawlessness, the commentators show their own times. Henry, like many Reformers, says the passage "exactly agrees with the system of popery." JFB is more careful: it sees Rome's claims as "forerunners" of the final Antichrist, who is still to come as an individual, and it treats the temple as literal. Guzik also reads the man of lawlessness as a future individual and the temple as literal, and he rejects the papal reading. JFB on v8: the Lord's "first gleam of His presence" is enough to destroy the lawless one. On v11, Guzik and JFB agree that the delusion is God's judicial sentence on those who first rejected the truth, not an arbitrary act.

Stand Firm (vv13–17)

  • "But we ought always to give thanks to God for you" (v13). The "But" is a sharp turn. After the darkness of vv9–12, Paul turns to the Thessalonians, who are not among the deceived. He repeats the "ought always to give thanks" of 1:3.
  • "Brothers beloved by the Lord" (v13). This echoes Deuteronomy 33:12, "The beloved of the LORD dwells in safety," said of Benjamin. Paul was from the tribe of Benjamin (Phil 3:5). "Lord" here is Jesus.
  • "God chose you as the firstfruits to be saved" (v13). The Greek manuscripts differ by a single letter here. Some read aparchēn, "as firstfruits," and others ap' archēs, "from the beginning." The ESV text takes "firstfruits" and footnotes "from the beginning." Firstfruits were the first part of a harvest given to God, a promise of more to come (Rom 16:5; 1 Cor 16:15). Either way, the point is that God chose them, and his choice comes before theirs. In a chapter about people who are deceived, Paul grounds their security in God's choosing, not their cleverness.
  • "Through sanctification by the Spirit and belief in the truth" (v13). God's choice works out through two means: the Spirit's work making them holy, and their own faith in the truth. Notice the contrast with v10–12. Others refused to love the truth, but they believed it.
  • "To this he called you through our gospel" (v14). God's choosing becomes visible in time when the gospel is preached and believed (1 Thess 1:4–5). The goal is "the glory of our Lord Jesus Christ," sharing in his glory (Rom 8:17, 30).
  • "Stand firm and hold to the traditions" (v15). "Stand firm" is the opposite of "shaken" in v2. "Traditions" (paradosis) means what is handed on. Jesus condemned "the tradition of the elders" when it overrode God's command (Mark 7:8–13), and Paul warns against "human tradition" (Col 2:8). But here "tradition" means the apostles' own teaching, handed on "by our spoken word or by our letter." Catholic and Orthodox Christians see in this verse support for authoritative tradition alongside Scripture. Protestants hold that the apostolic teaching once given orally is now preserved in the written New Testament, which is the final authority. All agree on the main point: hold fast to what the apostles taught, and don't be carried off by new claims.
  • "May our Lord Jesus Christ himself, and God our Father" (v16). Jesus is named first here, the reverse of the usual order (see 1 Thess 3:11). And the verbs after them are singular in Greek: "may [he] comfort… and establish." Paul writes of the Father and the Son acting as one.
  • "Who loved us and gave us eternal comfort and good hope through grace" (v16). The verbs are past tense, pointing to the cross. "Eternal comfort" outlasts every trouble, and "good hope" was a phrase sometimes used in the ancient world for hope beyond death. Paul fills it with real content.
  • "Comfort your hearts and establish them in every good work and word" (v17). "Establish" (stērizō) is the same word used for Timothy's mission (1 Thess 3:2) and Paul's prayer (1 Thess 3:13). Shaken people need to be made steady. Good work and good word belong together.
  • Commentators: JFB argues on v15 that the apostles' oral word "is not a supplementary oral tradition completing our written Word," and Guzik makes the same point. Guzik stresses that God's choosing came before the Thessalonians' choice, and that sanctification is the evidence of being chosen. JFB notes the manuscript split between "first-fruits" and "from the beginning." On v17, Guzik mentions Spurgeon's observation that "work" comes before "word": action before talk.

Pointing to Christ

The man of lawlessness is a dark mirror of Jesus: he has a coming, a revealing, signs and wonders, and a claim to be God. But Jesus' claim is true, and the counterfeit falls by a breath from his mouth. The safety of believers does not rest on working out every detail of the end. It rests on the God who chose them, the Spirit who sanctifies them, the truth they believed, and "our Lord Jesus Christ himself," who loved them and gave them eternal comfort and good hope through grace.

Reflection

  • Not Easily Shaken by False Claims (v.2) — Paul urges the Thessalonians not to be quickly unsettled by reports, even ones claiming his own authority, that the Day of the Lord had already come. How do you respond to sensational claims about prophecy or current events — do you test them against Scripture, or get swept along?
  • Lawlessness Already at Work (v.7) — Paul says the mystery of lawlessness is already active in the world, though currently restrained. Do you see evidence of this active but restrained evil around you, and does knowing there's a restraint on it give you confidence rather than fear?
  • Loving the Truth, Not Just Knowing It (v.10) — Those who perish are described as never having received a love for the truth. Is truth something you embrace affectionately, or merely acknowledge in your head without letting it change you?
  • Chosen for Salvation From the Beginning (v.13) — In the middle of a passage about deception and judgment, Paul reminds the Thessalonians of their election. How does the reality of God's sovereign choice steady you when the world around you feels chaotic?
  • Standing Firm in What You Were Taught (v.15) — Paul calls them to stand fast and hold to the traditions passed down to them. In a world full of shifting opinions and novel teaching, what is the truth you're actually anchored to?
