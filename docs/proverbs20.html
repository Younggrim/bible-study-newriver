Summary

This chapter gathers wisdom on a wide range of subjects, opening with a warning that wine and strong drink lead the deceived astray (v. 1). It touches on the sluggard who refuses to plow in season and has nothing to show at harvest (v. 4), the rarity of genuinely faithful people despite many claiming loyalty (v. 6), and the universal question of who can honestly claim a clean heart (v. 9). Several verses address just kingship and justice (vv. 2, 8, 26, 28), while others condemn dishonest weights and measures as detestable to the Lord (vv. 10, 23). The chapter closes with reminders that the Lord directs a person's steps (v. 24) and that vengeance belongs to him, not to us (v. 22).

Authorship & Background

Author: Primarily Solomon (1:1, 10:1, 25:1), with sections by Agur (ch.30) and King Lemuel (ch.31). Compiled over several centuries. Proverbs is wisdom literature — practical instruction for godly living. Hebrew title: 'Mishlei' — 'Proverbs/Comparisons.'
Historical Context: Chapter 20 opens with one of the strongest warnings about alcohol in Proverbs (v.1) and contains the haunting question "Who can say, I have made my heart clean?" (v.9). The chapter addresses kingship (vv.2, 8, 26, 28), God's sovereignty (vv.12, 24), the problem of human sinfulness (v.9), the sluggard (v.4), dishonest commerce (vv.10, 14, 23), and vengeance belonging to God (v.22). Verse 6 acknowledges the rarity of true faithfulness.
Themes in This Chapter: The Danger of Alcohol (v.1); Universal Sinfulness (v.9); God's Sovereignty Over Man's Steps (v.24); Kingship and Justice (vv.2, 8, 26, 28); Vengeance Belongs to God (v.22); Laziness and Excuses (v.4)
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: a warning about alcohol stated without any softening, wine is a mocker, strong drink is raging, a question that comes as near to a doctrine of universal guilt as the collection gets, who can say, I have made my heart clean, I am pure from my sin, the sharpest piece of market observation in the whole book, it is naught, it is naught, saith the buyer, when he is gone his way, then he boasteth, a prohibition the New Testament later takes up directly, say not thou, I will recompense evil, but wait on the LORD, and a closing pair contrasting the glory of young men's strength with the beauty of old men's gray hair
Wine, Kings and the Clean Heart (vv.1-11): The chapter opens on drink and does not soften it, wine is a mocker, strong drink is raging, and whosoever is deceived thereby is not wise. Then a run on the weight of a king's anger, the honour of leaving off strife, and the sluggard who will not plow by reason of the cold and so shall beg in harvest. The block turns on the question at verse 9, who can say, I have made my heart clean, I am pure from my sin, which is the nearest the collection comes to the doctrine of universal guilt that Paul argues at length in Romans 3. It closes on false weights being an abomination and on the observation that a child is known by his doings.
Trade, Counsel and the Hasty Inheritance (vv.12-21): The middle of the chapter is mostly about buying, selling and borrowing, and it contains the sharpest piece of market observation in the book, it is naught, it is naught, saith the buyer, when he is gone his way, then he boasteth. Around it sit the hearing ear and the seeing eye which the LORD hath made both of them, the warning not to love sleep, the pledge given for a stranger, and bread of deceit that is sweet before it turns to gravel. The block closes on money that arrives too easily, an inheritance may be gotten hastily at the beginning, but the end thereof shall not be blessed.
Vengeance Left to God, and the King's Sifting (vv.22-30): The last stretch begins with a prohibition the New Testament takes up directly, say not thou, I will recompense evil, but wait on the LORD, and he shall save thee. What follows is a group of sayings about limits on human knowledge and human self-rule, man's goings are of the LORD, how can a man then understand his own way, and the spirit of man is the candle of the LORD, searching all the inward parts. The king appears twice, scattering evil with his eyes and preserved by mercy and truth. The chapter ends on a pair about age and about pain that instructs, the glory of young men is their strength, and the beauty of old men is the gray head.

Deep Dive

Setting: The collection headed "The proverbs of Solomon" continues (10:1–22:16; see Proverbs 10 on how it was gathered and how to read a proverb). The ESV has no headings inside it, so the headings below simply group the sayings in order. Chapter 20 opens with wine and closes with wounds. In between, the king appears more than in any chapter so far (vv2, 8, 26, 28), as judge who winnows out evil and as one held up by steadfast love. The LORD appears as the one who made eye and ear (v12), who hates false weights (vv10, 23), who directs every step (v24) and who searches the heart with his lamp (v27). And at the center stands a question no one can answer with a yes: "Who can say, 'I have made my heart pure'?" (v9).

Wine, the King and the Quarrel (vv1–4)

  • "Wine is a mocker, strong drink a brawler, and whoever is led astray by it is not wise" (v1). Wine and "strong drink" (shekar, beer or other fermented drink) are personified: they turn the drinker into a mocker and a brawler. "Led astray" is literally "reels" or "staggers," the verb used of the drunk in Isaiah 28:7. The Tyndale notes are balanced: the Old Testament allows wine in moderation as God's gift (Ps 104:15) and strongly condemns drunkenness (23:29–35; 31:4–5). Henry: drunkards "render themselves fools and madmen, merely for the taste." Paul: "do not get drunk with wine, for that is debauchery, but be filled with the Spirit" (Eph 5:18).
  • "The terror of a king is like the growling of a lion; whoever provokes him to anger forfeits his life" (v2). Compare 19:12. In a world of absolute monarchs, angering the king could be fatal. Esther knew it when she went in uninvited (Esth 4:11, 16), and the Tyndale notes point to her story. JFB: "Men who resist authority injure themselves" (Rom 13:2). Henry draws the obvious lesson: if provoking an earthly king is dangerous, how much more the King of kings.
  • "It is an honor for a man to keep aloof from strife, but every fool will be quarreling" (v3). "Keep aloof" is literally "sit from" strife, which JFB notes means a settled habit of living without it. "Quarreling" is the verb "break out" from 17:14 and 18:1. Anyone can start a fight. It takes character to stay out of one. Abraham gave Lot first choice of the land to keep peace (Gen 13:8–9). Henry: "To engage in quarrels is the greatest folly that can be."
  • "The sluggard does not plow in the autumn; he will seek at harvest and have nothing" (v4). In Israel the rains came in autumn and winter, and plowing and sowing had to happen then, in cold and mud, for a harvest in spring. The word (choreph) means the rainy season. The sluggard waits for nicer weather, then goes looking for a crop that was never planted. "Seek" can mean "beg," as JFB notes. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's mock list of the sluggard's excuses for every kind of weather. Ecclesiastes: "He who observes the wind will not sow" (Eccl 11:4).
  • Commentators: Guzik counsels abstinence for church leaders and clear moderation for others on v1, in line with the Tyndale notes. On v3 Henry says it is wise to avoid strife even at the cost of yielding some rights. Henry applies v4 to spiritual seedtime as well: those who labor now for eternity will reap.

Deep Water and a Faithful Man (vv5–9)

  • "The purpose in a man's heart is like deep water, but a man of understanding will draw it out" (v5). Compare 18:4. A person's real motives lie deep, like water at the bottom of a well, and drawing them out takes a long rope and patience. A wise counselor asks good questions and listens. JFB: counsel in the heart is "deeply hidden." Henry: patient seekers can draw out something worth having. Guzik passes on the image of a deep well needing a long rope and bucket.
  • "Many a man proclaims his own steadfast love, but a faithful man who can find?" (v6). "Steadfast love" is hesed, loyal kindness. Plenty of people announce how loyal they are. Few prove it. JFB: "Boasters are unreliable." The question "who can find?" is asked again of the excellent wife (31:10). Jesus' disciples all promised loyalty the night before they scattered (Matt 26:35, 56). Henry: it is hard to find those who do more good than they talk about.
  • "The righteous who walks in his integrity—blessed are his children after him!" (v7). A parent's integrity is an inheritance that outlasts money. JFB: God's covenant and the parent's example "secure blessing for their children" (Ps 112:2). Guzik passes on the point that this answers the temptation to cut moral corners "for the children's sake."
  • "A king who sits on the throne of judgment winnows all evil with his eyes" (v8). Kings were chief judges (1 Kgs 3:16–28). Winnowing meant tossing grain in the air so the wind blew away the chaff. A just king's searching look sorts the guilty from the innocent. JFB notes this describes "a good king, not of all kings." Isaiah says the Messiah will judge not by what his eyes see but with perfect righteousness (Isa 11:3–4).
  • "Who can say, 'I have made my heart pure; I am clean from my sin'?" (v9). The center of the chapter, and one of the clearest statements of universal sin in the Old Testament. The question expects the answer "no one." Solomon prayed the same at the temple dedication: "there is no one who does not sin" (1 Kgs 8:46). Compare Ecclesiastes 7:20 and Job 14:4. The Tyndale notes: wise people know their weaknesses. Henry: some can say, "Through grace, we are cleaner than we have been," but the credit goes to the Spirit. John: "If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves… If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us" (1 John 1:8–9).
  • Commentators: JFB cites Job 15:14 and Ecclesiastes 7:20 on v9, and Guzik says realizing this drives people to look for a Savior. Guzik and Henry agree on v6 that real faithfulness is rare and doesn't need to advertise.

Weights, Eyes and the Marketplace (vv10–15)

  • "Unequal weights and unequal measures are both alike an abomination to the LORD" (v10). Literally "a stone and a stone, an ephah and an ephah." Merchants carried stones to weigh silver and baskets to measure grain, and a cheat kept two sets, a heavy one for buying and a light one for selling. The law forbade it (Deut 25:13–16). The theme comes back in v23 and earlier in 11:1 and 16:11. The Tyndale notes say merchants must not cheat with false weights. Henry: "The Lord will not bless what is thus gotten." Archaeologists have found Israelite stone weights marked with the same value that differ in actual weight, though not all the difference was dishonest.
  • "Even a child makes himself known by his acts, by whether his conduct is pure and upright" (v11). Character shows early. JFB: conduct is "the best test of principle," and he cites Jesus: "you will recognize them by their fruits" (Matt 7:16). Henry: parents should watch their children so they can guide each one well.
  • "The hearing ear and the seeing eye, the LORD has made them both" (v12). God made our senses, so they should be used for him. There is also a quiet warning in it, which JFB draws from Psalm 94:9: "He who planted the ear, does he not hear? He who formed the eye, does he not see?" The Tyndale notes: the ability to watch and learn is a gift. Jesus often said, "He who has ears to hear, let him hear" (Matt 11:15).
  • "Love not sleep, lest you come to poverty; open your eyes, and you will have plenty of bread" (v13). Sleep is a gift (Ps 127:2). Loving it is the problem. "Open your eyes" means wake up and get to work. Guzik notes the issue is laziness, not hours of sleep.
  • "'Bad, bad,' says the buyer, but when he goes away, then he boasts" (v14). A scene from the bazaar that still plays out today. The buyer runs down the goods to lower the price, then brags about his bargain. JFB: he "goes about boasting of his bargains." It may be only a smile at normal haggling, but Henry sees a lie in it: "a man ought to be ashamed of a fraud and a lie." The Tyndale notes warn sellers not to be fooled.
  • "There is gold and abundance of costly stones, but the lips of knowledge are a precious jewel" (v15). Gold and jewels exist in plenty. Wise speech is the rare treasure. Compare 3:14–15 and 8:11.
  • Commentators: Guzik, citing Waltke, notes that the "abomination" formula is used for false weights three times in Proverbs, and that marketplace honesty and true religion can't be separated. JFB and Henry both apply v12 to God's knowledge of all we do.

Pledges, Gravel and Gossip (vv16–21)

  • "Take a man's garment when he has put up security for a stranger" (v16). A lender could take a cloak as a pledge, though the law required it be returned at night for the poor man to sleep in (Exod 22:26–27). Someone who foolishly guaranteed a stranger's debt should be held to it. The Hebrew of the second line has two readings in the manuscripts: "foreigners" or "a foreign woman," and 27:13, which repeats this verse, has "foreign woman". JFB notes both. Either way the point is the same as 6:1–5 and 11:15: don't be surety for those you can't trust.
  • "Bread gained by deceit is sweet to a man, but afterward his mouth will be full of gravel" (v17). Bread baked from flour ground on stone mills always had some grit in it. Bread full of gravel is ruined, and breaks the teeth. Stolen bread tastes sweet (9:17) and ends in pain. JFB: the gravel "well expresses the pain and grief" at last. Henry: fraud "may be sweet" but "will be bitter in the reflection." Lamentations uses the picture of God making one's "teeth grind on gravel" (Lam 3:16).
  • "Plans are established by counsel; by wise guidance wage war" (v18). Compare 11:14, 15:22 and 24:6. Big decisions, and none bigger than war, need many advisers. Jesus used the same picture: a king considers whether he can win before going to war (Luke 14:31). Henry: "The word and Spirit of God are the best counsellors." Guzik applies it to spiritual warfare (Eph 6:10–18).
  • "Whoever goes about slandering reveals secrets; therefore do not associate with a simple babbler" (v19). The first line repeats 11:13. "Simple babbler" is literally "one who opens his lips," someone who can't keep his mouth shut, and the King James took it as a flatterer. Either way, the person who tells others' secrets will tell yours. Guzik notes some things must be brought into the light and others covered in love (1 Pet 4:8).
  • "If one curses his father or his mother, his lamp will be put out in utter darkness" (v20). The law made cursing parents a capital crime (Exod 21:17; Lev 20:9). "Lamp" stands for life and family line (13:9; 24:20). "Utter darkness" is literally "the pupil of darkness," the blackest center of it. Jesus quotes the law against those who dodged care for their parents (Matt 15:4).
  • "An inheritance gained hastily in the beginning will not be blessed in the end" (v21). The prodigal son demanded his inheritance early and wasted it (Luke 15:12–13). Absalom tried to seize his inheritance, the throne, before his time. JFB: hasty wealth is "contrary to God's providence" (28:20). Henry: "An estate suddenly raised, is often as suddenly ruined."
  • Commentators: Guzik lists people who found stolen bread sweet and then bitter: Adam with the fruit, Esau with the stew, Adonijah at his feast. Guzik and the Tyndale notes both follow "stranger" on v16 for the sake of the parallel lines.

Revenge, Steps and Rash Vows (vv22–25)

  • "Do not say, 'I will repay evil'; wait for the LORD, and he will deliver you" (v22). Proverbs forbids revenge long before the New Testament. The alternative is not doing nothing but waiting for God, who promises to act. Paul quotes this teaching: "Repay no one evil for evil… 'Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord'" (Rom 12:17–19). JFB adds Psalm 27:14. David refused to kill Saul when he had the chance and left judgment to God (1 Sam 24:12). Henry: "Wait on the Lord, attend his pleasure, and he will protect thee."
  • "Unequal weights are an abomination to the LORD, and false scales are not good" (v23). The repeat of v10 is not an accident. God cares about honest trade. Henry: "A bargain made by fraud will prove a losing bargain in the end."
  • "A man's steps are from the LORD; how then can man understand his way?" (v24). "Man" in the first line is geber, a strong man, and in the second adam, any human being. JFB notes this: even the "stately steppings of a strong man" are ordered by God. If that is so, no one fully understands his own path. Compare 16:9 and Jeremiah 10:23. The Tyndale notes say we don't need to understand everything, because God is in control. Henry: "How can we form plans, and conduct business, independently of the Lord?"
  • "It is a snare to say rashly, 'It is holy,' and to reflect only after making vows" (v25). To declare something "holy" meant dedicating it to God, often in a vow. Promising first and thinking later traps a person between breaking the vow and keeping a foolish one. Jephthah's vow is the tragic example (Judg 11:30–40), as the Tyndale notes point out. Ecclesiastes: "It is better that you should not vow than that you should vow and not pay" (Eccl 5:5). Jesus condemned using "Corban" (an offering to God) as a loophole to avoid helping parents (Mark 7:11–13).
  • Commentators: Guzik notes on v22 that waiting for God is not indifference to evil, and passes on the point that only God is fit to avenge because only he knows everything. Guzik also notes the law allowed rash vows to be redeemed, at a cost (Lev 27). Henry reads v25 as people's excuses to their own consciences.

The King's Wheel and the LORD's Lamp (vv26–30)

  • "A wise king winnows the wicked and drives the wheel over them" (v26). Threshing carts with wheels or sledges with stone teeth were driven over grain to break the husk, then the grain was winnowed. JFB: the image denotes severity (Amos 1:3; Isa 28:27–28). Compare v8. Henry: "Justice should crush the wicked, and separate them from the virtuous." John the Baptist used the same picture of Christ: "His winnowing fork is in his hand" (Matt 3:12).
  • "The spirit of man is the lamp of the LORD, searching all his innermost parts" (v27). "Spirit" here is neshamah, "breath," the breath of life God breathed into Adam (Gen 2:7). God gave people an inner light, conscience and self-awareness, and through it he searches the heart. "Innermost parts" is literally "the chambers of the belly." Henry: "The rational soul and conscience are as a lamp within us." JFB connects 1 Corinthians 2:11: "who knows a person's thoughts except the spirit of that person?" The Tyndale notes: God's light reaches deep into people's hearts. Compare Psalm 139:23–24.
  • "Steadfast love and faithfulness preserve the king, and by steadfast love his throne is upheld" (v28). Hesed and emet, the pair that describes God himself (Exod 34:6). A king's real security is not soldiers but loyal love and truth, his own and God's. The Tyndale notes point to God's covenant with David (2 Sam 7). Henry: "Mercy and truth are the glories of God's throne." Isaiah: "a throne will be established in steadfast love, and on it will sit in faithfulness in the tent of David" (Isa 16:5).
  • "The glory of young men is their strength, but the splendor of old men is their gray hair" (v29). Each age has its own beauty. Youth has energy. Age has wisdom and experience, and gray hair is "a crown of glory" (16:31). JFB: each age has its own excellence. Henry: neither should despise or envy the other. Churches need both (1 John 2:13–14).
  • "Blows that wound cleanse away evil; strokes make clean the innermost parts" (v30). "Blows that wound" are literally "bruises of a wound," and "innermost parts" again "chambers of the belly," tying this to v27. Guzik passes on the view that the setting is punishment by the king's officers. The point is that painful correction can reach deep and turn a person from evil. JFB: the stripes heal the soul by deterring from evil. Henry applies it to hardships that purify believers (Heb 12:11).
  • Commentators: Guzik and Waltke see the wheel of v26 going over the grain again and again. Guzik, following Kidner, notes the striking contrast between v30 and Isaiah 53:5, and the word for "blows" here is the same word Isaiah uses for the Servant's "stripes" (chabburah).

Pointing to Christ

Proverbs 20 asks the question every honest heart must answer: "Who can say, 'I have made my heart pure; I am clean from my sin'?" (v9). No one can. But the chapter also says the LORD's lamp searches our innermost parts (v27) and that wounds can cleanse them (v30). The gospel joins these in a way Solomon could not have guessed. The wounds that cleanse were not ours but Christ's: "with his wounds we are healed" (the same word as "blows" in v30) (Isa 53:5), and "the blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin" (1 John 1:7). He is the King who judges with perfect eyes (v8; Isa 11:3–4) and whose throne is upheld forever by steadfast love and faithfulness (v28). And because "vengeance is mine" belongs to him, his people can obey v22 and wait for the LORD rather than repay evil, trusting the one who "when he was reviled, did not revile in return" (1 Pet 2:23).

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik notes that v.1 personifies alcohol as a dangerous companion: "Wine mocks those who trust it; strong drink starts fights with those who rely on it." On v.22, Guzik states that personal vengeance is always premature — God's timing and methods are superior to ours.

Reflection

  • The Deceptive Power of Alcohol (v.1) — Wine and strong drink are pictured as mockers that lead a person astray from wisdom. Where might you be underestimating something's power to deceive you?
  • The Sluggard's Endless Excuses (v.4) — Refusing to work because of the season leaves a person with nothing at harvest. What excuse are you leaning on instead of doing the work in front of you?
  • True Faithfulness Is Rare (v.6) — Many people claim loyalty and goodness, but few can be found who actually live it out consistently. Does your track record match what you claim about yourself?
  • No One Can Claim a Clean Heart (v.9) — The question of who can say their heart is pure exposes universal sinfulness and the need for grace. How does this truth shape the way you extend patience toward others' failures?
  • Leaving Vengeance to God (v.22) — Rather than repaying evil ourselves, we're told to wait on the LORD to make things right. Where are you tempted to take justice into your own hands instead of trusting God's timing?
