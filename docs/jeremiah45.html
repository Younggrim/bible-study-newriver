Summary

The shortest chapter in the book is a brief, personal message directed to Baruch, Jeremiah's scribe, originally given years earlier when Baruch wrote out Jeremiah's scroll (v. 1). Baruch has expressed exhaustion and grief, feeling that God has added sorrow to his suffering and left him no rest (v. 3). God's answer reminds Baruch that He is tearing down what He built and uprooting what He planted throughout the whole land, and asks whether Baruch should be seeking great things for himself in such a time, instructing him not to (vv. 4-5). Even so, God promises to preserve Baruch's life wherever he goes, though it will be like escaping with nothing else (v. 5).

Authorship & Background

Author: Jeremiah son of Hilkiah (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Personal Oracle to Baruch
Key Themes: The faithful scribe's personal crisis, "seek not great things for thyself," God preserving life amid catastrophe, expectations adjusted to the era
Historical Context: Dated to "the fourth year of Jehoiakim" (605 BC) — when Baruch first wrote down Jeremiah's prophecies (ch.36). This short chapter reveals Baruch's personal crisis: writing down 23 years of doom-prophecy has left him exhausted and despairing. He expected more from life — perhaps prestige, comfort, or literary fame. God's word: the world is being torn down. In an era of demolition, don't expect construction projects for yourself. Seek survival, not success. Your life as "prey" (plunder) — you'll escape with your breath.
Baruch's Complaint (vv.1-3): Dated the fourth year of Jehoiakim, which places it with chapter 36, the year of the burned scroll, and it has been set here instead at the close of the biographical section. Baruch gets five verses of his own, which is more than any other prophet's assistant gets in the whole Bible, and what they record is a complaint, woe is me now, for the LORD hath added grief to my sorrow, I fainted in my sighing, and I find no rest. The man who wrote the book down says that writing it wore him out.
Seek Not Great Things for Thyself (vv.4-5): The answer offers no comfort of the ordinary kind. It begins by putting his trouble beside a larger one, behold, that which I have built will I break down, and that which I have planted I will pluck up, even this whole land. Then the instruction, seekest thou great things for thyself, seek them not. And then what he is given in place of the great things, which is precisely what Jeremiah himself was promised and nothing more, for behold, I will bring evil upon all flesh, but thy life will I give unto thee for a prey. Your life, and no more than that. It is the last word spoken to any individual in the book before the oracles against the nations begin.

Setting: This short chapter steps back about twenty years in time. It is dated "in the fourth year of Jehoiakim," 605 BC, the year Babylon crushed Egypt at Carchemish (46:2) and became master of the region. That was the year God told Jeremiah to write all his prophecies on a scroll, and Jeremiah dictated them to his scribe Baruch son of Neriah, who then read them aloud in the temple. King Jehoiakim cut the scroll up and burned it, and ordered both men arrested, "but the LORD hid them" (36:1–26). This chapter is God's word to Baruch at that time. It is placed here, after the last scenes of Jeremiah's ministry in Egypt (ch 44), perhaps because it shows that God kept his promise to Baruch through everything chapters 37–44 describe. In the Greek translation of Jeremiah, where the book's sections are arranged differently, this message also comes straight after the material of chapter 44. It is the only chapter in the book addressed to one ordinary believer.

Message to Baruch (vv1–5)

  • Who was Baruch? (v1). His name means "blessed." He was the son of Neriah and grandson of Mahseiah (32:12), from a family of officials. His brother Seraiah served as King Zedekiah's quartermaster and carried Jeremiah's scroll against Babylon to the Euphrates (51:59–64). Baruch was a trained scribe, someone who wrote legal documents and letters for others, and he witnessed Jeremiah's purchase of the field at Anathoth (32:12–16). He stood with Jeremiah for more than twenty years and was taken with him to Egypt (43:6). He was the kind of man who might have had a respectable career at court, and he gave it up to be the assistant of the most hated man in Jerusalem.
  • The Baruch seal impressions. In the 1970s a clay seal impression, or bulla, came onto the antiquities market reading "belonging to Berekhyahu son of Neriyahu the scribe," the longer form of Baruch's name. A second, which seemed to carry a fingerprint, appeared in the 1990s. For years they were celebrated as a direct link to Baruch himself. Neither was found in a controlled excavation, though, and since 2014 several experts have argued that both are probably modern forgeries. The question is still debated, so the bullae are best treated as uncertain. Baruch's reality does not depend on them.
  • "When he wrote these words in a book at the dictation of Jeremiah, in the fourth year of Jehoiakim" (v1). Literally "from the mouth of Jeremiah." Chapter 36 describes the scene: Jeremiah spoke, and Baruch wrote "all the words of the LORD" with ink on a scroll (36:4, 18). "These words" are the prophecies of judgment from Josiah's thirteenth year to that day, more than twenty years of warnings. Writing them all down was a heavy task in itself, and reading them aloud put Baruch's life in danger.
  • "Thus says the LORD, the God of Israel, to you, O Baruch" (v2). The God of Israel, who is about to judge whole nations, has a word for one tired secretary. The whole of chapters 46–51 will deal with Egypt, Babylon and the nations, but first comes this personal word, as if God stops to speak to one man.
  • "You said, 'Woe is me! For the LORD has added sorrow to my pain'" (v3). God quotes Baruch back to himself. What Baruch said in private, or in his heart, God heard. "Sorrow" is grief of heart, and "pain" is physical or emotional suffering. Baruch feels that God is piling one on the other. Jeremiah himself had cried "Woe is me" years before (15:10) and asked, "Why is my pain unceasing?" (15:18). The scribe is going through what his master went through.
  • "I am weary with my groaning, and I find no rest" (v3). This is almost the wording of a psalm of distress: "I am weary with my moaning" (Ps 6:6). Baruch is exhausted. He has written page after page of coming disaster, read it before hostile crowds, and now has to hide from a king who wants him dead. He finds "no rest," no place to settle. The complaint is honest, and God does not reject him for it. But God does answer it.
  • "Behold, what I have built I am breaking down, and what I have planted I am plucking up—that is, the whole land" (v4). These are the words of Jeremiah's call (1:10), the four verbs that run through the book (18:7–9; 24:6; 31:28; 42:10). God reminds Baruch of the bigger picture. God himself built and planted Israel, and God himself is now tearing it down. If Baruch feels pain, God feels it more. These are God's own people and God's own work being destroyed. Some commentators hear grief in the verse: the builder is breaking what he built.
  • "And do you seek great things for yourself? Seek them not" (v5). This is the heart of the chapter. God puts his finger on what lies under Baruch's weariness. Part of his distress was disappointed hope. Perhaps he hoped for position, recognition, a secure future, or even that his work would make a difference and the nation would repent. The text does not say exactly what "great things" he wanted, and commentators guess at office, fame or prophetic gifts. The point is "for yourself." In a time when the whole nation was falling, building a personal future was like painting a cabin on a sinking ship.
  • "For behold, I am bringing disaster upon all flesh, declares the LORD" (v5). "All flesh" means not only Judah but the nations of chapters 46–51. The whole region was about to be shaken. This was not the time for anyone to seek great things for himself.
  • "But I will give you your life as a prize of war in all places to which you may go" (v5). A "prize of war" is the spoil a soldier carries away from a battle. When everything else is lost, the survivor comes out with one thing: his life. The same phrase is promised to those who surrender to Babylon (21:9; 38:2) and to Ebed-melech, who rescued Jeremiah from the cistern (39:18). God does not promise Baruch comfort, honor or a settled home. He promises him his life, wherever he is driven. "In all places to which you may go" hints that he will be driven from place to place, which is exactly what happened, to hiding, through the siege, to Mizpah and finally to Egypt.
  • Was it kept? Yes. Baruch survived Jehoiakim's arrest order (36:26), the siege and fall of Jerusalem, Ishmael's massacre at Mizpah, and the forced journey to Egypt (43:6). Later Jewish tradition made much of him. The book of Baruch in the Apocrypha is written in his name, along with other writings, but these are much later works and not part of the Hebrew Bible. Scripture's last word on him is this promise.
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry is gentle but direct: "Baruch was a good man, but, we must say, this was his infirmity," and "God takes notice of the frets and discontents of his people and is displeased with them." He pictures the coming ruin of the whole nation and tells Baruch, "It is absurd for thee to be now painting thy own cabin." He finds the root of the complaint in desire: "It is our over-fondness for the good things of this present time that makes us impatient under its evil things." JFB says Jeremiah "does not spare his disciple," and observes that Jeremiah, who once made a similar complaint himself (ch 15), is now able to counsel Baruch. God lets his servants be tested so they can later help others. JFB places the chapter right after 36:8 in time, and Guzik agrees. Guzik notes the many "me" and "my" words in Baruch's complaint and tells how Charles Spurgeon, at about eighteen, recalled "seekest thou great things for thyself? seek them not" when a college place seemed lost. Spurgeon never went to college, yet became one of the best-known preachers in England.

Pointing to Christ

Baruch wanted great things for himself and was told to seek them not. Jesus is the one who truly could have sought great things and did not. Though he was in the form of God, he "made himself nothing, taking the form of a servant" (Phil 2:6–7), and he told his disciples that whoever would be great must be the servant of all (Mark 10:43–45). Baruch was given his life as a prize of war, nothing more. In Christ, believers are promised far more than survival, yet the principle is the same. Whoever tries to save their life will lose it, and whoever loses it for Christ's sake will find it (Matt 16:25). The God who stopped to speak to one tired scribe knows each of his servants by name.

Reflection

  • Honest Exhaustion in Faithful Work (v.3) — Baruch, faithful and obedient, admits he's overwhelmed with grief and weariness. Is it hard for you to admit exhaustion in a season of doing exactly what God has asked of you?
  • God Grieving What He Himself Is Tearing Down (v.4) — God explains that He is the one pulling down what He built and plucking up what He planted, in a season of loss even for Him. Does it change how you carry your own losses to know God isn't distant from the pain of tearing down?
  • Redefining What Counts as Success (v.5) — God tells Baruch not to seek great things for himself, but promises his life will be preserved wherever he goes. In a hard season, are you able to measure success by faithfulness and endurance rather than by personal ambition?
