1In the seventh year of Jehu, Joash became king, and he reigned in Jerusalem forty years. His mother’s name was Zibiah; she was from Beersheba.

2And Joash did what was right in the eyes of the LORD all the days he was instructed by Jehoiada the priest.

3Nevertheless, the high places were not removed; the people continued sacrificing and burning incense there.

4Then Joash said to the priests, “Collect all the money brought as sacred gifts into the house of the LORD—the census money, the money from vows, and the money brought voluntarily into the house of the LORD.

5Let every priest receive it from his constituency, and let it be used to repair any damage found in the temple.”

6By the twenty-third year of the reign of Joash, however, the priests had not yet repaired the damage to the temple.

7So King Joash called Jehoiada and the other priests and said, “Why have you not repaired the damage to the temple? Now, therefore, take no more money from your constituency, but hand it over for the repair of the temple.”

8So the priests agreed that they would not receive money from the people and that they would not repair the temple themselves.

9Then Jehoiada the priest took a chest, bored a hole in its lid, and set it beside the altar on the right side as one enters the house of the LORD. There the priests who guarded the threshold put all the money brought into the house of the LORD.

10Whenever they saw that there was a large amount of money in the chest, the royal scribe and the high priest would go up, count the money brought into the house of the LORD, and tie it up in bags.

11Then they would put the counted money into the hands of those who supervised the work on the house of the LORD, who in turn would pay those doing the work—the carpenters, builders,

12masons, and stonecutters. They also purchased timber and dressed stone to repair the damage to the house of the LORD, and they paid the other expenses of the temple repairs.

13However, the money brought into the house of the LORD was not used for making silver basins, wick trimmers, sprinkling bowls, trumpets, or any articles of gold or silver for the house of the LORD.

14Instead, it was paid to those doing the work, and with it they repaired the house of the LORD.

15No accounting was required from the men who received the money to pay the workmen, because they acted with integrity.

16The money from the guilt offerings and sin offerings was not brought into the house of the LORD; it belonged to the priests.

17At that time Hazael king of Aram marched up and fought against Gath and captured it. Then he decided to attack Jerusalem.

18So King Joash of Judah took all the sacred objects dedicated by his fathers—Jehoshaphat, Jehoram, and Ahaziah, the kings of Judah—along with his own consecrated items and all the gold found in the treasuries of the house of the LORD and the royal palace, and he sent them to Hazael king of Aram. So Hazael withdrew from Jerusalem.

19As for the rest of the acts of Joash, along with all his accomplishments, are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Judah?

20And the servants of Joash rose up and formed a conspiracy and killed him at Beth-millo, on the road down to Silla.

21His servants Jozabad son of Shimeath and Jehozabad son of Shomer struck him down, and he died. And they buried him with his fathers in the City of David, and his son Amaziah reigned in his place.

Summary

Joash becomes king of Judah at age seven after being hidden in the temple during Athaliah's purge, and does what is right throughout the years the priest Jehoiada instructs him, though the high places are never removed (vv. 1-3). Finding that the priests have collected temple-repair funds for years without making repairs, Joash sets up a chest by the altar so offerings go directly toward paying the workmen, who carry out the repairs with such integrity that no formal accounting of the funds is required (vv. 4-16). When Hazael of Syria threatens Jerusalem, Joash strips the temple and palace treasuries of gold to buy him off rather than face him in battle (vv. 17-18). The chapter closes with Joash's own officials conspiring against him and assassinating him, after which his son Amaziah reigns in his place (vv. 19-21).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Jeremiah or a prophetic school. Originally one book with 1 Kings. Covers approximately 250 years (850-586 BC) from Elijah's translation through the Babylonian exile.
Historical Context: Chapter 12 covers the forty-year reign of Joash (Jehoash) of Judah — the child hidden in the Temple who was crowned at age seven. His reign is characterized by one great achievement (Temple repair) and one great failure (buying off Hazael with Temple gold). The critical qualifier in verse 2 defines his entire reign: he did right "all his days wherein Jehoiada the priest instructed him." When Jehoiada's influence ended (through death, per 2 Chronicles 24:17-22), Joash's faithfulness ended with it. He was a king whose righteousness depended entirely on external guidance rather than internal conviction. The chapter also reveals institutional corruption among the priests, who collected Temple repair funds for twenty-three years without making repairs — forcing Joash to create a new system of accountability.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: Righteousness explicitly qualified as lasting only as long as Jehoiada the priest instructed him, twenty-three years passing with temple repair money collected but no repairs made, a chest with a hole in its lid replacing an accounting system that had failed for decades, temple treasures stripped to buy off Hazael by the very king who once restored them, and an assassination motivated, per the fuller account, by the blood of the priest's own son
Joash's Righteousness and Its Limits (vv.1-3): Joash reigns forty years — a long reign suggesting relative stability. His evaluation is positive but qualified: he did right "all his days wherein Jehoiada the priest instructed him" (v.2). The ESV renders this "because Jehoiada the priest instructed him" — making the dependence explicit. His righteousness was derivative, not personal. The high places remain — the persistent compromise that even "good" kings of Judah rarely addressed. The people continued unauthorized worship at local shrines rather than centralizing at Jerusalem.
Temple Repair (vv.4-16): Joash's primary initiative is repairing the Temple — damaged during Athaliah's reign and the years of Baal worship. He assigns the priests to collect funds and make repairs. Twenty-three years pass with no progress — the priests took the money but did not repair the house. Joash confronts them and creates a new system: a chest with a hole in the lid, placed beside the altar, where all offerings go directly. The king's scribe and the high priest count the money together (accountability), then pay the workmen directly. The workmen "dealt faithfully" (v.15) — no accounting was needed because their integrity was established. This passage reveals both institutional failure (priests hoarding funds) and practical wisdom (creating transparent systems). The Temple is repaired through honest labor and faithful giving.
Hazael's Threat and Joash's Failure (vv.17-18): Hazael of Syria conquers Gath and turns toward Jerusalem. Rather than seek God (as Hezekiah will later do in chapter 19), Joash strips the Temple and palace of all gold and sacred gifts — buying Hazael off with the very treasures he had worked to restore. The irony is painful: the king who repaired the Temple now empties it. 2 Chronicles 24:17-25 provides the fuller context: after Jehoiada's death, Joash abandoned God, killed Jehoiada's son Zechariah for prophesying against him, and was defeated by Syria as divine judgment.
Joash's Assassination (vv.19-21): Joash is murdered by his own servants in a conspiracy — the inglorious end of a king who began with such promise. 2 Chronicles 24:25 explains the motive: his servants killed him "for the blood of the sons of Jehoiada the priest." The man saved by a priest's family dies for murdering a priest's son. His reign illustrates the tragedy of borrowed faith — righteousness that depends on another person's influence rather than personal conviction.

Setting: About 835–796 BC in Judah. Joash (also spelled Jehoash), the boy rescued from his grandmother Athaliah's massacre and crowned at seven in the temple courts (ch 11), now reigns for forty years. For most of that time Jehoiada the priest, who hid him and made him king, guides him. Chapter 12 gives the official account of his reign in Kings. 2 Chronicles 24 tells the same story at greater length and adds a darker turn that Kings only hints at: after Jehoiada's death, Joash abandoned the LORD and had Jehoiada's son stoned in the temple courts. Reading the two together explains why a reign that began so well ends with Joash paying off an enemy and dying at the hands of his own officers.

Jehoash Reigns in Judah (vv1–3)

  • "In the seventh year of Jehu, Jehoash began to reign, and he reigned forty years" (v1). About 835–796 BC. Kings now dates Judah's kings by Israel's, and the reverse, as it moves back and forth between the two kingdoms. "His mother's name was Zibiah of Beersheba." Kings names the mothers of Judah's kings because the queen mother held real influence. Beersheba is at Judah's far southern edge, a long way from Jezebel's Phoenicia, and the name may hint that this king's mother was a better influence than Ahaziah's.
  • "Jehoash did what was right in the eyes of the LORD all his days, because Jehoiada the priest instructed him" (v2). The Hebrew can be read two ways: "all his days in which Jehoiada instructed him," or "all his days, because Jehoiada had instructed him." Chronicles settles it: "all the days of Jehoiada the priest" (2 Chr 24:2). Joash's faith leaned on another man's. That is right for a child, and dangerous for a grown king. Guidance from a godly mentor is a gift, but it is not a substitute for one's own walk with God.
  • "Nevertheless, the high places were not taken away" (v3). "High places" were local hilltop shrines. Some were used for worship of other gods, and some for worshiping the LORD outside the temple, contrary to the law's command to worship at the one place God chose (Deut 12:2–14). Even good kings of Judah left them standing (1 Kgs 15:14; 22:43) until Hezekiah and Josiah (18:4; 23:8). The people loved them, and removing them would have been costly. The writer marks it every time.
  • Commentators: Henry notes that a young ruler with good counsel is greatly blessed, and that Joash "did that which was right as long as Jehoiada lived to instruct him." He suggests even the priest may have tolerated the high places while the temple lay in poor repair. JFB credits Joash's right conduct "more to the salutary influence of his preserver and tutor, Jehoiada," and says popular attachment to the high places was too strong to break. Guzik calls this a half-way reform and, citing Patterson and Austel, ties v2 to Joash's later fall under ungodly advisors.

Jehoash Repairs the Temple (vv4–16)

  • Why the temple needed repair. Solomon's temple was now about 125 years old. Chronicles explains the damage: "the sons of Athaliah, that wicked woman, had broken into the house of God, and had also used all the dedicated things of the house of the LORD for the Baals" (2 Chr 24:7). The house of God had been plundered and neglected for years.
  • Three kinds of money (v4). "The money of the holy things" was the general term. Within it Joash names "the money for which each man is assessed," probably the half-shekel census tax that Moses set for the tabernacle (Exod 30:12–16; 2 Chr 24:6, 9); "the money from the assessment of persons," money paid to redeem a vow (Lev 27:1–8); and "the money that a man's heart prompts him to bring," freewill gifts (Exod 35:5, 29). Each priest was to take the money from his own "donor," probably the people he served, and use it to repair the house.
  • "By the twenty-third year of King Jehoash, the priests had made no repairs" (v6). Joash was now about thirty. Twenty-three years into his reign, and maybe many years after the order was given, nothing has been done. Money had come in, but it was going into the priests' own upkeep. It was not necessarily theft, since the priests had to live, but the repairs never got done.
  • "Why are you not repairing the house?" (v7). The king rebukes Jehoiada himself, the man who crowned him, now an old man (2 Chr 24:15 says he lived to 130). Joash takes the work out of the priests' hands. The priests agree: they will stop collecting, and they will not be responsible for the repairs (v8).
  • "Jehoiada the priest took a chest and bored a hole in the lid of it" (v9). This is an early offering box, and a simple one. It was set "beside the altar on the right side as one entered the house," where every worshiper would pass it. Chronicles adds that it was placed "outside the gate," that a proclamation went out across Judah, and that "all the princes and all the people rejoiced and brought their tax and dropped it into the chest until they had finished" (2 Chr 24:8–10). The problem had never been unwilling givers.
  • "The king's secretary and the high priest came up and they bagged and counted the money" (v10). Before coins (which came later), silver was weighed and tied in bags, sometimes sealed and labeled. A royal official and a priest counted it together. Two keys, two witnesses: the system was built so no one person handled the money alone (compare Paul's care in 2 Cor 8:20–21).
  • Carpenters, builders, masons, stonecutters, timber and quarried stone (vv11–12). The money went straight to the work: the supervisors paid the craftsmen and bought the materials.
  • "There were not made for the house of the LORD basins of silver, snuffers, bowls, trumpets" (v13). The structure came first, and the ornaments waited. Chronicles adds that when the repairs were finished, the leftover money was used for vessels of gold and silver (2 Chr 24:14). The two accounts fit: none of the repair money went to vessels while the work was going on.
  • "They did not ask for an accounting from the men… for they dealt honestly" (v15). "Honestly" is literally "in faithfulness" (be'emunah). After all the careful counting at the top, the foremen were trusted. The same phrase is used of the workmen in Josiah's day (22:7). Godly work needs both good systems and trustworthy people.
  • "The money from the guilt offerings and the money from the sin offerings… belonged to the priests" (v16). The law gave the priests a share of certain offerings (Lev 5:16; 7:7; Num 5:8–10). The reform did not take away their living. It only kept the repair money separate.
  • Commentators: Henry draws two lessons that still apply to church finances: "When public distributions are made faithfully public contributions will be made cheerfully," and, on v16, "Let not the servants of the temple be starved under colour of repairing the breaches of it." JFB lists the three sources of money as the half-shekel offering, redemption payments and voluntary gifts, and observes that sealed bags of money were still a common form of currency in the East in its day. Guzik blames the delay on priestly neglect, not a lack of money. Following Dilday and Wiseman, he notes that a visible box and a clear purpose brought out generous giving.

The Death of Joash (vv17–21)

  • What Kings leaves out. Chronicles fills the gap between vv16 and 17. After Jehoiada died, the princes of Judah came and bowed to Joash, and he listened to them. They abandoned the temple and served Asherah poles and idols. God sent prophets, and they would not listen. Then the Spirit came on Zechariah, Jehoiada's son, and he warned, "Because you have forsaken the LORD, he has forsaken you." At the king's command they stoned him "in the court of the house of the LORD," the same courts where Jehoiada had crowned Joash. Zechariah died saying, "May the LORD see and avenge!" (2 Chr 24:17–22). Jesus refers to this murder in Matthew 23:35 and Luke 11:51. Most scholars think he means this Zechariah, and that he names him as the last murder in the Hebrew Bible, since Chronicles comes last in its order, as Abel is the first.
  • "Hazael king of Syria went up and fought against Gath and took it" (v17). Hazael, whom Elisha wept over (8:12), had already taken Israel's land east of the Jordan (10:32–33). Now he marches down the coastal road. Gath was the Philistine city of Goliath, near Judah's western border. Some think a smaller town called Gittaim is meant, but most take it as the Philistine city. Excavations at Tell es-Safi, the likely site of Gath, have found a siege trench and a destruction layer from the late 800s BC, which many scholars connect with Hazael.
  • "When Hazael set his face to go up against Jerusalem" (v17). "Set his face" means he made up his mind. Chronicles adds that a small Syrian army defeated a much larger army of Judah, killed the princes and wounded Joash (2 Chr 24:23–25). It says plainly why: "because Judah had forsaken the LORD."
  • "Jehoash… took all the sacred gifts that Jehoshaphat and Jehoram and Ahaziah his fathers… had dedicated… and sent these to Hazael" (v18). The temple Joash had spent years restoring, he now strips to pay off an enemy. Asa had done the same (1 Kgs 15:18), and later kings would too (16:8; 18:15–16). It bought time. "Then Hazael went away from Jerusalem." But it was a sad picture: the king who once restored God's house now uses God's house to buy his safety instead of turning to God.
  • "His servants arose and made a conspiracy and struck down Joash in the house of Millo" (v20). "Millo" means "filling," probably the terraces built to support buildings on the slope of the City of David (1 Kgs 9:15). Silla is unknown. Chronicles says Joash was lying wounded in bed and that the conspirators acted "because of the blood of the son of Jehoiada the priest" (2 Chr 24:25).
  • "Jozacar the son of Shimeath and Jehozabad the son of Shomer" (v21). Chronicles adds that Shimeath was an Ammonite woman and Shomer (or Shimrith) a Moabite woman (2 Chr 24:26). Both assassins had foreign mothers, which may be a quiet comment on how far Judah's court had drifted. Amaziah, Joash's son, later executed them, but not their children, following the law (14:5–6; Deut 24:16).
  • "They buried him with his fathers in the city of David" (v21). Chronicles adds, "but they did not bury him in the tombs of the kings" (2 Chr 24:25). He lay in David's city but not with David's honored sons. His guardian, Jehoiada the priest, was buried among the kings, "because he had done good in Israel, and toward God and his house" (2 Chr 24:16).
  • Commentators: Henry reads the assassination as God's justice working even through men acting wrongly, and adds, "God usually sets marks of his displeasure upon apostates, even in this life." His summary of the reign: "Thus fell Joash, who began in the spirit and ended in the flesh." JFB has no comment here beyond cross-references to Chronicles. Guzik reads the surrender of the treasures as a failure to trust God, and notes there is no record that Joash ever repented. Citing Knapp, he draws the lesson of sowing and reaping.

Pointing to Christ

Joash's life is a warning in two parts. He was faithful while a godly man held him up, and when that support was gone, his faith showed itself borrowed. He also restored the temple and then stripped it. No king of Judah could be counted on to guard God's house to the end. Jesus is the King whose zeal for God's house never cooled (John 2:17), who said he would raise the true temple in three days (John 2:19–21), and who builds his people into a living temple that cannot be plundered (Eph 2:19–22; 1 Pet 2:5). And the blood of Zechariah, who cried "May the LORD see and avenge!", is answered by "the sprinkled blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel" (Heb 12:24): the blood of the One who prayed for his murderers.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes the tragedy of dependent faith: Joash was righteous only while Jehoiada lived. This is the danger of faith that relies on a mentor, pastor, or spiritual leader rather than developing personal conviction. Guzik notes that the priests' failure to repair the Temple for twenty-three years reveals how institutions can absorb resources without producing results — requiring accountability and structural reform.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Righteousness Borrowed From a Mentor (v.2) — Joash did right only as long as Jehoiada was there to instruct him, revealing that his faith was inherited rather than his own. Is your walk with God rooted in your own conviction, or is it leaning on a pastor, parent, or mentor who could someday be gone?
  • Collecting Funds Without Doing the Work (vv.6-9) — The priests take in Temple-repair offerings for over two decades without ever making the repairs, until Joash creates a transparent system to fix it. Where in your own responsibilities have good intentions substituted for actually finishing the work?
  • Trusted Enough to Skip the Audit (v.15) — The workmen handle the money so faithfully that no one even checks their accounting. What would it look like for your reputation for integrity to be so well-established that people simply trust you?
  • Trading Treasure for Safety Under Threat (vv.17-18) — Facing Hazael's advance, Joash strips the very Temple he restored and hands its gold over rather than seeking God as later kings will. When you're afraid, do you turn first to God or to whatever you can offer up to make the threat go away?
  • A Promising Start That Ends in Betrayal (vv.19-21) — The boy once hidden and saved by a priest's family dies at the hands of his own servants after turning against that same family. Does your life show a trajectory of finishing well, or has your walk with God quietly drifted from where it started?
