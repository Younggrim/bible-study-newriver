Summary

Paul urges that prayers, intercessions, and thanksgiving be offered for everyone, including kings and those in authority, so believers can live peaceful and godly lives, since God desires all people to be saved and come to know the truth (vv. 1-4). He grounds this in the truth that there is one God and one mediator between God and humanity, the man Christ Jesus, who gave himself as a ransom for all, a message Paul was appointed to preach as apostle to the Gentiles (vv. 5-7). He then instructs men to pray without anger or arguing, women to dress modestly and pursue good works rather than showy adornment, and women to learn in quietness and submission rather than teaching or exercising authority over men in the assembly, pointing to the order of creation and the deception of Eve (vv. 8-14). The chapter closes with the promise that women will be preserved through childbearing if they continue in faith, love, holiness, and self-control (v. 15).

Authorship & Background

Author: Paul the Apostle (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 2 addresses public worship in the church at Ephesus. Paul gives instructions on prayer (vv.1-8) and the conduct of women in worship (vv.9-15). Ephesus was home to the temple of Artemis (Diana) — a center of female-dominated pagan worship. The false teachers in Ephesus may have been influencing women in the church (cf. 2 Tim 3:6-7). Paul's instructions address the specific situation while establishing principles for church order.
Classification: Epistle — Pauline, Pastoral Epistle
Key Themes: Prayer urged for all men and specifically for rulers before any other instruction, one mediator and one ransom stated against a backdrop of many, modesty in dress set against costly adornment as the real point of contrast, teaching authority restricted and grounded in the order of creation rather than culture, and a disputed final verse that has divided readers for centuries
Prayer for All (vv.1-8): Paul urges prayer for "all men" — especially rulers and authorities (vv.1-2). The goal: a peaceful life in godliness (v.2). God desires all to be saved (v.4). There is one God and one mediator: Christ Jesus (v.5), who gave Himself as a ransom for all (v.6). Men should pray everywhere with holy hands, without anger or quarreling (v.8).
Women in Worship (vv.9-15): Women should adorn themselves with modesty and good works, not expensive clothing (vv.9-10). Paul restricts women from teaching or exercising authority over men in the church assembly (vv.11-12), grounding this in creation order (v.13) and the fall (v.14). Verse 15 is one of the most debated verses in the NT.

Deep Dive

Setting: Paul is still writing to Timothy in Ephesus, probably in the early 60s AD (see the setting for chapter 1). Chapter 1 dealt with the false teachers. Now Paul turns to the life of the church when it gathers, and chapters 2–3 belong together: how God's household should pray, how men and women should conduct themselves in worship, and who should lead (3:15). The background matters. The emperor was Nero, who had not yet turned on the Christians but soon would. Ephesus was the home of the great temple of Artemis, a city of wealth and display, and the false teaching seems to have spread especially through households and among some women (5:13; 2 Tim 3:6). The chapter contains one of the most debated paragraphs in the New Testament (vv11–15).

Pray for All People (vv1–15)

  • "First of all, then" (v1). "Then" ties this to chapter 1. The cure for a church drifting into speculation begins with prayer. "First of all" means first in importance, not just first in order.
  • "Supplications, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgivings" (v1). Four words, not four strict categories. Supplications are requests born of need. Prayers is the general word. Intercessions are appeals on behalf of others. Thanksgivings keep gratitude in the mix. Together they mean: pray in every way.
  • "For all people" (v1). This is the chapter's theme word. "All" appears again in vv2, 4 and 6. The false teachers' interest in genealogies and special knowledge narrowed the circle. Paul throws it wide open.
  • "For kings and all who are in high positions" (v2). The emperor was Nero. Jews in the temple offered sacrifices for the emperor's welfare rather than to him, and Jeremiah had told the exiles to "seek the welfare of the city" where they lived (Jer 29:7). Christians refused to worship Caesar, but they prayed for him. Early writers such as Tertullian made a point of this when Christians were accused of disloyalty.
  • "That we may lead a peaceful and quiet life, godly and dignified" (v2). The aim is not comfort for its own sake. A church left in peace can live out and spread the gospel without needless obstacles. "Godliness" (eusebeia) is a key word of these letters, used about ten times: true reverence for God shown in a whole way of life.
  • "Who desires all people to be saved" (v4). God's heart reaches to everyone, rulers included. Christians differ on how this verse relates to God's choosing. Arminian and Wesleyan readers take it at full stretch: God genuinely wills every person's salvation, and people can refuse him. Many Reformed readers take "all people" as "all kinds of people," matching vv1–2 (kings and commoners, Jews and Gentiles), or distinguish what God desires from what he decrees. Both agree on the practical point: no one is outside the reach of the church's prayer and preaching.
  • "There is one God, and there is one mediator" (v5). This has the rhythm of an early creed, and it echoes Israel's confession, "the LORD is one" (Deut 6:4). Because there is one God over all, his salvation is for all (Rom 3:29–30). A "mediator" (mesitēs) is a go-between who brings two estranged parties together. In a city crowded with gods, shrines and priests, Paul says there is only one way to God.
  • "The man Christ Jesus" (v5). Paul stresses Jesus' humanity. To stand between God and humanity, the mediator must truly belong to both sides. Jesus did not stop being human after the resurrection. He mediates now as the risen man (Heb 7:25).
  • "Gave himself as a ransom for all" (v6). "Ransom" is antilytron, a rare strengthened form: a price paid in place of someone. It echoes Jesus' words in Mark 10:45, "to give his life as a ransom for many." Paul's "for all" fits the chapter's theme.
  • "The testimony given at the proper time" (v6). Christ's coming and death were God's witness to the world, at the moment he had chosen (Gal 4:4).
  • "I am telling the truth, I am not lying" (v7). Timothy didn't doubt Paul, but the church in Ephesus would hear this letter read aloud, and some there were questioning Paul's authority. His calling as "teacher of the Gentiles" is itself proof that God's salvation is for all peoples.
  • "In every place the men should pray, lifting holy hands" (v8). "In every place" probably means wherever the churches gather (compare 1 Cor 1:2; Mal 1:11). Standing with hands raised was the normal posture for prayer among Jews and early Christians (1 Kings 8:22; Ps 28:2). What matters is that the hands are "holy," not stained with sin (Isa 1:15). "Men" here is anēr, males specifically, since Paul is now speaking to men and women in turn.
  • "Without anger or quarreling" (v8). Paul's word to the men is pointed. The false teaching bred "dissension, slander, evil suspicions" (6:4). Men who fight over words can't pray together. Jesus taught the same: be reconciled before you bring your gift (Matt 5:23–24).
  • "Women should adorn themselves in respectable apparel" (v9). Paul's word to the women is about display. "Modesty" here has a sense of decency and self-respect. "Self-control" (sōphrosynē) means sound judgment and restraint.
  • "Not with braided hair and gold or pearls or costly attire" (v9). In Paul's world, elaborate braided hairstyles woven with gold and pearls were marks of the very wealthy, and sometimes of courtesans. A rich woman arriving at worship dressed like that would draw attention to herself and away from God, and would shame poorer believers. Peter says much the same (1 Pet 3:3–4). The point is not that every braid is sinful, but that worship is no place for showing off wealth or allure.
  • "With good works" (v10). The real adornment of a woman who "professes godliness" is what she does for others. Dorcas, "full of good works" (Acts 9:36), is the picture.
  • "Let a woman learn quietly with all submissiveness" (v11). This was a positive command in its day. Many Jewish teachers of the time did not teach women the law, and Greek education was mostly for men. Paul says women must learn. "Quietly" is hēsychia, which can mean quietness, a calm and peaceable manner, or silence. A related adjective appears in v2 for the "quiet" life the whole church should lead, which suggests a settled, receptive spirit, not a gag. "Submissiveness" is the usual word for ordered submission, with no suggestion that women are inferior.
  • "I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man" (v12). "Exercise authority" is authenteō, found only here in the New Testament. The lexicons give "to have authority over" or "govern," and in some older and later Greek it carries a harsher sense of "domineer." This verse is the heart of a long and sincere disagreement:
  • The main views. Complementarians (much of the Reformed, Baptist and Catholic tradition) read v12 as a lasting principle: the office of authoritative teacher and elder in the church belongs to qualified men. They point out that Paul grounds it in creation (v13), not in local custom, and that the elder qualifications in 3:1–7 follow at once. Most of them stress that women teach other women and children (Titus 2:3–5), pray and prophesy (1 Cor 11:5), and serve widely. Egalitarians (many Pentecostal, Methodist and other traditions) read v12 as a specific ruling for a church where false teaching was spreading through untaught women, or as a ban on domineering, self-appointed teaching rather than all teaching. They point to Priscilla teaching Apollos (Acts 18:26), women co-workers like Phoebe and Junia (Rom 16:1, 7), and "neither male nor female" (Gal 3:28). Both sides take the text as the word of God. They differ on how far its application reaches.
  • "For Adam was formed first, then Eve" (v13). Paul reaches back to Genesis 2, where the man is formed first and given the command before the woman is made (Gen 2:7, 15–22). He makes a similar argument in 1 Corinthians 11:8–9, though there he adds that "in the Lord" man and woman depend on each other (1 Cor 11:11–12). Complementarians take this as the decisive reason for v12. Egalitarians suggest Paul is correcting a local myth that exalted Eve, perhaps tied to the Artemis cult in Ephesus, but the evidence for such teaching in the church is debated.
  • "Adam was not deceived, but the woman was deceived" (v14). Genesis 3:13 has Eve say, "The serpent deceived me." Adam sinned with his eyes open. Elsewhere Paul lays the main responsibility for the fall on Adam (Rom 5:12–19), so this is not a claim that women are guiltier. The point seems to be that when the God-given order was reversed in Eden, deception followed. Some also see a warning for Ephesus, where deceivers were preying on women (2 Tim 3:6), as the serpent once did.
  • "Yet she will be saved through childbearing" (v15). One of the hardest verses in Paul. "She" is Eve, standing for women in general, and "they" then shifts to Christian women. The main readings: (1) women are saved (in the full sense) while living out their God-given calling, with motherhood as the typical example, not as a work that earns salvation. Henry links this to the hope given in Genesis 3. (2) Women will be kept safe through childbirth, though faithful women do die in childbirth. (3) "Through the childbearing" (the Greek has the article), meaning the birth of the Messiah, the woman's offspring promised in Genesis 3:15. Through a woman's child salvation came. All readings agree that the condition is real faith: "if they continue in faith and love and holiness, with self-control."
  • The Ephesian background. The false teachers forbade marriage (4:3), and some younger widows were going from house to house spreading what they shouldn't (5:13–15). Against teaching that despised marriage and motherhood, v15 honours them. This background helps on almost any view of vv11–14.
  • Commentators: On v2 JFB says praying for rulers was "an effectual confutation" of those who accused Christians of disloyalty. On v6 JFB stresses substitution: "a substituted or equivalent ransom." Henry: "There is one Mediator, and that Mediator gave himself a ransom for all." Guzik argues the church should never limit its evangelism by guessing who is chosen. On v8 Henry: "We must pray in charity; without wrath, or malice, or anger at any person." On v10 Henry: "Good works are the best ornament." On vv11–14 all three take the complementarian reading. Guzik claims the word in v11 is the same one translated "peaceable" in v2; strictly, v2 uses a related adjective (hēsychios), though the point about a calm spirit still holds, and the lexicon places 2:11–12 under "silence." JFB allows that a woman "might teach, but not in public." Henry and JFB read v14 as saying women are more easily deceived, a view the text doesn't plainly state, and the Tyndale notes take a more cautious line that leaves room for a local cause. On v15 Guzik prefers the reading "the childbirth," the birth of the Messiah; Henry links the verse to the Saviour born of a woman; JFB takes it as the setting "amidst which" a woman's salvation is lived out.

Pointing to Christ

At the centre of a chapter about prayer and order stands "one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus, who gave himself as a ransom for all." That is why the church can pray for Nero, why every kind of person can be reached, and why men need not quarrel or women compete in display: there is one way to God, and it is open. Even the hardest verse points toward the Child born of a woman, the promised offspring who crushed the serpent's head. However Christians settle the debated lines, the chapter's heart is the ransom paid for all.

Reflection

  • Praying for Everyone, Including Leaders (vv.1-2) — Paul urges prayer for all people, specifically naming those in authority, so the church can live out its faith in peace. Does your prayer list include people in power you don't naturally agree with or admire?
  • One Mediator Between God and Man (v.5) — Paul states plainly that there is only one go-between connecting God and humanity: Christ Jesus. Do you approach God directly through Christ, or do you sometimes act as if you need something more?
  • A Ransom Offered to All (v.6) — Christ gave Himself as the payment for freedom, and that offer is extended to everyone, not a select few. Who in your life needs to hear that this gift is genuinely available to them?
  • Prayer That's Free of Anger (v.8) — Paul ties effective prayer to a heart free from anger and quarreling. What unresolved conflict might be getting in the way of your own prayer life right now?
  • Adornment That Actually Matters (vv.9-10) — Paul redirects attention from outward appearance to a life marked by good works and reverence for God. Where is your effort mostly invested — in how you look, or in what you do?
