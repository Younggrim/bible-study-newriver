1Am I not free? Am I not an apostle? Have I not seen Jesus our Lord? Are you yourselves not my workmanship in the Lord?

2Even if I am not an apostle to others, surely I am to you. For you are the seal of my apostleship in the Lord.

3This is my defense to those who scrutinize me:

4Have we no right to food and to drink?

5Have we no right to take along a believing wife, as do the other apostles and the Lord’s brothers and Cephas?

6Or are Barnabas and I the only apostles who must work for a living?

7Who serves as a soldier at his own expense? Who plants a vineyard and does not eat of its fruit? Who tends a flock and does not drink of its milk?

8Do I say this from a human perspective? Doesn’t the Law say the same thing?

9For it is written in the Law of Moses: “Do not muzzle an ox while it is treading out the grain.” Is it about oxen that God is concerned?

10Isn’t He actually speaking on our behalf? Indeed, this was written for us, because when the plowman plows and the thresher threshes, they should also expect to share in the harvest.

11If we have sown spiritual seed among you, is it too much for us to reap a material harvest from you?

12If others have this right to your support, shouldn’t we have it all the more? But we did not exercise this right. Instead, we put up with anything rather than hinder the gospel of Christ.

13Do you not know that those who work in the temple eat of its food, and those who serve at the altar partake of its offerings?

14In the same way, the Lord has prescribed that those who preach the gospel should receive their living from the gospel.

15But I have not used any of these rights. And I am not writing this to suggest that something be done for me. Indeed, I would rather die than let anyone nullify my boast.

16Yet when I preach the gospel, I have no reason to boast, because I am obligated to preach. Woe to me if I do not preach the gospel!

17If my preaching is voluntary, I have a reward. But if it is not voluntary, I am still entrusted with a responsibility.

18What then is my reward? That in preaching the gospel I may offer it free of charge, and so not use up my rights in preaching it.

19Though I am free of obligation to anyone, I make myself a slave to everyone, to win as many as possible.

20To the Jews I became like a Jew, to win the Jews. To those under the law I became like one under the law (though I myself am not under the law), to win those under the law.

21To those without the law I became like one without the law (though I am not outside the law of God but am under the law of Christ), to win those without the law.

22To the weak I became weak, to win the weak. I have become all things to all people so that by all possible means I might save some.

23I do all this for the sake of the gospel, so that I may share in its blessings.

24Do you not know that in a race all the runners run, but only one receives the prize? Run in such a way as to take the prize.

25Everyone who competes in the games trains with strict discipline. They do it for a crown that is perishable, but we do it for a crown that is imperishable.

26Therefore I do not run aimlessly; I do not fight like I am beating the air.

27No, I discipline my body and make it my slave, so that after I have preached to others, I myself will not be disqualified.

1Am I not an apostle? am I not free? have I not seen Jesus Christ our Lord? are not ye my work in the Lord?

4Have we not power to eat and to drink?

22To the weak became I as weak, that I might gain the weak: I am made all things to all men, that I might by all means save some.

23And this I do for the gospel’s sake, that I might be partaker thereof with you.

24Know ye not that they which run in a race run all, but one receiveth the prize? So run, that ye may obtain.

25And every man that striveth for the mastery is temperate in all things. Now they do it to obtain a corruptible crown; but we an incorruptible.

26I therefore so run, not as uncertainly; so fight I, not as one that beateth the air:

27But I keep under my body, and bring it into subjection: lest that by any means, when I have preached to others, I myself should be a castaway.

Summary

Paul defends his apostolic authority, pointing to his eyewitness encounter with the risen Christ and the Corinthians themselves as proof of his ministry, and argues from common sense, Old Testament law, and the temple system that gospel workers have a right to material support from those they serve (vv. 1-14). Yet he has deliberately not used this right, preferring to preach the gospel free of charge so that no one can accuse him of doing it for profit, since preaching itself is simply an obligation laid on him (vv. 15-18). Though free from everyone, Paul has made himself a servant to all, adapting his approach to Jews, to those under the law, to those without the law, and to the weak, becoming all things to all people so that he might save some by every possible means (vv. 19-22). He compares this discipline to an athlete training for a race, exercising self-control in all things to win a prize that, unlike a perishable wreath, will last forever (vv. 23-25). He closes by describing his own rigorous self-discipline, treating his body like an opponent to be mastered, so that after preaching to others he himself would not be disqualified (vv. 26-27).

Authorship & Background

Author: Paul (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 9 is Paul's personal example of the principle from chapter 8 — voluntarily limiting freedom for the Gospel's sake. He had the right to financial support, to marriage, to stop working — but he gave up these rights to remove any barrier to the Gospel. The chapter ends with the athletic metaphor of self-discipline.
Classification: Epistle — Pauline
Key Themes: Real rights established from common sense, the Law and Jesus' own command before any of them are surrendered, none of those rights actually used so no accusation of financial motive could stick, becoming all things to all men kept inside a chapter about surrendering rights rather than convictions, and an athlete's discipline applied to Paul's own body lest he himself be disqualified after preaching to others
Paul's Rights (vv.1-14): Paul establishes that he has legitimate rights as an apostle: the right to eat and drink (v.4), to be married (v.5), to stop working and be supported by the churches (v.6). He proves this from common sense (v.7), the Law (v.9), and Jesus' command (v.14). His rights are real and legitimate.
Paul's Voluntary Surrender (vv.15-18): "But I have used none of these things" (v.15). Paul gave up his rights voluntarily — not because they were wrong but because using them might hinder the Gospel. His reward: preaching the Gospel "without charge" (v.18) — removing any accusation of financial motivation.
All Things to All Men (vv.19-23): Though I be free from all men, yet have I made myself servant unto all. What follows is a list of accommodations, to the Jews I became as a Jew, to them that are under the law as under the law, to the weak I became as weak, and it has been quoted as an excuse for having no convictions ever since. The passage guards itself twice. The stated aim each time is that I might gain them, and the whole list sits inside a chapter about surrendering rights rather than principles. The summary is I am made all things to all men, that I might by all means save some, where the some is as honest as the all.
The Athletic Metaphor (vv.24-27): Paul compares the Christian life to athletic competition — running to win, exercising self-discipline, fighting with purpose. "I keep under my body, and bring it into subjection" (v.27) — lest after preaching to others, he himself be "a castaway" (disqualified).

Deep Dive

Setting: Paul writes from Ephesus in about AD 53–55 (16:8). Chapters 8–10 answer a question the Corinthians had sent him about food offered to idols. Some in the church, the "strong," knew an idol was nothing and felt free to eat temple meat, even at dinners in a pagan temple. Paul agreed that "an idol has no real existence" (8:4), but warned that their freedom could wound a "weak" brother whose conscience could not handle it. Chapter 8 ended: "I will never eat meat, lest I make my brother stumble" (8:13). Chapter 9 can look like a detour into Paul's pay and apostleship, but it is the heart of the argument. Paul offers himself as the example of a man with real rights who gives them up for others. Some in Corinth were also questioning his standing, partly because he refused to take their money (2 Cor 11:7–9), so the chapter is both a lesson and a defense. Chapter 10 returns to idol feasts, and the whole section closes with "Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ" (11:1).

Paul Surrenders His Rights (vv1–27)

  • "Am I not free? Am I not an apostle?" (v1). Four questions, each expecting "yes." "Free" picks up the word the strong used for themselves. If anyone in Corinth had freedom and rights, Paul did. The word for "right" that runs through the chapter (vv4, 5, 6, 12, 18) is exousia, the same word translated "liberty" in 8:9 ("this right of yours"). The lexicon gives its core sense as freedom or power to act. Paul is deliberately using the strong's own vocabulary.
  • "Have I not seen Jesus our Lord?" (v1). An apostle in the full sense was a witness of the risen Christ, sent by him personally (Acts 1:21–22). Paul's claim rests on the Damascus road (Acts 9:3–6) and he will list it with the other resurrection appearances in 15:8, "last of all… he appeared also to me."
  • "You are the seal of my apostleship in the Lord" (v2). A seal (sphragis) was the impression of a signet ring pressed into wax or clay to mark a document as genuine. The Corinthian church itself is Paul's certificate. Whatever others thought of him, the people he brought to faith (Acts 18:1–11) could hardly deny it.
  • "This is my defense to those who would examine me" (v3). Both words come from the courtroom. Apologia is a speech in defense (the word in Acts 22:1 and 1 Pet 3:15), and "examine" is anakrinō, the cross-questioning verb from 4:3. The Corinthians had put their teachers on trial in chapter 4, and some were still doing it.
  • "The right to eat and drink" (v4). Not idol food this time, but the right to be fed and housed at the church's expense while preaching (Luke 10:7–8).
  • "A believing wife" (v5). The Greek is literally "a sister as wife," as the ESV footnote notes: a fellow believer. The other apostles traveled with their wives, and the church supported both. "The brothers of the Lord" are James, Joses, Simon and Judas (Mark 6:3). Christians have long differed on who they were: most Protestants take them as later children of Mary and Joseph, Eastern Orthodox tradition as Joseph's sons by an earlier marriage, and Catholic tradition, following Jerome, as cousins. Cephas (Peter) was married, since Jesus healed his mother-in-law (Mark 1:30). Early church tradition remembered Peter's wife as a faithful believer who went with him on his travels.
  • "Only Barnabas and I" (v6). Paul and Barnabas had split over Mark (Acts 15:39), yet Paul still names him warmly as a partner in the same costly practice: both worked with their hands to support themselves.
  • Soldier, vinedresser, shepherd (v7). Three everyday pictures. No soldier pays for his own rations, no farmer plants a vineyard and never tastes the grapes, no shepherd goes without milk from the flock.
  • "You shall not muzzle an ox when it treads out the grain" (v9). Paul moves from common sense to Scripture, quoting Deuteronomy 25:4. An ox walked in circles over the grain on a threshing floor, and a muzzle would stop it eating as it worked. The command sits among laws about fair treatment of people (Deut 24–25), which helps explain how Paul reads it.
  • "Is it for oxen that God is concerned?" (vv9–10). Paul is not saying God does not care about animals. Scripture plainly says he does (Ps 104:27; Jonah 4:11). His point is about purpose: the law's deepest aim is not livestock but people. Jewish teachers often argued from lesser to greater this way: if an ox may eat from the grain it threshes, how much more the one who plows and threshes "in hope of sharing in the crop." 1 Timothy 5:18 later puts Deuteronomy 25:4 side by side with Jesus' saying "the laborer deserves his wages" and calls both Scripture.
  • "If we have sown spiritual things among you" (v11). The harvest image again. Eternal life given, a meal or a roof in return. Paul uses the same reasoning for the Jerusalem collection (Rom 15:27).
  • "We endure anything rather than put an obstacle in the way of the gospel" (v12). This is the turning point. Others had received support at Corinth, and Paul as founder had the stronger claim, but he never used it. "Obstacle" (enkopē) is a rare word. Commentators often describe it as a military term for breaking up a road to slow an enemy. The lexicon gives "incision" and "hindrance," which fits the idea of a cut in the road, though the specific military picture is not in the entry. The aim is plain: nothing in Paul's conduct must block the way to Christ. In Corinth, a traveling teacher who took money was easily lumped in with paid philosophers and orators, and a teacher funded by a wealthy patron could be seen as that patron's man. Paul would not let the gospel look like either.
  • "Those who serve at the altar share in the sacrificial offerings" (v13). Israel's priests and Levites ate portions of the offerings (Num 18:8–32; Deut 18:1–4). Pagan priests in Corinth's temples did the same, so the picture would make sense to every reader, Jewish or Gentile.
  • "The Lord commanded that those who proclaim the gospel should get their living by the gospel" (v14). This is one of the few places Paul directly cites a teaching of Jesus (also 7:10 and 11:23–25). It points to Jesus sending out his disciples: "the laborer deserves his wages" (Luke 10:7; Matt 10:10). So Paul's rights rest on four supports: apostleship, common sense, the Law of Moses, and the words of Jesus. That makes his refusal all the more striking.
  • "I would rather die than have anyone deprive me of my ground for boasting" (v15). The Greek sentence actually breaks off in the middle, as if Paul's feeling interrupts his grammar: "better for me to die than… no one shall empty my boast!" "Ground for boasting" (kauchēma) is not pride in himself. It is the one thing he can call his own, which v18 will name.
  • "Necessity is laid upon me. Woe to me if I do not preach the gospel!" (v16). Preaching itself earns Paul nothing, because he was commissioned and could not refuse (Acts 26:16–18). "Necessity" (anankē) is compulsion. Jeremiah felt the same: the word was "like a burning fire shut up in my bones" (Jer 20:9).
  • "I am still entrusted with a stewardship" (v17). This is a hard verse, but the idea is clear enough. A freeman who chooses a job is paid for it. A slave given charge of his master's house (oikonomia, stewardship) is simply doing his duty. Paul puts himself in the second group, the steward of 4:1–2 who must be "found faithful." His preaching is not a favor to God.
  • "What then is my reward? That… I may present the gospel free of charge" (v18). The irony is deliberate: Paul's pay is to take no pay. Adapanos, "free of charge," appears only here in the New Testament. He did accept gifts from other churches, such as Philippi, when he was not living among them (2 Cor 11:8–9; Phil 4:15–16). The principle was never "ministers must not be paid" (v14 says the opposite). It was that Paul would not let money, in Corinth, give anyone a reason to dismiss the message.
  • "Though I am free from all, I have made myself a servant to all" (v19). "Made myself a servant" is the verb douloō, to enslave. The freest man in the room chooses slavery, "that I might win more of them." "Win" (kerdainō) is a trader's word for making a profit, used five times in vv19–22. Paul has given up financial gain for a different kind of gain: people.
  • "To the Jews I became as a Jew" (v20). Among Jews Paul kept Jewish customs: he circumcised Timothy (Acts 16:3), took a vow (Acts 18:18) and joined in temple purification in Jerusalem (Acts 21:23–26). The words "though not being myself under the law" are missing from the later manuscripts behind the King James Version, but the earliest manuscripts include them, and modern translations follow them. They matter: Paul kept these customs as a free choice for the sake of others, not because his standing with God depended on them (Gal 2:16).
  • "Not being outside the law of God but under the law of Christ" (v21). Among Gentiles he ate their food and did not impose Jewish customs (Gal 2:11–14). But "outside the law" never meant lawless. Paul coins a phrase, ennomos Christou, "in-lawed to Christ," which the lexicon glosses as under law in relation to Christ. It sets up a wordplay: anomos, "without law," against ennomos, "within law." Paul is bound, but by Christ and his command of love (Gal 6:2), not by Moses.
  • "To the weak I became weak" (v22). This is the line that ties the chapter back to chapter 8. Notice the "as" drops out: not "as one weak," but simply "weak." Some later manuscripts add the "as," which is why the King James has "as weak," but the earlier text is more striking. Paul does not pretend. He actually limits himself, as he promised in 8:13 to give up meat. "All things to all people, that by all means I might save some." This is not a license for changing the message or joining in sin. Paul refuses idol feasts in the very next chapter (10:14–22). He flexes in things God has left free, never in what God has commanded or in the gospel itself (Gal 1:8).
  • "That I may share with them in its blessings" (v23). Literally, that he may be a "co-sharer" in the gospel. Paul is not standing above his converts handing out blessings. He wants to be among those who receive them, and that leads straight into the warning of vv24–27.
  • "In a race all the runners run, but only one receives the prize" (v24). The Corinthians knew this world well. The Isthmian Games were held every two years at the sanctuary of Poseidon on the Isthmus, about ten miles from the city, and Corinth ran them. They were among the four great festivals of Greece. Paul lived in Corinth for eighteen months around AD 50–51 (Acts 18:11), and many scholars think the games were held while he was there, though that is an informed guess. "Prize" (brabeion) is the word for a prize in the games, used again in Philippians 3:14. The point is not that only one Christian will be saved, but that every believer should run the way a would-be winner runs: "So run that you may obtain it."
  • "Every athlete exercises self-control in all things" (v25). The verb (enkrateuomai) is the same one used in 7:9 for sexual self-control, and its noun is the last fruit of the Spirit (Gal 5:23). Athletes gave up rich food, wine and comfort for months of training. "A perishable wreath" was the victor's crown (stephanos), a wreath of leaves, not a king's diadem. At the Isthmian Games it was made of pine, or at some periods wild celery, which withered within days. The Tyndale notes speak of laurel, but that was the prize at Delphi, not at Corinth's own games. Either way the contrast stands: the athlete's crown dies on his head, and the believer's is "imperishable" (2 Tim 4:8; 1 Pet 5:4).
  • "I do not run aimlessly; I do not box as one beating the air" (v26). Paul switches from runner to boxer. A runner without a finish line or a boxer swinging at nothing wastes all his effort. Paul's self-denial has a target.
  • "I discipline my body and keep it under control" (v27). Two vivid verbs. "Discipline" is hypōpiazō, literally to strike under the eye, to give a black eye. The only other New Testament use is in Jesus' parable of the persistent widow, where the judge fears she will wear him out (Luke 18:5). "Keep it under control" is doulagōgeō, to make a slave of, from "slave" and "lead." Paul is not hating his body or punishing it for its own sake. In a chapter about giving up rights, he means refusing to let appetite and comfort run his life, so that he can give them up for others.
  • "Lest after preaching to others I myself should be disqualified" (v27). "Preaching" here (kēryssō) is the word for what a herald did, and a herald at the games announced the contests and called the athletes. The picture is of the herald who calls others to the race and then is disqualified from it himself. "Disqualified" is adokimos: the lexicon's first sense is metal that fails the test (Prov 25:4; Isa 1:22), and for persons, "rejected after testing." Paul uses the same word for those God gave up to a debased mind (Rom 1:28) and for failing the test of faith (2 Cor 13:5–7). Chapter 10 begins at once with "For," and the example of Israelites who were baptized into Moses, ate spiritual food, and still fell in the wilderness (10:1–12).
  • What is at stake in v27? Faithful Christians read this verse differently. Many Wesleyan, Arminian and Catholic readers see a real danger of losing salvation through turning away, and they point to Paul's other uses of adokimos and to the Israelites in 10:5. Reformed readers agree the word points to final rejection, not just lost rewards, but hold that God keeps his people through such warnings: Paul's discipline is the very means by which those who belong to Christ persevere (Phil 2:12–13; 1:6). Others, including many dispensational and Free Grace readers, take "disqualified" to mean losing a reward or usefulness in ministry, not salvation (compare 3:15). All agree on the plain point: Paul did not treat his preaching or his past as a guarantee, and no believer should either.
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry sums up vv1–14: "Those who preach the gospel have a right to live by it." He adds that a minister "may waive his right, as Paul did, without being a sinner," and draws the lesson of vv15–18: "It is a high attainment in religion to renounce our own rights for the good of others." Guzik traces the courtroom words in v3 and notes the irony that Paul's refusal of pay made some in Corinth think less of him, since Greek culture looked down on manual labor. On vv19–22 all three set the same limit. Guzik says Paul changed his behavior, never his message. Henry: "He would not sin against God to save the soul of his neighbour," and "The rights of God he could not give up, but he might resign his own." JFB reads "the weak" as the inexperienced believers of 8:7 and puts v22 memorably: "The gain of even 'some' is worth the expenditure of 'all means.'" On the games, JFB and Guzik both name the Isthmian Games. JFB describes the crown as "soon withering, as being only of fir leaves," which fits the Isthmian pine better than the Tyndale notes' laurel. Guzik cites the ten months of training Olympic athletes swore to, a rule from Olympia rather than Corinth's own games, but a fair picture of the discipline involved. On v27, Guzik and JFB both explain the verb as striking under the eye, and JFB renders it "bruise the face under the eyes." The lexicon supports both. Guzik's rendering of the second verb as leading the body around like a slave fits its roots, though the lexicon gives the plain sense "make a slave." The three part ways on "disqualified." Guzik thinks it probably means losing the reward rather than salvation, since no Greek lost his citizenship by losing a race. JFB reads it as "failing shamefully of the prize myself, after I have called others to the contest," rejected by God as Judge. Henry is the bluntest: "A preacher of salvation may yet miss it," and he urges a holy fear of oneself. The Tyndale notes take the prize to be salvation itself.

Pointing to Christ

Paul's pattern is borrowed. Christ had every right there is, and "though he was in the form of God" he "made himself nothing, taking the form of a servant" (Phil 2:6–7), the same word Paul uses of himself in v19. Paul became weak to win the weak; Christ, though rich, "became poor" for the sake of others (2 Cor 8:9). Paul offered the gospel free of charge because the gospel is free: it was paid for in full by someone else. And the crown in v25 has a counterpart. Soldiers wove a stephanos of thorns for Jesus' head, and he was rejected and treated as a failure outside the city, so that those who belong to him might receive a crown that will never wither. Paul runs hard, not to earn that crown, but because he has seen the one who ran ahead of him, "the founder and perfecter of our faith" (Heb 12:2).

Reflection

  • Surrendering Rights for the Gospel (v.12) — Paul had legitimate rights as an apostle but chose not to use them so nothing would hinder the gospel's progress. What rights are you insisting on that might actually be getting in the way of reaching others?
  • A Compulsion to Preach (v.16) — Paul says he would be in trouble if he didn't preach the gospel — it wasn't optional to him but a necessity. Does sharing your faith feel like a genuine compulsion to you, or more like an occasional option?
  • Becoming All Things to All People (vv.19-22) — Paul describes adapting his approach to Jews, Gentiles, and the weak alike, without compromising the truth, so he might save some by every possible means. Where is the line for you between adapting your approach to reach someone and compromising what you actually believe?
  • Running to Win, Not Just to Finish (v.24) — Paul compares the Christian life to a race where only one receives the prize, urging believers to run in such a way as to win it. Are you pursuing your faith with real intensity and focus, or coasting as if the outcome is automatic?
  • Disciplined Like an Athlete (vv.25-27) — Paul describes athletes exercising strict self-control for a fading prize, then applies the same discipline to himself so he won't be disqualified after preaching to others. What area of your own life — habits, integrity, self-control — needs more discipline than you're currently giving it?
