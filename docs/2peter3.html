1Beloved, this is now my second letter to you. Both of them are reminders to stir you to wholesome thinking

2by recalling what was foretold by the holy prophets and commanded by our Lord and Savior through your apostles.

3Most importantly, you must understand that in the last days scoffers will come, scoffing and following their own evil desires.

4“Where is the promise of His coming?” they will ask. “Ever since our fathers fell asleep, everything continues as it has from the beginning of creation.”

5But they deliberately overlook the fact that long ago by God’s word the heavens existed and the earth was formed out of water and by water,

6through which the world of that time perished in the flood.

7And by that same word, the present heavens and earth are reserved for fire, being kept for the day of judgment and destruction of ungodly men.

8Beloved, do not let this one thing escape your notice: With the Lord a day is like a thousand years, and a thousand years are like a day.

9The Lord is not slow in keeping His promise as some understand slowness, but is patient with you, not wanting anyone to perish but everyone to come to repentance.

10But the Day of the Lord will come like a thief. The heavens will disappear with a roar, the elements will be destroyed by fire, and the earth and its works will be laid bare.

11Since everything will be destroyed in this way, what kind of people ought you to be? You ought to conduct yourselves in holiness and godliness

12as you anticipate and hasten the coming of the day of God, when the heavens will be destroyed by fire and the elements will melt in the heat.

13But in keeping with God’s promise, we are looking forward to a new heaven and a new earth, where righteousness dwells.

14Therefore, beloved, as you anticipate these things, make every effort to be found at peace—spotless and blameless in His sight.

15Consider also that our Lord’s patience brings salvation, just as our beloved brother Paul also wrote you with the wisdom God gave him.

16He writes this way in all his letters, speaking in them about such matters. Some parts of his letters are hard to understand, which ignorant and unstable people distort, as they do the rest of the Scriptures, to their own destruction.

17Therefore, beloved, since you already know these things, be on your guard so that you will not be carried away by the error of the lawless and fall from your secure standing.

18But grow in the grace and knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. To Him be the glory both now and to the day of eternity. Amen.

Summary

Peter reminds readers of the words of the prophets and the apostles, then warns that scoffers will come in the last days, mocking the promise of Christ's return and claiming everything continues exactly as it always has (vv. 1-4). He counters that these scoffers deliberately forget how God once destroyed the world by water, and that the present heavens and earth are being kept for judgment and destruction by fire (vv. 5-7). He reassures believers that the Lord is not slow to keep his promise — with the Lord a day is like a thousand years — and that he is patient, not wanting anyone to perish but all to reach repentance (vv. 8-9). Still, the day of the Lord will come unexpectedly, like a thief, dissolving the heavens and elements by fire (v. 10), so Peter urges holy living while waiting for the new heavens and new earth where righteousness dwells (vv. 11-13). He closes with a call to be found spotless and at peace, noting that some things in Paul's letters are hard to understand and are twisted by the unstable, and urging growth in the grace and knowledge of Jesus Christ (vv. 14-18).

Authorship & Background

Author: Simon Peter (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Historical Context: Chapter 3 addresses scoffers who mock Christ's return (vv.1-7), explains God's patience (vv.8-9), describes the Day of the Lord (vv.10-13), and gives final exhortations (vv.14-18). Some in the early church were losing hope because Christ hadn't returned yet. Scoffers used the delay to argue He would never come. Peter responds: God's timing is not human timing, His patience is for salvation, and the Day will come.
Classification: Epistle — General
Key Themes: Scoffers who turn a delay into their whole argument, a day with the Lord not counted the way a day is counted, patience read as the reason for waiting rather than proof against it, the heavens dissolving and the elements melting with fervent heat, and Paul's letters counted among the rest of scripture
Scoffers and the Promise (vv.1-7): In the last days, scoffers will mock: "Where is the promise of his coming?" (vv.3-4). They willfully ignore that God destroyed the world by water before (vv.5-6). The present heavens and earth are "reserved unto fire" for judgment day (v.7).
God's Patience (vv.8-9): One day with the Lord is as a thousand years (v.8). God is not slow — He is patient, "not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance" (v.9).
The Day of the Lord (vv.10-13): It will come "as a thief" — suddenly, unexpectedly (v.10). The heavens will pass away, elements melt, earth burned up (v.10). Since everything will be dissolved, "what manner of persons ought ye to be?" (v.11). We look for "new heavens and a new earth, wherein dwelleth righteousness" (v.13).
Final Exhortations (vv.14-18): Be diligent to be found in peace, spotless, blameless (v.14). God's patience = salvation (v.15). Paul wrote the same things (vv.15-16). Beware of being led astray (v.17). "Grow in grace, and in the knowledge of our Lord" (v.18).

Deep Dive

Setting: These are probably the last words we have from Peter. He is writing near the end of his life (1:14), most likely from Rome in the mid-60s AD, shortly before he was executed under Nero, as early church tradition holds. Chapter 2 exposed the false teachers inside the churches: greedy, immoral and contemptuous of authority. Now Peter gets to their central idea. They scoffed at the promise that Christ would return to judge the world, and that belief let them live as they pleased. Peter answers with creation, the flood, God's view of time and God's patience, then describes the end of the present world and the new creation to come. The letter ends with a warm word about Paul and a final call to grow in grace.

The Day of the Lord Will Come (vv1–13)

  • "This is now the second letter that I am writing to you, beloved" (v1). The first is most likely 1 Peter, so the readers are the churches of Asia Minor (1 Pet 1:1). "Beloved" appears four times in this chapter (vv1, 8, 14, 17). After the harsh words of chapter 2, Peter turns back to the people he loves.
  • "Stirring up your sincere mind by way of reminder" (v1). "Sincere" (eilikrinēs) may come from words meaning "tested by sunlight": something held up to the light and found pure. Their minds are sound, but they need waking up (1:13).
  • "The predictions of the holy prophets and the commandment of the Lord and Savior through your apostles" (v2). Peter sets the apostles' teaching beside the Old Testament prophets as equal authority, the same pairing as Ephesians 2:20. Both spoke of the day of the Lord, and the false teachers ignore both.
  • "Scoffers will come in the last days with scoffing" (v3). The repetition is a Hebrew way of stressing something: "mocking mockers." "The last days" in the New Testament means the whole time between Christ's first and second coming (Acts 2:17; Heb 1:2), so Peter's readers were already living in them. Jude 18 quotes the same apostolic warning. The real issue is moral: they are "following their own sinful desires." People who do not want to be judged have every reason to say there will be no judgment.
  • "Where is the promise of his coming?" (v4). This is the scoffers' question. "Coming" is parousia, the word Peter used for Christ's return (1:16). "Ever since the fathers fell asleep" could mean the Old Testament patriarchs, but more likely it means the first generation of Christians, some of whom had already died (1 Thess 4:13–15). The mockers' argument: it has been thirty years, people are dying, and nothing has changed.
  • "All things are continuing as they were from the beginning of creation" (v4). This is the heart of their view: the world runs on unchanging, unbroken patterns, and God does not step into history. Some scholars connect this with Epicurean philosophy, popular at the time, which taught that the gods do not intervene in the world and there is no judgment after death.
  • "They deliberately overlook this fact" (v5). They are not simply mistaken; they choose to forget. Peter's first answer is creation: the world did not always exist. "The earth was formed out of water and through water by the word of God" recalls Genesis 1, where God's word separates the waters and the dry land appears (Gen 1:2, 6–10). A world that began by God's word can be changed by God's word.
  • "The world that then existed was deluged with water and perished" (v6). Peter's second answer is the flood (Gen 7:11). The scoffers say nothing has changed since creation, but something enormous did: the same waters God used to form the world, he used to judge it. "Perished" does not mean the planet ceased to exist, but that the world as it was, with its people and their way of life, was destroyed. This is important for understanding v10.
  • "By the same word the heavens and earth that now exist are stored up for fire" (v7). As the first world was judged by water, the present world will be judged by fire. The prophets often link fire with God's coming judgment (Isa 66:15–16; Mal 4:1; Zeph 1:18). "Stored up" and "kept" describe something held in reserve, waiting for its appointed day.
  • "With the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day" (v8). Peter now turns to believers ("beloved") and gives a third answer. He draws on Psalm 90:4: "a thousand years in your sight are but as yesterday when it is past." God is not bound by time as we are. What feels like a long delay to us is no delay to him. This is not a code for calculating the end, as some have tried, as if each "day" of creation equals a thousand years of history. It is a statement about God's perspective.
  • "The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness" (v9). Here is the fourth answer and the most moving one. The delay is not weakness or forgetfulness. It is mercy. "Patient" (makrothymeō, "long-tempered") is the word for God holding back his anger. The word "toward you" in the oldest manuscripts shows Peter means people inside the church, as well as those who have not yet believed.
  • "Not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" (v9). This reveals God's heart (Ezek 18:23, 32; 1 Tim 2:4). Christians have read it in different ways. Some (often Arminian) take it as God's genuine desire for every person's salvation, which people can refuse. Others (often Reformed) take "any" and "all" in context as God's chosen people whom he patiently waits to gather in, or distinguish God's revealed desire from his decree. All agree on the plain point: every day the Lord delays is a day of opportunity to repent. It does not teach that everyone will in fact be saved, since v7 has just spoken of "destruction of the ungodly."
  • "The day of the Lord will come like a thief" (v10). Jesus used this image (Matt 24:43–44), and so did Paul (1 Thess 5:2) and John (Rev 3:3; 16:15). A thief does not send notice. The point is not stealth but surprise: the day will come when people least expect it, which is exactly why the scoffers' complacency is so dangerous. Peter may be recalling hearing Jesus say it himself.
  • "The heavens will pass away with a roar" (v10). "With a roar" (rhoizēdon) is a sound word, used of the whistling of an arrow, the rushing of wings or the crackling roar of flames. Jesus said, "heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away" (Matt 24:35).
  • "The heavenly bodies will be burned up and dissolved" (v10). "Heavenly bodies" translates stoicheia, "elements." The lexicon gives two possible senses here: the basic material components of the universe, as in Greek philosophy, or the sun, moon and stars. The ESV chooses the second, matching Isaiah 34:4 ("All the host of heaven shall rot away") and Jesus' words about the stars falling (Mark 13:24–25). Either way, the whole created order will be shaken to its foundations.
  • "The earth and the works that are done on it will be exposed" (v10). This is one of the hardest textual problems in the New Testament. The KJV, following later manuscripts, reads "shall be burned up." The oldest and best manuscripts read heurethēsetai, "will be found." Translations handle that differently: "exposed" (ESV), "laid bare" (NIV), "will be discovered" (NASB). The best sense is that the fire strips everything away, so that the earth and all human deeds stand fully uncovered before God's judgment (1 Cor 3:13). Some manuscripts add "not," which reads "will not be found." Some scribes changed the text because "will be found" seemed odd. But it is probably original and fits Peter's emphasis on being "found" by God at the end (v14).
  • Destruction or renewal? (vv10–13). Some Christians read this passage as the total annihilation of the present universe, replaced by a brand-new one. Others read it as a purging fire that cleanses and transforms the creation, as the flood "destroyed" the world without erasing the planet (v6), and as Romans 8:19–22 speaks of creation being "set free from its bondage to corruption." The word "new" in v13 (kainos) usually means new in quality rather than new in time, which tends to support renewal. Both views agree that the change will be radical and that the world in its present state will not last.
  • "What sort of people ought you to be in lives of holiness and godliness" (v11). Peter's teaching about the end is never only for curiosity. If everything people build will be dissolved, then the only lasting investment is a holy life. "Holiness" and "godliness" are plural in Greek, "holy behaviors and godly acts," the everyday details of a life.
  • "Waiting for and hastening the coming of the day of God" (v12). "Hastening" (speudō) normally means "to hurry." Used with an object, as here, it can mean to speed something up, or, according to the lexicon's second sense, to desire it eagerly. Many translations take the first: God's people can in some sense bring the day nearer. Peter himself preached in Jerusalem, "Repent therefore… that times of refreshing may come… and that he may send the Christ" (Acts 3:19–20). Jesus said the gospel must first be preached to all nations, and then the end will come (Matt 24:14). Prayer ("Your kingdom come"), witness and repentance are part of how God brings about the end he has planned. This does not mean God's timetable can be changed, but that he has appointed means as well as ends.
  • "The heavenly bodies will melt as they burn" (v12). The language echoes Isaiah 34:4 and Micah 1:4, where the mountains melt like wax before the Lord.
  • "According to his promise we are waiting for new heavens and a new earth in which righteousness dwells" (v13). The scoffers asked, "Where is the promise?" (v4). Peter answers with a promise. God promised in Isaiah, "I create new heavens and a new earth" (Isa 65:17; 66:22), and John sees it in Revelation 21:1–4. "Righteousness dwells" there: it will be at home, settled and permanent. The present world is full of injustice and the false teachers' corruption. The new world will have none of it.
  • Commentators: Guzik reads the scoffers' view as a naturalistic belief that God never intervenes, and Henry says the same thing in older language: "What he never has done, they fancy he never can do, or never will do." JFB stresses that "their ignorance is voluntary." On v8 Guzik calls the thousand years a general principle about God's perspective, not a prophetic formula, and Henry says that with God "there is no difference" between a day and a thousand years. On v9 Henry writes, "What men count slackness, is long-suffering, and that to us-ward." JFB says God does not want "even… the scoffers" to perish, and Guzik reads it as God's heart of love rather than a promise of universal salvation. On v12 Guzik says believers can hasten the day through holy living, evangelism and prayer. JFB agrees the verb can mean "hastening," "not that God's eternal appointment of the time is changeable." Guzik does not discuss the v10 textual problem, and the JFB page did not address it either. JFB pictures the new heavens as "new atmospheric heavens surrounding the renovated earth," which leans toward renewal.

Final Words (vv14–18)

  • "Since you are waiting for these, be diligent to be found by him without spot or blemish, and at peace" (v14). "Found" picks up v10: when everything is uncovered, believers want to be found ready. "Without spot or blemish" (aspiloi kai amōmētoi) is the reverse of the false teachers, "blots and blemishes" (2:13), and a picture of Christ, the spotless Lamb (1 Pet 1:19). "At peace" means at peace with God and settled, not anxious, at his coming.
  • "Count the patience of our Lord as salvation" (v15). The scoffers count the delay as proof the Lord is not coming. Believers should count it as mercy: every day of delay means more people saved (v9).
  • "Just as our beloved brother Paul also wrote to you" (v15). This is striking. Years before, in Antioch, Paul had publicly rebuked Peter for withdrawing from Gentile believers (Gal 2:11–14). Now, near the end of both men's lives, Peter calls Paul "our beloved brother" and praises "the wisdom given him." Peter and Paul had also stood together at the Jerusalem council (Acts 15:7–12). Early church tradition says both were executed in Rome under Nero, perhaps within months of each other.
  • "There are some things in them that are hard to understand" (v16). This is a comforting admission from one apostle about another. Even Peter found parts of Paul's letters difficult. Peter does not say they are wrong, only hard, and that the danger lies with those who misuse them. In context Paul's teaching about grace and freedom may be what the false teachers twisted (Rom 3:8; 6:1), turning freedom from the law into freedom to sin.
  • "Which the ignorant and unstable twist to their own destruction" (v16). "Twist" is strebloō, to twist or torture, used of stretching someone on the rack. They put the text on the rack until it says what they want. "Ignorant" here means untaught, not stupid: people who have never been grounded in the faith. "Unstable" (astēriktos) recalls the "unsteady souls" of 2:14.
  • "As they do the other Scriptures" (v16). This is one of the most important phrases for the history of the Bible. "The Scriptures" (graphai) was the standard term for the Old Testament, the sacred writings. By putting Paul's letters alongside "the other Scriptures," Peter treats them as carrying the same authority. It also shows that Paul's letters were already being collected and read across the churches during the apostles' lifetimes. Scholars who doubt Peter wrote the letter often point to this verse as a sign of a later date. Those who accept it note that Paul expected his letters to be read and passed on as authoritative while he was alive (1 Thess 5:27; Col 4:16; 1 Cor 14:37).
  • "Take care that you are not carried away with the error of lawless people and lose your own stability" (v17). "Carried away" is the word Paul used of Barnabas being "led astray" at Antioch (Gal 2:13), a painful memory for Peter, who was part of that same lapse. "Stability" (stērigmos) shares a root with "confirm" in Luke 22:32, where Jesus told Peter, "when you have turned again, strengthen your brothers," and with "unstable" in v16. Peter, who once lost his footing, now strengthens his brothers, just as Jesus asked.
  • "But grow in the grace and knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ" (v18). The best defense against error is not just watching out but growing. The letter began with grace and peace "in the knowledge of God and of Jesus our Lord" (1:2), and it ends the same way. "Grace" and "knowledge" frame the whole letter.
  • "To him be the glory both now and to the day of eternity. Amen" (v18). This doxology is addressed to Jesus, which assumes he is God (1:1). "The day of eternity" appears only here in the New Testament: the "day of the Lord" (v10) becomes a day that never ends. Peter's last recorded word is praise to Christ.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Only the diligent Christian will be the happy Christian in the day of the Lord." Guzik and JFB both highlight Peter's warmth toward Paul despite Galatians 2. JFB notes that "Peter praises the very Epistles which contain his condemnation." JFB also says the word "Scriptures" "was never applied in any of the fifty places where it occurs, save to the Old and New Testament sacred writings," which supports reading v16 as treating Paul's letters as Scripture. The Tyndale notes agree. Guzik adds that God's grace never grows, but believers grow in it. JFB says Christ is "the author" of the grace and "the object" of the knowledge. Henry closes with the charge: "Labour to know Christ more clearly, and more fully."

Pointing to Christ

The whole chapter hangs on one promise, "the promise of his coming" (v4). The scoffers laugh at it, but Peter had seen the King's glory on the mountain (1:16–18) and knows he is coming back. In the meantime, his patience is salvation (v15), and every delayed day is a day in which more people may turn to him. When he returns, everything will be exposed, and those who belong to him will be "found by him" in peace (v14), ready for the new heavens and new earth where righteousness is at home. Peter's last word is praise to "our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ," the one he once denied and now glorifies "both now and to the day of eternity."

Reflection

  • Scoffers Who Doubt His Return (vv.3-4) — Peter warns that scoffers driven by their own desires will mock the promise of Christ's return since nothing seems to have changed. Does the apparent delay ever tempt you toward the same doubt?
  • God's Patience Aimed at Repentance (v.9) — What looks like slowness is actually God's patience, since he wants none to perish but all to reach repentance. Are you using the time God is patiently giving to grow, or to drift?
  • Living in Light of What Will Be Dissolved (v.11) — Since everything temporary will one day be dissolved, Peter asks what kind of people that reality should make us. Does the certainty of the end actually shape your daily priorities, or stay purely theoretical?
  • Waiting for a Home of Righteousness (v.13) — Believers look forward to new heavens and a new earth where righteousness truly belongs. Does that hope sustain you when the world around you seems to reward the opposite?
  • Guarding Against Being Led Astray (v.17) — Knowing this in advance, believers are warned to stay on guard so they aren't carried away by the error of lawless people. What error or influence currently threatens your own stability?
  • Growing in Grace to the End (v.18) — Peter's final word is a call to keep growing in grace and in the knowledge of Christ. Where is your growth in Christ stalled right now, and what would restarting it look like?
