Summary

A psalm of trust in the face of pressure to flee danger. Advisers urge David to escape like a bird to the mountains because the wicked are preparing to attack the upright in secret (vv. 1-3), but he refuses, recalling that the LORD is enthroned in his holy temple, watching and testing all of humanity (vv. 3-4). God tests both the righteous and the wicked and hates those who love violence, promising to rain fiery judgment on the wicked as their portion (vv. 5-6). The psalm closes with the assurance that the LORD is righteous, loves righteous deeds, and that the upright will see his face (v. 7).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Trust/Confidence Psalm
Attributed Author: David — "To the chief Musician, A Psalm of David"
Key Themes: Refusing fear-based flight, God's sovereign observation from heaven, testing of the righteous, judgment on the wicked, beholding God's face
Historical Context: This short, powerful psalm likely dates from David's time fleeing Saul. Well-meaning advisors urge him to flee "like a bird to your mountain" — to abandon his post and run. David's response is not recklessness but theology: the LORD is in His temple, His throne is in heaven, His eyes see everything. Why flee when the sovereign God is watching? The psalm addresses the temptation to abandon faith-based action for fear-based escape. The question "If the foundations be destroyed, what can the righteous do?" (v.3) is one of Scripture's most profound questions about societal collapse.
How Say Ye to My Soul, Flee (vv.1-3): In the LORD put I my trust, how say ye to my soul, Flee as a bird to your mountain. The psalm opens by quoting advice and refusing it, and the advice is not cowardly but sensible, since the reason given is military, for lo, the wicked bend their bow, they make ready their arrow upon the string. And the counselors' closing argument is the strongest thing in the psalm, if the foundations be destroyed, what can the righteous do.
The LORD Is in His Holy Temple (vv.4-5): The answer does not address the arrows. The LORD is in his holy temple, the LORD's throne is in heaven, his eyes behold, his eyelids try, the children of men. The reply to the collapse of the foundations is a statement about where the throne is, which is not where the fighting is. And what the watching is for is stated in the next verse, the LORD trieth the righteous, so the observation includes the singer and is not only surveillance of the enemy.
Fire and Brimstone (v.6): Upon the wicked he shall rain fire and brimstone, and an horrible tempest, this shall be the portion of their cup. One verse, and the vocabulary is Sodom's, which is the psalter's shorthand for a judgment that comes from outside the human situation altogether. The cup as a measure of allotted fate runs from here through Jeremiah 25 to Gethsemane.
The Upright Shall Behold His Face (v.7): For the righteous LORD loveth righteousness, his countenance doth behold the upright. The psalm that began with an instruction to run away ends with a face rather than a fortress, and the reversal of the opening image is deliberate: the advice was to flee to a mountain, and what is offered instead is being looked at.

Deep Dive

Setting: The title is brief: "To the choirmaster. Of David." No occasion is given, but the psalm fits the years when David served in Saul's court and Saul turned against him. Saul twice threw a spear at him (1 Sam 18:11; 19:10), set traps to have him killed by the Philistines (18:25), and finally sent men to his house at night (19:11). David did eventually flee to the hills, and Matthew Henry dates the psalm to when David "began to feel the resentments of Saul's envy." Others connect it to Absalom's revolt. The psalm is short, seven verses, and built as a conversation. Someone, probably well-meaning friends, tells David to run for his life because everything is collapsing. David answers by looking up, to the LORD on his throne. Psalms 9 and 10 asked why God seems far off while the wicked prosper. Psalm 11 answers from the other side: God is not far off at all. He is enthroned, watching, testing, and he will act.

Flee Like a Bird to Your Mountain (vv1–3)

  • "In the LORD I take refuge" (v1). The first line settles the question before it is even asked. David has already found his shelter. The verb chasah is the one that closed Psalm 2 and opened Psalm 7: to run for cover in God. Everything that follows is an answer to the advice of fear, given from inside that refuge.
  • "How can you say to my soul" (v1). "You" here is plural in Hebrew: "flee, all of you." David is speaking to a group, perhaps advisers, friends or companions. Henry calls them "timorous friends," though he also allows that the words could be a taunt from enemies mocking his trust. Either way, the voice is pressing David to give in to fear.
  • "Flee like a bird to your mountain" (v1). The hills of Judah were full of caves and crags where hunted men hid. David knew them well: the cave of Adullam, the strongholds of En-gedi, the "crags of the wild goats" (1 Sam 22:1; 24:2). A small bird, startled, darts for the cliffs. The advice is to act like that: panicked, alone, running. Fleeing is not always wrong. David did flee from Saul, and Jesus told his disciples to flee persecution (Matt 10:23). The issue here is the reason. The advisers want him to run because they think God cannot be counted on.
  • "For behold, the wicked bend the bow; they have fitted their arrow to the string" (v2). The picture is of an archer with the bow already drawn. The danger is real and close.
  • "To shoot in the dark at the upright in heart" (v2). "In the dark" means secretly, from hiding, the way an assassin works. The upright are attacked by people who will not show their faces.
  • "If the foundations are destroyed, what can the righteous do?" (v3). This is the strongest argument the advisers have. "Foundations" means the base on which a society stands: law, justice, honest courts, a king who does right. When Saul himself, the anointed king, is trying to kill an innocent man, the foundations really are shaking. The question is a real one, and many believers in many centuries have asked it.
  • Two ways to read v3. The question can mean "what can the righteous do?" (nothing, so run) or "what has the righteous done?" (all his efforts have come to nothing). JFB takes the second sense, that the psalmist's work has failed. The ESV and most translations take the first. Either way the voice is despair.
  • Commentators: Guzik passes on Spurgeon that David "would rather dare the danger than exhibit a distrust in the Lord his God." He compares the friends' well-meant advice to Peter urging Jesus to avoid the cross (Matt 16:22–23): sincere, sympathetic and wrong. Henry says David saw the suggestion as an attack on the foundations of faith itself, since the principles of true religion are the foundations the righteous build on, and "must be held fast." JFB explains "privily" as "in darkness," that is, treacherously, and reads "flee ye" as addressed to David and his companions.

The LORD Is in His Holy Temple (vv4–5)

  • "The LORD is in his holy temple" (v4). David's answer is not an argument but a fact. When the foundations below seem to give way, there is a foundation above that cannot. The "temple" in David's day was not yet Solomon's building. It was the tabernacle, and behind it God's heavenly dwelling, of which the earthly tent was only a copy (Heb 8:5). Habakkuk, in a time of collapse, ends up in the same place: "the LORD is in his holy temple; let all the earth keep silence before him" (Hab 2:20).
  • "The LORD's throne is in heaven" (v4). The parallel line shows the temple and the throne are the same place. The God who is worshiped is the God who rules. Saul's throne may be corrupt, but heaven's throne is not.
  • "His eyes see, his eyelids test the children of man" (v4). A vivid picture: God narrowing his eyes to look closely, the way a person squints to examine something. "Test" (bachan) is a word for testing metals, assaying gold to find out what it really is (Zech 13:9; Job 23:10). God's watching is not idle. He is sifting every person.
  • "The LORD tests the righteous" (v5). The righteous are tested too. Their trouble is not a sign that God has lost interest, but that he is refining them. "When he has tried me, I shall come out as gold" (Job 23:10). James and Peter both pick this up: the testing of faith produces steadfastness and proves its genuineness (Jas 1:2–3; 1 Pet 1:6–7).
  • "But his soul hates the wicked and the one who loves violence" (v5). Strong language. God's "soul" means his whole being. The psalms speak this way of God's settled opposition to evil and those committed to it (5:5). "Violence" (chamas) is the word for the violence that filled the earth before the flood (Gen 6:11). The God who loves the world (John 3:16) is not neutral toward those who love cruelty.
  • Commentators: Henry says that though God is out of sight, his people are not out of his reach, and that "his eye-lids try" people the way a refiner tests gold, seeing through every appearance. He calls the testing of the righteous a trial meant for their good. JFB says that from heaven God "inspects men with close scrutiny," and that the righteous are tested with a view to their approval. Guzik turns the advisers' question around: if the LORD is still on his throne, what can't the righteous do? He adds that thinking about problems tends to breed fear, while praying about them breeds faith, and passes on Spurgeon: "What plots can men devise which Jesus will not discover?"

The Upright Shall Behold His Face (vv6–7)

  • "Let him rain coals on the wicked" (v6). The wicked rained arrows in the dark (v2). God will rain fire in the open. The standard Hebrew text reads "snares" (KJV: "upon the wicked he shall rain snares, fire and brimstone"). With one small change of vowels and a letter, the word becomes "coals of," which one ancient Greek translator (Symmachus) read, and the ESV follows that reading. Either way the point is the same.
  • "Fire and sulfur and a scorching wind" (v6). "Sulfur" is the older "brimstone." This is the language of Sodom and Gomorrah, where "the LORD rained on Sodom and Gomorrah sulfur and fire" (Gen 19:24). The "scorching wind" is the blasting east wind off the desert, the sirocco, which can wither crops in a day. These images become standard pictures of final judgment in the Bible (Ezek 38:22; Rev 14:10; 21:8).
  • "Shall be the portion of their cup" (v6). In the ancient world the host poured out each guest's cup, and you drank what you were given. "Cup" in Scripture often stands for one's lot in life, given by God, whether blessing ("my cup overflows," 23:5) or wrath (75:8; Isa 51:17; Jer 25:15). This is the cup Jesus prayed in Gethsemane would pass from him if possible, and then drank (Matt 26:39).
  • "For the LORD is righteous; he loves righteous deeds" (v7). This is the foundation that cannot be destroyed. The advisers said the foundations were gone. David answers: the character of God has not changed. He is righteous, and he loves what is right. "Righteous deeds" is plural, the concrete acts of justice and goodness that please him.
  • "The upright shall behold his face" (v7). The psalm ends with the highest hope in the Bible: seeing God. In the ancient world, to "see the king's face" meant being admitted to his presence and favor (2 Sam 14:24). The wicked shot "in the dark" (v2). The upright will see God's face in the light. The Hebrew can also be read "his face beholds the upright," God looking on them with favor (KJV: "his countenance doth behold the upright"). Both are true and belong together. Jesus says, "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God" (Matt 5:8), and the last chapter of the Bible promises of God's servants: "They will see his face" (Rev 22:4).
  • Flee or stay? The advisers said, "flee to your mountain." The psalm's final answer is to stand where you are and look to a higher mountain, the holy hill where God is enthroned (2:6; 3:4). The refuge is not a place to run to, but a Person to run to.
  • Commentators: Henry recalls Sodom and Gomorrah in "fire, and brimstone, and a horrible tempest" and says those who reject God must drink that cup, while those who choose him receive a blessed portion. He says the upright enjoy God's favor because "his countenance doth behold the upright." JFB calls the cup "a common image" for God's favor or wrath, and notes the Hebrew for "countenance" is literally plural, "faces." He links this either to the fullness of God's perfections or to the Trinity. The plural is a normal feature of the Hebrew word for "face," which is always plural in form, so the Trinity link should not be pressed. Guzik ties the cup to Gethsemane, where Jesus drank the wrath meant for others. He says the "behold" line can be read either way, and both readings are true.

Pointing to Christ

Jesus heard the same advice David heard. Some Pharisees told him, "Get away from here, for Herod wants to kill you," and he answered, "I must go on my way," because he would not run from the path his Father had set (Luke 13:31–33). When the foundations seemed destroyed, with God's Son condemned by Israel's leaders and Rome's governor, he committed himself to the Father who judges justly. And he took the cup of v6. The fire, sulfur and scorching wind of God's wrath against evil were poured into his cup at the cross, so that those who trust him are not given that cup to drink. Because of him, the last line of the psalm becomes the believer's future: the upright will behold God's face, made upright not by their own record but by his.

Reflection

  • Refusing Fear-Based Advice (v.1) — When counseled to flee like a bird, David chooses trust in the LORD instead of retreat. When fear urges you to abandon your post, do you test that counsel against your trust in God?
  • Looking Up When Foundations Shake (v.3) — Facing the question of what the righteous can do when society's foundations crumble, David's answer is to remember God's unshaken throne. When your circumstances feel unstable, where do you direct your attention?
  • Testing That Refines, Not Punishes (v.5) — God tests the righteous in the same way He examines the wicked, but with the purpose of refining rather than destroying. Can you distinguish between a season of God's refining and a fear that He is punishing you?
  • The Ultimate Reward Is God's Presence (v.7) — David ends not with a request for comfort or vindication but with the promise that the upright will see God's face. Is beholding God Himself the reward you are ultimately seeking, above easier circumstances?
