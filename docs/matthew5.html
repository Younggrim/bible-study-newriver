1When Jesus saw the crowds, He went up on the mountain and sat down. His disciples came to Him,

2and He began to teach them, saying:

3“Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.

4Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted.

5Blessed are the meek, for they will inherit the earth.

6Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they will be filled.

7Blessed are the merciful, for they will be shown mercy.

8Blessed are the pure in heart, for they will see God.

9Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called sons of God.

10Blessed are those who are persecuted because of righteousness, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.

11Blessed are you when people insult you, persecute you, and falsely say all kinds of evil against you because of Me.

12Rejoice and be glad, because great is your reward in heaven, for in the same way they persecuted the prophets before you.

13You are the salt of the earth. But if the salt loses its savor, how can it be made salty again? It is no longer good for anything, except to be thrown out and trampled by men.

14You are the light of the world. A city on a hill cannot be hidden.

15Neither do people light a lamp and put it under a basket. Instead, they set it on a stand, and it gives light to everyone in the house.

16In the same way, let your light shine before men, that they may see your good deeds and glorify your Father in heaven.

17Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets. I have not come to abolish them, but to fulfill them.

18For I tell you truly, until heaven and earth pass away, not a single jot, not a stroke of a pen, will disappear from the Law until everything is accomplished.

19So then, whoever breaks one of the least of these commandments and teaches others to do likewise will be called least in the kingdom of heaven; but whoever practices and teaches them will be called great in the kingdom of heaven.

20For I tell you that unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven.

21You have heard that it was said to the ancients, ‘Do not murder’ and ‘Anyone who murders will be subject to judgment.’

22But I tell you that anyone who is angry with his brother will be subject to judgment. Again, anyone who says to his brother, ‘Raca,’ will be subject to the Sanhedrin. But anyone who says, ‘You fool!’ will be subject to the fire of hell.

23So if you are offering your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you,

24leave your gift there before the altar. First go and be reconciled to your brother; then come and offer your gift.

25Reconcile quickly with your adversary, while you are still on the way to court. Otherwise, he may hand you over to the judge, and the judge may hand you over to the officer, and you may be thrown into prison.

26Truly I tell you, you will not get out until you have paid the last penny.

27You have heard that it was said, ‘Do not commit adultery.’

28But I tell you that anyone who looks at a woman to lust after her has already committed adultery with her in his heart.

29If your right eye causes you to sin, gouge it out and throw it away. It is better for you to lose one part of your body than for your whole body to be thrown into hell.

30And if your right hand causes you to sin, cut it off and throw it away. It is better for you to lose one part of your body than for your whole body to depart into hell.

31It has also been said, ‘Whoever divorces his wife must give her a certificate of divorce.’

32But I tell you that anyone who divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, brings adultery upon her. And he who marries a divorced woman commits adultery.

33Again, you have heard that it was said to the ancients, ‘Do not break your oath, but fulfill your vows to the Lord.’

34But I tell you not to swear at all: either by heaven, for it is God’s throne;

35or by the earth, for it is His footstool; or by Jerusalem, for it is the city of the great King.

36Nor should you swear by your head, for you cannot make a single hair white or black.

37Simply let your ‘Yes’ be ‘Yes,’ and your ‘No,’ ‘No.’ Anything more comes from the evil one.

38You have heard that it was said, ‘Eye for eye and tooth for tooth.’

39But I tell you not to resist an evil person. If someone slaps you on your right cheek, turn to him the other also;

40if someone wants to sue you and take your tunic, let him have your cloak as well;

41and if someone forces you to go one mile, go with him two miles.

42Give to the one who asks you, and do not turn away from the one who wants to borrow from you.

43You have heard that it was said, ‘Love your neighbor’ and ‘Hate your enemy.’

44But I tell you, love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you,

45that you may be sons of your Father in heaven. He causes His sun to rise on the evil and the good, and sends rain on the righteous and the unrighteous.

46If you love those who love you, what reward will you get? Do not even tax collectors do the same?

47And if you greet only your brothers, what are you doing more than others? Do not even Gentiles do the same?

48Be perfect, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect.

1And seeing the multitudes, he went up into a mountain: and when he was set, his disciples came unto him:

2And he opened his mouth, and taught them, saying,

3Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.

4Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted.

5Blessed are the meek: for they shall inherit the earth.

6Blessed are they which do hunger and thirst after righteousness: for they shall be filled.

7Blessed are the merciful: for they shall obtain mercy.

8Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God.

9Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God.

10Blessed are they which are persecuted for righteousness sake: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.

11Blessed are ye, when men shall revile you, and persecute you, and shall say all manner of evil against you falsely, for my sake.

12Rejoice, and be exceeding glad: for great is your reward in heaven: for so persecuted they the prophets which were before you.

13Ye are the salt of the earth: but if the salt have lost his savour, wherewith shall it be salted? it is thenceforth good for nothing, but to be cast out, and to be trodden under foot of men.

14Ye are the light of the world. A city that is set on an hill cannot be hid.

15Neither do men light a candle, and put it under a bushel, but on a candlestick; and it giveth light unto all that are in the house.

16Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.

17Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil.

18For verily I say unto you, Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled.

19Whosoever therefore shall break one of these least commandments, and shall teach men so, he shall be called the least in the kingdom of heaven: but whosoever shall do and teach them, the same shall be called great in the kingdom of heaven.

20For I say unto you, That except your righteousness shall exceed the righteousness of the scribes and Pharisees, ye shall in no case enter into the kingdom of heaven.

21Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not kill; and whosoever shall kill shall be in danger of the judgment:

22But I say unto you, That whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment: and whosoever shall say to his brother, Raca, shall be in danger of the council: but whosoever shall say, Thou fool, shall be in danger of hell fire.

23Therefore if thou bring thy gift to the altar, and there rememberest that thy brother hath ought against thee;

24Leave there thy gift before the altar, and go thy way; first be reconciled to thy brother, and then come and offer thy gift.

25Agree with thine adversary quickly, whiles thou art in the way with him; lest at any time the adversary deliver thee to the judge, and the judge deliver thee to the officer, and thou be cast into prison.

26Verily I say unto thee, Thou shalt by no means come out thence, till thou hast paid the uttermost farthing.

27Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not commit adultery:

28But I say unto you, That whosoever looketh on a woman to lust after her hath committed adultery with her already in his heart.

29And if thy right eye offend thee, pluck it out, and cast it from thee: for it is profitable for thee that one of thy members should perish, and not that thy whole body should be cast into hell.

30And if thy right hand offend thee, cut if off, and cast it from thee: for it is profitable for thee that one of thy members should perish, and not that thy whole body should be cast into hell.

31It hath been said, Whosoever shall put away his wife, let him give her a writing of divorcement:

32But I say unto you, That whosoever shall put away his wife, saving for the cause of fornication, causeth her to commit adultery: and whosoever shall marry her that is divorced committeth adultery.

33Again, ye have heard that it hath been said by them of old time, Thou shalt not forswear thyself, but shalt perform unto the Lord thine oaths:

34But I say unto you, Swear not at all; neither by heaven; for it is God’s throne:

35Nor by the earth; for it is his footstool: neither by Jerusalem; for it is the city of the great King.

36Neither shalt thou swear by thy head, because thou canst not make one hair white or black.

37But let your communication be, Yea, yea; Nay, nay: for whatsoever is more than these cometh of evil.

38Ye have heard that it hath been said, An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth:

39But I say unto you, That ye resist not evil: but whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also.

40And if any man will sue thee at the law, and take away thy coat, let him have thy cloke also.

41And whosoever shall compel thee to go a mile, go with him twain.

42Give to him that asketh thee, and from him that would borrow of thee turn not thou away.

43Ye have heard that it hath been said, Thou shalt love thy neighbour, and hate thine enemy.

44But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you;

45That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven: for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust.

46For if ye love them which love you, what reward have ye? do not even the publicans the same?

47And if ye salute your brethren only, what do ye more than others? do not even the publicans so?

48Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect.

Summary

Jesus goes up on a mountain and delivers the Beatitudes, pronouncing blessing on the poor in spirit, those who mourn, the gentle, those hungry for righteousness, the merciful, the pure in heart, peacemakers, and those persecuted for righteousness' sake (vv. 3-12). He calls his disciples the salt of the earth and the light of the world, meant to be visible rather than hidden (vv. 13-16). Jesus explains that he has come not to abolish the Law but to fulfill it, and that true righteousness must exceed that of the scribes and Pharisees (vv. 17-20). He then reinterprets several commandments, tracing anger back to the root of murder and lust back to the root of adultery, and addresses divorce, oaths, and retaliation, telling his followers to turn the other cheek rather than seek revenge (vv. 21-42). The chapter closes with the call to love one's enemies and pray for those who persecute them, since God shows kindness to both the evil and the good, ending with the command to be perfect as the heavenly Father is perfect (vv. 43-48).

Authorship & Background

Author: Matthew (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapters 5-7 contain the Sermon on the Mount — the most famous and comprehensive teaching of Jesus. It was delivered on a hillside near Capernaum, likely early in His Galilean ministry. Matthew presents it as the first of five major discourses in his Gospel (echoing the five books of Moses), establishing Jesus as the new Moses giving a new law from a new mountain.
Chapter 5 contains three sections: (1) The Beatitudes (vv.3-12) — the character of kingdom citizens (2) Salt and Light (vv.13-16) — the influence of kingdom citizens (3) The Law Fulfilled (vv.17-48) — the righteousness of kingdom citizens
The Six Antitheses ("You have heard... but I say"): Jesus does not contradict the Law — He deepens it, going from external behavior to internal motivation. Murder begins with anger; adultery begins with lust. The kingdom requires heart transformation, not just behavioral compliance.
Classification: Gospel — Narrative and Discourse
Key Themes: nine blessings delivered from a mountain in a teacher's posture, each pairing a condition nobody would choose with a promise attached to it, salt and light offered as two images for the same uselessness if withheld, a claim stronger than the objection it answers, I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil, a standard raised rather than relaxed, except your righteousness shall exceed the righteousness of the scribes and Pharisees, and six antitheses that trace commandments back to the heart, murder to anger, adultery to a look, ending on love your enemies and an imitation argument rather than a results-based one, for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good
The Beatitudes (vv.1-12): He goes up into a mountain and sits down, which is a teacher's posture, and the disciples come to him. Nine blessings, and the pattern is consistent: a condition nobody would choose, and a promise attached to it. Poor in spirit, mourning, meek, hungering after righteousness, merciful, pure in heart, peacemakers, persecuted. The kingdom is promised twice, at the beginning and at the eighth, which closes the set. Then the last one changes person from they to ye and gets three verses instead of one, blessed are ye, when men shall revile you, with a reason that puts the hearers in a line, for so persecuted they the prophets which were before you.
Salt and Light (vv.13-16): Two images for the same thing and both are useless if withheld. Salt that has lost its savour is good for nothing but to be trodden under foot. A candle is not lit to be put under a bushel, and a city set on an hill cannot be hid. Then the instruction, and the object of it is not the reputation of the person shining, let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.
Not to Destroy, But to Fulfil (vv.17-20): Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets. The denial is emphatic and the positive claim is stronger than the objection it answers, I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil, with a guarantee attached about the smallest written mark, one jot or one tittle. Then the sentence that governs everything after it and would have startled the audience most: except your righteousness shall exceed the righteousness of the scribes and Pharisees, ye shall in no case enter into the kingdom of heaven. The standard is being raised, not relaxed.
Anger, Lust, Divorce and Oaths (vv.21-37): Four of the six antitheses, and each follows the same form: ye have heard that it was said, but I say unto you. Killing is traced back to anger and to contempt, and the illustration is about worship, leave there thy gift before the altar, and go thy way, first be reconciled to thy brother. Adultery is traced to the look. The remedies proposed are deliberately extreme, if thy right eye offend thee, pluck it out. Divorce is restricted to one ground. And oaths are removed altogether in favor of plain speech, let your communication be, Yea, yea, Nay, nay, with the reasoning that a man who swears by heaven or earth or his own head is pledging things he does not own.
Love Your Enemies (vv.38-48): The last two antitheses. An eye for an eye is replaced not by pacifism in the abstract but by four specific responses, the other cheek, the cloak as well as the coat, the second mile, and lending without turning away. Then love your enemies, bless them that curse you, and the reason given is imitation rather than results, for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust. The argument closes by pointing out that loving people who love you back is not distinctive, do not even the publicans the same? And then the sentence the whole chapter has been building toward, be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect.

Setting: Galilee, early in Jesus' ministry, about AD 28. Jesus has been baptized, tested in the wilderness, has settled in Capernaum, called four fishermen, and been healing every disease. Crowds have come from Galilee, the Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea and beyond the Jordan (4:25). Now Matthew gives the first and most famous of five long blocks of Jesus' teaching in his Gospel, the Sermon on the Mount (chs 5–7). Luke has a shorter version on "a level place" (Luke 6:17–49). Many scholars think Matthew gathered teaching Jesus gave more than once into one sermon, while others take it as a single occasion. Either way, it is Jesus' description of life in the kingdom of heaven he has just announced (4:17). Chapter 5 opens with blessings, calls the disciples salt and light, and then shows what it means that Jesus came to fulfill the Law.

The Sermon on the Mount (v1)

  • "Seeing the crowds, he went up on the mountain" (v1). The traditional site is a hillside above the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee near Capernaum, now marked by the Church of the Beatitudes. Matthew's readers would think of Moses, who went up Mount Sinai and brought down God's law (Exod 19–20). Now a greater than Moses speaks from a mountain, not passing on God's word but saying "I say to you."
  • "When he sat down, his disciples came to him" (v1). Jewish teachers sat to teach (Luke 4:20; Matt 23:2). The sermon is addressed to disciples, with the crowds listening in (7:28). It describes the life of those who already follow Jesus, not a list of rules to earn entry.
  • Commentators: JFB says the mountain could be "any one" of several hills by the lake, "answering about equally well to the occasion." Guzik notes that "he opened his mouth" signals a solemn, weighty statement.

The Beatitudes (vv2–12)

  • "Blessed" (v3). Makarios means fortunate, well off, to be congratulated, in God's eyes. Latin beatus gives the name "Beatitudes." It is not a mood but a verdict. The Psalms begin the same way: "Blessed is the man" (Ps 1:1). Each Beatitude names an unlikely group and a promised reward. The first and eighth both end "theirs is the kingdom of heaven," framing the list. The rewards in between are future ("shall be"), but the blessing is already true.
  • "The poor in spirit" (v3). Greek had two words for poor: penēs, the working poor with little to spare, and ptōchos, the destitute who must beg. Jesus uses ptōchos. To be poor in spirit is to know one has nothing to offer God and must come as a beggar. Luke's version says simply "you who are poor" (Luke 6:20). In the Old Testament "the poor" are often the humble faithful who depend on God because they have nothing else (Ps 34:6; Isa 61:1; 66:2). The kingdom belongs to those who know they cannot earn it.
  • "Those who mourn" (v4). Mourning over sin, one's own and the world's, and over the suffering of God's people. Isaiah 61:1–3 lies behind the first Beatitudes: the Anointed One comes "to bring good news to the poor… to comfort all who mourn." "They shall be comforted" is in the passive voice, a reverent Jewish way of saying God will do it.
  • "The meek, for they shall inherit the earth" (v5). This is almost a quotation of Psalm 37:11. "Meek" (praus) is not weak. It describes strength under control, gentleness that does not grasp or retaliate. Moses was called the meekest man on earth (Num 12:3), and Jesus calls himself "gentle (praus) and lowly in heart" (11:29). In the psalm, "the land" meant Canaan. Jesus widens it to the whole renewed earth.
  • "Those who hunger and thirst for righteousness" (v6). In Matthew "righteousness" usually means living rightly before God, doing his will (5:20; 6:33). It may also include longing for God to set the world right, justice. Either way, the hunger is desperate, like a starving man's. They "shall be satisfied," filled to the full.
  • "The pure in heart, for they shall see God" (v8). "Who shall ascend the hill of the LORD?… He who has clean hands and a pure heart" (Ps 24:3–4). Purity of heart means an undivided, sincere heart, not one that is clean on the outside only (23:25–28). Moses was not allowed to see God's face (Exod 33:20). The pure in heart will (1 John 3:2; Rev 22:4).
  • "The peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God" (v9). Not just peace-lovers but peace-makers, people who work to reconcile. In a land where Zealots wanted to drive out Rome by force, this was pointed. God himself is the great peacemaker (Eph 2:14–17; Col 1:20), so those who make peace show the family likeness.
  • "Those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake" (vv10–12). The eighth Beatitude, then an expansion in the second person, "Blessed are you." It is persecution for righteousness and "on my account," not for being difficult. Jesus places his followers alongside "the prophets who were before you," who were also mistreated (Jer 20:2; 2 Chron 24:20–22). Remarkably, Jesus puts himself where God stands: suffering "on my account."
  • A portrait, not a ladder. The Beatitudes are not eight separate kinds of Christians or steps to climb. Together they describe one person, the disciple. They also describe Jesus himself: poor, mourning (Luke 19:41), meek, hungering for righteousness, merciful, pure, peacemaking and persecuted.
  • Commentators: Guzik draws out the two Greek words for "poor," and describes meekness as strength under control, like a trained horse. JFB says the poor in spirit are those who "in their deepest consciousness realize their entire need," and that peacemakers "not only study peace, but diffuse it." Henry says they "see their want, bewail their guilt, and thirst after a Redeemer," and that "Such mourners shall be comforted by their God."

Salt and Light (vv13–16)

  • "You are the salt of the earth" (v13). "You" is emphatic and plural: Jesus means his disciples, together. Salt preserved meat and fish from rotting and gave flavor. It was also used in covenant-making (Num 18:19) and on sacrifices (Lev 2:13). The disciples are to slow the decay of the world and give it taste.
  • "If salt has lost its taste" (v13). Pure sodium chloride does not lose saltiness. But salt in Palestine was often gathered from the Dead Sea marshes, mixed with other minerals. When damp, the real salt could leach out, leaving a bland white residue that looked like salt but was useless. A disciple who blends into the world loses the very thing that made him useful. The Greek word for "lost its taste" (mōrainō) can also mean "become foolish."
  • "You are the light of the world. A city set on a hill cannot be hidden" (v14). Jesus calls himself the light of the world (John 8:12). His followers carry his light. Israel was meant to be "a light for the nations" (Isa 42:6; 49:6), and Jerusalem was the city on the hill to which nations would stream (Isa 2:2–3). Now Jesus' disciples take up that calling. A town of white limestone on a Galilean hilltop was visible for miles, and at night its lamps could be seen across the valley.
  • "So that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father who is in heaven" (v16). Good works are meant to be seen, but the aim is God's glory, not ours. This balances 6:1, where Jesus warns against doing good "in order to be seen." The difference is motive: whose praise is being sought. This is the first time in Matthew that Jesus calls God "your Father," a theme that runs through the sermon.
  • Commentators: Guzik explains that impure ancient salt could lose its real salt and leave a useless residue. JFB defines the loss as losing "its saline or salting property," and asks whether living faith, once lost, can be replaced by anything else. Henry calls failed disciples "as salt that has lost its savour."

Christ Came to Fulfill the Law (vv17–20)

  • "But to fulfill them" (v17). "Fulfill" (plēroō) is Matthew's word for prophecy coming true (1:22; 2:15). Jesus fulfills the Old Testament by doing what it predicted, by obeying it perfectly, and by bringing out its full meaning, as the rest of this chapter shows. He is what the Law and Prophets pointed to. That is why some laws, such as the sacrifices and food laws, are no longer kept in the same way by Christians: they are not cancelled but completed in him (Mark 7:19; Heb 10:1–14).
  • "Not an iota, not a dot, will pass from the Law" (v18). The iota (Hebrew yod) is the smallest letter of the alphabet. The "dot" (KJV "tittle") is a tiny stroke that distinguishes one Hebrew letter from another. Not the smallest detail of God's word will fail. "Truly, I say to you" (amēn legō hymin) is Jesus' solemn formula. No prophet spoke this way. They said, "Thus says the LORD."
  • "Unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees" (v20). This would have shocked the listeners. The Pharisees were the most careful law-keepers in Israel. How could anyone exceed them? The answer is not more rules but a different kind of righteousness: from the heart, as vv21–48 will show. And in the larger story of the New Testament, it is a righteousness that comes as a gift (Phil 3:9; Rom 3:21–22) and then produces a changed life.
  • Commentators: JFB says the superiority required is "plainly in kind, not degree," inward and spiritual, and calls the yod "the smallest of the Hebrew letters." Guzik agrees that the difference is in kind and points to righteousness received by faith in Christ. Henry: "Let none suppose that Christ allows his people to trifle with any commands of God's holy law."

Anger (vv21–26)

  • "You have heard that it was said to those of old… But I say to you" (vv21–22). Six times in vv21–48 Jesus uses this pattern. "Those of old" means the generation at Sinai, and "it was said" refers to the Law as it was commonly taught. Jesus is not contradicting Moses. He is going beneath the letter to the heart, and often correcting the way teachers had narrowed it. "But I say" claims an authority above every rabbi (7:28–29).
  • "Everyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment" (v22). "You shall not murder" (Exod 20:13) concerns the act. Jesus traces it back to the root: anger and contempt. Some manuscripts add "without a cause," but the oldest lack it. Jesus himself was angry at sin (Mark 3:5), so he means the selfish, nursed anger that wishes harm. "Brother" here means a fellow member of God's people.
  • "Whoever insults his brother will be liable to the council" (v22). "Insults" translates Raca, an Aramaic word meaning "empty," like calling someone an empty-head. The "council" (synedrion) is the Sanhedrin, the Jewish high court.
  • "Whoever says, 'You fool!' will be liable to the hell of fire" (v22). "Fool" (mōros, from which "moron") meant more than unintelligent. In the Old Testament "the fool says in his heart, 'There is no God'" (Ps 14:1). It was a judgment on someone's worth before God. "The hell of fire" is literally "the Gehenna of fire." Gehenna was the Valley of Hinnom, south of Jerusalem, where kings Ahaz and Manasseh sacrificed children to Molech (2 Chron 28:3; 33:6; Jer 7:31). Josiah desecrated it (2 Kings 23:10), and Jewish writers came to use its name for the place of final judgment. The three steps are not a precise legal scale. They escalate to make the point: contempt is murder in seed form, and God judges it.
  • "Leave your gift there before the altar and go. First be reconciled to your brother" (v24). For a Galilean, the altar was in Jerusalem, several days' walk away. Picture leaving a sacrificial animal at the temple, walking home to make peace and coming back. Jesus' point is that God cares more about broken relationships than ritual (Hos 6:6; Matt 9:13). Notice too that the brother "has something against you." It is the one who caused the offense who must go.
  • Commentators: Henry calls rash anger "heart murder," rooted in pride, and urges quick reconciliation before worship. JFB links "hell-fire" to "the valley of the son of Hinnom" and thinks it "as useless as it is vain" to try to fix the exact difference in meaning between Raca and "fool." Guzik stresses that Jesus does not say anger is equal to murder, but that it breaks the heart of the command.

Lust (vv27–30)

  • "Everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (v28). The Greek suggests looking in order to desire, a deliberate, sustained gaze, not a passing glance or attraction. Job had made "a covenant with my eyes" (Job 31:1). Jesus addresses men, who held power in that world, and places the responsibility on the one looking, not on the woman.
  • "If your right eye causes you to sin, tear it out" (v29). "Causes you to sin" (skandalizō) means to trip someone up, from the stick that springs a trap. The right eye and right hand were thought the more valuable. Jesus is using deliberate exaggeration, a common Jewish teaching style. He does not mean literal self-harm, which would not cure a lustful heart anyway. He means: deal with sin ruthlessly. Remove whatever leads into it, however precious.
  • Commentators: JFB says the eye is meant "as an occasion of sin," not literally, and calls for "indignant promptitude" in cutting off what leads to sin. Henry says overcoming desire takes painful effort and that every sense must be guarded, with God's grace as the strength to do it. Guzik places the sin in cherished, persistent desire, and notes that adultery in the heart is sin, though not the same as the act.

Divorce (vv31–32)

  • "Whoever divorces his wife, let him give her a certificate of divorce" (v31). This summarizes Deuteronomy 24:1–4. Moses did not command divorce. He regulated it, requiring a written certificate that protected the woman by proving she was free to remarry. The rabbis argued about the grounds. Deuteronomy allows divorce for "some indecency." The school of Shammai said this meant sexual sin. The school of Hillel allowed divorce for almost anything, even burning a meal.
  • "Except on the ground of sexual immorality" (v32). The Greek is porneia, a broad word for sexual sin. Jesus sides with the stricter view and goes further. He says a man who divorces his wife for any other reason "makes her commit adultery," since in that world she would almost certainly need to remarry to survive, and the marriage was not truly ended in God's eyes. Jesus explains more in 19:3–9, where he points back to creation: "What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate."
  • Different readings. Christians have long disagreed on this text. Most Protestants read the exception as permitting divorce and remarriage when a spouse has committed adultery, and many add desertion by an unbelieving spouse (1 Cor 7:15). The Roman Catholic Church holds that a valid sacramental marriage cannot be dissolved, and reads porneia as an unlawful marriage. Some others think it refers to unfaithfulness during betrothal, as with Joseph (1:19). All agree that Jesus sets a very high view of marriage against the casual divorce of his day.
  • Commentators: JFB reads the exception as "conjugal infidelity," and says that otherwise the husband drives his wife toward adultery. Guzik explains the Shammai and Hillel debate, and notes that porneia means fornication at root but is used broadly. The Tyndale notes add that Jewish divorce certificates explicitly gave the woman freedom to marry another man.

Oaths (vv33–37)

  • "Do not take an oath at all, either by heaven… or by the earth… or by Jerusalem" (vv34–35). Some teachers ranked oaths: swearing by God's name was binding, but swearing by heaven or earth or one's head was less so, leaving room to wriggle out (23:16–22). Jesus says every oath involves God, because heaven is his throne, earth his footstool (Isa 66:1), and Jerusalem his city (Ps 48:2). There is no God-free zone to swear by.
  • "Let what you say be simply 'Yes' or 'No'" (v37). The goal is honesty so consistent that oaths are unnecessary. If a person needs an oath to be believed, their ordinary word is not trusted. James repeats this teaching (Jas 5:12).
  • Does this forbid all oaths? Some Christians, such as Quakers and Mennonites, refuse to take oaths, even in court, on the basis of this verse. Most traditions think Jesus forbids casual, evasive swearing in daily speech, not solemn oaths in court. God himself swears oaths (Heb 6:13–18), Jesus answered under oath at his trial (26:63–64), and Paul calls God as witness (2 Cor 1:23; Gal 1:20).
  • Commentators: JFB says the target is "swearing in common intercourse and on frivolous occasions." Henry agrees that oaths in court are allowed, but "all oaths taken without necessity, or in common conversation, must be sinful." Guzik cites God's oaths and Paul's as evidence that not every oath is forbidden.

Retaliation (vv38–42)

  • "An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth" (v38). This law (Exod 21:24; Lev 24:20; Deut 19:21) was given to judges, not individuals. It limited punishment to fit the crime and stopped blood feuds from escalating. By Jesus' day courts usually applied it as monetary compensation. The problem was using it to justify personal revenge.
  • "Do not resist the one who is evil" (v39). In context, Jesus means his followers must not retaliate against someone who wrongs them personally. It is not a statement about whether governments may restrain evil (Rom 13:1–4), nor a call to ignore abuse or injustice to others. Jesus himself objected when struck unjustly (John 18:22–23). But he did not strike back (1 Pet 2:23).
  • "If anyone slaps you on the right cheek, turn to him the other also" (v39). For a right-handed person to strike the right cheek, he must use the back of the hand. That was an insult more than an assault, and Jewish law fined it heavily. Jesus says: do not answer insult with insult.
  • "If anyone forces you to go one mile, go with him two miles" (v41). The verb angareuō is a technical term borrowed from Persian for forced service. Roman soldiers could make civilians carry their gear one mile. Simon of Cyrene was "compelled" with this same verb to carry Jesus' cross (27:32). Going a second mile turns forced labor into a free gift.
  • Commentators: Guzik explains the backhand slap as an insult punished by Jewish law, and notes Jesus is speaking of personal relationships, not the government's duty to restrain evil. JFB reads "resist not" as readiness "to submit meekly to another, without retaliation," and links "compel" to forced carrying of government dispatches. Henry: "Suffer any injury that can be borne, for the sake of peace, committing your concerns to the Lord's keeping."

Love Your Enemies (vv43–48)

  • "You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy" (v43). The first half is Leviticus 19:18. The second half is not in the Old Testament. It was a popular conclusion drawn by narrowing "neighbor" to fellow Israelites. The Dead Sea community taught its members to "hate all the sons of darkness." Leviticus itself commanded love for the stranger (Lev 19:34), and Proverbs said to feed a hungry enemy (Prov 25:21).
  • "Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (v44). Not just tolerate them but love them, actively seek their good, and pray for them. Jesus did this on the cross: "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). So did Stephen (Acts 7:60).
  • "You therefore must be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect" (v48). "Perfect" (teleios) means complete, whole, mature, reaching its goal. It echoes "You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy" (Lev 19:2). In context the perfection is love: complete, undivided love that includes enemies, as the Father's does. Luke's parallel reads "Be merciful, even as your Father is merciful" (Luke 6:36). No one achieves this fully in this life, and the sermon's opening blessing on the "poor in spirit" assumes that. But it is the direction and the goal of every disciple.
  • Commentators: JFB calls "hate thine enemy" "a detestable gloss" added by corrupt teachers, and says "perfect" refers "not of degrees of excellence, but of the kind of excellence" that marks the kingdom. Henry says "we must render good for evil," and that Christians should "press towards perfection in grace and holiness," imitating the Father.

Pointing to Christ

The Sermon on the Mount can sound impossible, and by human effort alone it is. No one has kept it except the one who preached it. Jesus was poor in spirit, meek and merciful. He never looked with lust, never gave a false word, turned the other cheek and prayed for those who crucified him. He did not abolish the Law. He fulfilled it, every iota and dot. The righteousness that exceeds the Pharisees' is first his, given to those who come to him as beggars, poor in spirit. Then, by his Spirit, it begins to grow in them, until they look like their Father.

Reflection

  • The Character of Kingdom Citizens (vv.3-12) — The Beatitudes describe a man who is spiritually poor, mourning over sin, meek, hungry for righteousness, merciful, pure in heart, and a peacemaker. Which of these describes you most, which describes you least, and what does that gap reveal?
  • Salt and Light (vv.13-16) — Jesus calls His followers to preserve what is good and add flavor and light wherever they go. Are you functioning as salt in your workplace, neighborhood, and family, or have you lost your distinctiveness by blending in?
  • A Righteousness of the Heart (v.20) — Jesus says His followers' righteousness must exceed that of the Pharisees, men who were externally impeccable but inwardly unchanged. Do you evaluate your own spiritual condition by outward behavior or by the true state of your heart?
  • Anger as the Root of Murder (v.22) — Jesus traces the sin of murder back to unresolved anger and contempt toward a brother. Is there someone you're carrying a slow burn of resentment toward, and who do you need to pursue reconciliation with?
  • Lust as the Root of Adultery (v.28) — Jesus locates the sin of adultery in the heart, in the look before the act. What disciplines do you have in place to guard your eyes and your mind, and are they actually working?
  • Love for Enemies (v.44) — Jesus calls for a supernatural love that blesses and prays for those who are hostile toward us, not just tolerance of them. Name one person who has wronged you — what would it look like to bless and pray for them this week?
