1Now there were also false prophets among the people, just as there will be false teachers among you. They will secretly introduce destructive heresies, even denying the Master who bought them—bringing swift destruction on themselves.

2Many will follow in their depravity, and because of them the way of truth will be defamed.

3In their greed, these false teachers will exploit you with deceptive words. The longstanding verdict against them remains in force, and their destruction does not sleep.

5if He did not spare the ancient world when He brought the flood on its ungodly people, but preserved Noah, a preacher of righteousness, among the eight;

6if He condemned the cities of Sodom and Gomorrah to destruction, reducing them to ashes as an example of what is coming on the ungodly;

7and if He rescued Lot, a righteous man distressed by the depraved conduct of the lawless

8(for that righteous man, living among them day after day, was tormented in his righteous soul by the lawless deeds he saw and heard)—

9if all this is so, then the Lord knows how to rescue the godly from trials and to hold the unrighteous for punishment on the day of judgment.

10Such punishment is specially reserved for those who indulge the corrupt desires of the flesh and despise authority. Bold and self-willed, they are unafraid to slander glorious beings.

11Yet not even angels, though greater in strength and power, dare to bring such slanderous charges against them before the Lord.

12These men are like irrational animals, creatures of instinct, born to be captured and destroyed. They blaspheme in matters they do not understand, and like such creatures, they too will be destroyed.

13The harm they will suffer is the wages of their wickedness. They consider it a pleasure to carouse in broad daylight. They are blots and blemishes, reveling in their deception as they feast with you.

14Their eyes are full of adultery; their desire for sin is never satisfied; they seduce the unstable. They are accursed children with hearts trained in greed.

15They have left the straight way and wandered off to follow the way of Balaam son of Beor, who loved the wages of wickedness.

16But he was rebuked for his transgression by a donkey, otherwise without speech, that spoke with a man’s voice and restrained the prophet’s madness.

17These men are springs without water and mists driven by a storm. Blackest darkness is reserved for them.

18With lofty but empty words, they appeal to the sensual passions of the flesh and entice those who are just escaping from others who live in error.

20If indeed they have escaped the corruption of the world through the knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ, only to be entangled and overcome by it again, their final condition is worse than it was at first.

21It would have been better for them not to have known the way of righteousness than to have known it and then to turn away from the holy commandment passed on to them.

22Of them the proverbs are true: “A dog returns to its vomit,” and, “A sow that is washed goes back to her wallowing in the mud.”

Summary

Peter warns that just as false prophets once arose among Israel, false teachers will infiltrate the church, secretly introducing destructive heresies and even denying the Master who bought them (vv. 1-3). He argues from precedent — God did not spare the sinning angels, did not spare the ancient world but preserved Noah through the flood, and reduced Sodom and Gomorrah to ashes while rescuing righteous Lot — proof that the Lord knows how to rescue the godly from trial and keep the unrighteous under punishment until judgment (vv. 4-9). He describes these false teachers as bold, willful, and driven by sensuality and greed, comparing them to irrational animals and to Balaam, who loved the wages of wrongdoing but was rebuked by his own donkey (vv. 10-16). Peter calls them waterless springs and storm-driven mist, luring back into sin those who had barely escaped a corrupt way of life, warning that it would have been better for them never to have known the way of righteousness than to know it and turn back (vv. 17-22).

Authorship & Background

Author: Simon Peter (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Historical Context: Chapter 2 is Peter's extended warning against false teachers — one of the most severe denunciations in the NT. These teachers were infiltrating the church with destructive heresies, denying Christ, exploiting believers for money, and living in sexual immorality. Peter uses three OT examples of God's judgment (fallen angels, the flood, Sodom) to assure readers that God will judge these teachers. The chapter parallels Jude closely.
Classification: Epistle — General
Key Themes: False teachers named by their greed before their doctrine, three Old Testament judgments cited as one argument for a fourth, God shown rescuing and reserving in the same verse, wells without water and clouds without rain, and an end pronounced worse than a beginning never reached
False Teachers Described (vv.1-3): They bring "damnable heresies" secretly (v.1). They deny the Lord (v.1). Many follow their immorality (v.2). They exploit with "feigned words" for greed (v.3). Their judgment is certain (v.3).
Three Examples of Judgment (vv.4-9): Angels who sinned — cast to hell (v.4). The ancient world — destroyed by flood, Noah saved (v.5). Sodom and Gomorrah — burned to ashes, Lot delivered (vv.6-8). Conclusion: God knows how to deliver the godly and reserve the unjust for judgment (v.9).
Character of False Teachers (vv.10-22): They follow the flesh, despise authority (v.10). They are bold, self-willed, slanderous (v.10). Like brute beasts (v.12). They feast with believers while deceiving (v.13). Eyes full of adultery, greedy (v.14). They follow Balaam's way — loving money (v.15). "Wells without water, clouds without rain" (v.17). They promise freedom but are slaves to corruption (v.19). Their end is worse than their beginning (vv.20-22).

Map & Geography

  • Sodom (v.6): City destroyed by God for its wickedness (Genesis 19).
  • Gomorrah (v.6): City destroyed alongside Sodom.

Setting: Peter is near the end of his life (1:14), probably writing from Rome in the mid-60s AD to the same churches in Asia Minor who received his first letter (3:1). Chapter 1 ended with the prophets of Scripture, who "spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit" (1:21). Now Peter turns to the dark side of that truth: Israel also had false prophets, and the church will have false teachers. Chapter 2 is the most severe chapter in the letter. It does not argue with the false teachers' ideas so much as expose their character: greedy, sexually immoral, arrogant and contemptuous of authority. Much of it runs closely parallel to the short letter of Jude (compare Jude 4–16). Most scholars think one used the other, and opinions differ on which came first; others think both drew on shared early Christian teaching. Peter's tone is fierce, but his aim is pastoral: to show that God knows how to judge the wicked and also how to rescue the godly.

False Prophets and Teachers (vv1–22)

  • "False prophets also arose among the people" (v1). "The people" is Israel. Moses warned about prophets who would lead the people after other gods (Deut 13:1–5; 18:20), and Jeremiah and Ezekiel fought prophets who said "Peace, peace" when there was no peace (Jer 6:14; 23:16–17; Ezek 13). Jesus warned that the pattern would continue (Matt 7:15; 24:11, 24), and so did Paul (Acts 20:29–30).
  • "There will be false teachers among you" (v1). Peter uses the future tense, but by v13 he speaks of them as already present, feasting with the church. He is recalling the prophecy and saying it is now being fulfilled. Note "among you": the danger is inside the church, not outside.
  • "Who will secretly bring in destructive heresies" (v1). "Secretly bring in" (pareisagō) is used of smuggling something in alongside. Paul uses a related word for "false brothers secretly brought in" (Gal 2:4). "Heresies" (haireseis) first meant "choices" or "parties," then factions, and here self-chosen teachings that divide and destroy.
  • "Even denying the Master who bought them" (v1). "Master" is despotēs, a slave owner. He "bought" them, a picture of buying slaves at market, the language of redemption (1 Cor 6:20; Rev 5:9). They may have denied Christ in words, or more likely by living as though he had no claim on them (Titus 1:16; Jude 4). This verse is also part of a long debate about the extent of the atonement. Some say Christ's death truly paid for these men even though they reject him, so he died for all. Others say Peter is describing them as they claimed to be, or uses "bought" in a looser sense of ownership. Either way, the point is the depth of their betrayal: they reject the very One who owns them.
  • "Many will follow their sensuality" (v2). "Sensuality" (aselgeia) is unrestrained, shameless indulgence. The false teaching was attractive because it gave permission. "The way of truth will be blasphemed": when people who claim Christ live immorally, outsiders mock the faith itself (Rom 2:24).
  • "In their greed they will exploit you with false words" (v3). "Exploit" is emporeuomai, to trade or do business; the lexicon gives "to make a gain of" people here. It gives English "emporium." They treat the church as a market and its members as merchandise. "False words" (plastos, "molded, fabricated") gives English "plastic": words shaped to fit whatever sells.
  • "Their condemnation… is not idle, and their destruction is not asleep" (v3). The false teachers will say God's judgment is idle and asleep (3:4). Peter answers before they ask: judgment is awake and on its way.
  • "If God did not spare angels when they sinned" (v4). This opens one long sentence (vv4–10) built on three "if" examples from Genesis and a "then" conclusion in v9. The first example is angels. "Cast them into hell" is the verb tartaroō, used only here in the Bible, from Tartarus, the Greek name for the deepest pit of the underworld, a place of punishment below Hades. Peter borrows a word his Gentile readers knew to name a real place of confinement.
  • Which angels? (v4). Scripture says little about the angels' sin. Jude 6 says they "did not stay within their own position of authority, but left their proper dwelling." Many Jewish writers of the time, especially the book of 1 Enoch, linked this to the "sons of God" who took human wives before the flood (Gen 6:1–4), and the Tyndale notes take that view. The order here, angels and then the flood, fits that reading. Others think of an original rebellion of angels with Satan, before the creation of humans. Peter does not settle it.
  • "Chains of gloomy darkness to be kept until the judgment" (v4). Some manuscripts read "pits" instead of "chains." Either way, these angels are held, not loose. Other fallen angels, including the devil, are clearly still active (1 Pet 5:8), so this seems to be a particular group.
  • "He did not spare the ancient world, but preserved Noah, a herald of righteousness, with seven others" (v5). The flood is the second example (Gen 6–8). Genesis does not record Noah preaching, but Jewish tradition did, and Peter says he was a "herald" (kēryx), a public announcer. He warned while he built. "With seven others" is literally "Noah, the eighth": Noah with his wife, three sons and their wives (1 Pet 3:20). The key pattern appears: God destroyed the many but kept the few who trusted him.
  • "Turning the cities of Sodom and Gomorrah to ashes" (v6). The third example (Gen 19). "Turning… to ashes" (tephroō) describes incineration. "An example" (hypodeigma) is a pattern or warning sign: what happened to Sodom previews what will happen to all the ungodly (Jude 7).
  • "He rescued righteous Lot" (vv7–8). This surprises many readers. In Genesis Lot chooses the best land near Sodom for himself (Gen 13:10–13), lives in the city, offers his daughters to a mob (Gen 19:8), lingers when the angels try to rescue him (19:16) and ends in a drunken disgrace (19:30–38). Yet Abraham's plea for Sodom assumed there were righteous people in it (Gen 18:23–32), and God spared Lot. Jewish writings also called him righteous (Wisdom of Solomon 10:6). Peter adds an insight Genesis only hints at: Lot was "greatly distressed" and "tormenting his righteous soul" day after day over what he saw and heard. He was a compromised believer, but a believer. He hated the sin around him, even while staying too close to it.
  • "Then the Lord knows how to rescue the godly from trials" (v9). Here is the conclusion of the long sentence. Two truths run side by side. God rescues the godly, as he did Noah and Lot, and God keeps the unrighteous "under punishment until the day of judgment," as he did the angels. For believers surrounded by corruption and pressure, this is comfort: the Lord knows how to bring them through, even when they cannot see how.
  • "Especially those who indulge in the lust of defiling passion and despise authority" (v10). Two marks of the false teachers stand out: sexual immorality and contempt for authority. "Authority" (kyriotēs, "lordship") may mean the Lord's own authority (v1), or the authority of angelic powers, or church leaders. The two sins belong together: those who refuse to be ruled will rule themselves by appetite.
  • "They do not tremble as they blaspheme the glorious ones" (v10). "Glorious ones" (doxai, "glories") most likely means angelic beings, probably evil ones. The false teachers mocked spiritual powers they did not understand, perhaps sneering at the idea of the devil or of judgment, or boasting that they were free from any spiritual danger.
  • "Angels… do not pronounce a blasphemous judgment against them before the Lord" (v11). Even good angels, far stronger, leave judgment of other spiritual beings to God. Jude gives the example of the archangel Michael, who, disputing with the devil over Moses' body, would not bring a railing judgment but said, "The Lord rebuke you" (Jude 9; compare Zech 3:2). Peter leaves out the story and states the principle.
  • "Like irrational animals, creatures of instinct, born to be caught and destroyed" (v12). For all their claims to knowledge, they behave like animals driven by appetite. "Blaspheming about matters of which they are ignorant": they mock what they cannot understand. "Will also be destroyed in their destruction" likely means they will perish like the animals they resemble.
  • "They count it pleasure to revel in the daytime" (v13). Even pagans who partied usually waited for night (1 Thess 5:7). These men had no shame left. "Blots and blemishes" (spiloi kai mōmoi) is the exact opposite of Christ, the lamb "without blemish or spot" (1 Pet 1:19), and of what believers are to be (3:14).
  • "Reveling in their deceptions, while they feast with you" (v13). The early church shared meals together, often with the Lord's Supper (1 Cor 11:20–22). The false teachers sat at those tables. Many manuscripts read apatais, "deceptions," followed by the ESV. Others read agapais, "love feasts," which is the word in the parallel Jude 12. The two words differ by only a few letters in Greek, and both make sense: they turned the love feasts into deceit.
  • "Eyes full of adultery, insatiable for sin" (v14). Literally "eyes full of an adulteress": every woman they saw was a possible conquest (Matt 5:28). "Entice" is deleazō, from delear, bait, to lure with bait. Peter the fisherman knows the picture: these men bait their hooks for "unsteady souls." James uses the same word for how desire lures a person into sin (James 1:14). "Hearts trained in greed": "trained" is the word for athletic exercise (gymnazō, giving English "gymnasium"). They have practiced greed until they are experts. "Accursed children!" is a Hebrew way of saying people under God's curse.
  • "They have followed the way of Balaam, the son of Beor" (v15). Balaam was a famous diviner from Mesopotamia hired by King Balak of Moab to curse Israel (Num 22–24). God would not let him curse, and he blessed Israel instead. But he later advised Moab to lead Israel into sexual sin and idolatry at Peor (Num 31:16; Rev 2:14), and Israel killed him (Num 31:8). He became the classic picture of a prophet for hire: he "loved gain from wrongdoing." Some manuscripts read "son of Bosor" instead of "Beor," which may reflect a Galilean pronunciation of the name or a wordplay on the Hebrew basar, "flesh."
  • "A speechless donkey spoke with human voice and restrained the prophet's madness" (v16). The famous scene in Numbers 22:21–35. The irony is sharp: the "seer" could not see the angel standing in his way, and his donkey could. A beast had more sense than the prophet, just as the false teachers are "like irrational animals" (v12) with less sense than one.
  • "Waterless springs and mists driven by a storm" (v17). In dry country, a spring that promises water and is dry can be deadly to a traveler. Mists that look like rain and blow away give nothing. Jude has "waterless clouds" (Jude 12). JFB notes that the oldest manuscripts here read "mists," and that "clouds" in some copies came from Jude. "The gloom of utter darkness" is the same darkness that holds the fallen angels (v4).
  • "Speaking loud boasts of folly" (v18). Big, swelling words with nothing inside. "They entice by sensual passions of the flesh": the bait again (deleazō). Their targets are "those who are barely escaping from those who live in error," new converts still finding their footing, the most vulnerable people in the church.
  • "They promise them freedom, but they themselves are slaves of corruption" (v19). This is the center of their appeal. They likely twisted the gospel of grace into a license: since we are saved by grace, the body and its sins do not matter (Rom 6:1; Gal 5:13; 1 Pet 2:16). Peter answers with a principle that was a common proverb: "Whatever overcomes a person, to that he is enslaved." In the ancient world, a soldier defeated in battle became the slave of his conqueror. Jesus said the same: "everyone who practices sin is a slave to sin" (John 8:34).
  • "The last state has become worse for them than the first" (v20). These are people who "escaped the defilements of the world through the knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ," and then went back. The phrase echoes Jesus' parable of the unclean spirit that returns with seven worse ones: "the last state of that person is worse than the first" (Matt 12:45). It may describe the false teachers, their followers, or both.
  • "Better for them never to have known the way of righteousness" (v21). Knowing the truth and turning from it brings greater accountability (Luke 12:47–48; Heb 6:4–6; 10:26–27). "Delivered" is the word for handing on authoritative teaching (Jude 3): the commandment was given to them, and they turned their backs on it.
  • Were these people ever truly saved? (vv20–22). Christians have long disagreed. Many in the Reformed tradition say they had outward knowledge of Christ and reformed their behavior for a while, but their nature never changed, which is exactly what the proverb in v22 suggests. Many in the Wesleyan and Arminian tradition say they were truly saved and fell away, which is why the warning is so sharp. Both agree on the practical point: a profession of faith that returns to its old sins gives no ground for confidence, and the warning is meant to be heard.
  • "The dog returns to its own vomit, and the sow, after washing herself, returns to wallow in the mire" (v22). The first proverb is Proverbs 26:11. The second was a well-known saying; a version appears in the ancient Story of Ahiqar. In Jewish eyes both dogs and pigs were unclean animals. The dog's insides were never changed, and the pig was washed on the outside but was still a pig. Outward cleanup without a new nature does not last.
  • Commentators: Guzik observes that no false teacher ever introduces himself as one, and JFB says false doctrine comes in "not at first openly and directly, but by the way." On v1 JFB says that "even the ungodly were bought by His 'precious blood,'" which is one side of the atonement debate. Guzik takes it to mean they appeared to be saved. JFB notes that tartaroō is used "nowhere else in New Testament or the Septuagint," which the lexicon confirms. Guzik and the Tyndale notes both affirm Lot was righteous in God's sight despite his compromise, and Guzik adds that his failure to separate cost him nearly everything. JFB says Lot's soul was "tormented." Henry draws the comfort of v9: "When God sends destruction on the ungodly, he commands deliverance for the righteous." JFB says on v11 that "judgment belongs to God, not the angels." On v19 JFB writes that "the liberty which they propose is such as fears not Satan, nor loathes the flesh." Guzik lays out the Reformed and Arminian readings of vv20–22 and warns against bitter quarrels over them. Henry: "A state of apostacy is worse than a state of ignorance," yet "it is not utterly hopeless." JFB reads the proverb as showing they were dogs and swine all along.

Pointing to Christ

Behind all the darkness of this chapter stands "the Master who bought them" (v1). The false teachers deny him, but they cannot change the fact that he paid the price. Noah and Lot are pictures of what Christ does for his people: the Lord "knows how to rescue the godly" (v9), carrying them through judgment as Noah was carried through the flood. The false teachers promise a freedom that becomes slavery. Jesus offers the real freedom: "if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed" (John 8:36). And where washing the outside fails, as with the sow, he gives what no proverb can, a new nature (1:4).

Reflection

  • Discerning False Teachers From Within (v.1) — Peter warns that false teachers arise from inside the community, not just outside it, secretly introducing destructive teaching. Do you actually test what you're taught against Scripture, or accept it because of who's saying it?
  • God Knows How to Rescue the Godly (v.9) — Drawing on Noah and Lot, Peter concludes that God knows how to rescue the godly out of trials even while judging the unrighteous. Do you trust God's ability to deliver you in the middle of a corrupt culture, or does that trust waver?
  • The Way of Balaam — Loving Wrong Gain (v.15) — False teachers are compared to Balaam, who loved the profit that came from wrongdoing more than obedience to God. Where might money or advantage be quietly steering your choices away from what's right?
  • Wells Without Water (v.17) — Peter pictures false teachers as dry wells and stormless clouds — promising refreshment but delivering nothing. Have you been drawn in by impressive words that turned out to have no real substance behind them?
  • False Promises of Freedom (v.19) — These teachers offer liberty while being enslaved themselves to corruption, since whatever masters a person becomes their master. What does the world call freedom in your life that is actually a form of slavery?
  • Worse Than Where They Started (vv.20-22) — Peter warns that returning to old sin after knowing the truth leaves a person worse off than before they ever knew it. What guards you against drifting back to a way of life you've already left behind?
