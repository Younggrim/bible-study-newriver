1This is the word that came to Jeremiah from the LORD after Nebuzaradan captain of the guard had released him at Ramah, having found him bound in chains among all the captives of Jerusalem and Judah who were being exiled to Babylon.

2The captain of the guard found Jeremiah and said to him, “The LORD your God decreed this disaster on this place,

3and now the LORD has fulfilled it; He has done just as He said. Because you people have sinned against the LORD and have not obeyed His voice, this thing has happened to you.

4But now, behold, I am freeing you today from the chains that were on your wrists. If it pleases you to come with me to Babylon, then come, and I will take care of you. But if it seems wrong to you to come with me to Babylon, go no farther. Look, the whole land is before you. Wherever it seems good and right to you, go there.”

5But before Jeremiah turned to go, Nebuzaradan added, “Return to Gedaliah son of Ahikam, the son of Shaphan, whom the king of Babylon has appointed over the cities of Judah, and stay with him among the people, or go anywhere else that seems right.” Then the captain of the guard gave him a ration and a gift and released him.

6So Jeremiah went to Gedaliah son of Ahikam at Mizpah and stayed with him among the people who were left in the land.

7When all the commanders and men of the armies in the field heard that the king of Babylon had appointed Gedaliah son of Ahikam over the land and that he had put him in charge of the men, women, and children who were the poorest of the land and had not been exiled to Babylon,

8they came to Gedaliah at Mizpah—Ishmael son of Nethaniah, Johanan and Jonathan the sons of Kareah, Seraiah son of Tanhumeth, the sons of Ephai the Netophathite, and Jezaniah son of the Maacathite—they and their men.

9Gedaliah son of Ahikam, the son of Shaphan, swore an oath to them and their men, assuring them, “Do not be afraid to serve the Chaldeans. Live in the land and serve the king of Babylon, and it will go well with you.

10As for me, I will stay in Mizpah to represent you before the Chaldeans who come to us. As for you, gather wine grapes, summer fruit, and oil, place them in your storage jars, and live in the cities you have taken.”

11When all the Jews in Moab, Ammon, Edom, and all the other lands heard that the king of Babylon had left a remnant in Judah and had appointed Gedaliah son of Ahikam, the son of Shaphan, over them,

12they all returned from all the places to which they had been banished and came to the land of Judah, to Gedaliah at Mizpah. And they gathered an abundance of wine grapes and summer fruit.

13Meanwhile, Johanan son of Kareah and all the commanders of the armies in the field came to Gedaliah at Mizpah

14and said to him, “Are you aware that Baalis king of the Ammonites has sent Ishmael son of Nethaniah to take your life?” But Gedaliah son of Ahikam did not believe them.

15Then Johanan son of Kareah spoke privately to Gedaliah at Mizpah. “Let me go and kill Ishmael son of Nethaniah,” he said. “No one will know it. Why should he take your life and scatter all the people of Judah who have gathered to you, so that the remnant of Judah would perish?”

16But Gedaliah son of Ahikam said to Johanan son of Kareah, “Do not do such a thing! What you are saying about Ishmael is a lie.”

Summary

After Jerusalem's fall, the captain of the guard releases Jeremiah at Ramah and gives him the choice to go to Babylon or remain in the land; Jeremiah chooses to stay and settles with Gedaliah, the governor Babylon has appointed over the remaining Judeans (vv. 1-6). Scattered Judean survivors and soldiers gradually return and gather around Gedaliah, and for a time the land experiences a measure of peace and even harvests wine and summer fruit (vv. 7-12). But Johanan warns Gedaliah that Ishmael, a member of the royal family, has been sent by the king of Ammon to assassinate him, and offers to eliminate the threat secretly (vv. 13-15). Gedaliah refuses to believe the warning and takes no action (v. 16).

Authorship & Background

Author: Jeremiah son of Hilkiah (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Historical Narrative — The Aftermath
Key Themes: Jeremiah freed, Gedaliah's brief governance, temporary peace, the assassination plot warned but ignored, Judean remnant gathering
Historical Context: After Jerusalem's destruction (586 BC), Nebuchadnezzar appoints Gedaliah son of Ahikam (Jeremiah's old protector's son, cf. 26:24) as governor over the remaining poor population. Gedaliah establishes administration at Mizpah (north of Jerusalem). Scattered refugees return from Moab, Ammon, and Edom. A brief period of normalcy follows — harvest gathered, wine and fruit collected. But Baalis king of Ammon sends Ishmael (a member of Judah's royal family) to assassinate Gedaliah. Johanan warns the governor; Gedaliah refuses to believe it. This sets up the tragedy of chapter 41.
Released at Ramah (vv.1-6): This account overlaps chapter 39 and reads as the fuller of the two. He is found among the deportees, bound in chains among all that were carried away captive of Jerusalem, which means the protective order had not reached whoever was doing the sorting. The captain of the guard's speech is remarkable in the mouth of a Babylonian officer, the LORD thy God hath pronounced this evil upon this place, now the LORD hath brought it, because ye have sinned against the LORD, and have not obeyed his voice. Then a choice, come with me to Babylon and be looked after, or stay. He stays, which after forty years of insisting that Babylon was where God's hand was, is a decision worth pausing over.
Gedaliah at Mizpah (vv.7-12): The captains still in the field hear that the king of Babylon has made Gedaliah the son of Ahikam governor. His family is why he is trustworthy in this book: Ahikam is the man who protected Jeremiah from the mob at 26:24, and one of Shaphan's sons. The policy he announces under oath is the counsel Jeremiah had been giving for decades, fear not to serve the Chaldeans, dwell in the land, and serve the king of Babylon, and it shall be well with you. And it worked. Refugees came back from Moab, Ammon and Edom, and gathered wine and summer fruits very much. One good harvest under a competent administration.
Johanan's Warning (vv.13-16): Johanan brings intelligence and names the sponsor, knowest thou certainly that Baalis the king of the Ammonites hath sent Ishmael the son of Nethaniah to slay thee, and then offers to handle it quietly, let me go, and I will slay Ishmael, and no man shall know it. Gedaliah refuses and calls the report a fabrication, thou shalt not do this thing, for thou speakest falsely of Ishmael. The warning was accurate and the remedy offered was a political murder, and refusing it is the last decision the last competent government in Judah ever takes.

Deep Dive

Setting: Jerusalem has fallen. In the summer of 587 or 586 BC the Babylonians broke through the walls, blinded King Zedekiah, burned the temple and the palace, and began marching the survivors off to Babylon (ch 39). Chapter 39 ended with Nebuchadnezzar's order to treat Jeremiah well. Chapter 40 opens a new part of the book (chs 40–44) that tells what happened to the people left behind in the land, and to Jeremiah with them. It is mostly narrative, and Jeremiah himself is quiet in it. For a few months it looks as if a humbled remnant might settle down under a decent governor and rebuild. This chapter is the hopeful beginning of that story, and it ends with a warning that no one heeds.

Jeremiah Remains in Judah (vv1–16)

  • "The word that came to Jeremiah from the LORD" (v1). This is the book's usual heading for a prophecy, but no prophecy follows for a long time. The actual word from the LORD does not come until 42:7. Most scholars think the heading covers the whole block of chapters 40–42, and the narrative in between sets the scene for it.
  • "After Nebuzaradan the captain of the guard had let him go from Ramah, when he took him bound in chains" (v1). Ramah lay about five miles north of Jerusalem on the road north, and the Babylonians used it as a holding camp for the deportees. Chapter 39:14 says Jeremiah was handed over to Gedaliah from the court of the guard. The two accounts fit if Jeremiah was released, then swept up with the crowds in the confusion, chained and marched to Ramah, and only there recognized and freed. Ramah also carries a memory. Jeremiah had pictured Rachel "weeping for her children" at Ramah (31:15). Matthew later quotes that verse at Herod's slaughter of the children of Bethlehem (Matt 2:18). The prophet who wrote that line now stands in chains among the weeping.
  • "The LORD your God pronounced this disaster against this place" (v2). The speaker is a pagan Babylonian officer. He has heard what Jeremiah preached, perhaps from deserters or from Jeremiah's letters to the exiles (ch 29), and he sums it up exactly. "Because you sinned against the LORD and did not obey his voice, this thing has come upon you" (v3). The "you" is plural in Hebrew. He is not blaming Jeremiah. He is speaking to the Judeans around him, and through Jeremiah to the whole nation. The kings and priests of Judah never admitted what a foreign soldier now states plainly.
  • "Now, behold, I release you today from the chains on your hands" (v4). For the second time in the book a foreigner frees Jeremiah, first Ebed-melech from the cistern (38:7–13), now Nebuzaradan from the chains. Jeremiah had told Ebed-melech that God would keep him alive because he trusted (39:18). The same God now keeps his prophet through the hand of Babylon.
  • "If it seems good to you to come with me to Babylon, come, and I will look after you well" (v4). Literally "I will set my eye on you," the same idiom as Nebuchadnezzar's order in 39:12. In Babylon Jeremiah would have been safe and honored, perhaps with a place at court. "See, the whole land is before you; go wherever you think it good and right to go." This echoes the words Abraham spoke to Lot (Gen 13:9), "Is not the whole land before you?" No other captive was given this choice.
  • "If you remain, then return to Gedaliah" (v5). The ESV footnote says the meaning of the Hebrew phrase is uncertain. Read literally it says something like "and while he was not yet turning back," and the ESV follows the Syriac translation. Some take it to mean that Jeremiah hesitated and said nothing, so Nebuzaradan made the decision easier for him: "Go back to Gedaliah, then." Either way the sense is clear. If he stays, he should go to the new governor.
  • Gedaliah "the son of Ahikam, son of Shaphan" (v5). His family had stood by God's word for three generations. His grandfather Shaphan was King Josiah's secretary, who read the newly found Book of the Law to the king (2 Kgs 22:8–10). His father Ahikam protected Jeremiah when the crowd wanted him killed (26:24). Shaphan's family also gave Jeremiah's letter safe passage to Babylon (29:3) and owned the room where Baruch read his scroll (36:10). Nebuchadnezzar did not choose a son of David to govern. He chose a man from a respected family of officials who had argued for submission. A clay seal impression found at Lachish reads "belonging to Gedaliah, who is over the house," a title for a royal steward. Some scholars think it is this Gedaliah before the fall, though the name was common and the link is not certain.
  • "An allowance of food and a present" (v5). The first word is a ration, the word used for King Jehoiachin's daily allowance in Babylon at the end of the book (52:34). The second is a gift. Jeremiah had probably lost everything in the siege. God had promised him long before, "I will make the enemy plead with you in the time of trouble" (15:11). Now the enemy feeds him.
  • "Then Jeremiah went to Gedaliah the son of Ahikam, at Mizpah" (v6). Mizpah means "watchtower." Most scholars place it at Tell en-Nasbeh, about eight miles north of Jerusalem in Benjamin. With Jerusalem in ruins, it became the capital. It was an old gathering place: Samuel judged Israel and set up the "Ebenezer" stone near there (1 Sam 7:5–12), and Saul was chosen king there (1 Sam 10:17–24). "And lived with him among the people who were left in the land." Jeremiah chose the ruined land and the poor remnant over safety in Babylon. He had told the exiles to settle and seek the welfare of Babylon (29:4–7), so this was not a judgment that Babylon was evil. He simply stayed with the people God had left in his care. He had bought a field at Anathoth as a sign that "houses and fields and vineyards shall again be bought in this land" (32:15), and he stayed in the land he had staked his hope on.
  • "All the captains of the forces in the open country" (v7). When Zedekiah's army broke and scattered (39:4–5; 52:8), some units escaped into the hills. They now hear that a Judean governor has been appointed and that the Babylonians have left "men, women, and children, those of the poorest of the land." The poor had been given vineyards and fields (39:10). The people who had owned nothing were now the ones left holding the land.
  • The captains' names (v8). Ishmael the son of Nethaniah is of royal blood (41:1; 2 Kgs 25:25), which matters for what follows. Johanan the son of Kareah becomes the leading figure in chapters 41–43. The sons of Ephai the Netophathite came from Netophah near Bethlehem, a town that had given David some of his mighty men (2 Sam 23:28–29). Jezaniah the son of the Maacathite is called Jaazaniah in 2 Kings 25:23. Maacah was a small Aramean region near Mount Hermon, so his family may have been of foreign descent. An agate seal found at Tell en-Nasbeh reads "belonging to Jaazaniah, servant of the king." It may be his, though the name was not rare.
  • "Do not be afraid to serve the Chaldeans" (v9). Gedaliah swears an oath, binding himself before God that the soldiers will not be punished for fighting Babylon. His message is exactly Jeremiah's: "Serve the king of Babylon and live" (27:17; compare 27:12). It is the advice that would have saved Jerusalem if Zedekiah had taken it. Now it comes too late for the city, but not too late for the remnant.
  • "I will dwell at Mizpah, to represent you before the Chaldeans" (v10). Literally "to stand before the Chaldeans." Gedaliah will be the go-between with the Babylonian officials and garrison. The people's task is ordinary: "gather wine and summer fruits and oil, and store them in your vessels." The city fell in the summer and burned a month later (52:12), so it was now the season of the grape, fig and olive harvest. The fields and orchards had survived the war. "Dwell in your cities that you have taken" means the towns they had occupied. Some had moved into homes and fields that deported owners had left behind.
  • "All the Judeans who were in Moab and among the Ammonites and in Edom" (v11). Many had fled east across the Jordan and south during the invasion. When they hear of the governor and the remnant, they come home. "And they gathered wine and summer fruits in great abundance" (v12). After eighteen months of siege and famine (52:6), this line is startling. It sounds like the restoration Jeremiah had promised, when the people "shall come and sing aloud on the height of Zion… over the grain, the wine, and the oil" (31:12). For a single season the land seems to be healing.
  • "Do you know that Baalis the king of the Ammonites has sent Ishmael the son of Nethaniah to take your life?" (v14). Ammon lay across the Jordan to the east, around modern Amman. It had joined the plotting against Babylon (27:3), and a Judean governor loyal to Babylon blocked its plans. Ishmael had the motive of a royal heir passed over for a commoner. In 1984 archaeologists at Tell el-Umeiri near Amman found a seal impression of "Milkom'ur, servant of Baalyasha." Many scholars take Baalyasha to be the same king as Baalis. So the plot rests on real people. "But Gedaliah the son of Ahikam would not believe them."
  • "Please let me go and strike down Ishmael… and no one will know it" (v15). Johanan offers a secret assassination to prevent one. His reasoning is sound: if Gedaliah dies, "all the Judeans who are gathered about you would be scattered, and the remnant of Judah would perish." That is exactly what happens in the next three chapters.
  • "You shall not do this thing, for you are speaking falsely of Ishmael" (v16). Gedaliah is right to refuse a murder, but wrong to dismiss the warning. He might have guarded himself, kept Ishmael at a distance, or asked the prophet living in his own town. The text gives no hint that anyone asked Jeremiah. The chapter ends with good intentions, a good harvest, and a governor who will not see the danger at his table.
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry pities Jeremiah in his chains: "Poor man! he seems to have been born to hardship and abuse—man of sorrows indeed!" On the captain's speech he writes, "Sooner or later men shall be made sensible that their sin is the cause of all their miseries." All three stress that a heathen officer believed God's word better than Judah did. JFB says God makes a pagan officer testify against the Jews, and Guzik says the captain understood the prophet's message more clearly than the covenant people. On v5 JFB takes the uncertain phrase as "Go, then, to Gedaliah," while Guzik reads it as a sign that Jeremiah hesitated. On v6 Henry praises Jeremiah, who "chose rather to dwell with the poor in the holy land than with princes in an unholy one," though he wonders whether it was the prudent choice. Guzik sees the choice as an act of love for his people. On Gedaliah, JFB calls his refusal to suspect Ishmael "generous, but unwise unsuspiciousness." Guzik suggests a good man could not easily imagine evil in others, and passes on Kidner's point that Gedaliah could have asked the LORD through Jeremiah. JFB adds the sharp observation that Gedaliah's cheerful words about the harvest leave out that much of it would go to Babylon as tribute.

Pointing to Christ

The chapter is full of second chances: a prophet unchained, soldiers pardoned, refugees home, a harvest gathered from a ruined land. They are real mercies, but they rest on a human governor who cannot see the knife coming and cannot protect himself, let alone his people. Judah needed more than a decent man at Mizpah. It needed the Shepherd Jeremiah had promised, the righteous Branch of David who would "execute justice and righteousness in the land" (23:5–6). Jesus knew exactly who would betray him and still did not lose one of those the Father gave him (John 6:64; 17:12). Where Gedaliah's trust left his flock scattered, Christ laid down his life so that his sheep would never perish (John 10:11, 28).

Reflection

  • Choosing Hardship Over Comfort (vv.4-6) — Given the option of comfortable treatment in Babylon, Jeremiah instead chooses to stay with the poor remnant left in the land. Where might faithfulness be calling you toward the harder, less comfortable path because that's where the people who need you actually are?
  • A Moment of Ordinary Peace After Catastrophe (vv.10-12) — After the devastation of Jerusalem's fall, the scattered people briefly return to gather harvest, wine, and summer fruit. Can you recognize and be grateful for small seasons of normalcy God gives even after hard chapters of life?
  • Ignoring a Warning You Don't Want to Hear (v.16) — Gedaliah dismisses Johanan's credible warning about a plot against his life, refusing to believe it. Is there a warning in your life right now that you're inclined to wave away because believing it would be inconvenient?
