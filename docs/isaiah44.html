1But now listen, O Jacob My servant, Israel, whom I have chosen.

2This is the word of the LORD, your Maker, who formed you from the womb and who will help you: “Do not be afraid, O Jacob My servant, Jeshurun, whom I have chosen.

3For I will pour water on the thirsty land, and currents on the dry ground. I will pour out My Spirit on your descendants, and My blessing on your offspring.

4They will sprout among the grass like willows by flowing streams.

5One will say, ‘I belong to the LORD,’ another will call himself by the name of Jacob, and still another will write on his hand, ‘The LORD’s,’ and will take the name of Israel.”

6Thus says the LORD, the King and Redeemer of Israel, the LORD of Hosts: “I am the first and I am the last, and there is no God but Me.

7Who then is like Me? Let him say so! Let him declare his case before Me, since I established an ancient people. Let him foretell the things to come, and what is to take place.

8Do not tremble or fear. Have I not told you and declared it long ago? You are My witnesses! Is there any God but Me? There is no other Rock; I know not one.”

9All makers of idols are nothing, and the things they treasure are worthless. Their witnesses fail to see or comprehend, so they are put to shame.

10Who fashions a god or casts an idol which profits him nothing?

11Behold, all his companions will be put to shame, for the craftsmen themselves are only human. Let them all assemble and take their stand; they will all be brought to terror and shame.

12The blacksmith takes a tool and labors over the coals; he fashions an idol with hammers and forges it with his strong arms. Yet he grows hungry and loses his strength; he fails to drink water and grows faint.

13The woodworker extends a measuring line; he marks it out with a stylus; he shapes it with chisels and outlines it with a compass. He fashions it in the likeness of man, like man in all his glory, that it may dwell in a shrine.

14He cuts down cedars or retrieves a cypress or oak. He lets it grow strong among the trees of the forest. He plants a laurel, and the rain makes it grow.

15It serves as fuel for man. He takes some of it to warm himself, and he kindles a fire and bakes his bread. He also fashions it into a god and worships it; he makes an idol and bows down to it.

16He burns half of it in the fire, and he roasts meat on that half. He eats the roast and is satisfied. Indeed, he warms himself and says, “Ah! I am warm; I see the fire.”

17From the rest he makes a god, his graven image. He bows down to it and worships; he prays to it and says, “Save me, for you are my god.”

18They do not comprehend or discern, for He has shut their eyes so they cannot see and closed their minds so they cannot understand.

19And no one considers in his heart, no one has the knowledge or insight to say, “I burned half of it in the fire, and I baked bread on its coals; I roasted meat and I ate. Shall I make something detestable with the rest of it? Shall I bow down to a block of wood?”

20He feeds on ashes. His deluded heart has led him astray, and he cannot deliver himself or say, “Is not this thing in my right hand a lie?”

21Remember these things, O Jacob, for you are My servant, O Israel. I have made you, and you are My servant; O Israel, I will never forget you.

22I have blotted out your transgressions like a cloud, and your sins like a mist. Return to Me, for I have redeemed you.

23Sing for joy, O heavens, for the LORD has done this; shout aloud, O depths of the earth. Break forth in song, O mountains, you forests and all your trees. For the LORD has redeemed Jacob, and revealed His glory in Israel.

24Thus says the LORD, your Redeemer who formed you from the womb: “I am the LORD, who has made all things, who alone stretched out the heavens, who by Myself spread out the earth,

25who foils the signs of false prophets and makes fools of diviners, who confounds the wise and turns their knowledge into nonsense,

26who confirms the message of His servant and fulfills the counsel of His messengers, who says of Jerusalem, ‘She will be inhabited,’ and of the cities of Judah, ‘They will be rebuilt, and I will restore their ruins,’

27who says to the depths of the sea, ‘Be dry, and I will dry up your currents,’

28who says of Cyrus, ‘My shepherd will fulfill all that I desire,’ who says of Jerusalem, ‘She will be rebuilt,’ and of the temple, ‘Let its foundation be laid.’”

Summary

The LORD promises to pour out his Spirit and blessing on Israel's offspring like water on thirsty ground, so that they will flourish and proudly claim to belong to him (vv. 1-5). He again declares himself the first and the last, the only true God, asking who else could have declared things to come as he has (vv. 6-8). Isaiah then mocks idol-makers at length: a craftsman uses half a piece of wood to warm himself and cook his food, then carves the other half into a god to worship, oblivious to the absurdity of bowing before what he made with his own hands (vv. 9-20). Israel is called back to remember these things, since the LORD has blotted out her sins like a cloud and redeemed her (vv. 21-23). The chapter closes with the LORD, who alone stretched out the heavens and formed the earth, naming Cyrus by name as the one who will command Jerusalem to be rebuilt and the temple's foundations to be laid (vv. 24-28).

Authorship & Background

Author: Isaiah son of Amoz (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — Spirit Promise, Idol Satire, and Cyrus Commission
Key Themes: The Spirit poured on descendants, God as first and last (Alpha and Omega), the absurdity of idol-making, Israel redeemed and not forgotten, Cyrus named as God's shepherd
Historical Context: This chapter combines tender promise (vv.1-5), theological declaration (vv.6-8), devastating satire (vv.9-20), and stunning prophecy (vv.24-28). The idol-satire section is Isaiah's longest — a detailed exposé of the absurdity of carving a god from the same wood you use for cooking. The chapter climaxes with God naming Cyrus by name (v.28) — over 150 years before Cyrus conquered Babylon (539 BC). This is one of Scripture's most remarkable predictive prophecies.
I Will Pour My Spirit upon Thy Seed (vv.1-5): Yet now hear, O Jacob my servant, and Israel, whom I have chosen. The promise is agricultural and generational, I will pour water upon him that is thirsty, and floods upon the dry ground, I will pour my spirit upon thy seed, and my blessing upon thine offspring. Then the result is described as people signing their names, one shall say, I am the LORD's, and another shall subscribe with his hand unto the LORD, and surname himself by the name of Israel. Voluntary enrolment, written down, which is a quiet answer to the register of 4:3.
I Am the First and I Am the Last (vv.6-8): Thus saith the LORD the King of Israel, and his redeemer the LORD of hosts, I am the first, and I am the last, and beside me there is no God. Then the challenge of chapter 41 stated once more and tied to Israel's own experience, have not I told thee from that time, and have declared it, ye are even my witnesses. And the closing image is domestic and unexpected in the middle of a court case, is there a God beside me, yea, there is no God, I know not any.
He Warmeth Himself, and Saith, Aha (vv.9-20): The longest and most detailed idol satire in the Bible, and the whole force of it is in following one piece of wood through a single afternoon. The smith works with tongs and hammers and gets hungry and thirsty. The carpenter measures with a line, marks it with a rule, and fits it with compasses. He cuts down a cedar and takes the wood home, and then the accounting begins: he burneth part thereof in the fire, with part thereof he eateth flesh, he roasteth roast, and is satisfied, yea, he warmeth himself, and saith, Aha, I am warm, I have seen the fire. And the residue thereof he maketh a god, and falleth down unto it, and saith, Deliver me, for thou art my god. The same log, half of it firewood and half of it a deity, and the man does not notice. The verdict is a failure of attention, they know not, neither understand, for he hath shut their eyes.
Remember These, O Jacob (vv.21-23): Remember these, O Jacob and Israel, for thou art my servant, I have formed thee, thou art my servant. The title is repeated three times in two verses, immediately after twelve verses about people who manufacture gods, and the contrast is the argument: this servant was formed rather than made. Then the pardon in a single image, I have blotted out, as a thick cloud, thy transgressions. And the response is ordered from the creation rather than from the people, sing, O ye heavens, break forth into singing, ye mountains, O forest, and every tree therein.
Cyrus Named (vv.24-28): The chapter ends by naming him. That saith of Cyrus, He is my shepherd, and shall perform all my pleasure, even saying to Jerusalem, Thou shalt be built, and to the temple, Thy foundation shall be laid. Cyrus took Babylon in 539 BC and issued the decree Ezra 1 records, and the naming of a Persian king a century and a half in advance is the single most disputed feature of the book: it is the main argument for a later author for chapters 40 to 66, and the main argument for predictive prophecy for those who hold the book unified. The chapter itself treats the naming as the point of the whole court case, since prediction is the criterion it has proposed four times, and it stacks the credentials immediately before it, that frustrateth the tokens of the liars, and maketh diviners mad.

Setting: Isaiah 40–55 speaks to Judeans who would be exiles in Babylon in the 500s BC. Chapter 43 ended with Israel's sin laid bare and their leaders disgraced, yet with the promise "I, I am he who blots out your transgressions." Chapter 44 opens once more with "But now," and moves from judgment to the gift of God's Spirit. At its center is the Bible's sharpest satire of idol-making: a man who cooks lunch over half a log and bows down to the other half. It ends with a name. For the first time Isaiah calls the coming conqueror Cyrus, the Persian king who took Babylon in 539 BC and the next year let the Jews go home to rebuild (Ezra 1:1–4). Readers have long debated how a prophet who lived around 700 BC could name him. The traditional view is that God revealed the name ahead of time, and the passage itself makes prediction its whole argument. Many modern scholars think these chapters were written by a later prophet during the exile, as Cyrus was rising.

Israel the LORD's Chosen (vv1–5)

  • "But now hear, O Jacob my servant, Israel whom I have chosen!" (v1). The same "But now" that opened chapter 43 turns again from the judgment of 43:28 to grace. God does not take back his choice of Israel because they failed.
  • "Who formed you from the womb and will help you" (v2). God's care for Israel goes back to its birth, as a parent's care does. The same phrase is used of the Servant in 49:1, 5.
  • "Jeshurun whom I have chosen" (v2). Yeshurun is a rare, affectionate name for Israel, found only here and in Deuteronomy 32:15; 33:5, 26. It probably comes from yashar, "upright." It is a pet name: "my upright one." After 43:22–28, it is pure grace. God calls his people what he will make them, not what they have been. In Deuteronomy 32:15 the name has a sad edge: "Jeshurun grew fat, and kicked."
  • "I will pour water on the thirsty land… I will pour my Spirit upon your offspring" (v3). The promise of water in the desert (41:18; 43:19–20) now becomes a picture of the Spirit poured out like rain on dry ground. Earlier Isaiah promised "the Spirit is poured upon us from on high" (32:15). Joel prophesied the same (Joel 2:28–29), and Peter said at Pentecost that it was happening (Acts 2:16–18). Jesus spoke of "rivers of living water," meaning the Spirit (John 7:38–39).
  • "Like willows by flowing streams" (v4). Willows and poplars grew quickly along riverbanks in the Near East. The next generation, the "offspring" of v3, will spring up green and strong. This is the opposite of the fading grass of 40:6–8.
  • "This one will say, 'I am the LORD's'… another will write on his hand, 'The LORD's'" (v5). Four people, each owning their belonging to God. "Write on his hand" may mean a mark on the hand, as slaves or soldiers sometimes bore their master's name, or signing one's name to a covenant. The Hebrew can also be read "write with his hand," which the older versions follow. Either way it is a public, personal commitment. Some think these include Gentiles who join Israel by choice, taking "the name of Israel" (56:3–8; Zech 8:23). Paul says believers are "sealed with the promised Holy Spirit" (Eph 1:13), God's own mark of ownership.
  • Commentators: Guzik dwells on the image of pouring: the Spirit given not in drops but in abundance, to those who are thirsty. JFB calls Jeshurun "a diminutive term of endearment," and connects the name with yashar. Henry also explains it as "the upright one," noting that only Israelites "in whom is no guile" are the true heirs of the promise. On v5 JFB reads "subscribe with his hand" as signing a covenant before witnesses, as in Jeremiah 32:10, and Henry ties it to the exiles who renewed their covenant on their return.

Besides Me There Is No God (vv6–8)

  • "Thus says the LORD, the King of Israel and his Redeemer, the LORD of hosts" (v6). Three titles: King, Redeemer (go'el, the next of kin who buys back, 41:14), and LORD of hosts, commander of heaven's armies.
  • "I am the first and I am the last; besides me there is no god" (v6). This is the strongest form yet of 41:4 and 43:10. Before him there was nothing, after him there will be nothing, and beside him there is no rival. In Revelation the risen Jesus takes this title for himself: "I am the first and the last" (Rev 1:17; 22:13).
  • "Who is like me? Let him proclaim it" (v7). The challenge to the idols is repeated. "Since I appointed an ancient people" means since God first formed Israel. God has been announcing and doing his plan through their whole history. Let any other god show the same track record.
  • "You are my witnesses! Is there a God besides me? There is no Rock; I know not any" (v8). "Rock" is an old name for God in Israel's songs: "The Rock, his work is perfect" (Deut 32:4), and "who is a rock, except our God?" (Ps 18:31). It means strength, shelter and stability. God's "I know not any" is almost humorous. If anyone would know of another god, he would.
  • Commentators: Guzik notes that an idol can never be "first" because someone has to make it, and never "last" because it wears out. He and JFB both see the title "the first and the last" applied to Jesus in Revelation as a claim to full deity. Henry stresses that the LORD of hosts rules angels and men alike, and that this is the comfort of all who trust him.

The Folly of Idolatry (vv9–20)

  • The style changes. In the ESV this section is printed as prose, not poetry. Many scholars think it is a kind of prose satire set into the poetry. It is one of the longest attacks on idols in the Bible (compare 40:18–20; 46:1–7; Ps 115:4–8; Jer 10:1–16).
  • "All who fashion idols are nothing, and the things they delight in do not profit" (v9). "Nothing" is tohu, the word for the formless void of Genesis 1:2. Idol-makers are building emptiness. "Their witnesses," the idols, can neither see nor know, so they cannot testify. God's witnesses (v8) may be blind and deaf, but they have a God who speaks. The idols' witnesses have nothing at all.
  • "The craftsmen are only human" (v11). A made thing cannot be greater than its maker. If the craftsmen are only human, the gods they make are less than human.
  • The ironsmith (v12). He works at the forge with hammers and his strong arm, and then gets hungry and thirsty and faint. The man making a god cannot keep himself going without lunch. That is a far cry from the God who "does not faint or grow weary" (40:28).
  • The carpenter (v13). He measures with a line, marks with a pencil (perhaps a scribing tool or chalk), planes and uses a compass. "He shapes it into the figure of a man, with the beauty of a man, to dwell in a house." The irony is sharp. God made man in his image (Gen 1:27). Here man makes a god in his own image, and it has to be given a house to live in because it cannot go anywhere.
  • The tree (v14). He cuts down a cedar, cypress or oak, or plants one and waits for "the rain" to make it grow. The rain comes from the God he is ignoring. The idol depends on the true God's care before it is even carved.
  • "Half of it he burns in the fire… and the rest of it he makes into a god" (vv15–17). This is the heart of the satire. The same log keeps him warm, bakes his bread and roasts his meat. "Aha, I am warm, I have seen the fire!" Then he takes what is left over, bows down and prays, "Deliver me, for you are my god!" The scrap left after lunch becomes his savior.
  • "He has shut their eyes, so that they cannot see" (v18). Literally their eyes are "smeared over." This is the hardening first announced at Isaiah's call (6:9–10). Is it God who blinds them, or do they blind themselves? Both, in Scripture's view. They choose not to "consider" (v19), and God gives them over to their choice (Rom 1:21–25).
  • "Shall I make the rest of it an abomination? Shall I fall down before a block of wood?" (v19). This is the one simple question he never asks. Idolatry is not a failure of intelligence but a failure to stop and think.
  • "He feeds on ashes" (v20). The idol is only one fire away from being ashes too. Worshiping it is like trying to live on ash. The Hebrew can be read "he shepherds ashes" (ro'eh 'efer). It is the same verb as "my shepherd" (ro'i) in v28, said of Cyrus. The idolater tends a pile of ashes, while God's shepherd leads his people home.
  • "A deluded heart has led him astray… 'Is there not a lie in my right hand?'" (v20). The right hand is the hand of strength and trust. He is holding the lie in his hand and cannot see it. Paul says the same of people who "exchanged the truth about God for a lie" (Rom 1:25). An idol today need not be carved. It is anything a person makes, depends on and expects to deliver them (1 John 5:21).
  • Commentators: JFB points out the dark joke of v10: "A made god, worshipped by its maker!" He reads v12 as the smith making the axe that the carpenter then uses on the wooden idol. On v18 he explains "shut" as "daubed, plastered up," and takes the blinding as God's providential judgment, not a direct act against their will. Guzik makes the same balance, comparing Pharaoh, who hardened his own heart and was hardened by God. Henry says that in this, "man has become worse than the beasts," since beasts at least act by their senses. On "feeds on ashes," JFB suggests the idol "ought to have been reduced to ashes like the other half."

The LORD Redeems Israel (vv21–28)

  • "Remember these things, O Jacob" (v21). The idolater "considers" nothing (v19). Israel is called to remember: they are God's servant, he formed them, and "you will not be forgotten by me." Later Zion will complain, "the Lord has forgotten me," and God will answer, "I will not forget you" (49:14–15).
  • "I have blotted out your transgressions like a cloud and your sins like mist" (v22). This repeats 43:25 with a new picture. A morning mist over the hills burns off when the sun rises and is simply gone. "Blotted out" (machah) means wiped away.
  • "Return to me, for I have redeemed you" (v22). The order matters. God does not say "return to me, and then I will redeem you." He says "return, for I have redeemed you." Forgiveness comes first, and turning back is the response to it, not the price of it. This is the gospel pattern (1 John 4:19).
  • "Sing, O heavens, for the LORD has done it" (v23). Heavens, depths, mountains, forests and every tree are called to sing. The trees that idolaters cut down for gods (v14) now break into praise of the true God. Paul writes that creation itself waits to be "set free from its bondage to corruption" when God's children are revealed (Rom 8:19–21).
  • "I am the LORD, who made all things, who alone stretched out the heavens, who spread out the earth by myself" (v24). "By myself" may be literally "who was with me?" He had no helper and no rival god at creation. The verse sets up a long list of God's credentials, in a series of "who" clauses that build to the name Cyrus.
  • "Who frustrates the signs of liars and makes fools of diviners" (v25). Babylon was famous for its omen-readers, who studied sheep livers, the flight of birds and the stars (47:12–13). God makes their forecasts fail. The "wise men" of Babylon reappear in Daniel 2, unable to tell the king his dream.
  • "Who confirms the word of his servant and fulfills the counsel of his messengers" (v26). In contrast, God makes his own prophets' words come true. "His servant" here is probably the prophet himself, or the prophets as a group.
  • "Who says of Jerusalem, 'She shall be inhabited'" (v26). When these words were heard, Jerusalem was a ruin, or in Isaiah's own day was still standing but doomed. Either way God speaks its restoration before anyone could see it coming.
  • "Who says to the deep, 'Be dry; I will dry up your rivers'" (v27). This recalls the Red Sea and the Jordan, both parted by God (Exod 14:21; Josh 3:16). It may also point to Babylon's fall. The Greek historian Herodotus says Cyrus's army entered Babylon by lowering the Euphrates, which ran through the city, and wading in along the riverbed. Babylonian records say the city fell without a battle. Whatever the exact means, the river that protected Babylon did not save it.
  • "Who says of Cyrus, 'He is my shepherd, and he shall fulfill all my purpose'" (v28). Here is the climax. Cyrus the Great founded the Persian Empire, conquered Media and Lydia, and took Babylon in 539 BC. "Shepherd" was a common title for kings in the ancient Near East. Cyrus's own inscription, the Cyrus Cylinder, boasts that he returned displaced peoples and their gods to their homelands. God calls a pagan king "my shepherd," even though Cyrus did not know him (45:4).
  • "Saying of Jerusalem, 'She shall be built,' and of the temple, 'Your foundation shall be laid'" (v28). Cyrus's decree ordered the temple rebuilt and sent back its gold and silver vessels (Ezra 1:2–4; 6:3–5). The foundation was laid about 536 BC, and the old men who remembered Solomon's temple wept while the young shouted for joy (Ezra 3:10–13). The work then stalled for years and was finished in 516 BC.
  • Commentators: Guzik reports Josephus's story that Cyrus was shown this prophecy and was moved to fulfill it. That is Josephus's account, written centuries later, and cannot be checked. Guzik firmly holds that Isaiah named Cyrus in advance, and notes that some scholars treat the name as the strongest reason for dating the chapters later. Henry sees the prediction as given "long before" so that the exiles might expect correction but "no reason to fear a final destruction." JFB applies v27 to the diverting of the Euphrates. He also reads Cyrus the shepherd as a "type of Messiah," as does Henry. JFB takes "his servant" in v26 to mean the prophets collectively, and ultimately the Messiah. On v22, JFB pictures the debt wiped from God's account book (Exod 32:32–33; Rev 20:12).

Pointing to Christ

The chapter sets two shepherds side by side. One is the idolater who feeds on ashes, holding a lie he cannot see in his hand. The other is Cyrus, a foreign king God used to bring his people home and lay a foundation. But Cyrus only laid stones, and only for one city. Jesus is the good shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep (John 10:11). He pours out the Spirit like water on thirsty ground (John 7:37–39), and he is the true temple, raised in three days (John 2:19–21). He is "the first and the last" (Rev 1:17). He blotted out sin like morning mist and says "return," because the redemption is already done.

Reflection

  • The Spirit Promised to Your Offspring (v.3) — God promises to pour out His Spirit not only on His people but on their descendants after them. Are you praying and trusting God for the spiritual life of your children and grandchildren, not just your own?
  • God Alone, First and Last (v.6) — God declares Himself the first and the last, with no other god existing beside Him. Does your daily life reflect that exclusive claim, or do other things quietly function as gods alongside Him?
  • One Log, Fire and False God (vv.15-17) — A man burns half a piece of wood for warmth and carves the other half into an idol he then worships. What ordinary thing in your life have you elevated to a place of worship it was never meant to hold?
  • Sin Blotted Out Like a Cloud (v.22) — God compares blotting out sin to clearing away a thick cloud that had been covering the sky. Are you still looking at where the cloud used to be instead of living in the clear sky God has already given you?
  • Cyrus Named Before He Was Born (v.28) — God names a specific future king by name generations before his birth, appointing him to rebuild Jerusalem. Does knowing God can name the specifics of the distant future change how you trust Him with the details of yours?
