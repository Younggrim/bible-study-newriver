1After David had finished speaking with Saul, the souls of Jonathan and David were knit together, and Jonathan loved him as himself.

2And from that day Saul kept David with him and did not let him return to his father’s house.

3Then Jonathan made a covenant with David because he loved him as himself.

4And Jonathan removed the robe he was wearing and gave it to David, along with his tunic, his sword, his bow, and his belt.

5So David marched out and prospered in everything Saul sent him to do, and Saul set him over the men of war. And this was pleasing in the sight of all the people, and of Saul’s officers as well.

6As the troops were returning home after David had killed the Philistine, the women came out of all the cities of Israel to meet King Saul with singing and dancing, with joyful songs, and with tambourines and other instruments.

7And as the women danced, they sang out: “Saul has slain his thousands, and David his tens of thousands.”

8And Saul was furious and resented this song. “They have ascribed tens of thousands to David,” he said, “but only thousands to me. What more can he have but the kingdom?”

9And from that day forward Saul kept a jealous eye on David.

10The next day a spirit of distress sent from God came upon Saul, and he prophesied inside the house while David played the harp as usual. Now Saul was holding a spear,

11and he hurled it, thinking, “I will pin David to the wall.” But David eluded him twice.

12So Saul was afraid of David, because the LORD was with David but had departed from Saul.

13Therefore Saul sent David away and gave him command of a thousand men. David led the troops out to battle and back,

14and he continued to prosper in all his ways, because the LORD was with him.

15When Saul saw that David was very successful, he was afraid of him.

16But all Israel and Judah loved David, because he was leading them out to battle and back.

17Then Saul said to David, “Here is my older daughter Merab. I will give her to you in marriage. Only be valiant for me and fight the LORD’s battles.” But Saul was thinking, “I need not raise my hand against him; let the hand of the Philistines be against him.”

18And David said to Saul, “Who am I, and what is my family or my father’s clan in Israel, that I should become the son-in-law of the king?”

19So when it was time to give Saul’s daughter Merab to David, she was given in marriage to Adriel of Meholah.

20Now Saul’s daughter Michal loved David, and when this was reported to Saul, it pleased him.

21“I will give her to David,” Saul thought, “so that she may be a snare to him, and the hand of the Philistines may be against him.” So Saul said to David, “For a second time now you can be my son-in-law.”

22Then Saul ordered his servants, “Speak to David privately and tell him, ‘Behold, the king is pleased with you, and all his servants love you. Now therefore, become his son-in-law.’”

23But when Saul’s servants relayed these words to David, he replied, “Does it seem trivial in your sight to be the son-in-law of the king? I am a poor man and lightly esteemed.”

24And the servants told Saul what David had said.

25Saul replied, “Say to David, ‘The king desires no other dowry but a hundred Philistine foreskins as revenge on his enemies.’” But Saul intended to cause David’s death at the hands of the Philistines.

26When the servants reported these terms to David, he was pleased to become the king’s son-in-law. Before the wedding day arrived,

27David and his men went out and killed two hundred Philistines. He brought their foreskins and presented them as payment in full to become the king’s son-in-law. Then Saul gave his daughter Michal to David in marriage.

28When Saul realized that the LORD was with David and that his daughter Michal loved David,

29he grew even more afraid of David. So from then on Saul was David’s enemy.

30Every time the Philistine commanders came out for battle, David was more successful than all of Saul’s officers, so that his name was highly esteemed.

Summary

After David's victory, Jonathan's soul is knit to David's in deep friendship, and the two make a covenant, with Jonathan giving David his robe, armor, sword, and bow (vv. 1-4). David serves successfully under Saul, but when the women celebrate his return singing that Saul has slain his thousands and David his ten thousands, Saul grows jealous and suspicious that only the kingdom remains for David to gain (vv. 5-9). The next day, tormented by an evil spirit, Saul twice hurls a javelin at David while he plays the harp, and afterward, fearing David because the LORD is with him, removes him to command a thousand men, where David continues to succeed and grow in the people's love (vv. 10-16). Saul then offers his daughter Merab to David as a trap meant to expose him to the Philistines, but she is given to another man instead; when Michal, Saul's other daughter, is found to love David, Saul demands a dowry of a hundred Philistine foreskins hoping David will be killed in the attempt (vv. 17-25). David instead kills two hundred Philistines and marries Michal, but Saul, seeing that the LORD is with David, becomes his lifelong enemy even as David continues to prove more skilled than all of Saul's servants (vv. 26-30).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Samuel (for the early chapters), Nathan, and Gad (1 Chronicles 29:29). Originally one book with 2 Samuel in the Hebrew Bible. Written approximately 1050-950 BC. The book covers the transition from the judges to the monarchy — from the birth of Samuel through the death of Saul (approximately 1105-1010 BC).
Historical Context: Chapter 18 marks the beginning of Saul's active persecution of David. After the Goliath victory, David enters Saul's court permanently and two defining relationships form: his covenant friendship with Jonathan and his growing enmity with Saul. The chapter traces a devastating progression in Saul — from jealousy (v.8) to suspicion (v.9) to murderous rage (v.11) to fear (v.12) to scheming (v.17, 21, 25) to permanent enmity (v.29). Meanwhile, David's trajectory is the opposite: success, wisdom, love from the people, and the LORD's presence. The contrast is stark and intentional — everything David touches prospers because "the LORD was with him" (vv.12, 14, 28), while Saul spirals downward because the LORD "was departed from Saul" (v.12). Jonathan's covenant with David is remarkable: the crown prince voluntarily surrenders his royal insignia (robe, armor, sword, bow, belt) to the man who will take his throne. This is not political naivety but spiritual discernment — Jonathan recognizes God's hand on David and submits to it. The women's song — "Saul hath slain his thousands, and David his ten thousands" (v.7) — becomes the catalyst for Saul's jealousy. From this point forward, Saul's life is consumed with one obsession: destroying David. Every scheme fails because God protects His anointed, and each failure only deepens Saul's fear and hatred.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: A crown prince's soul knit to David's and royal robe and armor handed over as though the throne itself is being surrendered, a celebration song turned into a threat the moment Saul starts counting thousands against ten thousands, a spear hurled twice at a man playing the harp for the very king who throws it, a bride price of a hundred Philistine foreskins set as a trap that David survives by doubling, and success that keeps multiplying no matter how many ways Saul tries to redirect it toward the Philistines instead
Jonathan's Covenant with David (vv.1-4): After David's speech to Saul (end of ch.17), "the soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David" (v.1). This is one of Scripture's great friendships — a bond of souls, not merely of convenience. Jonathan "loved him as his own soul" (v.1, 3). They make a covenant (v.3), and Jonathan gives David his robe, armor, sword, bow, and belt (v.4). These are royal insignia — Jonathan is symbolically transferring his right to the throne. The crown prince recognizes God's choice and willingly yields. This is the opposite of Saul's response: where Saul clings to power, Jonathan surrenders it.
David's Success and the Women's Song (vv.5-9): David succeeds in every mission Saul gives him and is promoted over the men of war (v.5). He "behaved himself wisely" (KJV) / "was successful" (ESV) — Hebrew "maskil" = acted prudently, prospered. Upon returning from battle, the women of Israel sing: "Saul hath slain his thousands, and David his ten thousands" (v.7). This is a celebration song, not a political statement — but Saul takes it as a threat: "what can he have more but the kingdom?" (v.8). From this day, "Saul eyed David" (v.9) — watched him with suspicion and jealousy. The Hebrew "ayin" (to eye) suggests hostile surveillance.
Saul's First Murder Attempts (vv.10-16): The evil spirit returns, and Saul "prophesied in the midst of the house" (KJV) / "raved within his house" (ESV) — Hebrew "yithnabe" can mean prophesied or raved ecstatically. While David plays the harp, Saul hurls his spear twice (v.11). David evades both times. Saul's fear grows because "the LORD was with him, and was departed from Saul" (v.12). Saul removes David from his presence and makes him a field commander — hoping the Philistines will kill him. Instead, David succeeds even more (v.14), and "all Israel and Judah loved David" (v.16).
Saul's Schemes: Merab and Michal (vv.17-30): Saul offers his elder daughter Merab as wife — but the condition is fighting "the LORD's battles" (v.17). Saul's hidden motive: "Let not mine hand be upon him, but let the hand of the Philistines be upon him" (v.17). He wants David killed in battle. David responds with humility (v.18), but Saul breaks his promise and gives Merab to Adriel (v.19). When Michal (Saul's younger daughter) loves David, Saul sees another opportunity: "she may be a snare to him" (v.21). He sets the bride-price at 100 Philistine foreskins — expecting David to die in the attempt (v.25). David brings 200 (v.27), doubling the requirement. Saul must give Michal to David. The result: "Saul saw and knew that the LORD was with David" (v.28) — and "Saul was David's enemy continually" (v.29).

Setting: Right after David's victory over Goliath in the Valley of Elah, around 1020 BC. David has just stood before Saul with the giant's head in his hand and said, "I am the son of your servant Jesse the Bethlehemite" (17:58). The reader knows what no one at court knows: Samuel has already anointed this young man, the Spirit of the LORD is on him, and the Spirit has left Saul (16:13–14). Chapter 18 traces how everyone around Saul comes to love David (Jonathan, the army, all Israel and Judah, Michal, even Saul's servants) while Saul himself moves from admiration to suspicion to fear to open hatred. The key refrain is "the LORD was with him" (vv12, 14, 28). Its shadow is Saul's growing fear (vv12, 15, 29).

David and Jonathan's Friendship (vv1–5)

  • "The soul of Jonathan was knit to the soul of David" (v1). "Knit" (qashar) means bound or tied together. Genesis uses the same expression of Jacob, whose life was "bound up" in the life of his son Benjamin (Gen 44:30). Later in 1 Samuel the same verb is used for "conspiring" (22:8, 13), when Saul accuses Jonathan of being bound to David against him. Jonathan was himself a man of great faith and courage, the hero of Michmash (ch 14). He recognized in David the same trust in the LORD that had sent him up the cliff, and their hearts were joined.
  • "Jonathan loved him as his own soul" (v1). In Hebrew, "soul" (nephesh) means one's life or self. Jonathan loved David as he loved himself, the language of Leviticus 19:18. In the ancient Near East, "love" was also the language of covenant loyalty between kings and allies (1 Kgs 5:1, where Hiram "always loved David"). Jonathan's love is both deep personal affection and committed loyalty. Some modern interpreters read a romantic or sexual relationship into the text. The text itself describes a covenant friendship, and that is how Jews and Christians have read it through the centuries. David's lament, "your love to me was extraordinary, surpassing the love of women" (2 Sam 1:26), speaks of a self-giving loyalty that cost Jonathan a throne.
  • "Saul took him that day and would not let him return" (v2). David's part-time service (17:15) ends. He becomes a permanent member of the court.
  • "Jonathan made a covenant with David" (v3). A covenant (berit) was a binding commitment sworn before God. This one will be renewed twice (20:8, 16–17, 42; 23:18), and David will keep it long after Jonathan's death by caring for his son Mephibosheth (2 Sam 9:1–7).
  • "Jonathan stripped himself of the robe… and his armor, and even his sword and his bow and his belt" (v4). This is much more than a gift of clothes. The robe (me'il) was a garment of rank. Saul's robe was the one Samuel's had torn in the sign of losing the kingdom (15:27–28), and later David will cut the edge of Saul's robe (24:4). The sword was rare in Israel; only Saul and Jonathan had one (13:22). Jonathan, the crown prince, is handing over the symbols of his status to David. Later he says it out loud: "You shall be king over Israel, and I shall be next to you" (23:17). Saul had tried to dress David in his armor (17:38–39), and it did not fit. Jonathan's gift is freely given and freely received.
  • "David went out and was successful wherever Saul sent him" (v5). "Successful" translates sakal, which means to act wisely and so to prosper. It recurs in vv14, 15 and 30. It sounds very like a different Hebrew verb Samuel used to rebuke Saul: "You have done foolishly" (13:13). Saul acted foolishly. David acts wisely. "This was good in the sight of all the people and also in the sight of Saul's servants." At this point, everyone approves.
  • Commentators: Henry on the friendship: "Where God unites hearts, carnal matters are too weak to separate them." He applies it to believers: "Those who love Christ as their own souls, will be willing to join themselves to him in an everlasting covenant." JFB says the two were "nearly of an age" (which is uncertain, since Jonathan was already commanding troops early in Saul's reign) and calls receiving a prince's garment "the highest honor which can be conferred on a subject." Guzik sees Jonathan setting aside his own claim to the throne because he recognized God's choice, and notes that David received the gifts without grasping for power. Guzik and the Tyndale notes both reject reading a sexual relationship into the text.

Saul's Jealousy of David (vv6–16)

  • "The women came out of all the cities of Israel, singing and dancing" (v6). Women traditionally greeted returning victors with music and dancing. Miriam led the women after the Red Sea (Exod 15:20–21), and Jephthah's daughter came out to meet her father with tambourines (Judg 11:34).
  • "Saul has struck down his thousands, and David his ten thousands" (v7). In Hebrew poetry, the second line often heightens the first: "thousands… ten thousands" is a standard pair meaning "many… very many" (Deut 32:30; Ps 91:7; Mic 6:7). The women probably meant to praise both men. But Saul hears a ranking, and in the most literal sense the song gives David ten times the credit. The song became famous. Even the Philistines knew it (21:11; 29:5).
  • "What more can he have but the kingdom?" (v8). Saul sees the threat. He knows from Samuel that the kingdom has been given to "a neighbor of yours, who is better than you" (15:28). Now he suspects who it is.
  • "Saul eyed David from that day on" (v9). The verb ('awan) comes from the word for "eye." Saul kept a jealous, suspicious eye on him. The KJV kept it: "Saul eyed David." Jealousy has begun its work.
  • "A harmful spirit from God rushed upon Saul, and he raved within his house" (v10). "Raved" is the Hebrew verb usually translated "prophesied" (nava', in its reflexive form). It describes a person in an ecstatic or frenzied state, whether from God's Spirit (10:10) or, as here, from a harmful spirit. The same outward behavior can come from very different sources. "Rushed upon" is the verb used when God's Spirit came on Saul to save Jabesh (11:6). The contrast is chilling.
  • "Saul had his spear in his hand… 'I will pin David to the wall'" (vv10–11). Saul's spear appears again and again in his story, almost as a symbol of his kingship and his violence (19:10; 20:33; 22:6; 26:7). David plays music to ease the king's torment, and the king tries to kill him. "David evaded him twice" may mean twice on this occasion or once now and once later (19:10).
  • "Saul was afraid of David because the LORD was with him but had departed from Saul" (v12). The writer explains the real reason for Saul's fear. It is not only David's popularity. It is God's presence. The LORD's presence that once rested on Saul now rests on another man, and Saul knows it.
  • "Made him a commander of a thousand" (v13). Saul removes David from the palace, possibly hoping the battlefield will do what his spear could not (as he plans openly in v17). It backfires. "He went out and came in before the people" is the Hebrew idiom for leading in war (Num 27:17; Josh 14:11). It was what the people had wanted in a king (8:20). Now David is doing it, in full view of everyone.
  • "He stood in fearful awe of him" (v15). Saul's fear grows. Earlier (v12) he "was afraid." Now he dreads David. The word (gur) is a stronger term for fear and dread.
  • "All Israel and Judah loved David" (v16). The separate mention of "Judah" looks ahead to the day when Judah crowns David first (2 Sam 2:4) and then all the tribes come to him (2 Sam 5:1–3).
  • Commentators: Henry observes that "David's troubles not only immediately follow his triumphs, but arise from them." JFB says the women "committed a great indiscretion by praising a subject at the expense of their sovereign," and explains "Saul eyed David" as looking at him "with secret and malignant hatred." On "raved," JFB says the term "denotes one under the influence either of a good or a bad spirit," and here describes "a frenzy." Guzik makes the same point and calls "prophesied" (as older versions have it) a misleading translation here. He notes that the hands that killed Goliath were now serving a tormented king, and that David dodged the spear without throwing it back. JFB calls David's new command "an honorable exile," which only made him more popular.

David Marries Michal (vv17–30)

  • "Here is my elder daughter Merab… Only be valiant for me and fight the LORD's battles" (v17). Saul had promised his daughter to whoever killed Goliath (17:25). Now he finally offers her, but with conditions. The pious phrase "fight the LORD's battles" covers a murderous plan: "let the hand of the Philistines be against him." Saul cannot bring himself to kill David openly. He wants the enemy to do it.
  • "Who am I, and who are my relatives… that I should be son-in-law to the king?" (v18). David's humility is real. He says the same kind of thing later when God promises him a dynasty (2 Sam 7:18). His family was not wealthy or powerful. It may also be a polite way of saying he could not pay a royal bride-price (v23).
  • "She was given to Adriel the Meholathite" (v19). Saul breaks his promise. Adriel was from Abel-meholah in the Jordan valley, Elisha's hometown (1 Kgs 19:16). This marriage has a dark sequel. Merab's five sons were later handed over to the Gibeonites and executed in payment for Saul's crimes (2 Sam 21:8–9, where most Hebrew manuscripts name Michal, but most scholars, and the ESV, read Merab).
  • "Saul's daughter Michal loved David" (v20). This is the only place in the Old Testament stories where a woman is said to love a man. Saul sees an opportunity: "that she may be a snare for him" (v21). A snare is a bird trap. Saul plans to use his own daughter's love as bait.
  • "The king has delight in you, and all his servants love you" (v22). Saul has his servants carry the flattery in private. The words are true of the servants and false of the king.
  • "I am a poor man and have no reputation" (v23). A bride-price (mohar) was paid by the groom to the bride's family (Gen 34:12; Exod 22:16–17). For a king's daughter, it would be enormous. David has nothing to offer.
  • "A hundred foreskins of the Philistines" (v25). Saul names a price David can pay only in blood. It sounds generous, as if Saul wants nothing but revenge on his enemies, and it has a grim logic. Only proof of uncircumcised dead could show they were Philistines, since most of Israel's other neighbors practiced circumcision. But the narrator states the real aim: "Saul thought to make David fall by the hand of the Philistines."
  • "David arose and went, along with his men, and killed two hundred" (v27). David does not just meet the price. He doubles it, and before the deadline. (The Greek translation reads one hundred, matching David's later words in 2 Sam 3:14.) Saul's trap only proves again that the LORD is with David. "Saul gave him his daughter Michal for a wife." Saul's own scheme puts David into the royal family.
  • "Saul saw and knew that the LORD was with David, and that Michal, Saul's daughter, loved him" (v28). Two facts drive Saul to despair. God is with David, and Saul's own children love him. In the next chapter both Jonathan and Michal will protect David from their father.
  • "So Saul was David's enemy continually" (v29). The phrase is final. From here on Saul's hostility is settled, and it will last for the rest of his life.
  • "David had more success than all the servants of Saul, so that his name was highly esteemed" (v30). The chapter closes with sakal again, "acted wisely and succeeded," and with David's "name" becoming precious. The LORD will later promise, "I will make for you a great name" (2 Sam 7:9).
  • A shorter Greek text. The main Greek manuscript (Codex Vaticanus) lacks several parts of this chapter, including vv1–5, 10–11, 17–19 and 29b–30. As with chapter 17, some scholars think this preserves an earlier, shorter version of the story, while others think the Greek translator shortened it to smooth over tensions. The Hebrew text, which English Bibles follow, reads as one coherent account.
  • Commentators: JFB says Saul "had found it convenient to forget his former promise" and calls him "the fickle and perfidious monarch." On the bride-price, JFB says Saul's demand "had an air of liberality" but "covered unprincipled malice against David," and suggests David brought two hundred to show respect and hold the king to his word. Guzik likens David's doubling of the price to going the second mile, turning Saul's manipulation into a free act. He also links David's humility to his protection. Henry draws an application for believers: "Opposition and perverseness in others, will not excuse wrong tempers in us," and, since David counted it an honor to become the king's son-in-law, "how should we magnify the honour of being sons to the King of kings!" The Tyndale notes add that the word for the king's "delight" in v22 is the same word used for Jonathan's genuine delight in David (19:1), which makes Saul's use of it all the more false.

Pointing to Christ

David is the anointed king who is not yet crowned. He is loved by the people, faithful in every task, humble about his own worth, and hated without cause by the ruler who should have welcomed him. He receives the robe and sword of the king's son freely and pays the bride-price in his own blood and danger. Jesus walked the same road with far greater weight. He was loved by crowds and hated by rulers who feared losing their place (John 11:47–48). "They hated me without a cause" (John 15:25). Jonathan stripped off his royal robe for David. The Son of God "emptied himself" of the glory he had with the Father (Phil 2:7). And Jesus paid the bride-price for his bride, the church, not with the lives of his enemies but with his own (Eph 5:25). As Henry put it, if David counted it a great honor to become a king's son-in-law, we should treasure the honor of being made children of the King of kings.

Reflection

  • Friendship That Surrenders Its Own Claim (vv.1-4) — Jonathan's heart is bound to David's, and he hands over his royal robe and weapons — his own claim to the throne — without being asked. Is your love for others generous enough to give away something valuable, or do you keep score of what you get in return?
  • When Someone Else's Success Feels Threatening (v.9) — After hearing the women's song crediting David with even greater victories than his own, Saul begins watching him with suspicion instead of celebrating him. Whose success tempts you to feel threatened rather than glad?
  • Playing Music for a Man Who Wants You Dead (v.11) — David keeps serving Saul with his harp even after Saul hurls a spear at him, not once but twice. How do you respond when the people you're trying to help or serve turn on you?
  • A Trap Disguised as an Opportunity (vv.20-25) — Saul offers his daughter Michal in marriage but sets a bride price he expects will get David killed in battle. Have you ever seen a generous-looking offer turn out to have hidden motives, and how did you discern the difference?
  • Seeing the Truth and Still Refusing to Change (v.29) — Saul recognizes plainly that the Lord is with David, yet that recognition only deepens his hostility instead of leading him to repentance. When you sense God at work in a situation you don't like, do you yield to it or dig in against it?
