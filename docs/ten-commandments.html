The Foundation

What Are the Ten Commandments?

The Ten Commandments were given by God to Israel at Mount Sinai, shortly after He rescued them from slavery in Egypt (Exodus 20:1-17). This order matters: God did not hand Israel a list of rules to earn His favor and then rescue them once they proved themselves. He rescued them first — by grace, before they had done anything to deserve it — and then gave them the commandments as the pattern of life fitting for a people He had already redeemed. The commandments were never a ladder to climb toward God's love; they were a description of what a life shaped by that love looks like.

The Ten Commandments divide naturally into two groups, sometimes called the "two tables" of the law. The first four commandments govern our relationship with God: having no other gods, making no idols, honoring God's name, and keeping the Sabbath. The remaining six govern our relationships with other people: honoring parents, and prohibitions against murder, adultery, theft, false witness, and covetousness. Jesus summarized this same two-part structure when He was asked which commandment was greatest: love the Lord your God with all your heart, and love your neighbor as yourself — "on these two commandments depend all the Law and the Prophets" (Matthew 22:37-40).

Jesus did not abolish the Ten Commandments — He deepened them. Throughout the Sermon on the Mount, He repeatedly took a commandment that addressed external behavior and revealed the internal condition it was really targeting: not just murder, but the anger that leads to it; not just adultery, but the lust that precedes it. This was not a stricter, harder version of the Law — it was the Law's true meaning, always aimed at the heart and not merely at outward compliance. This is why the tenth commandment, "you shall not covet," functions almost as a summary of the other nine: it is the only one of the ten that names a desire rather than an action, exposing that every external sin begins with an internal one.

For Christians, the Ten Commandments remain a vital picture of God's character and a trustworthy guide for a flourishing life — not because keeping them earns salvation, but because they reveal what love for God and neighbor actually looks like in practice. Even believers who understand they are saved by grace through faith, not by law-keeping, still find in the Ten Commandments an honest mirror: a way of measuring where our hearts have drifted, and a description of the very transformation the Holy Spirit is producing in everyone who belongs to Christ.

Exodus 20:3

No Other Gods

The Commandment

The first commandment, "You shall have no other gods before Me" (Exodus 20:3), stands as the foundation upon which all other commandments rest. Spoken by God to Israel at Mount Sinai after their deliverance from Egypt, this command establishes the exclusive nature of the relationship between Yahweh and His people. In the ancient Near East, where polytheism was the norm, God's declaration was radical — there is one God, and He alone deserves the totality of human devotion.

This commandment is not merely a prohibition against worshiping carved statues; it strikes at the heart of human loyalty. Anything that occupies the supreme place in our affections — career, relationships, wealth, pleasure, or even religious performance — can become a "god before Me." Jesus affirmed this commandment as the greatest of all when He declared, "You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your mind" (Matthew 22:37).

Heart of the Law

The heart of the first commandment is not restriction but relationship. God is not a jealous tyrant demanding obedience; He is a loving Father who knows that divided loyalties will always lead to disappointment and destruction. Every idol promises what only God can deliver — security, identity, significance, and satisfaction. Living under the first commandment means conducting a regular inventory of our hearts — asking honest questions about what we fear losing most, what we think about when our minds are idle, what we would be devastated to live without.

Exodus 20:4-6

No Idols

The Commandment

The second commandment forbids the making of graven images or any likeness of God for the purpose of worship (Exodus 20:4-6). While the first commandment addresses who we worship, the second addresses how we worship. God cannot be reduced to any created form — not to wood, stone, gold, or even to our mental constructions of who we think He should be. When Israel fashioned the golden calf at Sinai, they were not necessarily worshiping a different god — they were trying to represent Yahweh in a form they could see and control. This is the subtle danger the commandment addresses.

The New Testament extends this prohibition beyond physical statues to the idols of the heart. Paul identifies covetousness as idolatry (Colossians 3:5), recognizing that anything we elevate to ultimate value functions as an idol.

Heart of the Law

At its heart, the prohibition against idols protects the integrity of our knowledge of God. Every idol — whether physical or conceptual — diminishes God, compressing the infinite Creator into something manageable, predictable, and controllable. But a god we can control is no God at all. For modern believers, idolatry often takes subtler forms than carved statues — we create idols when we reduce God to only His love without His justice, or insist He must act according to our expectations. The second commandment invites us into the humility of mystery — worshiping a God who is always greater than our grandest conceptions of Him.

Exodus 20:7

Do Not Take God's Name in Vain

The Commandment

The third commandment, "You shall not take the name of the LORD your God in vain" (Exodus 20:7), guards the holiness of God's revealed name. In ancient Israel, a name was not merely a label but a revelation of character and essence. To "take up" this name in vain meant to carry it emptily, to invoke it without weight or reverence, to use it as a tool for personal advantage — swearing falsely, making vows without intending to keep them, or claiming to speak for God when He has not spoken.

Jesus deepened this commandment in the Sermon on the Mount, teaching His disciples to simply let their "yes" be yes and their "no" be no, rather than invoking elaborate oaths (Matthew 5:33-37).

Heart of the Law

The heart of this commandment is reverence — a deep, settled awareness that God's name represents His very person, and that to invoke it is to stand in His presence. For Christians who bear the name of Christ, this commandment carries special weight — every interaction, every business dealing, every relationship either hallows or profanes the name we carry. Living in alignment with this commandment means asking: does my life make God's name look beautiful, trustworthy, and good?

Exodus 20:8-11

Remember the Sabbath

The Commandment

The fourth commandment calls God's people to "Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy" (Exodus 20:8-11). Unlike the other commandments that primarily prohibit harmful actions, this one commands a positive practice: stopping. For a people who had been slaves in Egypt — forced to produce without ceasing — the command to rest was revolutionary. It declared that their value was not determined by their productivity.

Jesus declared Himself "Lord of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28) and taught that the Sabbath was made for humanity, not humanity for the Sabbath. The New Testament presents Jesus Himself as our Sabbath rest — the One in whom we cease striving to earn God's favor and enter into the finished work of grace.

Heart of the Law

The Sabbath principle speaks to one of our deepest spiritual struggles: the belief that our worth depends on our output. In a culture of constant productivity, the fourth commandment is countercultural in the extreme — it says: stop, trust, remember that God is God and you are not. For the Christian, Sabbath rest points ultimately to the gospel itself: we enter God's rest by faith in Christ's finished work, and practicing regular rhythms of rest becomes a weekly rehearsal of the gospel — I am loved not for what I produce, but for who I am in Christ.

Exodus 20:12

Honor Your Father and Mother

The Commandment

The fifth commandment — "Honor your father and your mother, that your days may be long in the land" (Exodus 20:12) — serves as the bridge between our duties toward God (commandments 1-4) and our duties toward others (commandments 6-10). It is the first commandment with a promise attached, indicating its foundational importance. The Hebrew word for "honor" (kabed) means to give weight to, to treat as significant and valuable — this was countercultural in the ancient world where the elderly were often discarded once they could no longer contribute economically.

Paul reaffirms this commandment for the Christian community in Ephesians 6:1-3. Jesus also upheld its ongoing validity while condemning religious traditions that allowed people to avoid caring for parents under the guise of dedicating resources to God (Matthew 15:4-6).

Heart of the Law

At its deepest level, this commandment teaches us about the nature of authority, gratitude, and generational faithfulness. Honoring parents is training ground for honoring God. This commandment also carries special weight for those with difficult or broken parental relationships — honoring does not mean excusing abuse or pretending dysfunction did not occur, but refusing to carry bitterness and recognizing the humanity and image-bearing dignity of our parents even when they failed us.

Exodus 20:13

You Shall Not Murder

The Commandment

The sixth commandment, "You shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13), establishes the sanctity of human life as a core principle of God's moral order. The Hebrew word used here (ratsach) refers specifically to unlawful killing — the intentional, unjustified taking of human life. Every human being is created in the image of God (Genesis 9:6), and to destroy that image-bearer is to assault the Creator Himself.

Jesus radically expanded this commandment in the Sermon on the Mount, teaching that murder begins in the heart long before it reaches the hands: "Everyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment" (Matthew 5:22). John states it plainly: "Everyone who hates his brother is a murderer" (1 John 3:15).

Heart of the Law

The heart of this commandment is not merely the absence of violence but the presence of active love for human life — seeing every person, regardless of their utility, status, or likability, as bearing the sacred image of God. For followers of Jesus, the sixth commandment becomes a call to radical peacemaking: refusing to repay evil with evil, choosing forgiveness over vengeance, and examining our hearts for the seeds of murder — contempt, rage, bitterness — before they bear their deadly fruit.

Exodus 20:14

You Shall Not Commit Adultery

The Commandment

The seventh commandment, "You shall not commit adultery" (Exodus 20:14), protects the sacred covenant of marriage and the trust that forms its foundation. In God's design, marriage is a one-flesh union — a reflection of His own faithful, covenant love for His people. Throughout the Old Testament, marriage fidelity serves as a metaphor for Israel's relationship with God — Hosea's marriage to unfaithful Gomer becomes a living parable of God's heartbreak over His people's idolatry.

Jesus deepened this commandment beyond the physical act to the condition of the heart: "Everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (Matthew 5:27-28).

Heart of the Law

The heart of this commandment is faithfulness — the choice to honor one's covenant commitments even when desire, opportunity, or dissatisfaction tempt us otherwise. It calls us to view marriage not as a consumer relationship, evaluated by what it gives us, but as a covenant relationship, defined by what we have promised. In a culture saturated with sexual imagery, this commandment calls believers to a radically different vision of sexuality — one rooted in commitment, trust, and self-giving love rather than self-gratification.

Exodus 20:15

You Shall Not Steal

The Commandment

The eighth commandment, "You shall not steal" (Exodus 20:15), establishes the principle of private property and the dignity of honest labor. In Israel's law, theft encompassed far more than pickpocketing — it included fraudulent business practices, withholding wages from workers, and moving boundary stones to steal land. The prophets thundered against those who "join house to house" and "add field to field" through unjust means (Isaiah 5:8).

Paul transforms this commandment from mere prohibition into positive calling: "Let the thief no longer steal, but rather let him labor, doing honest work with his own hands, so that he may have something to share with anyone in need" (Ephesians 4:28).

Heart of the Law

The heart of the eighth commandment is contentment and trust in God's provision. People steal because they believe God's provision is insufficient or that they deserve what belongs to others. For modern believers, theft takes many subtle forms: padding expense reports, taking credit for others' work, wasting an employer's time, or exploiting others' generosity without reciprocating. The transformed life moves from taking to giving, from grasping to generosity, from scarcity-thinking to gospel abundance.

Exodus 20:16

You Shall Not Bear False Witness

The Commandment

The ninth commandment, "You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor" (Exodus 20:16), protects truth as the foundation of justice and community. Originally situated in the context of legal testimony, this commandment extends to all forms of dishonesty: lying, slander, gossip, half-truths, misleading silence, and the manipulation of facts to harm others. In Israelite law, a false witness who was discovered bore the punishment that would have fallen on the accused (Deuteronomy 19:18-19).

The New Testament consistently calls believers to radical truthfulness. Paul commands, "Having put away falsehood, let each one of you speak the truth with his neighbor" (Ephesians 4:25). James calls the tongue "a restless evil, full of deadly poison" (James 3:8).

Heart of the Law

The heart of this commandment is that God is a God of truth, and His people are called to reflect His character — Satan is identified as "the father of lies" (John 8:44), and every act of deception aligns us with his character rather than God's. Living truthfully requires courage; we lie because we fear consequences, crave approval, or want to control how others perceive us. The ninth commandment calls us to trust God with the results of truth-telling, even when truth is costly.

Exodus 20:17

You Shall Not Covet

The Commandment

The tenth commandment, "You shall not covet your neighbor's house... wife... or anything that belongs to your neighbor" (Exodus 20:17), is unique among the Ten Commandments because it addresses not an external action but an internal disposition. While the previous commandments prohibit behaviors, this one goes to the root from which all those sins grow: the disordered desire of the heart. David's adultery began with coveting; Achan's theft at Jericho began with coveting.

Paul testified that it was specifically this commandment that revealed to him the depth of his sinfulness: "I would not have known what it is to covet if the law had not said, 'You shall not covet'" (Romans 7:7). It is the commandment that drives us most directly to the gospel, because no amount of external obedience can cure a covetous heart.

Heart of the Law

The heart of the tenth commandment is contentment — the deep, settled peace that comes from trusting that God's provision for your life is good, sufficient, and perfectly tailored to His purposes for you. Coveting is ultimately a failure of worship: when we covet, we are saying that God has not been good enough, wise enough, or generous enough in what He has given us. The antidote is not merely suppressing desire but redirecting it — learning to desire God Himself above all His gifts.
