1“Next, have your brother Aaron brought to you from among the Israelites, along with his sons Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar, to serve Me as priests.

2Make holy garments for your brother Aaron, to give him glory and splendor.

3You are to instruct all the skilled craftsmen, whom I have filled with a spirit of wisdom, to make garments for Aaron’s consecration, so that he may serve Me as priest.

4These are the garments that they shall make: a breastpiece, an ephod, a robe, a woven tunic, a turban, and a sash. They are to make these holy garments for your brother Aaron and his sons, so that they may serve Me as priests.

5They shall use gold, along with blue, purple, and scarlet yarn, and fine linen.

6They are to make the ephod of finely spun linen embroidered with gold, and with blue, purple, and scarlet yarn.

7It shall have two shoulder pieces attached at two of its corners, so it can be fastened.

8And the skillfully woven waistband of the ephod must be of one piece, of the same workmanship—with gold, with blue, purple, and scarlet yarn, and with finely spun linen.

9Take two onyx stones and engrave on them the names of the sons of Israel:

10six of their names on one stone and the remaining six on the other, in the order of their birth.

11Engrave the names of the sons of Israel on the two stones the way a gem cutter engraves a seal. Then mount the stones in gold filigree settings.

12Fasten both stones on the shoulder pieces of the ephod as memorial stones for the sons of Israel. Aaron is to bear their names on his two shoulders as a memorial before the LORD.

13Fashion gold filigree settings

14and two chains of pure gold, made of braided cord work; and attach these chains to the settings.

15You are also to make a breastpiece of judgment with the same workmanship as the ephod. Construct it with gold, with blue, purple, and scarlet yarn, and with finely spun linen.

16It must be square when folded over double, a span long and a span wide.

17And mount on it a setting of gemstones, four rows of stones: In the first row there shall be a ruby, a topaz, and an emerald;

18in the second row a turquoise, a sapphire, and a diamond;

19in the third row a jacinth, an agate, and an amethyst;

20and in the fourth row a beryl, an onyx, and a jasper. Mount these stones in gold filigree settings.

21The twelve stones are to correspond to the names of the sons of Israel, each engraved like a seal with the name of one of the twelve tribes.

22For the breastpiece, make braided chains like cords of pure gold.

23You are also to make two gold rings and fasten them to the two corners of the breastpiece.

24Then fasten the two gold chains to the two gold rings at the corners of the breastpiece,

25and fasten the other ends of the two chains to the two filigree settings, attaching them to the shoulder pieces of the ephod at the front.

26Make two more gold rings and attach them to the other two corners of the breastpiece, on the inside edge next to the ephod.

27Make two additional gold rings and attach them to the bottom of the two shoulder pieces of the ephod, on its front, near its seam just above its woven waistband.

28The rings of the breastpiece shall be tied to the rings of the ephod with a cord of blue yarn, so that the breastpiece is above the waistband of the ephod and does not swing out from the ephod.

29Whenever Aaron enters the Holy Place, he shall bear the names of the sons of Israel over his heart on the breastpiece of judgment, as a continual reminder before the LORD.

30And place the Urim and Thummim in the breastpiece of judgment, so that they will also be over Aaron’s heart whenever he comes before the LORD. Aaron will continually carry the judgment of the sons of Israel over his heart before the LORD.

31You are to make the robe of the ephod entirely of blue cloth,

32with an opening at its top in the center. Around the opening shall be a woven collar with an opening like that of a garment, so that it will not tear.

33Make pomegranates of blue, purple, and scarlet yarn all the way around the lower hem, with gold bells between them,

34alternating the gold bells and pomegranates around the lower hem of the robe.

35Aaron must wear the robe whenever he ministers, and its sound will be heard when he enters or exits the sanctuary before the LORD, so that he will not die.

36You are to make a plate of pure gold and engrave on it as on a seal: HOLY TO THE LORD.

37Fasten to it a blue cord to mount it on the turban; it shall be on the front of the turban.

38And it will be worn on Aaron’s forehead, so that he may bear the iniquity of the holy things that the sons of Israel consecrate with regard to all their holy gifts. It shall always be on his forehead, so that they may be acceptable before the LORD.

39You are to weave the tunic with fine linen, make the turban of fine linen, and fashion an embroidered sash.

40Make tunics, sashes, and headbands for Aaron’s sons, to give them glory and splendor.

41After you put these garments on your brother Aaron and his sons, anoint them, ordain them, and consecrate them so that they may serve Me as priests.

42Make linen undergarments to cover their bare flesh, extending from waist to thigh.

43Aaron and his sons must wear them whenever they enter the Tent of Meeting or approach the altar to minister in the Holy Place, so that they will not incur guilt and die. This is to be a permanent statute for Aaron and his descendants.

Summary

God instructs Moses to set apart Aaron and his sons as priests and to have skilled craftsmen design sacred garments that reflect dignity and honor (vv. 1-5). These include an ornate ephod, a breastpiece set with twelve gemstones representing Israel's tribes and containing the Urim and Thummim for seeking God's guidance, a blue robe hung with bells and pomegranates, and a gold plate on the turban inscribed as holy to the LORD (vv. 6-39). Simpler garments are also prescribed for Aaron's sons, including undergarments to preserve modesty when they serve at the altar (vv. 40-43).

Authorship & Background

Author: Moses (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 28 describes the priestly garments — specifically the high priest's garments "for glory and for beauty" (v.2). Aaron is appointed as Israel's first high priest, with his sons as priests. The high priest's garments are rich with symbolism: he carries Israel's names on his shoulders (strength) and over his heart (love). The golden plate on his forehead reads "HOLINESS to the LORD" (v.36). Every detail points to Christ, our great High Priest (Hebrews 4:14): He carries us on His shoulders and over His heart, He bears our iniquity, and He is holy to the Lord. The garments include: the ephod, the breastplate of judgment (with twelve stones and the Urim and Thummim), the blue robe with bells and pomegranates, the linen coat, the turban with the gold plate, and the sash.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: garments made for glory and for beauty rather than for utility alone, two shoulder stones and twelve breastplate stones carrying the whole nation's names into the priest's presence with God, bells and pomegranates alternating on a robe's hem so the priest's movement inside the holy place is literally audible, a gold plate reading HOLINESS TO THE LORD worn on the forehead, and an admission built into the garments themselves that even Israel's worship needs a priest to bear its guilt
The Priestly Appointment (vv.1-5): Aaron and his sons are set apart for priestly service (v.1). Holy garments "for glory and for beauty" (v.2). Skilled craftsmen "filled with the spirit of wisdom" make them (v.3). The garments listed: breastplate, ephod, robe, embroidered coat, turban, sash (v.4).
The Ephod (vv.6-14): Gold, blue, purple, scarlet, fine linen (v.6). Two shoulder pieces (v.7). Two onyx stones on the shoulders — six tribal names on each (vv.9-10). "Aaron shall bear their names before the LORD upon his two shoulders for a memorial" (v.12).
The Breastplate of Judgment (vv.15-30): Same materials as the ephod (v.15). Square, doubled, a span by a span (v.16). Twelve precious stones in four rows — each engraved with a tribal name (vv.17-21). Attached to the ephod by gold chains and blue cord (vv.22-28). "Aaron shall bear the names of the children of Israel in the breastplate of judgment upon his heart" (v.29). The Urim and Thummim placed inside — for discerning God's will (v.30).
The Blue Robe (vv.31-35): All blue (v.31). Pomegranates and golden bells alternating on the hem (vv.33-34). "His sound shall be heard when he goeth in unto the holy place... that he die not" (v.35).
The Gold Plate (vv.36-38): Pure gold, engraved: "HOLINESS to the LORD" (v.36). On the turban, on Aaron's forehead (v.37). "Aaron may bear the iniquity of the holy things" (v.38) — even Israel's worship is imperfect; the priest bears its guilt.
The Other Garments (vv.39-43): Embroidered coat, linen turban, sash (v.39). Aaron's sons: coats, sashes, caps — "for glory and for beauty" (v.40). Linen undergarments for modesty (v.42). "A statute for ever" (v.43).

Setting: Moses is still on Mount Sinai in the cloud, receiving God's plans for the tabernacle (24:18). Chapters 25–27 described the house of God: the ark, the table, the lampstand, the tent, the altar and the courtyard. The last verses of chapter 27 introduced Aaron and his sons as the ones who would keep the lamp burning. Now God turns from the house to the people who will serve in it. Chapter 28 describes the clothing of the priests, especially the high priest, and chapter 29 their ordination. A holy God could not be approached casually, and in Israel's worship, clothing spoke. Every piece of the high priest's dress said something about who he was, whom he represented, and what he carried into God's presence. More space is given here to the breastpiece than to any other single item in the tabernacle.

The Priests' Garments (vv1–43)

  • "Bring near to you Aaron your brother, and his sons with him… to serve me as priests" (v1). Up to now, family heads and firstborn sons had offered sacrifices (24:5). Now God sets apart one family to be priests for the whole nation: Aaron, Moses' older brother, and his four sons. "Bring near" is a key idea. A priest is one brought near to God on behalf of others. Hebrews notes that "no one takes this honor for himself, but only when called by God, just as Aaron was" (Heb 5:4). Two of these sons, Nadab and Abihu, would soon die for offering unauthorized fire (Lev 10:1–2). Eleazar and Ithamar carried on the line, and Eleazar became high priest after Aaron (Num 20:25–28).
  • "You shall make holy garments for Aaron your brother, for glory and for beauty" (v2). "Glory" is kavod, the same word used for God's glory on the mountain (24:16–17). The high priest's dress reflected, in a small way, the glory of the God he served. "Beauty" (tiferet) means splendor or adornment. God cares about beauty, and worship is not meant to be drab.
  • "All the skillful, whom I have filled with a spirit of skill" (v3). Literally "the wise of heart, whom I have filled with a spirit of wisdom." In Hebrew thought, wisdom includes practical skill, the craftsman's know-how. This is one of the first places Scripture speaks of God filling people with his Spirit (compare 31:3), and it is said of artisans. Bezalel and Oholiab will be named as leaders of this work (31:1–6). Making garments and building furniture for God was Spirit-filled work.
  • "A breastpiece, an ephod, a robe, a coat of checker work, a turban, and a sash" (v4). These six items, plus the gold plate (v36) and linen undergarments (v42), made up the high priest's dress. They were worn in layers: linen undergarments, a linen coat (tunic), a blue robe, the ephod, the breastpiece, and the turban with its gold plate.
  • "They shall make the ephod of gold, of blue and purple and scarlet yarns, and of fine twined linen" (v6). The ephod was probably a kind of apron or vest, with front and back pieces joined by shoulder straps and tied at the waist with a woven band (vv7–8). Its threads included gold, hammered thin and cut into strands (39:3). The same colors as the curtains and veil: the high priest was dressed to match the tabernacle. Later, plainer linen ephods were worn by others in priestly service, including the boy Samuel and David (1 Sam 2:18; 2 Sam 6:14). Gideon's gold ephod became an object of idolatry (Judg 8:27).
  • "Two onyx stones, and engrave on them the names of the sons of Israel, six… on the one stone… six on the other… in the order of their birth" (vv9–10). The Hebrew word (shoham) is uncertain, possibly onyx, carnelian or lapis lazuli. It is one of the stones of Eden's river land (Gen 2:12). "As a jeweler engraves signets" (v11). A signet was an engraved seal used to stamp a name, a personal signature. "Aaron shall bear their names before the LORD on his two shoulders for remembrance" (v12). The shoulder is the place of carrying burdens and of strength. Isaiah says of the coming child, "the government shall be upon his shoulder" (Isa 9:6), and Jesus pictures the shepherd who finds his lost sheep and "lays it on his shoulders, rejoicing" (Luke 15:5). The high priest literally carried Israel into God's presence. "For remembrance" does not mean God might forget. It means the priest presents the people before God, asking him to act on their behalf.
  • "You shall make a breastpiece of judgment" (v15). "Judgment" (mishpat) here means a decision or ruling, because the Urim and Thummim inside it were used to learn God's decisions (v30). "Square and doubled, a span its length and a span its breadth" (v16). A span was about nine inches (23 cm), the width of a spread hand. "Doubled" means folded over to form a pouch.
  • "Four rows of stones" (vv17–20). Twelve gems, three in each row, each engraved with the name of one tribe (v21). The ancient names for gems do not map neatly onto modern ones, so the ESV's "sardius, topaz, and carbuncle… emerald, sapphire, diamond…" are educated guesses. ("Diamond" is unlikely, since diamonds were too hard to engrave with ancient tools; many think the stone was some other hard gem.) Every tribe, large or small, had its own stone. Revelation picks up the picture: the foundations of the new Jerusalem are adorned with twelve precious stones, many of the same ones (Rev 21:19–20), and its gates bear the names of the twelve tribes (Rev 21:12).
  • The chains and rings (vv22–28). Gold cords and rings tied the breastpiece to the shoulder pieces at the top and to the ephod's band at the bottom with a blue lace, "so that the breastpiece shall not come loose from the ephod" (v28). The names on the shoulders and the names on the heart were bound together.
  • "So Aaron shall bear the names of the sons of Israel in the breastpiece of judgment on his heart" (v29). On the shoulders for strength; on the heart for love. In Hebrew the heart is the center of thought, will and affection. The Song of Songs uses the same picture: "Set me as a seal upon your heart, as a seal upon your arm, for love is strong as death" (Song 8:6). "To bring them to regular remembrance before the LORD." Every time he went into the Holy Place, the people went in with him.
  • "In the breastpiece of judgment you shall put the Urim and the Thummim" (v30). No one knows exactly what these were. The names may mean "lights" and "perfections." Some have noticed that Urim begins with the first letter of the Hebrew alphabet and Thummim with the last, suggesting completeness. Most scholars think they were two objects, perhaps stones, kept in the pouch and used to receive a yes-or-no answer from God (compare 1 Sam 14:41, where the Greek text describes such a use). They were used to discern God's will in serious matters (Num 27:21; 1 Sam 28:6). After the exile they had been lost, and the leaders had to wait for "a priest to consult Urim and Thummim" (Ezra 2:63). The text does not describe them, and it is wise not to claim more than it says. "Thus Aaron shall bear the judgment of the people of Israel on his heart." He carries both the people and God's decisions about them.
  • "You shall make the robe of the ephod all of blue" (v31). A long robe of one color, worn under the ephod. Blue was the color of the sky, often linked with heaven, and later every Israelite was to wear a blue cord on the tassels of his garment as a reminder of God's commands (Num 15:38–40). "An opening for the head… with a woven binding… so that it may not tear" (v32). It was woven in one piece (39:22). Leviticus 21:10 forbids the high priest to tear his garments. At Jesus' trial, the high priest Caiaphas "tore his robes" (Matt 26:65), and when the soldiers divided Jesus' clothes they found his tunic "seamless, woven in one piece from top to bottom" and would not tear it (John 19:23–24).
  • "Pomegranates of blue and purple and scarlet yarns… with bells of gold between them" (v33). Pomegranates were a symbol of fruitfulness and of the good land (Num 13:23; Deut 8:8). They also decorated the pillars of Solomon's temple (1 Kgs 7:18–20). The bells and fruit alternated all around the hem.
  • "Its sound shall be heard when he goes into the Holy Place before the LORD, and when he comes out, so that he does not die" (v35). The bells announced his coming and going before the King. A servant did not burst unannounced into a king's presence. The bells also told the people outside that their priest was alive and still serving. A later Jewish tradition says a rope was tied to the high priest's ankle on the Day of Atonement so his body could be pulled out if he died in the Most Holy Place, but that tradition is late and not in the Bible.
  • "A plate of pure gold… 'Holy to the LORD'" (v36). The plate was tied with a blue cord to the front of the turban (v37), right on the forehead where everyone could read it. Exodus 39:30 calls it "the holy crown." The whole priesthood was summed up in three words: this man belongs to the LORD.
  • "Aaron shall bear any guilt from the holy things that the people of Israel consecrate as their holy gifts" (v38). This is a remarkable statement. Even the people's best gifts, their offerings and worship, were touched by their sin: wrong motives, hidden faults, mistakes in the ritual. The high priest, bearing the sign of holiness, carried that guilt for them, "that they may be accepted before the LORD." Even worship needs a mediator. "Bear guilt" is the language later used of the suffering servant, who "shall bear their iniquities" (Isa 53:11).
  • "The coat in checker work of fine linen… a turban of fine linen… a sash embroidered with needlework" (v39). The tunic was a long linen shirt, probably woven in a checkered or patterned weave. The turban (mitsnefet) was wound linen. On the Day of Atonement the high priest set aside his glorious garments and wore only plain white linen (Lev 16:4, 23–24), entering the Most Holy Place dressed humbly.
  • "For Aaron's sons you shall make coats and sashes and caps… for glory and beauty" (v40). The ordinary priests' clothes were simpler, but they too were "for glory and beauty."
  • "Anoint them and ordain them and consecrate them" (v41). Three verbs. "Anoint" means pour oil on them, marking them out for God's service (29:7). "Ordain" is literally "fill their hand," an old idiom for installing someone in office, possibly from placing the first offerings in the new priest's hands (29:9, 24). "Consecrate" means set them apart as holy. Chapter 29 describes the ceremony in full.
  • "Linen undergarments to cover their naked flesh… from the hips to the thighs" (v42). This completes the law against altar steps in 20:26. The Tyndale notes point out that some pagan priests served naked, and sexual rites were common in Canaanite religion. Nothing of that kind was to touch the worship of Israel's God. "Lest they bear guilt and die" (v43). Even modesty in worship was a matter of life and death before a holy God.
  • Commentators: Henry says the priesthood "was confined to the family of Aaron only," and he loves the picture of the breastpiece: the high priest "carries them in his bosom with tender affection," and "how small and poor soever the tribe was, it was as a precious stone in the breastpiece of the high priest." He renders Urim and Thummim "light and integrity." JFB praises Moses' humility in "readily complying with the command to invest his brother with the sacred office," and describes the ephod as "a very gorgeous robe made of byssus" (fine linen). JFB renders the Urim and Thummim "lights" and "perfections," and suggests they may have been the breastpiece stones themselves, a view most scholars do not hold. JFB has no note on v38. Guzik points out that the priests were born into the priesthood rather than choosing it, as believers become priests by new birth (1 Pet 2:9), and that some of the first people Scripture describes as filled by God's Spirit were craftsmen. He lists a series of contrasts between Aaron's glorious garments and Jesus at the cross: a mocking purple robe instead of the ephod, a crown of thorns instead of a linen turban, and nakedness instead of linen undergarments. Guzik also notes that "fill the hand" lies behind the word for ordaining.

Pointing to Christ

Aaron went into God's presence with Israel's names on his shoulders and over his heart, "Holy to the LORD" on his forehead, and the guilt of the people's worship on himself. Every piece of his dress pointed to someone greater. Jesus is "a great high priest who has passed through the heavens" (Heb 4:14). He carries his people on his shoulders like a found sheep and their names on his heart, and he "always lives to make intercession for them" (Heb 7:25). He is not only dressed in holiness; he is "the Holy One of God" (Mark 1:24). And he did not carry the guilt of his people's worship on a gold plate. He bore their sin "in his body on the tree" (1 Pet 2:24), stripped of his seamless robe and crowned with thorns. Because of him, the "holy things" we bring, even our flawed prayers and praise, are "acceptable to God through Jesus Christ" (1 Pet 2:5).

Reflection

  • Clothed for Glory and Beauty (v.2) — Aaron's priestly garments are explicitly designed to reflect the honor of his calling. How does understanding your identity in Christ change the way you carry yourself day to day?
  • Carried on His Shoulders (v.12) — Aaron bears the names of Israel's tribes engraved on stones over his shoulders as a memorial before the Lord. What comfort is it to you that Christ carries your name before the Father?
  • Carried Over His Heart (v.29) — The same names are placed on the breastplate directly over Aaron's heart. Does God's love for you feel like strength alone, or also like genuine affection?
  • Heard Before He's Seen (v.35) — Bells on the hem of the robe let people know the priest is alive and ministering even before he comes into view. How visible is your walk with God to the people around you?
  • Holiness Covering Imperfect Worship (v.36) — The gold plate on Aaron's forehead allows him to bear the guilt of even Israel's imperfect offerings. Where do you need to trust that Christ covers the flaws in even your best attempts at worship?
