Summary

The chapter shifts to a communal prayer as the whole people ask the Lord to remember and see the disgrace that has come upon them (v. 1). They catalog their losses: their inheritance given to strangers, their homes to foreigners, and orphans and widows left without support (vv. 2-3). They must now pay for water and wood that were once free, and they are exhausted from forced labor under foreign rule (vv. 4-5). Women have been violated, princes hanged, elders shown no honor, and even the joy of dancing has turned to mourning (vv. 11-15). The people confess that the crown has fallen from their head because of their sin, yet close by affirming that the Lord reigns forever, pleading with Him to restore them as in days of old even while uncertain whether He has utterly rejected them (vv. 16-22).

Authorship & Background

Author: Traditionally Jeremiah (the "weeping prophet"), written after Jerusalem's fall in 586 BC. This final chapter functions as a communal prayer offered on behalf of the surviving remnant — those left in the devastated land or beginning their exile.
Classification: Communal Lament / National Prayer
Key Themes: Communal suffering and corporate prayer, the loss of inheritance and identity, the consequences falling on the next generation, longing for restoration, the uncertainty of whether God will restore, the permanence of God vs. the transience of human suffering
Historical Context: Unlike chapters 1 to 4, chapter 5 is not an acrostic, though it still runs to 22 verses. Abandoning the strict form may represent a final dissolution of order: the people have lost even the capacity for structured grief, and what is left is unadorned pleading. The chapter catalogues the specific losses of the community left after the destruction — their inheritance, with land handed to foreigners; their families, fatherless and widowed; their basic resources, paying for water and wood that had been free; their labor, grinding like slaves; and their dignity, with women raped, princes hanged and elders shown no respect. The ending is one of the most debated conclusions in Scripture. “Turn thou us unto thee... unless thou hast utterly rejected us” can be read as a condition or as an emphatic denial, and the Hebrew supports either. Jewish liturgical practice repeats v.21 after v.22 when the book is read aloud, so that it does not end on the harder line.
Remember, O LORD, What Is Come Upon Us (vv.1-3): The final chapter is a communal prayer and it opens with a request to be seen: remember, consider, behold our reproach. The losses named first are inheritance and family — the land turned over to strangers, the houses to aliens, and a community describing itself as fatherless, with mothers as widows. The “we” is unbroken through the chapter, which is what distinguishes it from the personal voice of chapter 3.
Paying for Water and Wood (vv.4-7): The detail is deliberately mundane and therefore effective: they buy their own water and pay for their own wood. Things that had been free in their own land now cost money in it. They give the hand to Egypt and Assyria to get bread, returning to the alliances the prophets had condemned. Verse 7 states the grievance that runs under the whole chapter — our fathers have sinned and are not, and we have borne their iniquities.
Servants Rule Over Us (vv.8-10): “Servants have ruled over us: there is none that doth deliver us out of their hand.” The complaint is about order inverted rather than only about hardship. Getting bread means risking their lives because of the sword of the wilderness, so the countryside is no longer safe to cross. Verse 10's skin black like an oven describes the physical effect of prolonged hunger.
Women Ravished, Elders Dishonored (vv.11-18): The catalogue of indignity is specific: women ravished in Zion, princes hanged, elders shown no respect, young men grinding at the mill, children falling under wood they are made to carry. The elders have left the gate and the young their music, so both justice and joy have stopped. Verse 16's “the crown is fallen from our head” is followed immediately by “woe unto us, that we have sinned”, and the chapter ends this section with foxes walking on the desolate mountain of Zion.
Thou Remainest For Ever: Turn Us Again (vv.19-22): Against everything listed, one fixed point: thou, O LORD, remainest for ever, thy throne from generation to generation. The prayer that follows asks why God would forget for ever, and then makes its request — turn thou us unto thee, and we shall be turned; renew our days as of old. The restoration asked for is something God must do to them rather than something they undertake. The book then ends on its unresolved final line, which is why readers have argued about it ever since.

Deep Dive

Setting: The last of the five laments is different from the other four. It has twenty-two lines, the number of letters in the Hebrew alphabet, but it does not follow the alphabet. It is shorter, the lines are brief, and almost all of it is spoken as "we," the community praying together. Some ancient manuscripts and the Latin Vulgate gave it the title "The Prayer of Jeremiah." Where chapters 1–4 mostly describe the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC, this one describes life afterward, under Babylonian occupation: land in foreign hands, water and firewood that must be bought, forced labor, assaults, and a temple mount where wild animals roam. The poem moves from a plea for God to look (v1), through a long list of present miseries (vv2–18), to a confession of God's eternal reign and a final, uncertain plea for restoration (vv19–22). The book ends not with an answer but with a question held open before God.

Restore Us to Yourself, O Lord (vv1–22)

  • "Remember, O LORD, what has befallen us; look, and see our disgrace!" (v1). Three verbs: remember, look, see. To ask God to "remember" is not to suggest he has forgotten facts, but to ask him to act on what he knows, as he "remembered" Noah and Rachel (Gen 8:1; 30:22). The whole chapter is a prayer hung on this opening line.
  • "Our inheritance has been turned over to strangers, our homes to foreigners" (v2). The land was Israel's inheritance from God, divided by tribe and family (Num 26:52–56; Josh 13–21). Family land was not supposed to be sold permanently, because it belonged to the LORD (Lev 25:23). Now it was in foreign hands. Babylon gave some vineyards and fields to the poor who remained (Jer 39:10), and neighboring peoples like Edom moved into southern Judah (Ezek 35:10; 36:5).
  • "We have become orphans, fatherless; our mothers are like widows" (v3). Many fathers had died in the siege or been deported. But the line may also mean the nation itself has lost its protector. Orphans and widows were the most defenseless people in Israel, the very ones the law told Israel to protect (Exod 22:22–24; Deut 10:18). Israel had failed to care for them (Jer 7:6; 22:3). Now Israel has become them.
  • "We must pay for the water we drink; the wood we get must be bought" (v4). Under the occupation even basic resources, wells and firewood, were controlled and taxed. People had to pay for what had once been free in their own land.
  • "Our pursuers are at our necks; we are weary; we are given no rest" (v5). Literally "upon our neck," like a yoke. The covenant promise had been rest in the land (Deut 12:9–10). The covenant curse was no rest (Deut 28:65).
  • "We have given the hand to Egypt, and to Assyria, to get bread enough" (v6). "Giving the hand" means making a pact or submitting (2 Kgs 10:15; Jer 50:15). Judah's kings had played the two great powers against each other for a century, going to Egypt for help against Mesopotamia and back again. Jeremiah and Hosea both mocked this ("calling to Egypt, going to Assyria," Hos 7:11; Jer 2:18, 36). "Assyria" here may stand for the Mesopotamian empire in general, now ruled from Babylon. The result of all that politics: they begged for bread.
  • "Our fathers sinned, and are no more; and we bear their iniquities" (v7). This sounds like the proverb Jeremiah and Ezekiel both quoted: "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge" (Jer 31:29; Ezek 18:2). Ezekiel answered that each person is responsible for their own sin (Ezek 18:3–4, 20). But this verse is not a complaint that God is unfair. It is honest about what had happened: generations of sin, especially under King Manasseh (2 Kgs 24:3–4), had piled up, and the children inherited the consequences. And in v16 the speakers confess their own guilt too: "woe to us, for we have sinned!" Both are true. They suffer for their ancestors' sins, and they had added their own.
  • "Slaves rule over us; there is none to deliver us from their hand" (v8). Babylon's governors and officials were technically "servants" of Nebuchadnezzar. Even minor officials of a foreign king now lorded it over Judah. Proverbs counts "a slave when he becomes king" among the things the earth cannot bear (Prov 30:21–22). Israel was meant to be a kingdom of priests (Exod 19:6), and now it is ruled by underlings.
  • "We get our bread at the peril of our lives, because of the sword in the wilderness" (v9). Raiders from the desert attacked anyone who went out to the fields or traveled for food.
  • "Our skin is hot as an oven with the burning heat of famine" (v10). Fever from starvation. The skin feels as if it is burning.
  • "Women are raped in Zion, young women in the towns of Judah" (v11). The poet names the horror. Sexual violence was a common part of ancient conquest (Zech 14:2). Scripture does not hide it, and it treats it as a terrible wrong.
  • "Princes are hung up by their hands; no respect is shown to the elders" (v12). Probably the bodies of executed leaders were impaled or hung up for public display, a common Assyrian and Babylonian practice. Possibly they were hung by "their" hands, meaning by the enemies' hands. Either way, leaders and the old, who should have been honored (Lev 19:32), were humiliated.
  • "Young men are compelled to grind at the mill, and boys stagger under loads of wood" (v13). Grinding grain with a hand mill was hard, repetitive work, usually done by women and slaves (Exod 11:5; Isa 47:2). The Philistines set the captured Samson to grinding (Judg 16:21). The youths of Judah have become forced laborers.
  • "The old men have left the city gate, the young men their music" (v14). The city gate was where elders sat to hear cases and give counsel (Ruth 4:1–2; Prov 31:23). Music was the sound of feasts and weddings. Jeremiah had warned that God would silence "the voice of mirth and the voice of gladness" (Jer 7:34; 16:9; 25:10). Both civic life and joy have stopped.
  • "The joy of our hearts has ceased; our dancing has been turned to mourning" (v15). The reverse of Psalm 30:11, "You have turned for me my mourning into dancing."
  • "The crown has fallen from our head; woe to us, for we have sinned!" (v16). The crown may mean the Davidic king, now in prison (4:20), or more broadly all of Judah's honor and glory (Jer 13:18). And then, at the center of the list of miseries, comes the confession. This is the only line in the chapter that names the cause, and it is "we," not just "our fathers."
  • "For this our heart has become sick, for these things our eyes have grown dim" (v17). Grief has worn them down, body and soul.
  • "For Mount Zion which lies desolate; jackals prowl over it" (v18). The worst loss of all is placed last: the temple mount itself. The animals are shu'alim, foxes or jackals, which haunt ruins (Neh 4:3; Ezek 13:4). The place where God had chosen to put his name (Deut 12:5; 1 Kgs 9:3) is now a den for scavengers. Micah had foretold it a century earlier: "Zion shall be plowed as a field… and the mountain of the house a wooded height" (Mic 3:12; Jer 26:18).
  • "But you, O LORD, reign forever; your throne endures to all generations" (v19). The great turn. Zion's throne has fallen, but God's throne has not. His earthly temple lies in ruins, but he still reigns in heaven (Ps 102:12; 103:19). The temple was his footstool (2:1). His throne was never on earth alone. This single line is the ground of every hope in the chapter. Because God reigns, he can still act.
  • "Why do you forget us forever, why do you forsake us for so many days?" (v20). And yet: if he reigns, why does he not help? The psalmists ask this too (Ps 13:1; 44:23–24; 74:1, 10; 77:7–9). Faith here does not mean having no questions. It means bringing them to the one who reigns.
  • "Restore us to yourself, O LORD, that we may be restored!" (v21). The Hebrew repeats one verb: "Turn us back to you, and we will turn back." This is the word shub, the main word for repentance and return in Jeremiah. The people know they cannot even repent on their own. God must turn them. Jeremiah 31:18 has Ephraim pray the same words: "bring me back that I may be restored." The prayer is for both: a change of heart toward God and a return to the land. "Renew our days as of old." Not just a restoration of buildings, but of the relationship they had when God first brought them into the land.
  • "Unless you have utterly rejected us, and you remain exceedingly angry with us" (v22). The final line is among the hardest in the Old Testament to translate. The two small Hebrew words at its start (ki 'im) can mean "unless," "but instead," "even if," or "for if." So the ending may be a fearful question ("…unless you have utterly rejected us?"), a sad statement ("but you have utterly rejected us"), or a plea ("even though you were very angry with us"). The ESV and many modern versions leave the doubt in place. The book ends on an open question. That honesty is part of its gift: it shows that grief and faith can sit together, unresolved, before God.
  • A final repetition. In the synagogue, when Lamentations is read aloud on Tisha b'Av, the congregation repeats v21 after v22, so the reading does not end on a note of rejection. The same custom is followed at the end of Isaiah, Malachi and Ecclesiastes. The rest of Scripture also answers the question. God had not utterly rejected his people. Within seventy years exiles were returning to Zion (Ezra 1), and the prophets were promising a new covenant and a new heart (Jer 31:31–34; Ezek 36:26).
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry opens with a principle: "Is any afflicted? Let him pray; and let him in prayer pour out his complaint to God." On v16: "All our woes are owing to our own sin and folly." On v19: "whatever changes there are on earth, God is still the same," and on v21: "God never leaves any till they first leave him." Guzik cites Adam Clarke's suggestion that the acrostic was dropped because anguish had made such craft feel out of place. On v7 he points to Ezekiel 18, where God rejects the idea that children are simply punished for their fathers. On v21 he stresses that the people had no power even to repent, so they had to ask God to turn them, and he presents that prayer as one any believer can pray. He also notes the synagogue custom of repeating v21, and that Lamentations was never meant to be the last word. JFB calls the chapter a "closing recapitulation" of the earlier laments, reads the "crown" of v16 as "all our glory, the kingdom and the priesthood," and calls God's eternal reign in v19 "their ground of hope of restoration." On v22 he records Calvin's reading, "Unless Thou art implacable, which is impossible, hear our prayer," and notes that Jews repeated v21 "to avoid the ill-omen of a mournful closing sentence."

Pointing to Christ

Lamentations ends with an open question: "Unless you have utterly rejected us?" The New Testament answers it in a person. In Christ, God did not utterly reject his people. He sent his Son, and on the cross the Son was the one who cried, "Why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34), so that those who trust him would never be forsaken (Heb 13:5). The prayer "Restore us to yourself, O LORD, that we may be restored" is answered when the Father draws people to Christ (John 6:44) and gives them new hearts. The jackals on Mount Zion are not the end of the story. Jesus is the new temple (John 2:19–21), and the book of Revelation closes with what Lamentations longed for: the throne of God that "endures to all generations," a city made new, and God dwelling with his people, wiping every tear from their eyes (Rev 21:3–5). "Renew our days as of old" becomes, at the end, "Behold, I am making all things new."

Reflection

  • A Prayer to Simply Be Seen (v.1) — The chapter opens with the community asking God only to remember and look at their disgrace, not yet asking for a fix. Can you bring God your pain honestly, trusting Him to see it, even before you know how it will be resolved?
  • Bearing Consequences Not Fully Our Own (v.7) — The people acknowledge that they're suffering in part for the sins of previous generations. What generational patterns or inherited burdens are you carrying, and how do you separate what was handed to you from what is genuinely your own to repent of?
  • The Unshaken Throne (v.19) — In contrast to everything else in the chapter that has fallen — the crown, the joy, the inheritance — the Lord's throne is declared to endure forever. When everything around you feels unstable, where is your identity actually rooted?
  • Asking God to Do What We Cannot (v.21) — The people ask the Lord to turn them back to Himself, recognizing that genuine restoration has to originate with Him, not their own effort. Are you trying to manufacture your own spiritual renewal, or are you asking God to do the turning?
  • Faith in an Unresolved Ending (v.22) — The book closes without a clear resolution, leaving open whether God will fully restore His people. Can you keep praying and hoping in a situation where you genuinely don't know how it will end?
