Summary

The chapter opens with what appears to be Israel's call to return to the LORD, confidently expecting him to heal what he has torn and to revive them after a short time (vv. 1-3). God responds that their love is fickle, like a morning cloud or dew that vanishes quickly, and that he has used the prophets' words to cut them down in judgment (vv. 4-5). He declares his true desire: steadfast love rather than sacrifice, and knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings (v. 6). Instead they have broken the covenant like Adam, and priests and people alike are stained with treachery and murder (vv. 7-10). The chapter closes noting that a harvest of judgment is appointed for Judah as well, whenever God restores the fortunes of his people (v. 11).

Authorship & Background

Author: Hosea son of Beeri. Prophesied to the northern kingdom (Israel) approximately 755-715 BC. Contemporary with Isaiah and Micah. Key theme: God's unfailing love for unfaithful Israel, illustrated through Hosea's marriage to Gomer (a prostitute). Despite Israel's spiritual adultery, God says: "I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely" (14:4).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — Superficial Repentance and God's Desire for Steadfast Love
Key Themes: False repentance that evaporates, the third-day resurrection, God's desire for mercy over sacrifice, the knowledge of God, covenant breaking like Adam
Historical Context: Chapter 6 opens with what appears to be a beautiful call to repentance (vv.1-3), but God's response (v.4) reveals it as shallow. Their "goodness" (hesed/love) evaporates like morning dew. This is the tragedy of Israel: not the absence of religious feeling but its shallowness. They come to God with emotion but without substance. God responds with one of the most quoted verses in the prophets: "I desired mercy, and not sacrifice" (v.6) — quoted by Jesus twice (Matthew 9:13, 12:7). The chapter reveals that God's primary desire is not ritual but relationship.
Israel's Call to Repentance (vv.1-3): Come, and let us return unto the LORD. Taken by itself this is the best thing said in the book by anyone other than God. He hath torn and he will heal us, after two days he will revive us, in the third day he will raise us up. It is confident, well phrased, and correct in its theology, and the phrase about the third day has been read as resurrection language ever since. It reads as the answer the previous chapter was waiting for.
God's Lament: Your Love is Like Morning Dew (v.4): God's reply exposes it. O Ephraim, what shall I do unto thee? your goodness is as a morning cloud, and as the early dew it goeth away. The word rendered goodness is hesed, covenant loyalty, the very quality God is looking for, and the complaint is not that they lack feeling but that the feeling does not last past the morning. This is the hardest verse in the chapter because the prayer it answers sounded sincere. Repentance that evaporates is the specific failure named here.
God's True Desire: Mercy, Not Sacrifice (vv.5-6): Therefore have I hewed them by the prophets, and my judgments are as the light that goeth forth. Then the verse that outlasts the book: I desired mercy, and not sacrifice, and the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings. It is not an abolition of the sacrificial system, which God commanded, it is a statement of priority inside it. Jesus quotes it twice, at Matthew 9:13 over eating with tax collectors and at Matthew 12:7 over the Sabbath, both times against people who had the ritual right and the mercy wrong.
The Catalogue of Crimes (vv.7-11): The evidence follows the principle. They like men have transgressed the covenant, which can also be read as they have transgressed like Adam, the first covenant broken in a garden. Then place by place: Gilead a city of workers of iniquity, Shechem a road where priests murder travelers, Bethel the site of lewdness. The priesthood appears here as a gang. Judah is named at the end, with a harvest set for it too.

Setting: Hosea is preaching to the northern kingdom of Israel (Ephraim) in its last turbulent decades before Assyria destroyed it in 722 BC, probably around the crisis of 734–732 BC, when Israel and Judah were at war with each other and both were courting Assyria. Chapter 5 ended with God as a lion who tears his people and then goes back to his den "until they acknowledge their guilt and seek my face" (5:15). Chapter 6 opens with exactly that: a song of return. But God's answer shows that their repentance is as thin as morning mist. Then he names the reasons: broken covenant, bloodshed, priests who act like bandits. The chapter holds two of the most quoted lines in the prophets, "on the third day he will raise us up" and "I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice." The ESV heading starting here, "Israel and Judah Are Unrepentant," runs on through chapter 7.

Israel and Judah Are Unrepentant (vv1–11)

  • "Come, let us return to the LORD" (v1). The speaker is not named. It may be the people, the priests, or Hosea himself calling them. It answers 5:15 directly: God said he would wait until they sought him, and here they say "let us return." "Return" (shuv) is the main Old Testament word for repentance.
  • "He has torn us, that he may heal us; he has struck us down, and he will bind us up" (v1). This picks up the lion of 5:14 ("I will tear") and the wound of 5:13 that Assyria could not heal. They now admit God is the one who struck them, and the one who can heal. Deuteronomy 32:39 says the same: "I wound and I heal." That is good theology. The question v4 raises is whether it is sincere.
  • "After two days he will revive us; on the third day he will raise us up" (v2). "Two days… the third day" is a Hebrew way of saying "in a short time" (compare the "three… four" pattern of Amos 1–2). The people expect a quick recovery, as if God's healing were automatic. "Revive" and "raise up" are resurrection words. The nation is pictured as dead and brought back to life, as in Ezekiel's valley of dry bones (Ezek 37:1–14).
  • Does v2 point to Jesus' resurrection? Paul says Christ "was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures" (1 Cor 15:4), and Jesus said Scripture foretold that "the Christ should suffer and on the third day rise from the dead" (Luke 24:46). Neither quotes Hosea by name, but this is the clearest Old Testament verse about being raised on the third day, and many in the early church (Tertullian among them) read it that way. Some Jewish interpreters also read it of the final resurrection. Most modern scholars say Hosea meant national restoration. These readings fit together: Israel's hope of being raised after a short time is fulfilled in the Messiah, who represents Israel and was raised on the third day, and in whom his people are raised (Rom 6:4–5). JFB applies it first to Israel and then "in its full accuracy" to Messiah. Henry calls it "a type and figure of Christ's rising the third day."
  • "Let us know; let us press on to know the LORD" (v3). This is exactly what was missing: "no knowledge of God in the land" (4:1, 6; 5:4). "Press on" means to pursue, chase after, the verb used of an army chasing its enemy. Knowledge of God is not passive; it must be pursued. JFB: it is "practice, not mere theory."
  • "His going out is sure as the dawn; he will come to us as the showers, as the spring rains" (v3). They are confident God will appear as reliably as the sunrise. In Israel the "early rain" fell in autumn (October–November) to soften the ground for plowing, and the "latter" or "spring rain" fell in March–April to ripen the grain. Without both, harvest failed. The irony is that they now expect from the LORD what they used to expect from Baal (2:5, 8), as if God were simply a better weather god. They still think of him as a natural force to be counted on, not a husband who has been betrayed.
  • "What shall I do with you, O Ephraim? What shall I do with you, O Judah?" (v4). God answers like a grieving parent who has tried everything (compare Isa 5:4, "What more was there to do for my vineyard?"). The tone is anguish, not just anger. In 11:8 the same heart cries, "How can I give you up, O Ephraim?"
  • "Your love is like a morning cloud, like the dew that goes early away" (v4). "Love" is hesed, steadfast covenant love, the very thing missing in 4:1. They said God would come "as sure as the dawn" (v3). God says their love is like the morning mist: gone by the time the sun is up. In Israel's dry climate, dew is precious, but it burns off fast. Their repentance was real feeling but had no depth. It is the seed on rocky ground in Jesus' parable, which "has no root in himself, but endures for a while" (Matt 13:21).
  • "Therefore I have hewn them by the prophets; I have slain them by the words of my mouth" (v5). "Hewn" is the word for cutting stone or chopping wood. God's word through the prophets was like an axe, cutting them down to size. The prophets' words did not just predict judgment; they carried it (Jer 1:9–10; 23:29; Heb 4:12). "My judgment goes forth as the light": a small change in how the Hebrew letters are divided gives "my judgment goes forth like light," which most modern versions follow; the traditional text reads "your judgments are light that goes forth." Either way, God's verdict is as clear as daylight. JFB suggests "lightning" as another possible sense.
  • "For I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice, the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings" (v6). This is the key verse of the book. "Not sacrifice" means "more than sacrifice," as the parallel line shows ("rather than burnt offerings"). JFB says the "not" is comparative. God had commanded the sacrifices. What he rejects is sacrifice used as a substitute for loyalty and knowing him. Samuel had told Saul the same: "to obey is better than sacrifice" (1 Sam 15:22). Amos (5:21–24), Isaiah (1:11–17) and Micah (6:6–8) all say it in their own way.
  • Jesus quotes v6 twice. When the Pharisees complained that he ate with tax collectors and sinners, Jesus said, "Go and learn what this means: 'I desire mercy, and not sacrifice'" (Matt 9:13). When they condemned his disciples for picking grain on the Sabbath, he said, "If you had known what this means… you would not have condemned the guiltless" (Matt 12:7). The Greek Old Testament had translated hesed as eleos, "mercy," and Jesus used that wording. He applied it to people who were careful about religious rules and careless about mercy. Hosea's sentence was still describing the religious leaders of Jesus' day.
  • "But like Adam they transgressed the covenant" (v7). This line is read in three main ways. (1) Like Adam, the first man, who broke the command God gave him in Eden. This is the ESV's reading, and many see it as Scripture's hint that God dealt with Adam by a covenant. (2) Like men, that is, as ordinary people break agreements. The Hebrew word adam also means "mankind," and the KJV and JFB take it this way: Israel treated God's covenant as lightly as people treat everyday promises. (3) At Adam, a town in the Jordan Valley where the waters of the Jordan piled up when Israel crossed into the land (Josh 3:16). This view points to the word "there" in the next line: "there they dealt faithlessly with me," as if a place has just been named. Many modern scholars hold this view, and it fits the list of places that follows (Gilead, Shechem). All three readings are possible. The Tyndale notes take the first, tracing rebellion from Adam through Israel's cities.
  • "Gilead is a city of evildoers, tracked with blood" (v8). Gilead was the region east of the Jordan; here it may stand for one of its towns, such as Ramoth-gilead or Mizpah (5:1). "Tracked with blood" pictures bloody footprints. When Pekah murdered King Pekahiah in Samaria, he had "fifty men of the people of Gilead" with him (2 Kings 15:25). Ramoth-gilead was also a city of refuge, where those who had killed by accident could flee (Josh 20:8). JFB draws out the irony: a place meant to protect life had become a place of bloodshed.
  • "As robbers lie in wait for a man, so the priests band together; they murder on the way to Shechem" (v9). Shechem was another city of refuge (Josh 20:7) and an ancient covenant site, where Joshua led Israel to renew the covenant (Josh 24). The road to it ran through the hill country, a natural route for pilgrims heading to Bethel. The priests, who should have protected travellers and taught the law, are pictured as a gang ambushing them. Whether this means actual killings, perhaps of pilgrims going to Jerusalem instead of Bethel, or exploiting worshipers until it amounted to robbery, is not clear. Either way it fits the charge against the priests in 4:4–9.
  • "In the house of Israel I have seen a horrible thing" (v10). The word for "horrible" is rare. Jeremiah uses it for Judah's unfaithfulness (Jer 5:30; 18:13; 23:14). It describes something that makes one shudder. "Ephraim's whoredom is there; Israel is defiled" repeats 5:3. The marriage theme returns.
  • "For you also, O Judah, a harvest is appointed" (v11). Judah is not exempt. Harvest is a common picture of judgment (Joel 3:13; Jer 51:33): what is sown will be reaped (8:7). Some read it hopefully, as a harvest of restoration, since the next line speaks of restored fortunes, but the context favours judgment. Guzik takes it as a harvest of returning exiles, who mainly settled in Judah after the exile.
  • "When I restore the fortunes of my people" (v11). The ESV makes this line the start of the next sentence, which continues in 7:1: "when I would heal Israel, the iniquity of Ephraim is revealed." God wants to restore and heal, but every time he moves to do so, their sin comes to the surface. The chapter division here cuts a sentence in half.
  • Commentators: All three see v2 pointing to Christ's resurrection. Henry calls it "a type and figure of Christ's rising the third day," JFB applies it first to Israel's national revival and then "in its full accuracy" to Messiah, and Guzik sees it as a faint, veiled prophecy of the resurrection. Henry also says God "can heal though we be ever so ill torn," and in Christ is "both the latter and the former rain." Guzik, quoting Spurgeon on v3, says God's past grace does not exhaust him: there is always more. On v4 JFB and Guzik both stress how quickly the "morning cloud" vanishes. On v6 Guzik notes Jesus' double use in Matthew and adds that Israel offered animals but never themselves (Rom 12:1). On v7, JFB prefers "like men," the common sort who break agreements lightly, while the ESV and Tyndale notes read "like Adam." JFB connects Gilead with the conspiracy against Pekahiah and both Gilead and Shechem with the cities of refuge.

Pointing to Christ

Israel's song of return expected God to raise them "on the third day," but their love evaporated like dew. The one who fulfilled that hope was the true Israel, Jesus, whose love for the Father never faded. He was "torn" and "struck down" for his people (Isa 53:4–5) and was raised on the third day, "that we may live before him" (v2; Rom 4:25). He also took up v6 as his own: he came for sinners, not for the self-righteous, and he showed what God desires, mercy and the knowledge of God, by giving himself as the one sacrifice that never needs repeating (Heb 10:10–14). Where Adam and Israel broke the covenant, he kept it.

Reflection

  • Devotion That Evaporates Like Dew (v.4) — God laments that Israel's love disappears as quickly as morning dew, showing genuine grief rather than mere anger over their inconsistency. Does your own devotion to God last beyond a moment of emotion, or does it fade once the feeling passes?
  • Mercy Over Sacrifice (v.6) — God states plainly that He desires mercy rather than sacrifice, and the knowledge of Himself rather than mere burnt offerings. Are you substituting religious activity for the compassion and relational faithfulness God actually wants from you?
  • Personal Knowledge, Not Just Facts About God (v.6) — The knowledge God desires here is relational and experiential, not simply theological information. Is your understanding of God producing an actual, personal knowing of Him, or just accurate facts about a distant figure?
  • Repeating Adam's Pattern (v.7) — Israel's covenant-breaking is compared directly to Adam's transgression: given blessing and boundaries, and choosing rebellion anyway. Where in your own life has God given you good gifts and clear boundaries that you've crossed regardless?
