Summary

This chapter opens the second Solomonic collection, copied out by King Hezekiah's scribes some two hundred fifty years after Solomon's reign (v. 1), and its proverbs range widely across kingship, speech, and self-control. It contrasts the glory of God in concealing a matter with the glory of kings in searching it out, and counsels removing the wicked from a king's presence to establish his throne (vv. 2-7). On speech, a word fitly spoken is compared to apples of gold in settings of silver, and a wise rebuke to an attentive ear is like a gold earring (vv. 11-15). The chapter urges moderation, warning against eating too much honey and comparing a man without self-control to a city with broken-down walls (vv. 16-17, 28), and it calls for kindness to a hungry enemy, feeding him bread and giving him water to drink (vv. 21-22).

Authorship & Background

Author: Primarily Solomon (1:1, 10:1, 25:1), with sections by Agur (ch.30) and King Lemuel (ch.31). Compiled over several centuries. Proverbs is wisdom literature — practical instruction for godly living. Hebrew title: 'Mishlei' — 'Proverbs/Comparisons.'
Historical Context: Chapter 25 begins the second Solomonic collection (25:1-29:27), introduced with the editorial note: "These are also proverbs of Solomon, which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out" (v.1). Hezekiah reigned c.715-686 BC — roughly 250 years after Solomon. His scribes collected, edited, and preserved Solomonic proverbs that had not been included in the earlier collection (10:1-22:16). This chapter is rich in vivid comparisons and similes, covering topics of kingship (vv.2-7), speech (vv.11-15), self-control (vv.16-17, 28), responding to enemies (vv.21-22), and the famous "apples of gold" image (v.11).
Themes in This Chapter: The Glory of Kings and the Glory of God (vv.2-7); The Power of Appropriate Speech (vv.11-15); Self-Control and Moderation (vv.16-17, 27-28); Kindness to Enemies (vv.21-22); Vivid Similes and Comparisons Throughout
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: an editorial note found nowhere else quite like it in the Old Testament, these are also proverbs of Solomon, which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out roughly two and a half centuries after they were first spoken, court material built around the glory of concealing versus the honour of searching out a matter, similes almost entirely organized around knowing when to stop, hast thou found honey, eat so much as is sufficient for thee, an instruction Paul later quotes directly in Romans, if thine enemy be hungry, give him bread to eat, and a closing observation that even careful speech eventually fails against a whispering tongue
Hezekiah's Men, the King's Court and Words Fitly Spoken (vv.1-14): Verse 1 is a piece of editorial history no other book in the Old Testament supplies about itself, these are also proverbs of Solomon, which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out. Hezekiah reigned around 700 BC, roughly two and a half centuries after Solomon, so the note records a collection being gathered and copied long after its sayings were first spoken. What follows is court material. It is the glory of God to conceal a thing, but the honour of kings is to search out a matter. Put not forth thyself in the presence of the king, better it be said unto thee, Come up hither, which Jesus turns into a parable about wedding seats in Luke 14. And the chapter's best known line about language, a word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in pictures of silver.
Honey, Neighbors and Coals of Fire (vv.15-28): The second half is built almost entirely of similes, and most of them are about knowing when to stop. Hast thou found honey, eat so much as is sufficient for thee. Withdraw thy foot from thy neighbour's house, lest he be weary of thee. A soft tongue breaketh the bone. The block contains the instruction Paul quotes in Romans 12, if thine enemy be hungry, give him bread to eat, together with the promise attached to it, thou shalt heap coals of fire upon his head, which is read either as shaming him into repentance or as leaving the judgment to God. The chapter closes on the failure all these measures are guarding against, he that hath no rule over his own spirit is like a city that is broken down, without walls.

Setting: Chapter 25 opens a new collection: "These also are proverbs of Solomon which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied" (v1). It runs through chapter 29. Hezekiah reigned about 715–686 BC, more than two centuries after Solomon, and was one of Judah's great reforming kings, who cleansed the temple and restored worship (2 Chr 29–31). His "men" were probably royal scribes who gathered Solomon's sayings from older written sources and copied them into a new scroll. Solomon spoke 3,000 proverbs (1 Kgs 4:32), far more than the book contains. This collection has a different flavor from 10:1–22:16. Chapters 25–27 are full of vivid comparisons ("Like… is…"), drawn from weather, farming, metalwork and the household, and many sayings deal with life at the royal court. Chapter 25 opens with God and kings, moves through words fitly spoken, and includes the famous command to feed a hungry enemy, which Paul quotes in Romans 12.

More Proverbs of Solomon: God, Kings and Humility (vv1–7)

  • "These also are proverbs of Solomon which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied" (v1). "Copied" is literally "moved" or "transferred," from one scroll to another. JFB: "transferred from another book," not written from memory. This is a rare glimpse of how part of the Bible was compiled: God-fearing scribes collecting, copying and arranging earlier inspired material. Guzik passes on Poole's suggestion that the "men" might include prophets such as Isaiah and Micah, who lived in Hezekiah's day, though nobody knows.
  • "It is the glory of God to conceal things, but the glory of kings is to search things out" (v2). God's glory includes what he keeps hidden, the depths of his purposes that no human can fathom (Deut 29:29; Rom 11:33). A king's glory is to investigate, to get to the bottom of a case or a problem, as Solomon did in the case of the two mothers (1 Kgs 3:16–28) and as he studied plants and animals (1 Kgs 4:33). Guzik applies it to science: God has hidden wonders in creation, and searching them out is a noble calling. JFB: God's unsearchableness should fill us with awe, and kings, being finite, need counsel. Henry: "it is the honour of rulers to search out matters."
  • "As the heavens for height, and the earth for depth, so the heart of kings is unsearchable" (v3). Ordinary people can't fathom what's in a king's mind. JFB: wise kings keep "state secrets." The Tyndale notes say young men in royal service must accept that some of the king's reasons will be beyond them. Only God searches every heart (Jer 17:10).
  • "Take away the dross from the silver, and the smith has material for a vessel; take away the wicked from the presence of the king, and his throne will be established in righteousness" (vv4–5). Silver refining was a familiar picture (17:3). The impurities rose to the surface of the molten metal and were skimmed off. A court full of corrupt advisers can't produce justice. Remove them and the throne is "established in righteousness" (16:12). David resolved, "No one who practices deceit shall dwell in my house" (Ps 101:7). JFB: removing wicked counselors leaves a "wise and beneficent government."
  • "Do not put yourself forward in the king's presence or stand in the place of the great, for it is better to be told, 'Come up here,' than to be put lower in the presence of a noble" (vv6–7). Court etiquette with a spiritual edge. Don't push yourself into the place of honor. Better to be invited up than sent down. Jesus turned this into a parable about wedding guests: "when you are invited, go and sit in the lowest place, so that… he may say to you, 'Friend, move up higher'" (Luke 14:8–11), and the Tyndale notes add the disciples' quarrel over greatness (Matt 20:20–28). JFB cites Luke 14 as well. Henry: anyone who has seen Christ's glory will "feel his own unworthiness."
  • Commentators: Guzik and the Tyndale notes both mention Hezekiah's scribes and Solomon's 3,000 proverbs. On v2 Guzik passes on Morgan's view that all scientific progress rests on this principle, and Garrett's that the verse honors Solomon and Hezekiah as scholar-kings. All three tie vv6–7 to Jesus' parable.

Going to Court and Keeping Confidences (vv8–10)

  • "What your eyes have seen do not hastily bring into court, for what will you do in the end, when your neighbor puts you to shame?" (v8). The ESV takes the end of v7 ("What your eyes have seen") with v8. You may think you saw clearly what happened, but rushing to court is risky. When the other side tells their story (18:17), you may end up humiliated. JFB: don't rush into a dispute you may lose.
  • "Argue your case with your neighbor himself, and do not reveal another's secret, lest he who hears you bring shame upon you, and your ill repute have no end" (vv9–10). Settle it privately. And in the heat of an argument don't spill someone else's secret to win your point, or you'll earn a reputation as someone who can't be trusted, and it won't go away. Jesus gave the same order of steps: "go and tell him his fault, between you and him alone" (Matt 18:15). Paul rebuked believers for taking each other to court (1 Cor 6:1–8). Henry: "War must at length end, and might better be prevented."
  • Commentators: Guzik links vv8–10 to Matthew 18:15 and passes on Clarke's mocking of the cost and folly of lawsuits. JFB and Henry agree that private settlement is wiser than public conflict.

Apples of Gold and Snow at Harvest (vv11–14)

  • "A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in a setting of silver" (v11). "Fitly" is a rare Hebrew word that may mean "on its wheels," that is, running smoothly in the right circumstances, or "at the right time." JFB: spoken at just the right time. "Apples of gold" may be golden-colored fruit (apricots, citrus or apples) carved in gold, set in silver filigree. The point is beauty and fit: the right word, at the right moment, in the right way. Compare 15:23: "a word in season, how good it is!" Isaiah's Servant says God gave him "the tongue of those who are taught, that I may know how to sustain with a word him who is weary" (Isa 50:4). Henry: such a word, "rightly spoken, is especially beautiful."
  • "Like a gold ring or an ornament of gold is a wise reprover to a listening ear" (v12). A rebuke can be jewelry, but only if the ear is listening. Wise correction plus a teachable heart is a beautiful combination. JFB: those who want to do right value good counsel most.
  • "Like the cold of snow in the time of harvest is a faithful messenger to those who send him; he refreshes the soul of his masters" (v13). Snow didn't fall at harvest in Israel's heat. The picture is snow carried down from Mount Hermon or Lebanon and stored to chill drinks, a rare luxury. JFB: snow from the mountains cooling a drink. 26:1 says snow at harvest would be out of place, so this is about the coolness of a snow-chilled drink. A reliable messenger who gets the job done is that refreshing (13:17). Paul said Stephanas, Fortunatus and Achaicus "refreshed my spirit" (1 Cor 16:17–18), and Epaphroditus risked his life to bring the Philippians' gift (Phil 2:25–30).
  • "Like clouds and wind without rain is a man who boasts of a gift he does not give" (v14). In a dry land, clouds and wind raised hope of rain. When none came, the disappointment was bitter. So is a person who promises generosity and never delivers. Jude uses the same image for false teachers: "waterless clouds, swept along by winds" (Jude 12), as the Tyndale notes observe. Guzik gives Ananias and Sapphira, who wanted credit for a gift bigger than the one they gave (Acts 5:1–11). Henry: he "disappoints those who look for rain."
  • Commentators: Guzik passes on Trapp's rendering of v11, a word "spoken upon his wheels," well-ordered and timely. JFB suggests golden apples in a silver basket or silver embroidery. The Tyndale notes say good advice and fair criticism are beautiful and rare.

Patience, Honey and Wearing Out a Welcome (vv15–20)

  • "With patience a ruler may be persuaded, and a soft tongue will break a bone" (v15). A paradox: something soft breaks something hard. Patient, gentle words can change a ruler's mind where force fails. Gideon's soft answer turned away Ephraim's anger (Judg 8:1–3). Abigail's gentle plea stopped David's sword (1 Sam 25:23–33). Compare 15:1. JFB: gentleness can overcome powerful, stubborn opposition. Guzik points to William Wilberforce's patient decades-long campaign to end the British slave trade.
  • "If you have found honey, eat only enough for you, lest you have your fill of it and vomit it" (v16). Honey is good (24:13), but too much makes you sick. Good things need limits. Henry: God lets us enjoy good things but "cautioned against excess." Guzik passes on Kidner's line that ever since Eden people have wanted more than God's "enough."
  • "Let your foot be seldom in your neighbor's house, lest he have his fill of you and hate you" (v17). The same verb "have one's fill" ties v17 to v16. Too much of a good neighbor is like too much honey. Friendship needs space. Guzik passes on an old Hebrew saying: on the first day a guest, the second a burden, the third a nuisance. Henry adds the contrast with God: "The oftener we come to him, the more welcome."
  • "A man who bears false witness against his neighbor is like a war club, or a sword, or a sharp arrow" (v18). Three weapons: one that crushes up close, one that cuts at arm's length, and one that strikes from far away. A lying witness can destroy a person at any distance. Guzik passes on Waltke's point about the three ranges. JFB: a false witness harms reputation as weapons harm the body.
  • "Trusting in a treacherous man in time of trouble is like a bad tooth or a foot that slips" (v19). A rotten tooth hurts when you bite. A sprained or wobbly foot gives way when you step. Relying on an unfaithful person fails exactly when you need it. JFB: treachery in a crisis "annoys as well as deceives." Henry: such a person "not only fails, but makes us feel for it."
  • "Whoever sings songs to a heavy heart is like one who takes off a garment on a cold day, and like vinegar on soda" (v20). "Soda" is natron, a natural mineral used for washing. Pour vinegar on it and it fizzes and is ruined. Forced cheerfulness to someone grieving doesn't help. It irritates and makes things worse. Paul: "weep with those who weep" (Rom 12:15). Job's friends did best in the first seven days, when they sat silently with him (Job 2:13). JFB: sadness deepens for lack of sympathy. Henry: "We take a wrong course" in this.
  • Commentators: Guzik and JFB both read v15 as gentleness overcoming stubbornness. The Tyndale notes apply the honey of v16 broadly, as Guzik and Poole do, to other good things. Guzik passes on Bridges' point that slander wounds four: the speaker, the victim, the hearers and God's name.

Coals of Fire and Good News (vv21–28)

  • "If your enemy is hungry, give him bread to eat, and if he is thirsty, give him water to drink, for you will heap burning coals on his head, and the LORD will reward you" (vv21–22). One of the Old Testament's clearest commands to love an enemy, and Paul quotes it word for word in Romans 12:20, adding "Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good" (Rom 12:21). Elisha fed the Syrian army God had delivered into his hands, and the raids stopped (2 Kgs 6:21–23). What "burning coals" means is debated. (1) The burning shame and remorse of receiving kindness from someone you wronged, which can lead to repentance. Augustine and Jerome took this view, and many still do. (2) Some scholars point to an Egyptian ritual in which a penitent carried a pan of burning coals on his head to show repentance. (3) Some suggest a neighborly act of giving coals to someone whose fire had gone out. JFB uses the picture of coals melting metal: kindness softens the heart. All views agree that the goal is not to hurt the enemy but to win him. Henry: "The precept to love even our enemies is an Old Testament commandment," and Christ kept it by loving us while we were his enemies (Rom 5:10).
  • "The north wind brings forth rain, and a backbiting tongue, angry looks" (v23). A puzzle, since in Israel rain usually comes from the west, off the Mediterranean, and the north wind is usually dry. Some read "brings forth" as "drives away," or think of a northwest wind. Whatever the weather, the second line is clear: secret slander produces angry faces. JFB: "a concealed or slandering tongue" brings anger. Henry adds that slander dies when people refuse to listen.
  • "It is better to live in a corner of the housetop than in a house shared with a quarrelsome wife" (v24). A word-for-word repeat of 21:9, the sort of repetition the Tyndale notes list across the book..
  • "Like cold water to a thirsty soul, so is good news from a far country" (v25). In a world where news traveled by foot and could take months, word from a faraway son or friend was a deep refreshment. Jacob's spirit revived when he heard Joseph was alive in Egypt (Gen 45:27). Henry applies it to the gospel, good news from heaven. Guzik makes the same application.
  • "Like a muddied spring or a polluted fountain is a righteous man who gives way before the wicked" (v26). In dry country a spring was life to a whole village, and to foul one was a disaster (Ezek 34:18–19). When a righteous person caves in to the wicked, whether by compromise or fear, those who depended on him lose their source. Guzik passes on Trapp's examples: Abraham lying about Sarah before Abimelech (Gen 20), Samson among the Philistines, and Peter's denial. The Tyndale notes: what was once life-giving becomes harmful. JFB reads it as the righteous oppressed by the wicked so that "their power for good is lessened or destroyed."
  • "It is not good to eat much honey, nor is it glorious to seek one's own glory" (v27). Honey again, linking back to v16. The second line is hard in Hebrew and is read several ways; the ESV's is common. Seeking glory for yourself is like eating too much honey: it ends in sickness. Compare 27:2: "Let another praise you, and not your own mouth." Jesus: "I do not seek my own glory" (John 8:50). JFB notes the alternative reading "is grievous."
  • "A man without self-control is like a city broken into and left without walls" (v28). A city's walls were its whole defense. Without them, any enemy could walk in. A person who can't rule their own spirit, temper and appetites is open to every attack. 16:32 says the opposite: "he who rules his spirit [is better] than he who takes a city." Paul lists self-control as fruit of the Spirit (Gal 5:23). Henry: "The man who has no command over his anger, is easily robbed of peace." JFB: such people are exposed to "successful temptations."
  • Commentators: Guzik gives both the shame reading and the neighborly-coals reading of v22, and passes on Clarke's metal-smelting picture, which matches JFB. The Tyndale notes say kindness works better than anger. Henry and Guzik both read v25 as the gospel.

Pointing to Christ

Proverbs 25 says it is God's glory to conceal things (v2), yet in Christ the hidden things have been revealed: "the mystery hidden for ages and generations but now revealed to his saints" (Col 1:26). Jesus is the humble one who did not put himself forward but "made himself nothing," and so was told "Come up here" and exalted to the highest place (vv6–7; Phil 2:7–9). His words were the word "fitly spoken" (v11) that sustains the weary (Isa 50:4), and he is the faithful messenger who refreshes the soul of the One who sent him (v13; John 4:34). Above all, he obeyed vv21–22 perfectly. While we were his enemies, he fed us and gave us living water (John 4:10; 6:35), and he overcame our evil with good by dying for us (Rom 5:8–10). The gospel itself is "good news from a far country" (v25), cold water to the thirsty soul.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik notes the significance of v.1: Hezekiah's men "copied out" these proverbs — showing that Scripture preservation involved human effort guided by divine providence. On v.11, he explains: "'Apples of gold in settings of silver' — the right word is both inherently beautiful (gold) and perfectly framed (silver setting). Content and context both matter." On v.28, he emphasizes that ancient cities without walls were abandoned as indefensible — so the person without self-control is practically ruined.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Take the Lower Seat (vv.6-7) — Better to be invited up in front of others than to claim honor for yourself and be sent back down. Where are you tempted to promote yourself instead of letting your position come from others?
  • A Word Rightly Spoken (v.11) — Fitting words are compared to gold set in silver — valuable because they're both true and well-timed. When was the last time you got the content right but the timing or tone wrong?
  • The Danger of Too Much of a Good Thing (vv.16-17) — Even honey, something sweet and good, becomes sickening in excess, and the same is true of overstaying your welcome with a neighbor. Where do you need to practice restraint with something that's good in moderation but harmful in excess?
  • Active Kindness Toward an Enemy (vv.21-22) — Feeding a hungry enemy is more than withholding revenge — it's deliberate goodness that can soften a hardened heart. Who's someone who's wronged you that you could actively bless instead of merely avoid?
  • A City Without Walls (v.28) — A person who lacks self-control is compared to a city with broken-down defenses, exposed on every side. Where in your life — anger, spending, appetite — are your walls down?
