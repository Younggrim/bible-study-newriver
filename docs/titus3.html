Summary

Paul instructs Titus to remind believers to submit to civil authorities, stay ready for good work, and treat everyone with gentleness, recalling that they themselves were once foolish, disobedient, and enslaved to sin (vv. 1-3). But when God's kindness and love appeared, he saved them — not because of their own righteous deeds but because of his mercy — through the washing of new birth and renewal by the Holy Spirit, poured out generously through Jesus Christ (vv. 4-6). Justified by grace, believers become heirs with hope of eternal life, and Paul wants Titus to insist on this so that those who believe stay devoted to good works (vv. 7-8). He warns Titus to avoid foolish arguments, genealogies, and quarrels about the law, and to reject a divisive person after two warnings (vv. 9-11). Paul closes with travel plans, instructions to help Zenas and Apollos, a reminder to keep learning good works, and final greetings and a blessing of grace (vv. 12-15).

Authorship & Background

Author: Paul the Apostle (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 3 concludes Titus with instructions about civic responsibility (vv.1-2), a magnificent summary of salvation (vv.3-7), the importance of good works (v.8), warnings about divisive people (vv.9-11), and personal instructions (vv.12-15). Verses 3-7 are considered one of the clearest and most beautiful summaries of the gospel in all of Paul's writings.
Classification: Epistle — Pauline, Pastoral Epistle
Key Themes: Obedience to pagan magistrates commanded rather than debated, a former life catalogued without excuse, salvation by mercy against a works clause it explicitly rules out, the Spirit poured out and renewal named as regeneration, and a letter that ends in errands and unfinished travel plans
Civic Responsibility (vv.1-2): Be subject to authorities, obey, be ready for good works, speak evil of no one, be gentle and meek toward all.
The Gospel Summary (vv.3-7): "We ourselves also were" — foolish, disobedient, deceived, enslaved (v.3). "But" — God's kindness and love appeared (v.4). "Not by works" — but by mercy, through regeneration and the Spirit's renewal (v.5). The Spirit poured out abundantly through Christ (v.6). Justified by grace, made heirs of eternal life (v.7).
Good Works and Divisive People (vv.8-11): Believers should "maintain good works" (v.8). Avoid foolish controversies (v.9). Warn a divisive person twice; then reject him (vv.10-11).
Travel Plans and Final Greeting (vv.12-15): The letter ends in logistics, which is where the shape of the work shows. Artemas or Tychicus will be sent so that Titus can leave Crete and come to Nicopolis for the winter, meaning the man is not being left there permanently. Zenas the lawyer and Apollos are to be sent on their journey diligently, that nothing be wanting unto them. Then the instruction that ties the arrangements back to the letter's argument, let ours also learn to maintain good works for necessary uses, that they be not unfruitful. Grace be with you all.

Deep Dive

Setting: The last chapter of Paul's letter to Titus, whom he had left on Crete to put the young churches in order (1:5), probably around AD 62–64 during the travels that most readers place between Paul's two Roman imprisonments. (Scholars who doubt Paul wrote the Pastoral Letters date them later and do not accept this reconstruction.) Chapter 1 dealt with leaders and false teachers, and chapter 2 with how each member of a Christian household should live, grounded in "the grace of God" that "has appeared" (2:11). Chapter 3 widens the circle to how believers should live toward outsiders, especially rulers and unbelieving neighbors, and grounds that too in the gospel, in a dense Trinitarian summary of salvation (vv4–7). Then come final warnings about divisive people and a handful of travel plans and names. A theme runs through all three chapters and comes to rest here: we are saved not by good works (v5) but for them (vv1, 8, 14).

Be Ready for Every Good Work (vv1–11)

  • "Remind them" (v1). The Greek tense means "keep reminding." None of this is new. Christians forget the basics more often than they lack information.
  • "Submissive to rulers and authorities, to be obedient" (v1). Crete was a Roman province (joined to Cyrene in North Africa), and its people had a long reputation for unruliness (1:10–12). Paul says the same in Romans 13:1–7, and Peter in 1 Peter 2:13–17. This is not blind obedience: the apostles also said, "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). But the ordinary posture of believers toward government is respect and cooperation, partly so that the gospel is not dismissed as a movement of troublemakers.
  • "Ready for every good work" (v1). Obedience by itself could be passive, just keeping out of trouble. Paul wants Christians to be the people in town who are known for doing good. The false teachers were "unfit for any good work" (1:16). Real believers are the opposite.
  • Toward everyone (v2). "Speak evil of no one" uses the verb blasphēmeō, to slander or speak abusively. "Avoid quarreling," "be gentle," "show perfect courtesy toward all people." "Gentle" is epieikēs: fair, reasonable, not insisting on every right. It is not weakness: Paul speaks of "the meekness and gentleness of Christ" (2 Cor 10:1), using the noun from the same root. "Perfect courtesy" is literally "all meekness" (prautēs), the quality Jesus claimed for himself (Matt 11:29). "All people" includes pagan neighbors, Roman officials and, by implication, the hostile.
  • "For we ourselves were once" (v3). Why be gentle with difficult outsiders? Because we were once just like them. Paul includes himself in seven descriptions: "foolish" (without understanding), "disobedient," "led astray," "slaves to various passions and pleasures," "passing our days in malice and envy," "hated by others and hating one another." It is a bleak picture of life without God, and Paul paints it so that Christians cannot look down on anyone (Eph 2:1–3; 1 Cor 6:9–11).
  • "But when the goodness and loving kindness of God our Savior appeared" (v4). The great turn. "Goodness" (chrēstotēs) is the kindness that leads to repentance (Rom 2:4). "Loving kindness" is philanthrōpia, "love for humankind," the source of "philanthropy." The only other New Testament use describes the islanders of Malta who welcomed shipwrecked Paul (Acts 28:2). Greek writers praised it as a virtue of good kings and gods. Paul says it is what God showed. "Appeared" is the same word as in 2:11: God's kindness dawned in the coming of Jesus.
  • One long sentence (vv4–7). In Greek, verses 4–7 are a single sentence, and it is Trinitarian: God the Father, "our Savior," saved us (vv4–5); by the renewal of "the Holy Spirit" (v5); whom he poured out "through Jesus Christ our Savior" (v6). The title "Savior" passes from the Father to the Son, as it does throughout this letter (1:3–4; 2:10, 13). The compact, balanced phrasing has led many scholars to think Paul is quoting or summarizing an early Christian confession, which v8 calls "the saying."
  • "He saved us, not because of works done by us in righteousness, but according to his own mercy" (v5). Not even our good works, done "in righteousness," are the reason. The cause lies entirely in God's mercy. This is Paul's gospel in every letter (Eph 2:8–9; Rom 3:20–24; 2 Tim 1:9). And it is the reason for v2's gentleness: people saved by mercy alone cannot treat others as beneath them.
  • "By the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit" (v5). "Washing" is loutron, a bath or washing, used elsewhere only in Ephesians 5:26 ("the washing of water with the word"). "Regeneration" is palingenesia, "birth again." Stoic philosophers used it for the rebirth of the universe after its periodic destruction by fire, and Jesus used it for the renewal of all things at the end (Matt 19:28, "the new world"). Here it is personal: each believer's new birth is a foretaste of the world's. "Renewal" (anakainōsis) is found only here and in Romans 12:2 ("the renewal of your mind"). Many read regeneration as the one-time new birth and renewal as its lifelong continuation, though the grammar can also take both as one act of the Spirit. The background is Ezekiel 36:25–27: "I will sprinkle clean water on you… And I will put my Spirit within you" (compare John 3:5).
  • Is this baptism? Christians have read the "washing" differently. Catholic, Orthodox and Lutheran traditions, following most early church writers, see baptism here as the means by which God gives new birth, which is God's work, not ours, so it fits "not because of works." Many Anglicans and classic Reformed readers see baptism as the sign and seal of an inward washing that the Spirit performs, closely joined to it but not tied to the moment of the water. Baptists and many evangelicals read the washing as the Spirit's inner cleansing, which baptism later pictures and confesses. Notice that Paul does not name baptism, and that in the sentence the Holy Spirit, not water, is the one who acts. All sides agree that the source is God's mercy, not human effort.
  • "Whom he poured out on us richly" (v6). "Poured out" is the language of Joel 2:28 ("I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh"), which Peter said was fulfilled at Pentecost (Acts 2:17, 33). "Richly" means generously, not measured out in drops (Rom 5:5).
  • "So that being justified by his grace we might become heirs" (v7). "Justified" is a law-court word: declared right with God. It is "by his grace," a free gift (Rom 3:24). And the goal is family: "heirs according to the hope of eternal life," which takes the letter back to its opening, the hope "which God, who never lies, promised before the ages began" (1:2; Rom 8:17).
  • "The saying is trustworthy" (v8). A formula found only in the Pastoral Letters (1 Tim 1:15; 3:1; 4:9; 2 Tim 2:11), marking a well-known statement that can be relied on. Here it points back to vv4–7. "I want you to insist on these things," Paul says, and the purpose is practical: "that those who have believed in God may be careful to devote themselves to good works." Saved by mercy, and for good works (Eph 2:10).
  • "These things are excellent and profitable for people" (v8). Sound teaching and good deeds do real good, in contrast to what comes next, which is "unprofitable and worthless" (v9).
  • "Avoid foolish controversies, genealogies, dissensions, and quarrels about the law" (v9). This is the false teaching of 1:10–16 again: speculation built on Old Testament family trees and arguments about the law. Paul does not say debate is always wrong; he argued with people constantly (Acts 17:17). He says these debates produce nothing, and arguing over them is easier than doing good.
  • "A person who stirs up division" (v10). The word is hairetikos, from which "heretic" comes. Here it means factious, a person who chooses his own line and gathers a party around it (compare "divisions," Gal 5:20; 1 Cor 11:19). Its later sense of someone who holds a condemned doctrine grew from this, but the stress here is on behavior that splits the church.
  • "After warning him once and then twice, have nothing more to do with him" (v10). "Warning" is nouthesia, literally "putting in mind," admonition aimed at changing someone's thinking. The pattern echoes Jesus in Matthew 18:15–17: patient correction, more than once, and then separation if the person will not listen. "Have nothing more to do with him" means stop arguing with him and stop giving him a platform, not treat him cruelly.
  • "Warped and sinful; he is self-condemned" (v11). "Warped" (ekstrephō) means turned inside out, twisted. "Self-condemned" (autokatakritos) is found nowhere earlier in Greek. After repeated warnings, the person's continuing choice is his own verdict on himself. The church does not have to win the argument to be faithful.
  • Commentators: All three agree on the heart of vv4–7: salvation flows from mercy, not merit, and good works are its fruit. JFB on v3: "Our own past sins should lead us to be lenient towards those of others." Henry on v4: "God's pity and mercy to man in misery were the first wheel," and on v7, "The meanest believer is a great heir." On v5 they differ. Henry says baptism saves "figuratively and sacramentally, where it is rightly used," and JFB calls the washing the baptismal font, "the visible instrument of regeneration," adding that "regeneration is a thing once for all done; renewing is a process daily proceeding." Guzik argues instead that Paul does not name baptism, that the only other use of the word (Eph 5:26) is cleansing by the word, and that the washing is an inward work of God that baptism publicly confesses. The lexicon gives loutron as "washing" or "bath," not "font," so JFB's "laver" is his interpretation. On v10, JFB says the word "originally meant a division resulting from individual self-will," which matches the lexicon, and that rejecting means "not formal excommunication." Henry warns against throwing the label "heretic" around rashly. JFB on v11: "He cannot say, no one told him better."

Final Instructions and Greetings (vv12–15)

  • "When I send Artemas or Tychicus to you" (v12). Titus's role on Crete was temporary. One of these two would replace him. Artemas appears nowhere else. Tychicus, from the province of Asia, carried Ephesians and Colossians and was one of Paul's most trusted messengers (Acts 20:4; Eph 6:21–22; Col 4:7). Paul later sent him to Ephesus (2 Tim 4:12), which may mean Artemas was the one who went to Crete.
  • "Come to me at Nicopolis, for I have decided to spend the winter there" (v12). "Nicopolis" means "victory city," and several cities had the name. Most think this was Nicopolis in Epirus, on the west coast of Greece, founded by Augustus to celebrate his victory at Actium in 31 BC. Sea travel largely stopped in winter, roughly November to March, so Paul planned where to wait it out. "There" shows Paul had not yet arrived, so the old note in some Bibles saying the letter was written from Nicopolis is mistaken. From Epirus the road ran north toward Dalmatia, and later Titus went there (2 Tim 4:10).
  • "Zenas the lawyer and Apollos" (v13). Zenas is named only here. "Lawyer" (nomikos) in the Gospels means an expert in the Jewish law (Luke 10:25), but his name is Greek ("gift of Zeus"), so he may have been a Roman jurist. Commentators divide. Apollos is the eloquent Alexandrian Jew who knew the Scriptures well (Acts 18:24–28) and whose name some Corinthians made a party slogan (1 Cor 1:12; 3:4–6). Years later Paul is still sending him on his way and caring for him. They were partners, not rivals. The two men probably carried this letter to Crete.
  • "Speed… on their way; see that they lack nothing" (v13). "Speed on their way" (propempō) was the standard term for equipping travelling missionaries with food, money and sometimes companions (Rom 15:24; 3 John 6–8). Hospitality on the elder list (1:8) becomes concrete here.
  • "Let our people learn to devote themselves to good works" (v14). The letter's refrain one last time, now very practical: "to help cases of urgent need, and not be unfruitful." Good works are not only private virtue. They are money and food and help for people who need it, including gospel workers on the road. Cretans had been called "lazy gluttons" (1:12). Grace makes them generous and fruitful.
  • "All who are with me send greetings… Grace be with you all" (v15). The "you" is plural. The letter was addressed to Titus, but it was always meant for the whole church to hear. It ends, as every letter of Paul's ends, with grace.
  • Commentators: Henry draws the point about Nicopolis from the word "there." JFB places it in Epirus, "the city of victory," named from Actium, and thinks Paul wrote from Corinth in the autumn. On Zenas, JFB thinks he was a converted Jewish scribe who kept the title, while the Tyndale notes lean toward a Roman legal expert and Henry leaves the question open. JFB reads the good works of v14 as supplying "the necessary wants of Christian missionaries and brethren." Guzik draws out Paul's real friendships in the names, and notes how often this short letter returns to fruitfulness and good works.

Pointing to Christ

The chapter's center is a portrait of grace: people who were foolish, enslaved and hateful, met by "the goodness and loving kindness of God our Savior." That kindness has a name. It appeared in Jesus Christ, through whom the Spirit is poured out and by whose grace sinners are declared righteous and made heirs. Everything else in the chapter, gentle words to hostile neighbors, patience with the divisive, provisions packed for travelling preachers, is what that kindness looks like when it lives on in his people.

Reflection

  • Living Peaceably Under Authority (vv.1-2) — Paul instructs believers to submit to authorities, avoid slander, and show gentleness to everyone. How do you talk about people in leadership you disagree with, even in private?
  • Remembering What You Once Were (v.3) — Paul reminds Titus that believers themselves were once foolish, disobedient, and enslaved to their own desires. Does remembering where you came from make you more patient with people who are still there?
  • Saved by Mercy, Not Merit (v.5) — Paul is emphatic that salvation came through God's mercy and the Spirit's renewal, not anything believers accomplished. Are you still quietly trying to earn what God already gave you freely?
  • Made Heirs of Eternal Life (v.7) — Paul says believers are justified by grace and made heirs with a confident hope of eternal life. Do you actually live with the confidence of someone who has that inheritance secured?
  • Avoiding Pointless Arguments (v.9) — Paul tells Titus to steer clear of foolish controversies and disputes that produce nothing useful. What arguments or debates are eating up your time and energy without moving anything forward?
