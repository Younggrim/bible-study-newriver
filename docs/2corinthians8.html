1Now, brothers, we want you to know about the grace that God has given the churches of Macedonia.

2In the terrible ordeal they suffered, their abundant joy and deep poverty overflowed into rich generosity.

3For I testify that they gave according to their ability and even beyond it. Of their own accord,

4they earnestly pleaded with us for the privilege of sharing in this service to the saints.

5And not only did they do as we expected, but they gave themselves first to the Lord and then to us, through the will of God.

6So we urged Titus to help complete your act of grace, just as he had started it.

7But just as you excel in everything—in faith, in speech, in knowledge, in complete earnestness, and in the love we inspired in you—see that you also excel in this grace of giving.

8I am not giving a command, but I am testing the sincerity of your love through the earnestness of others.

9For you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though He was rich, yet for your sake He became poor, so that you through His poverty might become rich.

10And this is my opinion about what is helpful for you in this matter: Last year you were the first not only to give, but even to have such a desire.

11Now finish the work, so that you may complete it with the same eager desire, according to your means.

12For if the eagerness is there, the gift is acceptable according to what one has, not according to what he does not have.

13It is not our intention that others may be relieved while you are burdened, but that there may be equality.

14At the present time, your surplus will meet their need, so that in turn their surplus will meet your need. This way there will be equality.

15As it is written: “He who gathered much had no excess, and he who gathered little had no shortfall.”

16But thanks be to God, who put into the heart of Titus the same devotion I have for you.

17For not only did he welcome our appeal, but he is eagerly coming to you of his own volition.

18Along with Titus we are sending the brother who is praised by all the churches for his work in the gospel.

19More than that, this brother was chosen by the churches to accompany us with the gracious offering we administer to honor the Lord Himself and to show our eagerness to help.

20We want to avoid any criticism of the way we administer this generous gift.

21For we are taking great care to do what is right, not only in the eyes of the Lord, but also in the eyes of men.

22And we are sending along with them our brother who has proven his earnestness to us many times and in many ways, and now even more so by his great confidence in you.

23As for Titus, he is my partner and fellow worker among you. As for our brothers, they are messengers of the churches, the glory of Christ.

24In full view of the churches, then, show these men the proof of your love and the reason for our boasting about you.

Summary

Paul highlights the Macedonian churches as an example of generosity, noting that despite severe trial and deep poverty, their overflowing joy produced abundant giving, even beyond their means and entirely of their own will (vv. 1-5). He urges Titus to help the Corinthians complete the same grace of giving they had begun, since they already excel in faith, speech, knowledge, and love (vv. 6-7). Paul points to Christ himself as the ultimate model of generosity, who though rich became poor for their sake so they might become rich (v. 9). He advises them to follow through on their earlier willingness, giving according to what they have, so that abundance and need are balanced among believers, as with Israel's manna (vv. 10-15). Finally, Paul commends Titus and two other trusted brothers who will carry the gift, taking care to handle the funds with full transparency before both God and people (vv. 16-24).

Authorship & Background

Author: Paul (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapters 8-9 address the collection for the poor believers in Jerusalem — the most extended teaching on Christian giving in the NT. Paul uses the Macedonian churches' example (vv.1-5), Christ's example (v.9), and practical principles (vv.10-24) to motivate generous giving.
Classification: Epistle — Pauline
Key Themes: Macedonian churches giving beyond their power out of deep poverty rather than surplus, giving themselves to the Lord named as the act that precedes giving their money, Christ's own poverty offered as the pattern for a collection rather than as an illustration, willingness measured against ability rather than against a fixed amount, and a delegation of several men arranged so no one could blame Paul over money he never touched alone
The Macedonian Example (vv.1-5): The churches of Macedonia (Philippi, Thessalonica, Berea) gave generously despite "deep poverty" (v.2). They gave "beyond their power" (v.3) and "first gave their own selves to the Lord" (v.5). Their giving flowed from self-surrender — they gave themselves before they gave their money.
Proving the Sincerity of Your Love (vv.6-8): Titus is sent to finish what was started, that he would also finish in you the same grace. The appeal is framed as consistency rather than duty, as ye abound in every thing, in faith, and utterance, and knowledge, see that ye abound in this grace also. Then a careful disclaimer that shapes the whole chapter, I speak not by commandment, but by occasion of the forwardness of others, and to prove the sincerity of your love. Giving is treated as evidence, and it is not being ordered.
Christ's Example (v.9): "Though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, that ye through his poverty might be rich." The ultimate model of generosity — Christ left heaven's riches to become poor so we could become rich. The Incarnation is the greatest act of giving in history.
Principles of Giving (vv.10-15): Give willingly (v.12), proportionally (v.12), and with equality in mind (vv.13-14). God doesn't ask for what you don't have — He asks for willingness with what you do have.
Titus and the Two Brethren (vv.16-24): The end of the chapter is administrative and unusually careful. Titus goes, and with him a brother whose praise is in the gospel throughout all the churches, chosen by the churches themselves to travel with the money, and a third man besides. The reason is given without embarrassment: avoiding this, that no man should blame us in this abundance, providing for honest things, not only in the sight of the Lord, but also in the sight of men. A collection is handled by a committee nobody appointed from Paul's own circle, and he says plainly why.

Deep Dive

Setting: Paul writes from Macedonia (northern Greece) in about AD 55–56. Titus has just brought good news: most of the Corinthian church has repented and its love for Paul has been renewed (7:6–16). With the relationship mended, Paul turns to unfinished business. For several years he had been gathering a gift from his mostly Gentile churches for the poor believers in Jerusalem (Gal 2:10; Rom 15:25–27). He had told the Corinthians how to set money aside week by week (1 Cor 16:1–4), and they had started eagerly a year earlier, but the quarrel with Paul seems to have stalled it. Chapters 8–9 are the longest teaching on giving in the New Testament. They are also an argument for unity, because a gift from Greek churches to the Jewish mother church would show that Jew and Gentile were one body. Remarkably, Paul never uses the ordinary words for money. He calls it a grace, a fellowship, a service and a blessing.

Encouragement to Give Generously (vv1–15)

  • "The grace of God that has been given among the churches of Macedonia" (v1). Paul does not begin with what the Macedonians did but with what God did in them. The word is charis, "grace," and it runs all through these chapters. The ESV renders it "grace" (vv1, 6, 7, 9), "favor" (v4), "thanks" (v16) and "act of grace" (v19). Generosity is pictured as God's gift working in people, before it is people's gift to others.
  • The churches of Macedonia. These are Philippi, Thessalonica and Berea, whose founding Luke tells in Acts 16–17. All three were born in trouble. Paul was beaten and jailed in Philippi, and mobs drove him out of Thessalonica and Berea. The Thessalonians "received the word in much affliction" (1 Thess 1:6), and the Philippians had supported Paul's work again and again from the start (Phil 4:15–16). Macedonia had been stripped by Roman wars and heavy taxation, and was much poorer than Achaia, the southern province where Corinth was a wealthy commercial city.
  • "A severe test of affliction… extreme poverty… a wealth of generosity" (v2). This verse is built on surprising combinations: affliction with joy, deep poverty with rich generosity. "Extreme poverty" is literally poverty "down to the depth." "Generosity" is haplotēs, whose root sense is singleness or simplicity. It describes an undivided heart that gives without calculating or holding back, and the lexicon lists "liberality" as its meaning here and in 9:11, 13.
  • "According to their means… and beyond their means, of their own accord" (v3). Paul vouches for it personally ("as I can testify"). They gave what they could, and then more than they could, and no one had to ask. Jesus praised the widow who gave two small coins "out of her poverty" (Luke 21:1–4). The Macedonians are a whole church doing the same.
  • "Begging us earnestly for the favor of taking part" (v4). The usual roles are reversed. The poor are pleading with the apostle to be allowed to give. Paul seems to have hesitated to ask them at all, given their poverty. "Taking part" is koinōnia, the word for fellowship. Romans 15:26 uses the same word for this collection, rendered "contribution." To give was to share in the life of the Jerusalem church. "Relief" is diakonia, "service" or "ministry."
  • "They gave themselves first to the Lord and then by the will of God to us" (v5). "Not as we expected" means they went beyond anything Paul had hoped. Before any coins changed hands, the Macedonians had handed themselves over to Christ, and then to Paul's leadership in this work. The money followed the surrender.
  • "We urged Titus… so he should complete among you this act of grace" (v6). Titus had started the collection in Corinth on an earlier visit, probably when he carried the "tearful letter" (7:6–8). Now he will go back, carrying this letter, to finish it.
  • "As you excel in everything… see that you excel in this act of grace also" (v7). The Corinthians prized spiritual gifts: speech, knowledge, faith (1 Cor 1:5–7). Paul names their strengths sincerely, but he adds one they are missing. Giving belongs on the list of spiritual gifts (Rom 12:8). A church rich in knowledge and poor in generosity has not yet understood love (1 Cor 13:2–3).
  • "I say this not as a command" (v8). Paul does not set an amount or impose a tax. Love that is ordered is not proven. The Macedonians' eagerness is a test that lets the Corinthians show, freely, that their love is real.
  • "Though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor" (v9). This is the heart of the chapter, and one of the great short statements of the gospel. "Rich" points to the glory the Son shared with the Father before the world existed (John 17:5). "Became poor" (ptōcheuō, used only here in the New Testament) means to become poor as a beggar. It covers the whole downward road of Philippians 2:6–8: the incarnation, a life with "nowhere to lay his head" (Matt 8:20), and the cross. "Become rich" means rich in every blessing of salvation, which Paul has just described as glory, righteousness and a new creation (3:9; 5:17–21). The argument is quiet but strong. Paul does not ask the Corinthians to match Christ's gift. He reminds them of it, and lets it reshape how they hold their money. Readers have long noted that this verse assumes Christ existed and was "rich" before he became poor, so it quietly confesses his pre-existence.
  • "In this matter I give my judgment" (v10). Again advice, not command. "A year ago" shows that this letter comes at least a year after 1 Corinthians 16. The Corinthians were first not only to act but to want to act, so finishing is in their own interest. An unfinished pledge does a church no good.
  • "Out of what you have" (vv11–12). Readiness must become completion, but the measure is what a person has, "not according to what he does not have." God does not ask anyone to give what they do not own or to go into debt to give. A small gift from a willing heart is fully "acceptable." This guards against two errors: guilt that demands the impossible, and good intentions that never reach the bank.
  • "That there may be fairness" (vv13–14). Paul is not asking the Corinthians to impoverish themselves so that Jerusalem can live comfortably. The word isotēs means "equality" or "fairness." The idea is mutual care across time: today Corinth has plenty and Jerusalem has need, and someday it may be the other way around. Paul may also mean that Jerusalem already supplies a richer kind of abundance, since the Gentiles have shared in their spiritual blessings (Rom 15:27). This is voluntary sharing between churches, not a state program. Christians have drawn very different economic conclusions from these verses, but the text itself is about believers freely meeting one another's real needs.
  • "Whoever gathered much had nothing left over" (v15). Paul quotes Exodus 16:18, the manna in the wilderness. Some Israelites gathered more and some less, but when it was measured out, everyone had what they needed, and hoarded manna rotted overnight (Exod 16:20). God provided enough for the whole people. Paul applies the lesson: surplus in one part of God's family is meant to meet need in another.
  • Commentators: Guzik stresses that grace means both the opportunity and the willingness to give, and that the Macedonians saw giving as a privilege they begged for. He reads v9 as plain evidence of Christ's deity and pre-existence. He notes the New Testament never directly commands a tithe, but he treats ten percent as a useful benchmark, while granting that a smaller step may be a real sacrifice for some. Henry calls the Macedonians' self-giving the right order: first themselves, then their money. On vv13–15 he sees Providence spreading wealth unevenly so that the abundance of some may supply the wants of others, aiming at "some sort of equality," not a forced leveling of property. JFB is close to Henry: "Their liberality was not of themselves naturally, but of God's grace bestowed on them," and "The greater was the depth of their poverty, the greater was the abundance of their joy." On v9 it adds the balancing note that Christ's example is not a demand that the Corinthians beggar themselves. On v13 it puts it simply: "Love thy neighbour as thyself," not more than thyself. JFB also suggests the main Macedonian givers were the Philippians, the church that had supported Paul from the start.

Commendation of Titus (vv16–24)

  • "Thanks be to God, who put into the heart of Titus the same earnest care" (v16). "Thanks" is charis again. Even a coworker's zeal is God's gift. Titus is a Greek believer whom Paul once took to Jerusalem as a test case, and the apostles there did not require him to be circumcised (Gal 2:1–3). It is fitting that he carries a Gentile gift to Jewish believers.
  • "He is going to you of his own accord" (v17). Paul asked, but Titus needed no pushing. He had just seen the Corinthians' repentance with his own eyes, and his affection for them had grown (7:15). The ESV's "is going" translates a Greek past tense. Paul writes from the point of view of the readers, for whom Titus will already have set out by the time the letter is read aloud.
  • "The brother who is famous among all the churches for his preaching of the gospel" (v18). Paul does not name him. His fame was in "the gospel," which may mean his preaching or his service to the gospel more broadly. Guesses include Luke, Barnabas, Silas, Apollos and Aristarchus. An old tradition favors Luke, and some even took "the gospel" here to mean his written Gospel, but that is almost certainly too early and reads a later meaning into the word. Most scholars conclude that we cannot know. The Corinthians knew, and that was enough.
  • "Appointed by the churches" (v19). The verb is cheirotoneō, originally "to vote by a show of hands" in a Greek assembly. The lexicon gives this verse as an appointment by vote. This brother was not Paul's choice but the churches' own representative, there to keep watch over the money with Paul. Acts 20:4 lists men from several churches traveling with Paul toward Jerusalem, and they are very likely the delegates who carried this gift.
  • "For the glory of the Lord himself and to show our good will" (v19). The collection had two aims: to honor Christ, and to show Paul's eagerness to help Jerusalem's poor, which he had promised to remember (Gal 2:10).
  • "So that no one should blame us" (v20). Paul's critics had already accused him of craftiness (12:16–18). A large sum of money handled by one man would be an easy target. So he arranges that others, chosen by the churches, travel with it. "Generous gift" is literally "abundance," a hint that the total was expected to be large.
  • "Honorable not only in the Lord's sight but also in the sight of man" (v21). Paul adapts Proverbs 3:4 (in its Greek form). A clear conscience before God is not enough when handling other people's money. Integrity also has to be visible. This verse has shaped how faithful churches handle offerings ever since: more than one person, open records, nothing in the dark.
  • "Our brother whom we have often tested" (v22). A second unnamed man, perhaps one of Paul's regular companions. He has been proved earnest "in many matters," and is now even more eager because of what he has heard about Corinth. Some suggest Tychicus or Trophimus, who both traveled with Paul to Jerusalem (Acts 20:4), but no one knows.
  • "My partner and fellow worker… messengers of the churches, the glory of Christ" (v23). Titus carries Paul's own authority. The two brothers are apostoloi of the churches. Here the word has its ordinary sense of "envoys" or "delegates," not apostles in the sense of the Twelve or Paul (compare Epaphroditus, Phil 2:25). And they are "the glory of Christ." In the chapter where Paul showed believers "being transformed… from one degree of glory to another" (3:18), these ordinary messengers are living reflections of Christ's splendor.
  • "Give proof before the churches of your love" (v24). When these men arrive, the Corinthians' welcome and their completed gift will speak to all the churches the delegates represent. Paul has been boasting about Corinth (9:2), and he wants that boast to prove true.
  • Commentators: Guzik reads vv18–22 as Paul's deliberate system of accountability. He lists the usual candidates for the first brother but concludes the identity is unknown and does not matter. Henry thinks the first brother was Luke and suggests the second was Apollos. He says Paul arranged things to cut off "unjust suspicion," being careful about what is honest "not only in the sight of the Lord" but before others too. JFB says "Luke may be meant" for v18, notes that this brother was "chosen—by vote: so the Greek," which the lexicon supports, and passes on Birks's guess that the second brother was Trophimus. It also points out that "messengers" in v23 is literally "apostles," in the looser sense of delegates. The Tyndale notes simply say the two companions cannot be identified. All of them agree that Paul's care over money was part of his ministry, not a distraction from it.

Pointing to Christ

This chapter on money stands on one verse about Jesus. The Son, rich in the Father's glory, became poor all the way to the cross, so that people with nothing to offer God might be made rich in him (v9). Every other part of the chapter flows from that: the Macedonians giving themselves first, joy in poverty, fairness between churches, and honest handling of the gift. Christian giving is not a fee or a tax. It is grace received, passed on, and carried by people who reflect "the glory of Christ" (v23).

Reflection

  • Giving Themselves Before Their Money (v.5) — Paul notes the Macedonians gave themselves to the Lord first, and their generosity followed from that surrender. Have you given yourself to God first, or are you trying to be generous without that deeper surrender?
  • Generosity as a Grace to Grow In (v.7) — Paul lists giving alongside faith, knowledge, and love as a grace believers should excel in. Are you actively growing in generosity, or has it stayed stagnant?
  • Christ's Poverty, Our Riches (v.9) — Paul points to Christ becoming poor for our sakes as the ultimate model of sacrificial giving. How does Christ's example challenge the way you currently give?
  • Willingness Over Amount (v.12) — Paul says a gift is acceptable based on willingness, not on how much someone has to give. Is your giving marked by cheerful willingness, or reluctant obligation?
