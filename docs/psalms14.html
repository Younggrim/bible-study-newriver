Summary

A wisdom psalm lamenting the corruption of humanity apart from God, closely paralleled later in Psalm 53. It opens by describing the fool who denies God in his heart, leading to universal corruption in which no one does good (vv. 1-3). These evildoers consume God's people carelessly and never call on the LORD, yet the psalm warns that great terror will overtake them because God is present with the righteous (vv. 4-5). Though the wicked mock the plans of the poor, the LORD himself is their refuge (v. 6). The psalm closes with a longing for Israel's salvation and restoration to come out of Zion (v. 7).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Wisdom/Prophetic Lament
Attributed Author: David — "To the chief Musician, A Psalm of David"
Key Themes: Practical atheism ("no God"), universal human corruption, God's search for the righteous, none found good, God as refuge for the poor, longing for Zion's salvation
Historical Context: Psalm 14 is nearly identical to Psalm 53 (an Elohistic variant in Book II). Paul quotes vv.1-3 extensively in Romans 3:10-12 to establish universal human sinfulness — "there is none righteous, no, not one." The "fool" (nabal) is not intellectually deficient but morally corrupt — the same word used for Nabal in 1 Samuel 25. This psalm presents the divine perspective: God looks down from heaven searching for anyone who understands or seeks Him — and finds no one. The picture is devastating: universal departure from God, universal corruption, universal moral failure. Only divine salvation (v.7) can remedy human depravity.
The Fool Hath Said (v.1): The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God. The denial is located in the heart rather than in argument, and the word for fool in Hebrew is moral rather than intellectual: nabal is a person whose judgment is corrupt, not one who has reasoned badly. And the consequence follows in the same verse, they are corrupt, they have done abominable works, there is none that doeth good.
There Is None That Doeth Good (vv.2-3): The LORD looked down from heaven upon the children of men, to see if there were any that did understand, and seek God. The search is described as a survey and its result is total, they are all gone aside, they are all together become filthy, there is none that doeth good, no, not one. Paul quotes these verses at length in Romans 3:10-12 as the center of his case that the charge falls on everybody.
Have All the Workers of Iniquity No Knowledge (vv.4-6): Have all the workers of iniquity no knowledge, who eat up my people as they eat bread. The oppression is described as routine, which is the force of the comparison with bread: not an atrocity but a meal. Then a fear that comes from nowhere, there were they in great fear, for God is in the generation of the righteous. And the closing line names who was being counted on, ye have shamed the counsel of the poor, because the LORD is his refuge.
Oh That the Salvation of Israel Were Come (v.7): Oh that the salvation of Israel were come out of Zion. One verse, and it turns a psalm about universal corruption into a request for a specific rescue from a specific place. And the outcome is stated as an emotion rather than a victory, when the LORD bringeth back the captivity of his people, Jacob shall rejoice, and Israel shall be glad.

Deep Dive

Setting: The title is "To the choirmaster. Of David." No occasion is given, and Matthew Henry calls the usual guesses about Saul or Absalom "mere conjectures." Psalm 14 appears twice in the Psalter. Psalm 53 is almost the same poem, with God's name given as Elohim ("God") instead of YHWH ("the LORD"), and with a different version of vv5–6. It was probably adapted for a different collection or occasion. Psalm 14 is often called a wisdom psalm, because it talks about the "fool" and about who "understands," but it also has the tone of a prophet's indictment and ends like a lament. It closes the first group of David's psalms after Psalm 8. Psalm 8 showed humanity crowned with glory as God made it. Psalm 14 shows what humanity has become. The wicked man of Psalm 10 said "there is no God" in his heart (10:4). Psalm 14 opens with the same words and then says something shocking: this is not only true of a few oppressors, but of the whole human race. Paul builds his argument in Romans 3 on this psalm.

The Fool Says in His Heart (v1)

  • "The fool says in his heart" (v1). "Fool" in English suggests someone stupid. The Hebrew nabal means something worse: a person who is morally empty, who refuses to live as if God matters. The clearest picture is Nabal, Abigail's husband, who insulted David and repaid kindness with contempt. Abigail said of him, "as his name is, so is he. Nabal is his name, and folly is with him" (1 Sam 25:25). A nabal may be clever, rich and successful. His folly is moral, not mental.
  • "There is no God" (v1). As in 10:4, the Hebrew is two words, "No God." In the ancient world almost no one denied that gods existed. This is not mainly a philosophical claim. It is a heart decision: "God is not here, not for me, not watching, not going to judge." It is spoken "in his heart," privately. Many people who would never say it aloud live this way.
  • "They are corrupt, they do abominable deeds" (v1). The singular "fool" becomes the plural "they." What starts in the heart shows up in life. "Corrupt" (shachat) is the word used of the earth before the flood: "the earth was corrupt in God's sight" (Gen 6:11–12). "Abominable" means detestable to God. Denying God in the heart and doing evil with the hands go together. Paul says the same: suppressing the truth about God leads to darkened hearts and corrupt lives (Rom 1:21–32).
  • "There is none who does good" (v1). This is the first statement of the psalm's sweeping verdict, repeated and strengthened in v3.
  • Commentators: Guzik says nabal means moral perversity, not lack of intelligence, and links it to Nabal in 1 Samuel 25. He passes on Boice's paraphrase, "No God for me," and G. Campbell Morgan's question about folly and atheism: "Which is cause, and which is effect?" Guzik also notes that David himself once "said in his heart" something fearful and faithless (1 Sam 27:1), a reminder that believers are not immune. JFB says the wicked are called fools because they think and act against right reason, and that "in his heart" means "to himself."

The LORD Looks Down from Heaven (vv2–3)

  • "The LORD looks down from heaven on the children of man" (v2). The scene shifts to God's view. This echoes Genesis, where "the LORD saw that the wickedness of man was great" before the flood (Gen 6:5), and where "the LORD came down to see the city and the tower" of Babel (Gen 11:5). Psalm 11:4 said the LORD's eyes "test the children of man." Here he looks, and the results come in.
  • "To see if there are any who understand, who seek after God" (v2). "Understand" (maskil) is the word for the wise, people who act with insight. It is the opposite of the fool. Real understanding, in the Bible, begins with "the fear of the LORD" (Prov 9:10). "Seek after God" means turning to him, praying and asking for his help (9:10; 10:4). The test is not intelligence or religious activity, but whether anyone is truly turned toward God.
  • "They have all turned aside" (v3). Like sheep wandering off a path, or an army deserting. Isaiah 53:6 says the same: "All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned, every one, to his own way."
  • "Together they have become corrupt" (v3). This is a different, rarer verb (alach) than in v1, used only three times in the Old Testament (here, 53:3 and Job 15:16). A related Arabic word describes milk turning sour. Humanity has gone off, like food left out too long.
  • "There is none who does good, not even one" (v3). The verdict is total. It does not mean no one ever does a kind thing. It means no one, left to themselves, lives for God as they ought, with a heart wholly turned toward him. Even the best human goodness is mixed.
  • Paul's use of this psalm (Rom 3:10–12). Paul quotes vv1–3 from the ancient Greek translation to open a chain of Old Testament texts: "None is righteous, no, not one; no one understands; no one seeks for God. All have turned aside; together they have become worthless; no one does good, not even one." He uses it to prove that "all, both Jews and Greeks, are under sin" (Rom 3:9). The psalm itself moves on to speak of "my people" and "the generation of the righteous" (vv4–5), but Paul sees the deeper truth: even the righteous are righteous only by God's grace, not by nature. A curious footnote: some Greek manuscripts and the Latin Vulgate later added the rest of Paul's chain (Rom 3:13–18) into Psalm 14:3 itself. Older English prayer books printed this longer version. The Hebrew text does not have it.
  • Commentators: Henry reads the psalm through Paul's use of it, as a description of "the sinfulness of the sin we are conceived and born in," and concludes that "not his enemies only, but all the children of men, were thus corrupted." JFB explains "filthy" (KJV) as "literally, 'spoiled,' or, 'soured,' 'corrupted'," which fits the lexicon's note on alach. Guzik passes on Kidner's point that "we are too included," and warns that human religious seeking often produces idols, not the true God.

Those Who Eat Up My People (vv4–6)

  • "Have they no knowledge, all the evildoers" (v4). The question is one of astonishment. How can they be so blind? Their problem is not lack of information but refusal to know God (Hos 4:1, 6). "Evildoers" (poale aven) is the same phrase as 5:5 and 6:8, "workers of evil."
  • "Who eat up my people as they eat bread" (v4). "My people" shows that the speaker here is God himself, or the psalmist speaking for him. The oppressors devour the poor as casually as eating a meal. Micah uses the same picture of leaders "who eat the flesh of my people" (Mic 3:3). Denying God in the heart (v1) leads, step by step, to treating people as food.
  • "And do not call upon the LORD" (v4). Prayerlessness is listed alongside cruelty. To never call on God is the practical outworking of "there is no God."
  • "There they are in great terror" (v5). Literally "There they feared a fear." "There" points to a moment and a place, as if the psalmist sees the scene: just when the oppressors are feasting, terror falls on them. Israel's history had many such moments, such as the Egyptians at the Red Sea (Exod 14:24–25) and the Philistines before Jonathan (1 Sam 14:15).
  • "For God is with the generation of the righteous" (v5). This is why the terror falls. God is not on the side of the fools. He is "with" the righteous, the same promise as Immanuel, "God with us" (Isa 7:14). "Generation" means a type of people, as in 12:7.
  • "You would shame the plans of the poor, but the LORD is his refuge" (v6). The psalmist turns to address the evildoers directly. They mock the poor man's hopes and make fun of his trust in God. But they are wrong about who stands behind him. "Refuge" (machseh) is from the same root as "take refuge" in 2:12, 7:1 and 11:1.
  • Psalm 53's version. In Psalm 53:5 these lines read differently: "There they are, in great terror, where there is no terror! For God scatters the bones of him who encamps against you." The words in Hebrew are similar enough that one version seems to have been adapted from the other, possibly to celebrate a specific victory over a besieging army.
  • Commentators: JFB says their conduct shows "indifference rather than ignorance of God," reads "eat up my people" as "beastly fury" (Prov 30:14), and takes "call on the Lord" as worship. Guzik says prayerlessness is one of the marks of practical atheism, and passes on Spurgeon's word to mocked believers: "If they can sneer you out of your religion, you have not got any worth having." Guzik and Maclaren both read v4 as astonishment at the blindness of the wicked.

Salvation Out of Zion (v7)

  • "Oh, that salvation for Israel would come out of Zion!" (v7). The psalm ends with a longing. If all have turned aside, help cannot come from humanity. It has to come from God. Zion is where God has chosen to dwell (2:6; 9:11). Salvation must come from where he is.
  • "When the LORD restores the fortunes of his people" (v7). The KJV has "bringeth back the captivity of his people." The Hebrew phrase (shuv shevut) can mean either. Some think it shows this line was added during or after the Babylonian exile. But the phrase is also used more generally for any great reversal of misfortune, as when the LORD "restored the fortunes of Job" (Job 42:10). David could have used it of any time of national distress.
  • "Let Jacob rejoice, let Israel be glad" (v7). "Jacob" and "Israel" are the same person and the same people. Jacob was the patriarch's birth name, the one who grasped and schemed. Israel was the name God gave him after he wrestled with God (Gen 32:28). Both names together remind the people where they came from and what grace made them.
  • The psalm turns outward. A psalm that begins with the fool ends with prayer for salvation. That is the true opposite of the fool: not a clever person but someone who looks to God for rescue.
  • Commentators: JFB says "captivity" denotes "any great evil," and that Zion is God's dwelling from which he reveals his merciful purposes. Guzik agrees that the captivity here means any oppression, not only Babylon. Henry calls v7 a prayer for Israel's salvation "with confident hope in it."

Pointing to Christ

Psalm 14 is the Old Testament's plainest verdict on the human heart: "none who does good, not even one." Paul quotes it to shut every mouth (Rom 3:19), and then turns at once to the good news: God's righteousness has been revealed "through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe" (Rom 3:21–22). The prayer of v7, "Oh, that salvation for Israel would come out of Zion!" is answered in Jesus. Paul quotes a related promise in Romans 11:26: "The Deliverer will come from Zion, he will banish ungodliness from Jacob." When God looked down from heaven and found no one, he did not leave. He came down, in his Son, the one man who truly sought God with his whole heart and did good without fail, and he bore the judgment of those who had turned aside.

Reflection

  • Practical Atheism Is a Heart Problem (v.1) — The fool's denial of God flows from a desire to escape accountability, not from honest reasoning. Is there an area where you live as if God is not watching, even while affirming that He exists?
  • No One Is Righteous on Their Own (v.3) — The psalm describes universal moral failure, with no one doing good on their own merit. Does this honest diagnosis of human sinfulness make you more grateful for grace rather than more despairing?
  • Oppression of the Vulnerable Is Personal to God (v.4) — Those who casually consume the vulnerable as if eating bread are described as facing sudden reckoning. Are you alert to how you treat those with less power than you, knowing God takes it personally?
  • Salvation Must Come From Outside Us (v.7) — Since no one is righteous by their own effort, the psalmist longs for salvation to come from God alone. Are you still trying to earn what only God can give, or resting in salvation that comes from Him?
