Summary

The Song opens with the Bride longing for her Beloved's kisses and love, which she says is better than wine (vv. 1-4). She explains to the Daughters of Jerusalem that though sun-darkened from tending vineyards, she considers herself lovely, and asks where he pastures his flock so she can find him (vv. 5-8). He compares her beauty to a prized mare among Pharaoh's chariots and praises her ornamented cheeks and neck (vv. 9-11). She in turn describes him as fragrant myrrh and henna blossoms while they are near each other (vv. 12-14). The chapter closes with the couple exchanging tender compliments, delighting in each other and their surroundings (vv. 15-17).

Authorship & Background

Author: Solomon (1:1). Also called 'Canticles' or 'Song of Songs' (the greatest song). A love poem celebrating marital love — the only extended treatment of romantic/sexual love in Scripture. Interpreted on multiple levels: (1) literal — celebrating human love within marriage; (2) allegorical — picturing God's love for Israel or Christ's love for the Church (Ephesians 5:25-32).
Classification: Wisdom Literature — Love Poetry
Key Themes: A working woman rather than a princess, longing stated before the beloved appears, self-consciousness about her sun-darkened skin, and admiration that begins at once and runs both ways
Speakers: The Bride (Shulammite): vv.2-7, 12-14, 16; The Bridegroom (Solomon): vv.8-11, 15; The Daughters of Jerusalem: v.4 (partial), v.11; Title/Narrator: v.1
Historical Context: Chapter 1 opens the Song with its title verse identifying Solomon as author (v.1). Solomon composed 1,005 songs (1 Kings 4:32), yet this is called "The Song of Songs" — his greatest, the superlative of all songs. The setting is the royal court and countryside of ancient Israel. The Bride (the Shulammite) introduces herself to the Daughters of Jerusalem, expressing her longing for the Bridegroom while acknowledging her humble appearance. She is darkened by the sun from vineyard labor — not a palace princess but a working woman. The mutual admiration between Bride and Bridegroom begins immediately, establishing the passionate tone that governs the entire poem. Allegorically, this chapter pictures the soul's first longing for communion with God — the initial drawing, the sense of unworthiness, and the Bridegroom's reassuring affirmation of beauty.
The Song of Songs (v.1): The title is a Hebrew superlative, the song of songs in the way that holy of holies means the holiest place. Solomon is credited with 1,005 songs at 1 Kings 4:32, and this is presented as the best of them. Nothing else in the verse prepares a reader for what follows, and the book never mentions God by name.
The Bride: Draw Me, We Will Run After Thee (vv.2-4): The Song opens mid-desire, with no introduction and no scene set: let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth. The shift between “he” and “thou” within two verses is characteristic and untidy in the way real speech is. “Draw me, we will run after thee” moves from singular to plural, bringing the daughters of Jerusalem in as a chorus.
The Bride: I Am Black, but Comely (vv.5-7): She addresses her appearance directly and without apology: dark from working in the vineyards because her mother's children were angry with her, so she kept vineyards rather than herself. This is a labourer, not a court beauty, which matters for how the book's praise later lands. Verse 7 asks where he feeds his flock, so she is looking for him and unwilling to wander among the other shepherds' companies.
The Bridegroom: As a Company of Horses (vv.8-11): His first speech compares her to a company of horses in Pharaoh's chariots, which sounds odd until one knows that Egyptian chariot horses were the most admired animals in the region and elaborately ornamented. The compliment is about bearing rather than size. He then promises jewelry, borders of gold with studs of silver, spoken in the plural as though by attendants.
The Bride: A Bundle of Myrrh (vv.12-14): Three short images in her own voice, all of them scent rather than sight: spikenard, a bundle of myrrh lying all night between her breasts, a cluster of camphire from the vineyards of En-gedi. En-gedi is a real oasis by the Dead Sea, which anchors the poetry in a place someone could walk to.
Mutual Praise: Our Bed Is Green (vv.15-17): The chapter ends in exchange rather than monologue: he calls her fair and her eyes doves, she calls him fair and pleasant. Then “our bed is green” and the beams of the house are cedar and the rafters fir, so the setting moves outdoors into a wood. The alternation of voices established here governs the whole book.

Setting: The Song of Solomon (also called the Song of Songs, from its first line) is a collection of love poems between a young woman and the man she loves, with a group of friends, "the daughters of Jerusalem," as a chorus. It sits among the wisdom books, next to Proverbs and Ecclesiastes. The title links it to Solomon, who reigned about 970–930 BC and composed 1,005 songs (1 Kgs 4:32). Most conservative scholars accept Solomon as author or at least as the poems' royal figure. Others think the title means "concerning Solomon" or "in Solomon's tradition" and date the collection later. The Song never mentions God directly (except possibly in 8:6) and never explains itself, which is why it has been read in so many ways. Jewish tradition read it as a picture of God's love for Israel, and Rabbi Akiva famously called it the "Holy of Holies" among the sacred writings. Most church fathers and later preachers read it as Christ's love for the church or the soul. Bernard of Clairvaux preached eighty-six sermons on it and only reached the start of chapter 3. Matthew Henry (1710) and JFB (1871) both read it that way. Most modern interpreters, including Guzik and the Tyndale notes, read it first as what it plainly is: poetry celebrating romantic and married love as God's good gift, with God's love for his people as a true but secondary echo. Even among those readers, some take it as a story about two people, Solomon and his bride, and others as a three-person drama in which a country girl, taken into Solomon's court, stays loyal to her shepherd love back home. The Tyndale notes lay out both, and Guzik takes the two-person view. Still others see a loose anthology of songs. The ESV prints no section headings in the Song. It labels the speakers ("She," "He," "Others"), and the headings below follow those exchanges. Who says what is sometimes uncertain, since Hebrew poetry doesn't use quotation marks. The clues are mainly the gender of the Hebrew pronouns and verbs.

The Song of Songs (v1)

  • "The Song of Songs, which is Solomon's" (v1). "Song of Songs" is a Hebrew way of saying "the best song," like "Holy of Holies" (the most holy place) or "King of kings." The Tyndale notes: it is the highest and most sublime of songs, likely the best of Solomon's 1,005. "Which is Solomon's" can mean written by him, dedicated to him, or about him. The Hebrew allows each.
  • Solomon and love. There is a sad irony here. The man linked to this song of one faithful love later had seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines, who "turned away his heart" (1 Kgs 11:3). Guzik notes the irony and suggests the Song shows what Solomon knew love should be, even if he did not live it. Others see the Song as written early in his reign, before his fall.
  • Commentators: Henry reads the whole book as about Christ and his people: "it is wholly taken up with describing the excellences of Christ." JFB says Solomon's name stands at the head because "his personality is hid under that of Christ," the true Prince of Peace (Solomon's name is related to shalom, peace). Guzik takes the book as first a description of love in courtship and marriage, and says it never presents itself as an allegory. Its secondary picture of God's love for his people is still valid, he adds. He also passes on Luther's warning that allegory alone is a shaky foundation.

She Longs for Him (vv2–4)

  • "Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth!" (v2). The Song opens with the woman's voice, and she speaks more than anyone else in the book. She is not passive. The Tyndale notes observe that she often takes the lead. She wants real closeness, not just a polite greeting.
  • "For your love is better than wine" (v2). "Love" here is dodim, a plural word for love expressed: caresses, lovemaking (also 4:10; 7:12; Prov 7:18). Wine was the great pleasure of feasts (Ps 104:15). His love is better. She shifts from "him" to "you" in the same verse, a common move in Hebrew poetry, as if she turns from thinking about him to speaking to him.
  • "Your anointing oils are fragrant; your name is oil poured out" (v3). Scent fills the Song. In a hot land with little bathing, perfumed oils were a luxury. There is a wordplay: "name" is shem and "oil" is shemen. His name, meaning his character and reputation, is like perfume poured out, filling the room. Ecclesiastes echoes it: "A good name is better than precious ointment" (Eccl 7:1). "Therefore virgins love you." "Virgins" ('alamot) are young women of marriageable age. Others admire him too. The Tyndale notes: his appeal is his reputation as well as his looks, and she feels fortunate that he loves her back.
  • "Draw me after you; let us run. The king has brought me into his chambers" (v4). "The king" may be Solomon himself, or a loving title, the way a bride in Israel could call her groom "king" on the wedding day. Guzik reads "his chambers" as being welcomed into his private world, his heart. In the three-person reading, she has been brought into Solomon's palace against her will and wants her shepherd to take her away. The Tyndale notes observe that the Hebrew doesn't say which rooms or whether she chose to go.
  • "We will exult and rejoice in you… rightly do they love you" (v4). The ESV gives these lines to "Others," the friends. They celebrate the couple. Friends who rejoice with a couple play a real role in the Song, as at any wedding.
  • Commentators: JFB calls the kiss "the token of peace from the Prince of Peace" and reads it as Israel's longing for Messiah or a soul seeking reconciliation. Henry likewise takes the kisses as Christ's assurances of pardon, bringing peace and joy. Guzik notes that she admires his character and reputation, not just his looks, and draws the lesson that a wise woman chooses a man others respect.

Dark but Lovely (vv5–6)

  • "I am very dark, but lovely, O daughters of Jerusalem" (v5). She speaks to the city women, who have soft, pale skin from staying indoors. She has been working outside. "But lovely" can also be translated "and lovely" (the Hebrew waw can mean either), and many scholars prefer that: she is not apologizing for her looks but owning them. The Tyndale notes: her darkness comes from the sun, not her race, and she keeps her self-respect and asserts her beauty.
  • "Like the tents of Kedar, like the curtains of Solomon" (v5). Kedar was a nomad tribe of the Arabian desert, descended from Ishmael (Gen 25:13; Ps 120:5). Their tents were woven from black goat hair, still the pattern of Bedouin tents today. Solomon's curtains were rich and fine. The two lines match her two words: dark as Kedar's tents, lovely as Solomon's hangings.
  • "Do not gaze at me because I am dark, because the sun has looked upon me" (v6). The sun "looked" at her, so don't you stare. In the ancient world pale skin marked a woman of leisure, and sunburned skin a laborer.
  • "My mother's sons were angry with me; they made me keeper of the vineyards" (v6). Her brothers (half-brothers, perhaps, since she says "my mother's sons," and no father is ever mentioned) put her to work. In that culture brothers often oversaw a sister's affairs until she married. They appear again at the end of the book (8:8–9).
  • "But my own vineyard I have not kept!" (v6). Her "own vineyard" is herself: her looks, perhaps her own life. "Vineyard" is used for the woman herself later in the Song (2:15; 8:12). Busy with their vineyards, she had no time to care for her own. The Tyndale notes offer a second reading: her protest shows she thinks she is ready for love.
  • Commentators: JFB reads her blackness as "the darkness of man's natural state," and her beauty as "His comeliness put upon her." It sees the dark tents of the Arabs as the Gentile church. Henry says the same: black by sin, lovely by grace. He adds that her neglect of her own vineyard came from mistreatment by "children of the church" who were not children of God. Guzik, reading literally, says she should not believe that hardship makes her unworthy of love.

Where Do You Pasture Your Flock? (vv7–8)

  • "Tell me, you whom my soul loves, where you pasture your flock" (v7). "You whom my soul loves" becomes her name for him (3:1–4). He is here a shepherd. At midday shepherds rested their flocks in the shade, and she wants to know where so she can find him.
  • "Why should I be like one who veils herself beside the flocks of your companions?" (v7). A woman wandering among shepherds' camps, veiled, asking for a man, might be taken for a prostitute. Tamar veiled herself by the roadside and Judah took her for one (Gen 38:14–15). She wants to go straight to him, not wander around looking. Some ancient versions read "one who wanders" instead of "one who veils herself," a difference of two letters swapped. The point is the same.
  • "If you do not know, O most beautiful among women, follow in the tracks of the flock, and pasture your young goats beside the shepherds' tents" (v8). Either his reply or the chorus's, teasing and kind. The Tyndale notes take it as his first words, gently calming her worry. Follow the trail and you'll find me. He calls her "most beautiful among women," answering her worry about her looks in v6.
  • Two kings or one? In the three-person reading, this shepherd is a different man from "the king" of v4, and the Tyndale notes point out that shepherding is not a job anyone linked with Solomon. In the two-person reading, "king" and "shepherd" are both loving titles for the same man, much as a later poet might call her love both a prince and a farmer. Both readings take the text seriously.
  • Commentators: Henry turns it to the seeker of Christ: follow the footsteps of the flock and "sit under the direction of good ministers." JFB says no single image covers all Christ's work, so he appears as king and shepherd both. Guzik, reading literally, says she does not want to look like a loose woman who veils herself.

Ornaments of Gold (vv9–11)

  • "I compare you, my love, to a mare among Pharaoh's chariots" (v9). "My love" is ra'yati, "my companion," "my friend," his favorite name for her (used nine times). Egyptian horses were famous and Solomon imported them (1 Kgs 10:28–29). Chariot horses were stallions, so a mare among them would cause a stir. The Tyndale notes pass on an ancient tactic: releasing a mare in heat to break up a charge of stallions. Her beauty turns every head. JFB, following the KJV, reads "company of horses" and links the image to the deliverance from Pharaoh at the Red Sea. The Hebrew form is singular and feminine, so "mare" is more likely.
  • "Your cheeks are lovely with ornaments, your neck with strings of jewels" (v10). Egyptian horses were decked out with ornaments on their bridles and harness, and he moves smoothly from the mare to her jewelry, earrings or hanging ornaments framing her face, beads around her neck.
  • "We will make for you ornaments of gold, studded with silver" (v11). The "Others" (or the man, speaking for himself and his household) offer finer jewelry still. The Tyndale notes compare Jesus' saying, "to the one who has, more will be given" (Matt 13:12). Guzik sees how a man's honoring of a woman moves others to honor her too.
  • Commentators: Henry: "In the sight of Christ believers are the excellent of the earth," and the ornaments are the graces Christ gives. JFB praises Egyptian horses as "celebrated for beauty, swiftness, and ardor" and rejects the idea that the Song is mainly about Solomon's marriage to Pharaoh's daughter (1 Kgs 3:1).

My Beloved Is to Me (vv12–14)

  • "While the king was on his couch, my nard gave forth its fragrance" (v12). "Couch" may mean a dining couch at a banquet, since people reclined to eat. Nard (spikenard) was a costly perfume made from a plant of the Himalayas, imported from India. Mary of Bethany poured a pound of it over Jesus, worth a year's wages, and "the house was filled with the fragrance" (John 12:3). The Tyndale notes: the Hebrew word may mean a couch or a round table, and "lying" is not in the text.
  • "My beloved is to me a sachet of myrrh that lies between my breasts" (v13). "My beloved" is dodi, from the same root as "love" in v2, her favorite name for him (more than twenty-five times). Myrrh was a fragrant resin from Arabia and East Africa. Women wore a small pouch of it on a cord around the neck, and it scented them all day and night. He is that close to her heart. The Tyndale notes: myrrh was imported at great cost. Guzik addresses readers who are uncomfortable with the frankness: the Bible is not embarrassed by married love, and neither should believers be.
  • "My beloved is to me a cluster of henna blossoms in the vineyards of Engedi" (v14). En-gedi is an oasis on the western shore of the Dead Sea, a burst of green with waterfalls in a barren desert. David hid from Saul in its caves (1 Sam 24:1). Henna is a shrub with sweet-smelling white flowers, whose leaves give a reddish dye (the KJV's "camphire"). The Tyndale notes picture a traveler longing for water at En-gedi. In a desert life, he is her oasis.
  • Commentators: JFB reads the cluster as Christ, "manifold in His graces," and the myrrh as his "abundant preciousness." Henry takes the scents as fellowship with Christ in worship. Guzik notes that his love comforts her even when he is away.

You Are Beautiful (vv15–17)

  • "Behold, you are beautiful, my love; behold, you are beautiful; your eyes are doves" (v15). "Behold" means "look!" He repeats himself because once is not enough. Doves' eyes may mean soft and gentle, or the shimmer of a dove's grey, or eyes that flutter. The Tyndale notes say the image is unclear and might refer to color or a gentle expression. The same praise returns in 4:1.
  • "Behold, you are beautiful, my beloved, truly delightful" (v16). She answers him word for word, "beautiful" in the masculine. The praise flows both ways. Love in the Song is mutual: each delights in the other.
  • "Our couch is green; the beams of our house are cedar; our rafters are pine" (vv16–17). Their "house" is the countryside. The green grass is their couch, the cedar trees their beams, the branches their rafters. "Pine" is uncertain and may be cypress or juniper. Love doesn't need a palace. The Tyndale notes: God's creation is more than enough for her and her true love.
  • Commentators: JFB sees "perfect oneness" here: cedar beams "never eaten by worms," a lasting home. Henry: "a sense of the Divine presence will turn the place into a paradise," whether in worship, work, sickness or prison. Guzik reads vv16–17 as a picture of a walk in the countryside.

Pointing to Christ

The Song is first a celebration of love between a man and a woman, and its place in Scripture says that God made that love, delights in it, and calls it good (Gen 2:23–25). But Scripture also uses marriage to picture God's love for his people (Isa 54:5; Hos 2:19–20) and Christ's love for the church (Eph 5:25–32), so the old allegorical readers were not wrong to hear more in it. A bride who knows she is dark from labor yet hears herself called "most beautiful among women" (vv5–8) echoes the church, unworthy in herself yet loved, "without spot or wrinkle," by her Bridegroom (Eph 5:27). The one whose name is "oil poured out" (v3) is the Christ, the Anointed One, and the costly nard of v12 was poured on him before his death (John 12:3). The book doesn't need to be forced into allegory to point to him. Every true love in the Song is a small picture of the love that "is better than wine."

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik notes that the Song opens with unashamed longing — the Bride does not apologize for her desire. This is God's design for marital love: passionate, expressive, mutual. The Bride's self-consciousness ("I am black") is met not with correction but with affirmation ("thou art fair"). Guzik emphasizes the allegorical dimension: every believer approaches Christ feeling unworthy, yet Christ's response is always to affirm what He sees in His redeemed Bride.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • An Opening Cry of Longing (v.2) — The Bride's very first words ask for her beloved's kisses and declare his love better than wine. Where has familiarity dulled a longing — for your spouse, or for God's presence — that used to feel urgent?
  • Beauty Spoken Over Self-Doubt (v.5) — She describes herself as darkened by the sun and self-conscious about her appearance, yet is called beautiful anyway. What insecurity are you carrying that you assume disqualifies you from being fully loved?
  • Neglecting Your Own Vineyard (v.6) — She admits that tending everyone else's vineyard left her own unattended. What relationship or part of your own soul are you neglecting because you're too busy managing everyone else's needs?
  • Love That Actively Seeks (v.7) — She doesn't wait to be found — she asks where he is and goes looking for him. Are you pursuing the people and the God you love, or passively waiting for connection to happen to you?
