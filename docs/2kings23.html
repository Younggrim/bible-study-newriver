2 Kings — Chapter 23

1Then the king summoned all the elders of Judah and Jerusalem.

2And he went up to the house of the LORD with all the people of Judah and Jerusalem, as well as the priests and the prophets—all the people small and great—and in their hearing he read all the words of the Book of the Covenant that had been found in the house of the LORD.

3So the king stood by the pillar and made a covenant before the LORD to follow the LORD and to keep His commandments, decrees, and statutes with all his heart and all his soul, and to carry out the words of the covenant that were written in this book. And all the people entered into the covenant.

4Then the king commanded Hilkiah the high priest, the priests second in rank, and the doorkeepers to remove from the temple of the LORD all the articles made for Baal, Asherah, and all the host of heaven. And he burned them outside Jerusalem in the fields of Kidron and carried their ashes to Bethel.

5Josiah also did away with the idolatrous priests ordained by the kings of Judah to burn incense on the high places of the cities of Judah and in the places all around Jerusalem—those who had burned incense to Baal, to the sun and moon, to the constellations, and to all the host of heaven.

6He brought the Asherah pole from the house of the LORD to the Kidron Valley outside Jerusalem, and there he burned it, ground it to powder, and threw its dust on the graves of the common people.

7He also tore down the quarters of the male shrine prostitutes that were in the house of the LORD, where the women had woven tapestries for Asherah.

8Then Josiah brought all the priests from the cities of Judah and desecrated the high places, from Geba to Beersheba, where the priests had burned incense. He tore down the high places of the gates at the entrance of the gate of Joshua the governor of the city, which was to the left of the city gate.

9Although the priests of the high places did not come up to the altar of the LORD in Jerusalem, they ate unleavened bread with their fellow priests.

10He also desecrated Topheth in the Valley of Ben-hinnom so that no one could sacrifice his son or daughter in the fire to Molech.

11And he removed from the entrance to the house of the LORD the horses that the kings of Judah had dedicated to the sun. They were in the court near the chamber of an official named Nathan-melech. And Josiah burned up the chariots of the sun.

12He pulled down the altars that the kings of Judah had set up on the roof near the upper chamber of Ahaz, and the altars that Manasseh had set up in the two courtyards of the house of the LORD. The king pulverized them there and threw their dust into the Kidron Valley.

13The king also desecrated the high places east of Jerusalem, to the south of the Mount of Corruption, which King Solomon of Israel had built for Ashtoreth the abomination of the Sidonians, for Chemosh the abomination of the Moabites, and for Milcom the abomination of the Ammonites.

14He smashed the sacred pillars to pieces, cut down the Asherah poles, and covered the sites with human bones.

15He even pulled down the altar at Bethel, the high place set up by Jeroboam son of Nebat, who had caused Israel to sin. Then he burned the high place, ground it to powder, and burned the Asherah pole.

16And as Josiah turned, he saw the tombs there on the hillside, and he sent someone to take the bones out of the tombs, and he burned them on the altar to defile it, according to the word of the LORD proclaimed by the man of God who had foretold these things.

17Then the king asked, “What is this monument I see?” And the men of the city replied, “It is the tomb of the man of God who came from Judah and proclaimed these things that you have done to the altar of Bethel.”

18“Let him rest,” said Josiah. “Do not let anyone disturb his bones.” So they left his bones undisturbed, along with those of the prophet who had come from Samaria.

19Just as Josiah had done at Bethel, so also in the cities of Samaria he removed all the shrines of the high places set up by the kings of Israel who had provoked the LORD to anger.

20On the altars he slaughtered all the priests of the high places, and he burned human bones on them. Then he returned to Jerusalem.

21The king commanded all the people, “Keep the Passover of the LORD your God, as it is written in this Book of the Covenant.”

22No such Passover had been observed from the days of the judges who had governed Israel through all the days of the kings of Israel and Judah.

23But in the eighteenth year of Josiah’s reign, this Passover was observed to the LORD in Jerusalem.

24Furthermore, Josiah removed the mediums and spiritists, the household gods and idols, and all the abominations that were seen in the land of Judah and in Jerusalem. He did this to carry out the words of the law written in the book that Hilkiah the priest had found in the house of the LORD.

25Neither before nor after Josiah was there any king like him, who turned to the LORD with all his heart and with all his soul and with all his strength, according to all the Law of Moses.

26Nevertheless, the LORD did not turn away from the fury of His burning anger, which was kindled against Judah because of all that Manasseh had done to provoke Him to anger.

27For the LORD had said, “I will remove Judah from My sight, just as I removed Israel. I will reject this city Jerusalem, which I chose, and the temple of which I said, ‘My Name shall be there.’”

28As for the rest of the acts of Josiah and all his accomplishments, are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Judah?

29During Josiah’s reign, Pharaoh Neco king of Egypt marched up to help the king of Assyria at the Euphrates River. King Josiah went out to confront him, but Neco faced him and killed him at Megiddo.

30From Megiddo his servants carried his body in a chariot, brought him to Jerusalem, and buried him in his own tomb. Then the people of the land took Jehoahaz son of Josiah, anointed him, and made him king in place of his father.

31Jehoahaz was twenty-three years old when he became king, and he reigned in Jerusalem three months. His mother’s name was Hamutal daughter of Jeremiah; she was from Libnah.

32And he did evil in the sight of the LORD, just as his fathers had done.

33And Pharaoh Neco imprisoned Jehoahaz at Riblah in the land of Hamath so that he could not reign in Jerusalem, and he imposed on Judah a levy of a hundred talents of silver and a talent of gold.

34Then Pharaoh Neco made Eliakim son of Josiah king in place of his father Josiah, and he changed Eliakim’s name to Jehoiakim. But Neco took Jehoahaz and carried him off to Egypt, where he died.

35So Jehoiakim paid the silver and gold to Pharaoh Neco, but to meet Pharaoh’s demand he taxed the land and exacted the silver and the gold from the people, each according to his wealth.

36Jehoiakim was twenty-five years old when he became king, and he reigned in Jerusalem eleven years. His mother’s name was Zebidah daughter of Pedaiah; she was from Rumah.

14And he brake in pieces the images, and cut down the groves, and filled their places with the bones of men.

15Moreover the altar that was at Bethel, and the high place which Jeroboam the son of Nebat, who made Israel to sin, had made, both that altar and the high place he brake down, and burned the high place, and stamped it small to powder, and burned the grove.

20And he slew all the priests of the high places that were there upon the altars, and burned men’s bones upon them, and returned to Jerusalem.

21And the king commanded all the people, saying, Keep the passover unto the LORD your God, as it is written in the book of this covenant.

23But in the eighteenth year of king Josiah, wherein this passover was holden to the LORD in Jerusalem.

26Notwithstanding the LORD turned not from the fierceness of his great wrath, wherewith his anger was kindled against Judah, because of all the provocations that Manasseh had provoked him withal.

27And the LORD said, I will remove Judah also out of my sight, as I have removed Israel, and will cast off this city Jerusalem which I have chosen, and the house of which I said, My name shall be there.

1The king summoned all the leaders of Judah and Jerusalem.

2The king went up to the Lord’s temple, accompanied by all the people of Judah, all the residents of Jerusalem, the priests, and the prophets. All the people were there, from the youngest to the oldest. He read aloud all the words of the scroll of the covenant that had been discovered in the Lord’s temple.

3The king stood by the pillar and renewed the covenant before the Lord, agreeing to follow the Lord and to obey his commandments, laws, and rules with all his heart and being, by carrying out the terms of this covenant recorded on this scroll. All the people agreed to keep the covenant.

4The king ordered Hilkiah the high priest, the high-ranking priests, and the guards to bring out of the Lord’s temple all the items that were used in the worship of Baal, Asherah, and all the stars of the sky. The king burned them outside of Jerusalem in the terraces of Kidron, and carried their ashes to Bethel.

5He eliminated the pagan priests whom the kings of Judah had appointed to offer sacrifices on the high places in the cities of Judah and in the area right around Jerusalem. (They offered sacrifices to Baal, the sun god, the moon god, the constellations, and all the stars in the sky.)

6He removed the Asherah pole from the Lord’s temple and took it outside Jerusalem to the Kidron Valley, where he burned it. He smashed it to dust and then threw the dust in the public graveyard.

7He tore down the quarters of the male cultic prostitutes in the Lord’s temple, where women were weaving shrines for Asherah.

8He brought all the priests from the cities of Judah and ruined the high places where the priests had offered sacrifices, from Geba to Beer Sheba. He tore down the high place of the goat idols situated at the entrance of the gate of Joshua, the city official, on the left side of the city gate.

9(Now the priests of the high places did not go up to the altar of the Lord in Jerusalem, but they did eat unleavened cakes among their fellow priests.)

10The king ruined Topheth in the Valley of Ben Hinnom so that no one could pass his son or his daughter through the fire to Molech.

11He removed from the entrance to the Lord’s temple the statues of horses that the kings of Judah had placed there in honor of the sun god. (They were kept near the room of Nathan Melech the eunuch, which was situated among the courtyards.) He burned up the chariots devoted to the sun god.

12The king tore down the altars the kings of Judah had set up on the roof of Ahaz’s upper room, as well as the altars Manasseh had set up in the two courtyards of the Lord’s temple. He crushed them and threw the dust in the Kidron Valley.

13The king ruined the high places east of Jerusalem, south of the Mount of Destruction, that King Solomon of Israel had built for the detestable Sidonian goddess Astarte, the detestable Moabite god Chemosh, and the horrible Ammonite god Milcom.

14He smashed the sacred pillars to bits, cut down the Asherah poles, and filled those shrines with human bones.

15He also tore down the altar in Bethel at the high place made by Jeroboam son of Nebat, who encouraged Israel to sin. He burned all the combustible items at that high place and crushed them to dust, including the Asherah pole.

16When Josiah turned around, he saw the tombs there on the hill. So he ordered the bones from the tombs to be brought; he burned them on the altar and defiled it, just as in the Lord’s message that was announced by the prophet while Jeroboam stood by the altar during a festival. Then the king turned and saw the grave of the prophet who had foretold this.

17He asked, “What is this grave marker I see?” The men from the city replied, “It’s the grave of the prophet who came from Judah and foretold these very things you have done to the altar of Bethel.”

18The king said, “Leave it alone! No one must touch his bones.” So they left his bones undisturbed, as well as the bones of the Israelite prophet buried beside him.

19Josiah also removed all the shrines on the high places in the cities of Samaria. The kings of Israel had made them and angered the Lord. He did to them what he had done to the high place in Bethel.

20He sacrificed all the priests of the high places on the altars located there, and burned human bones on them. Then he returned to Jerusalem.

21The king ordered all the people, “Observe the Passover of the Lord your God, as prescribed in this scroll of the covenant.”

22He issued this edict because a Passover like this had not been observed since the days of the judges who led Israel; it was neglected for the entire period of the kings of Israel and Judah.

23But in the eighteenth year of King Josiah’s reign, such a Passover of the Lord was observed in Jerusalem.

24Josiah also got rid of the ritual pits used to conjure up spirits, the magicians, personal idols, disgusting images, and all the detestable idols that had appeared in the land of Judah and in Jerusalem. In this way he carried out the terms of the law recorded on the scroll that Hilkiah the priest had discovered in the Lord’s temple.

25No king before or after repented before the Lord as he did, with his whole heart, soul, and being in accordance with the whole law of Moses.

26Yet the Lord’s great anger against Judah did not subside; he was still infuriated by all the things Manasseh had done.

27The Lord announced, “I will also spurn Judah, just as I spurned Israel. I will reject this city that I chose—both Jerusalem and the temple, about which I said, ‘I will live there.’”

28The rest of the events of Josiah’s reign and all his accomplishments are recorded in the scroll called the Annals of the Kings of Judah.

29During Josiah’s reign Pharaoh Necho king of Egypt marched toward the Euphrates River to help the king of Assyria. King Josiah marched out to fight him, but Necho killed him at Megiddo when he saw him.

30His servants transported his dead body from Megiddo in a chariot and brought it to Jerusalem, where they buried him in his tomb. The people of the land took Josiah’s son Jehoahaz, poured olive oil on his head, and made him king in his father’s place.

31Jehoahaz was twenty-three years old when he became king, and he reigned three months in Jerusalem. His mother was Hamutal the daughter of Jeremiah, from Libnah.

32He did evil in the sight of the Lord as his ancestors had done.

33Pharaoh Necho imprisoned him in Riblah in the land of Hamath and prevented him from ruling in Jerusalem. He imposed on the land a special tax of 100 talents of silver and a talent of gold.

34Pharaoh Necho made Josiah’s son Eliakim king in Josiah’s place, and changed his name to Jehoiakim. He took Jehoahaz to Egypt, where he died.

35Jehoiakim paid Pharaoh the required amount of silver and gold, but to meet Pharaoh’s demands Jehoiakim had to tax the land. He collected an assessed amount from each man among the people of the land in order to pay Pharaoh Necho.

36Jehoiakim was twenty-five years old when he became king, and he reigned for eleven years in Jerusalem. His mother was Zebidah the daughter of Pedaiah, from Rumah.

Summary

Josiah gathers the people and reads them the Book of the Covenant, renewing Judah's commitment to the LORD (vv. 1-3). He then leads the most sweeping reformation in Judah's history, removing idolatrous vessels and priests from the temple, tearing down the Asherah and the high places from Geba to Beersheba, defiling Topheth where children had been sacrificed, and destroying the altar at Bethel in fulfillment of an old prophecy (vv. 4-20). He celebrates a Passover unlike any kept since the days of the judges, and no king before or after him turned to the LORD with such wholehearted devotion (vv. 21-25). Yet the LORD's fierce anger against Judah does not relent because of the sins Manasseh had committed, and He resolves to remove Judah from His sight as He had Israel (vv. 26-27). Josiah is killed in battle against Pharaoh Necho at Megiddo, and the chapter closes with the brief, evil reigns of his sons Jehoahaz and Jehoiakim, both controlled by Egypt (vv. 28-37).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Jeremiah or a prophetic school. Originally one book with 1 Kings. Covers approximately 250 years (850-586 BC) from Elijah's translation through the Babylonian exile.
Historical Context: Chapter 23 describes the most sweeping religious reform in the history of Judah — Josiah's systematic destruction of every form of idolatry throughout the land. Following the discovery of the Book of the Law (chapter 22), Josiah moves from conviction to action with extraordinary thoroughness. He cleanses the Temple, tears down the high places from Geba to Beersheba (the full extent of Judah), defiles Topheth where children were sacrificed, removes astral worship, destroys the shrines Solomon had built for foreign wives' gods (standing for 350 years!), and extends his purge into former Israelite territory — including the altar at Bethel that Jeroboam I had erected. He then celebrates a Passover of unprecedented scope. Yet the chapter ends with devastating irony: despite all this, "the LORD turned not from the fierceness of his great wrath" (v.26).
The historical setting is the collapse of Assyria (c. 627-609 BC). Josiah exploited Assyrian weakness to extend Judah's sovereignty into the former northern kingdom, explaining his authority at Bethel and the cities of Samaria (vv.15-20). His death at Megiddo in 609 BC came when Pharaoh Neco marched to support the last Assyrian remnant against Babylon at Carchemish. Josiah tried to stop Neco — perhaps seeing Babylon as a lesser threat, or perhaps fulfilling his covenantal obligation against Egypt (Deuteronomy 17:16). Whatever his reasoning, he was killed, and with him died Judah's last hope of reform. His four successors — Jehoahaz, Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin, and Zedekiah — were uniformly evil. The chapter functions as Judah's finest hour and its funeral: the greatest king, the greatest reform, the greatest Passover — all unable to turn aside God's determined judgment because of Manasseh's irreversible corruption.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: An entire book of the covenant read aloud to elders, priests, prophets and people together before a single reform begins, cleansing that starts inside the temple itself before it ever reaches the countryside, a three-hundred-fifty-year-old shrine built for Solomon's foreign wives finally torn down, a prophecy from three centuries earlier fulfilled exactly when bones are burned on Bethel's altar, and one unnamed prophet's tomb deliberately left undisturbed in the middle of an otherwise total purge
Covenant Renewal and Temple Cleansing (vv.1-14): Josiah gathers all Judah — elders, priests, prophets, and "all the people, both small and great" (v.2) — and reads the entire Book of the Covenant aloud. He then stands by the pillar (the traditional place of covenant-making) and commits the nation to obedience "with all their heart and all their soul" (v.3). The cleansing begins in the Temple itself: vessels for Baal, Asherah, and the host of heaven are burned in the Kidron Valley (v.4). He deposes the idolatrous priests, removes the Asherah pole from the Temple, breaks down the houses of cult prostitutes (v.7), defiles Topheth to prevent child sacrifice (v.10), removes the horses and chariots dedicated to the sun god (v.11), and destroys the altars Manasseh had built in the Temple courts (v.12). He even defiles the high places Solomon built for Ashtoreth, Chemosh, and Milcom (v.13) — shrines that had stood for over 350 years.
Bethel, the Passover, and Josiah's Death (vv.15-37): Josiah's reform extends to Bethel, where he tears down Jeroboam's altar and high place — burning it to powder and burning the Asherah (v.15). He then fulfills the prophecy of 1 Kings 13:2: taking bones from nearby tombs, he burns them on the altar to defile it (v.16). When he sees the tomb of the unnamed prophet who originally spoke against this altar (1 Kings 13), Josiah commands it be left undisturbed (vv.17-18). He extends this purge through the cities of Samaria (v.19) and executes the priests of the high places (v.20). Returning to Jerusalem, he commands a Passover "as it is written in the book of this covenant" (v.21) — a Passover unmatched since the days of the judges (v.22). Verse 25 gives the ultimate eulogy: "like unto him was there no king before him... nor did any like him arise after him." Yet verse 26 shatters the hope: God's wrath remains kindled because of Manasseh. Josiah dies at Megiddo fighting Pharaoh Neco (v.29), and the rapid succession of evil kings — Jehoahaz (3 months), Jehoiakim (11 years) — begins Judah's final descent.

Deep Dive

This Deep Dive was put together with AI help, drawing on the commentators named below. I’m a learner, not a scholar, so test everything against Scripture. Spot a mistake or have an idea? Let me know. How this site is made

Setting: Jerusalem, 622–609 BC. In Josiah's eighteenth year, while the temple was being repaired, Hilkiah the high priest found "the Book of the Law" (22:8). When it was read to him, Josiah tore his clothes, because Judah had ignored it for generations. The prophetess Huldah confirmed that the curses written in it would fall on Jerusalem, but promised that Josiah himself would die before the disaster came (22:15–20). Assyria, which had dominated the region for a century, was collapsing: Babylon would take Nineveh in 612 BC. That left Josiah free to act, even in the old northern kingdom. This chapter tells what he did with the book, and then how quickly everything fell apart after him. 2 Chronicles 34–35 tells the same story with more detail on the Passover and the battle.

Josiah's Reforms (vv1–20)

  • "All the elders… all the people, both small and great" (vv1–2). Josiah does not keep the book to his court. He gathers the whole nation, including "the priests and the prophets." Jeremiah, who began preaching five years earlier (Jer 1:2), may well have been in the crowd. Deuteronomy told Israel to read the law aloud to everyone, men, women, children and foreigners (Deut 31:10–13), and Joshua did exactly that (Josh 8:34–35). The Tyndale notes point to both.
  • "He read in their hearing" (v2). The king himself reads. Deuteronomy pictured the king as a man with his own copy of the law, reading it "all the days of his life" (Deut 17:18–19). "The Book of the Covenant" here is the same scroll found in 22:8, called by what it is: the terms of the relationship between God and his people.
  • "The king stood by the pillar and made a covenant" (v3). The pillar was the king's place in the temple court. Joash was crowned standing there (11:14). Covenant renewals mark turning points all through Israel's history: Joshua at Shechem (Josh 24), Solomon at the dedication (1 Kings 8), Jehoiada with the boy Joash (11:17). "With all his heart and all his soul" echoes the great command of Deuteronomy 6:5.
  • "All the people joined in the covenant" (v3). Literally "stood to the covenant." They took their stand with the king. Whether their hearts went as far as their feet is the question the rest of the chapter, and Jeremiah, will answer.
  • Cleaning the temple first (v4). The cleansing starts at the center. "The vessels made for Baal, for Asherah, and for all the host of heaven" had been sitting in the LORD's own house. Josiah burns them in the Kidron Valley, east of the city, and carries the ashes to Bethel. Bethel was the site of Jeroboam's rival altar (1 Kings 12:28–29). Dumping pagan ashes there defiled it for good.
  • "He deposed the priests" (v5). The word is kemarim, which the lexicon gives as "idol-priests." In the Old Testament it is used only for priests of false gods (also Hos 10:5; Zeph 1:4, where Zephaniah, preaching in Josiah's day, names them too). These were not Levites but men the kings of Judah had appointed. "The constellations" is probably the signs of the zodiac. Star worship came in with Assyrian influence under Manasseh (21:3–5).
  • The Asherah burned and its dust thrown "upon the graves of the common people" (v6). Manasseh had set up a carved Asherah pole, the symbol of a Canaanite mother goddess, inside the temple (21:7). Josiah burns it, grinds it to powder, and scatters it on a common burial ground. Graves were unclean under the law (Num 19:16), so the idol ends as defilement on top of defilement.
  • "The houses of the male cult prostitutes… in the house of the LORD" (v7). The Tyndale notes call it what it was: shocking. Canaanite fertility religion used ritual sex, and it had been happening in rooms inside the temple precincts, where women also wove "hangings" for the Asherah. JFB thinks the "houses" were tents. Earlier kings had tried to clear these men out (1 Kings 15:12; 22:46). They kept coming back.
  • "From Geba to Beersheba" (v8). That is Judah from its northern border to its southern edge. Josiah brings the country priests into Jerusalem and defiles their local shrines. "The gate of Joshua the governor of the city" appears only here. It was probably a city official's gate, not the famous Joshua.
  • "The priests of the high places did not come up to the altar" (v9). These priests were probably Levites who had served at local shrines. Deuteronomy allowed a country Levite to serve in Jerusalem (Deut 18:6–8), but these men had worshiped wrongly, so they could not serve at the altar. Josiah still let them eat the priests' share of unleavened bread among their brothers. He removed them from office without throwing them out of the family.
  • "He defiled Topheth… that no one might burn his son or his daughter as an offering to Molech" (v10). Topheth was in the Valley of Hinnom, on Jerusalem's southwest side, where children had been burned as sacrifices. Ahaz and Manasseh both did this to their own sons (16:3; 21:6). Jeremiah later says the valley will be renamed "the Valley of Slaughter" (Jer 7:31–32). In Hebrew the valley is Ge Hinnom, which came into Greek as Gehenna, the word Jesus used for hell (Mark 9:43). JFB passes on the old rabbinic idea that the name came from toph, "drum," beaten to drown out the children's cries. That is a tradition, not something the text says.
  • "The horses that the kings of Judah had dedicated to the sun… the chariots of the sun" (v11). Across the ancient Near East the sun god was pictured driving a chariot across the sky, and the Tyndale notes say archaeology points to sun worship in Israel well before this. Ezekiel, a generation later, still sees men in the temple court with their backs to the sanctuary, bowing to the sun in the east (Ezek 8:16). The ESV footnotes that the word for "precincts" is uncertain.
  • Rooftop altars and Manasseh's altars (v12). Flat roofs were used for offerings to the stars (Jer 19:13; Zeph 1:5). Even Manasseh's late repentance had only gone so far: Chronicles says he removed these altars (2 Chr 33:15), but here they are again, and Josiah smashes them for good.
  • "The mount of corruption" (v13). This is the southern end of the Mount of Olives, facing the temple. The lexicon notes that har hammashchit, "mount of destruction," is probably a deliberate wordplay on har hammishchah, "mount of anointing" (from the olive oil). For more than three hundred years Solomon's shrines to Ashtoreth, Chemosh and Milcom had stood there (1 Kings 11:5–7). No king, not even Hezekiah, had removed them. Josiah does.
  • "Filled their places with the bones of men" (v14). Human bones made a place permanently unclean. No one would rebuild a shrine on a graveyard.
  • Bethel (v15). Josiah now crosses into the old northern kingdom, which Assyria had held since 722 BC. With Assyria failing, no one stopped him. Jeroboam's altar, "who made Israel to sin," the refrain of all of Kings, is pulled down and burned. The ESV notes that the Greek translation reads "broke in pieces its stones."
  • The bones on the altar (v16). Here a prophecy made three centuries earlier comes true to the letter. A man of God from Judah stood at this altar while Jeroboam offered incense and cried: "a son shall be born to the house of David, Josiah by name, and he shall sacrifice on you the priests of the high places… and human bones shall be burned on you" (1 Kings 13:2). JFB calls it "one of the most remarkable prophecies in the Bible." The narrator says it plainly: "according to the word of the LORD."
  • "Let him be; let no man move his bones" (vv17–18). The men of Bethel still remember whose tomb it is. That prophet himself had asked to be buried beside the man of God (1 Kings 13:31–32), and now the bones of both men are spared. The word of God honors the man who spoke it, even though he died for disobeying it (1 Kings 13:24).
  • "He sacrificed all the priests of the high places who were there" (v20). This is harsher than what Josiah did in Judah (v9). These northern priests ran a rival cult that had never been part of the LORD's worship, and Deuteronomy commands death for those who lead the people to other gods (Deut 13:5; 17:2–5). It also fulfills 1 Kings 13:2 word for word. Modern readers recoil at it, and it is right to see it as the old covenant's severe judgment, not a model for Christian practice.
  • Commentators: Henry outlines the chapter as Josiah "rooting out idols and idolatry, with all the relics thereof, in all places" as far as his power reached. Guzik notes that the cleansing began at the temple and worked outward, and that Josiah dealt with the priests who promoted the idols, not only the idols. He also credits the people's response in v3 to God's Spirit moving on a crowd. JFB says the ashes were taken to Bethel "to associate ideas of horror" with the place, and that the men of Bethel in v17 were Israelites who remembered the story, "not the Assyrian colonists" brought in after the exile of 722 BC (17:24).

Josiah Restores the Passover (vv21–27)

  • "Keep the Passover… as it is written in this Book of the Covenant" (v21). The reform ends with a feast, not just a demolition. Passover remembered the night God brought Israel out of Egypt (Exod 12), and Deuteronomy said it must be kept "at the place that the LORD will choose" (Deut 16:1–8), which is why Josiah holds it in Jerusalem.
  • "No such Passover had been kept since the days of the judges" (v22). Hezekiah held a great Passover about a century earlier (2 Chr 30), but it was a month late and many who ate it were ceremonially unclean (2 Chr 30:2–3, 18). Josiah's kept the book exactly. Chronicles gives the details: thousands of animals, the Levites organized by family, the singers in their places (2 Chr 35:1–19). The Tyndale notes draw the same contrast.
  • "Mediums and the necromancers and the household gods" (v24). Josiah goes after private, household religion as well as public shrines. Manasseh had practiced all of this himself (21:6). The "household gods" (teraphim) were small figures kept at home, like the ones Rachel stole from Laban (Gen 31:19).
  • "Before him there was no king like him… nor did any like him arise after him" (v25). This is the highest praise in Kings. Hezekiah was praised for trust (18:5); Josiah is praised for obedience. The words "with all his heart and with all his soul and with all his might" are the great command of Deuteronomy 6:5, and Josiah is the only person in the Bible of whom they are said.
  • "Still the LORD did not turn from the burning of his great wrath" (v26). This is the hardest verse in the chapter. The best king Judah ever had, after the most thorough reform ever carried out, and judgment is still coming. The reason given is "all the provocations with which Manasseh had provoked him." Manasseh's sin was not only his own. The nation followed him gladly for fifty-five years, and Jeremiah shows the change under Josiah did not go deep. God said of Judah in Josiah's day that she "did not return to me with her whole heart, but in pretense" (Jer 3:10). One king's heart could not stand in for a nation's.
  • "I will remove Judah also out of my sight, as I have removed Israel" (v27). God does not deny that he chose Jerusalem and put his name there (1 Kings 8:29). He says the choice does not protect a people who will not keep the covenant. Jeremiah preached the same thing at the temple gate: do not trust in the words "the temple of the LORD" (Jer 7:4).
  • Commentators: Guzik calls Josiah's reform an Old Testament picture of putting Scripture above everything, but stresses that it stayed largely outward, and cites Jeremiah 3:10 for the people's pretense. He also notes that a king can command the feast but not the heart. Henry agrees: the people "were turned by force" and kept their love for idols. JFB simply repeats the verdict that "the Lord turned not from the fierceness of his wrath," and offers no separate notes on vv24–25 or v27.

Josiah's Death in Battle (vv28–30)

  • "Pharaoh Neco king of Egypt went up to the king of Assyria" (v29). The year is 609 BC. Nineveh had fallen, and the last Assyrian king was holding out at Haran, in northern Mesopotamia. Egypt now wanted Assyria alive as a buffer against Babylon, so Neco marched north to help. The KJV reads "against the king of Assyria," and older commentators followed that. The Babylonian Chronicle has since shown that Egypt was Assyria's ally, and the Hebrew preposition can mean "to" or "toward." Modern translations follow the evidence.
  • "King Josiah went to meet him" (v29). Kings gives no reason. Josiah may have wanted to keep Assyria from recovering, or to stop Egypt controlling the road through his land. Chronicles adds that Neco warned him, "God has commanded me to hurry… Cease opposing God," that Josiah "did not listen to the words of Neco from the mouth of God," and that he disguised himself and was hit by archers (2 Chr 35:21–23).
  • "At Megiddo" (v29). Megiddo guarded the pass where the coastal road crossed into the Jezreel Valley, one of the great battlefields of the ancient world. Its Hebrew name lies behind "Armageddon," Har-Megiddo (Rev 16:16). Zechariah later uses the mourning for Josiah, "the mourning for Hadad-rimmon in the plain of Megiddo," as a picture of Israel mourning "him whom they have pierced" (Zech 12:10–11).
  • "Buried him in his own tomb" (v30). Huldah had said he would be gathered to his grave "in peace" and not see the disaster (22:20). He died in battle, but he was buried in Jerusalem with honor before any foreign army entered the city. Chronicles adds that Jeremiah wrote laments for him and singers still sang them years later (2 Chr 35:25).
  • "The people of the land took Jehoahaz" (v30). "The people of the land," the landowning men of Judah, had put Josiah himself on the throne (21:24). Now they skip his older son and choose Jehoahaz, probably because he would continue his father's anti-Egyptian line.
  • Commentators: Henry is blunt: "We cannot justify Josiah's conduct," since he had no call to fight and did not seek God. He also sees mercy in it, since Josiah was spared the sight of what came next, and judgment, since the people learned his worth only by losing him. Guzik is just as critical, drawing on Chronicles: the warning came from God through Neco, and Josiah ignored it. He locates the mourning of Zechariah 12:11 at this battle.

Jehoahaz's Reign and Captivity (vv31–35)

  • "Twenty-three years old… three months" (v31). His personal name was Shallum (1 Chr 3:15; Jer 22:11). Jehoahaz, "the LORD has grasped," was the name he took as king. His mother Hamutal was from Libnah, a town in the lowlands. Her father "Jeremiah" is not the prophet, who came from Anathoth and never married (Jer 16:2).
  • "He did what was evil" (v32). Three months is long enough for the verdict. Josiah's reform did not pass to his sons. The Tyndale notes observe that the same verdict falls on every one of them (vv32, 37; 24:9, 19).
  • "Pharaoh Neco put him in bonds at Riblah" (v33). Riblah was on the Orontes River in Syria, a military base where great powers summoned their vassals. Nebuchadnezzar later uses the same place to pass sentence on Zedekiah (25:6). The tribute was a hundred talents of silver and one of gold. The ESV footnote puts a talent at about 75 pounds, so about three and three-quarter tons of silver.
  • "He came to Egypt and died there" (v34). Jeremiah wrote of him: "Weep bitterly for him who goes away, for he shall return no more" (Jer 22:10–12). He is the first king of Judah to die in exile.
  • Eliakim renamed Jehoiakim (v34). Renaming a vassal king showed who was in charge, and the Tyndale notes say this followed Assyrian practice. Neco does not even change the god in the name: Eliakim, "God raises up," becomes Jehoiakim, "the LORD raises up." The point is that Pharaoh did the naming.
  • "He taxed the land… from everyone according to his assessment" (v35). The king pays Egypt by squeezing his people. Jeremiah adds that while they paid, Jehoiakim built himself a fine palace with unpaid forced labor (Jer 22:13–17).
  • Commentators: Guzik notes that Shallum was a throne-named younger son chosen over the custom, and that Jehoahaz is missing from Matthew's genealogy of Jesus. Henry passes quickly over "the bad reigns of his two sons." JFB has no notes on vv30–37 at all.

Jehoiakim Reigns in Judah (vv36–37)

  • "Twenty-five years old… eleven years" (v36). About 609–598 BC. He was older than Jehoahaz, so the people had passed him over the first time. His mother Zebidah was from Rumah, a town that may have been in Galilee; if so, Josiah had married into the north.
  • "He did what was evil" (v37). Kings gives one line. Jeremiah fills it in: Jehoiakim had the prophet Uriah hunted down in Egypt and killed (Jer 26:20–23). When Jeremiah's scroll was read to him, he cut it up column by column and threw it into the fire (Jer 36:23). His father had torn his clothes when he heard God's word (22:11). The son tore up the word itself.
  • Commentators: Guzik draws the same contrast from Jeremiah 22 and 36: forced labor for his palace, and a scroll burned in the brazier. Henry gives no separate comment on these verses.

Pointing to Christ

Josiah is the best of the kings, the only one who loved the LORD with all his heart, soul and might, and it was not enough. He cleaned the temple, kept the Passover and fulfilled an ancient prophecy, but he could not change the hearts of his people, and he died at Megiddo with judgment still coming. His sons undid his work in months. The chapter shows the limit of every good king and every outward reform. What Judah needed was a King who would keep the great command perfectly and also write God's law on his people's hearts (Jer 31:33). Jesus kept the Passover in Jerusalem and then became the Passover lamb (1 Cor 5:7). Zechariah took the mourning for Josiah and turned it into a picture of a nation mourning "him whom they have pierced." That mourning leads to a fountain opened "to cleanse them from sin" (Zech 13:1).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik draws attention to the thoroughness of Josiah's reform — it was not selective or partial but comprehensive and violent. He destroyed everything associated with false worship, from the Temple to Bethel to the cities of Samaria. Guzik also emphasizes the tragic irony: the greatest reform in Judah's history was insufficient to avert judgment. This demonstrates that external reformation, however thorough, cannot substitute for deep heart-change across a people. One king's faithfulness cannot atone for a nation's apostasy.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Faith Must Be Personally Chosen (v.3) — Josiah stood by the pillar and renewed the covenant for his own generation rather than coasting on his ancestors' faith. Have you made your own wholehearted commitment to God, or are you relying on someone else's?
  • Thorough Reform, Not Half-Measures (vv.4-20) — Josiah didn't just condemn idolatry — he burned, broke, and demolished every trace of it across the land. What do you know needs to be completely removed from your life rather than just managed?
  • A 300-Year-Old Word Still Comes True (v.16) — The prophecy naming Josiah by name was spoken three centuries before he was born, and it landed exactly as promised. Does the length of time a promise or warning has gone unfulfilled shake your confidence in it, or strengthen it?
  • The Best Human Effort Still Isn't Enough (v.26) — Despite the most sweeping reform in Judah's history, God's wrath over Manasseh's sins remained. Where are you hoping your own effort will accomplish what only a Savior can actually do?
  • Faithfulness Isn't Measured by a Peaceful Ending (v.29) — Judah's best king died young in battle, which looked like failure but wasn't. Do you measure God's approval of your life by outcomes you can see, or by simple obedience?
