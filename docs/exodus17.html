Summary

When Israel finds no water at Rephidim, the people quarrel with Moses and God instructs him to strike the rock at Horeb, from which water flows out for the people (vv. 1-7). Amalek then attacks Israel, and as long as Moses holds up his hands with the staff of God, Joshua and the army prevail in battle, with Aaron and Hur supporting Moses' arms when he grows tired (vv. 8-13). The LORD tells Moses to record this victory as a lasting memorial and declares that He will eventually blot out Amalek entirely, and Moses builds an altar there, naming it after the LORD as Israel's banner of victory (vv. 14-16).

Authorship & Background

Author: Moses (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 17 records two critical events: (1) Water from the rock at Rephidim — God provides from an impossible source; (2) The battle with Amalek — Israel's first military engagement. Both events reveal essential truths: God provides what nature cannot (water from rock), and victory depends on God's power (Moses' raised hands), not human strength alone. Joshua appears for the first time as a military leader. Moses names the altar "Jehovah-nissi" — "The LORD is my banner." God declares perpetual war against Amalek — the enemy who attacked Israel's weakest (Deuteronomy 25:17-18).
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: water struck from a rock that God says he will be standing on when it is struck, a place named twice for the same complaint, once for testing and once for quarreling, a battle whose outcome tracks not the sword in the valley but a pair of raised hands on the hill, arms grown too heavy to hold up without two men bracing them on a stone, and an altar named the LORD is my banner over an enemy marked for a hostility God intends to outlast the generation
Water from the Rock (vv.1-7): Israel camps at Rephidim — no water (v.1). They "chide" with Moses: "Give us water" (v.2). Moses: "Why do ye tempt the LORD?" (v.2). The people are "almost ready to stone me" (v.4). God instructs: take the rod, go to the rock at Horeb, "thou shalt smite the rock, and there shall come water out of it" (v.6). God says "I will stand before thee there upon the rock" — God positions Himself on the rock that is struck (v.6). Moses obeys; water flows (v.6). He names the place "Massah" (testing) and "Meribah" (quarreling) (v.7). Israel's question: "Is the LORD among us, or not?" (v.7).
The Battle with Amalek (vv.8-16): Amalek attacks (v.8) — descendants of Esau, attacking Israel's rear (Deuteronomy 25:18). Moses sends Joshua to fight and goes to the hilltop with the rod of God (v.9). When Moses' hands are raised, Israel prevails; when lowered, Amalek prevails (v.11). Moses' hands grow heavy — Aaron and Hur support them on a stone until sunset (v.12). Joshua defeats Amalek (v.13). God commands: "Write this for a memorial" — the first command to write Scripture (v.14). God declares: "I will utterly put out the remembrance of Amalek" (v.14). Moses builds an altar: "Jehovah-nissi" — "The LORD is my banner" (v.15).

Deep Dive

Setting: The Sinai peninsula, some weeks after the exodus. God has given Israel quail and the daily manna in the wilderness of Sin (ch 16), and the people are still making their way south toward the mountain where God first met Moses. Chapter 17 brings two tests, one from within and one from outside. At Rephidim there is no water, and the people turn on Moses and put God on trial. Then the Amalekites attack, and Israel fights its first battle, won not by the sword alone but by Moses' lifted hands. Both stories were remembered for centuries: Massah and Meribah as a warning (Ps 95:8–9; Heb 3:7–11), and Amalek as Israel's lasting enemy.

Water from the Rock (vv1–7)

  • "Moved on from the wilderness of Sin by stages, according to the commandment of the LORD" (v1). Israel camps where God leads them, by the cloud. "And camped at Rephidim, but there was no water." God led them to a dry place. Like Marah (15:22–25), it is a test he arranged. Rephidim's location is uncertain. Many have placed it in Wadi Feiran, a valley on the way to the traditional site of Mount Sinai, though Feiran has an oasis, and others suggest a different valley nearby.
  • "The people quarreled with Moses" (v2). "Quarreled" is riv, the word for a legal dispute or lawsuit. They are bringing charges. "Give us water to drink." The demand is a natural need, but it comes as an accusation, not a prayer.
  • "Why do you test the LORD?" "Test" (nasah) means to put to the proof. God tests his people to strengthen them (15:25; 16:4). When people test God, they demand that he prove himself on their terms. The Tyndale notes draw the line well: there is a test of faith that says "I believe; help my unbelief" (Mark 9:24), and a test of doubt that puts God in the dock. Jesus quotes the law that came from this very place, "You shall not put the Lord your God to the test" (Deut 6:16; Matt 4:7).
  • "To kill us and our children and our livestock with thirst?" (v3). The same charge as in 14:11 and 16:3. Within weeks of the sea, they forget all over again.
  • "What shall I do with this people? They are almost ready to stone me" (v4). Moses does the right thing: he cries to the LORD. His prayer is honest and desperate. Stoning was a community's way of executing someone. This is a real threat.
  • "Pass on before the people, taking with you some of the elders" (v5). Moses is to walk out in front of the angry crowd, with witnesses. "Take in your hand the staff with which you struck the Nile." That staff had turned water into blood. Now it will bring water from stone.
  • "I will stand before you there on the rock at Horeb" (v6). Normally people stand before God. Here God says he will stand before Moses, on the rock. "Horeb" is another name for the region of Mount Sinai (3:1), so Israel is close to the mountain.
  • "You shall strike the rock, and water shall come out of it." In a desert, rock is the last place to look for water. The psalms remember the abundance: God "split rocks in the wilderness and gave them drink abundantly as from the deep" (Ps 78:15–16; 105:41). "Moses did so, in the sight of the elders." The elders are witnesses that this was God's doing.
  • The two rocks. About forty years later, at another Meribah, God tells Moses to speak to the rock, and Moses angrily strikes it twice. For that he is barred from entering the land (Num 20:2–13). Here, striking is exactly what God commanded.
  • "He called the name of the place Massah and Meribah" (v7). Massah means "testing" and Meribah means "quarreling," from the two verbs in v2. The place is named for Israel's sin, not God's gift. Later Scripture uses the names as a warning: "Do not harden your hearts, as at Meribah, as on the day at Massah in the wilderness" (Ps 95:8; Heb 3:7–11).
  • "Is the LORD among us or not?" This is the heart of the test. They have the pillar of cloud over them, manna on the ground every morning, and the sea behind them. Still they ask. Unbelief does not lack evidence. It refuses it.
  • Commentators: Henry's outline puts it simply: "In their want they chided Moses," and he closes the chapter by saying "these things which happened to them are written for our instruction in our spiritual journey and warfare," echoing 1 Corinthians 10:11. JFB places Rephidim at Wady Feiran and notes that the people's conduct "amounted even to 'a tempting of the Lord,'" that Massah and Meribah mean "temptation" and "chiding, strife," and that Meribah's word lies behind "provocation" in Hebrews 3:8. Guzik says the people were right to be worried but wrong to blame Moses instead of looking to God, and that God's answer made no human sense and required Moses to step out in front of a hostile crowd. He links the water from the rock with Jesus' promise of living water, which John explains as the Spirit (John 7:37–39).

Israel Defeats Amalek (vv8–16)

  • "Then Amalek came and fought with Israel at Rephidim" (v8). The Amalekites descended from Esau's grandson Amalek (Gen 36:12), so they were distant relatives of Israel. They were desert raiders who ranged across the Negev and Sinai. Deuteronomy adds what Exodus leaves out: they attacked "when you were faint and weary, and cut off your tail, those who were lagging behind you, and he did not fear God" (Deut 25:17–18). Their attack picked off the weakest stragglers.
  • "Moses said to Joshua" (v9). This is Joshua's first appearance. He is introduced without explanation, already Moses' trusted helper. His name was originally Hoshea, "salvation," and Moses later called him Joshua, "the LORD saves" (Num 13:16). In Greek it is Iēsous, the same name as Jesus. "Choose for us men, and go out and fight." Israel had never fought a battle. At the sea, they were told to be still (14:14). Now they must fight, but the victory will still be God's.
  • "I will stand on the top of the hill with the staff of God in my hand." The staff, now called "the staff of God," is the sign of God's power at work.
  • "Moses, Aaron, and Hur went up to the top of the hill" (v10). Hur appears here for the first time. He will help Aaron lead the people while Moses is on the mountain (24:14). Jewish tradition, followed by Josephus, says he was Miriam's husband, but the Bible does not say so. A Hur of the tribe of Judah was grandfather of Bezalel, the tabernacle's master craftsman (31:2), and that may be the same man.
  • "Whenever Moses held up his hand, Israel prevailed" (v11). The lifted hand with the staff may picture prayer, God's power, or both. Lifted hands are a posture of prayer in Scripture (Ps 28:2; 1 Tim 2:8), and Moses later "stretched out his hands" in prayer (9:29). The point is clear: the battle in the valley depended on what happened on the hill. Joshua's sword mattered, but it was not what decided the day.
  • "But Moses' hands grew weary" (v12). "Weary" is literally "heavy," kaved again. Moses cannot do this alone. "They took a stone and put it under him, and he sat on it, while Aaron and Hur held up his hands." Even the greatest leader needs others to hold him up. "His hands were steady until the going down of the sun." "Steady" is emunah, firmness or faithfulness, the root behind the word "amen." It is usually translated "faithfulness."
  • "Joshua overwhelmed Amalek and his people with the sword" (v13). "Overwhelmed" means weakened or defeated. They are beaten but not destroyed. Amalek returns later (Num 14:45; Judg 6:3).
  • "Write this as a memorial in a book" (v14). This is the first time God tells anyone to write something down in a book (a scroll). The command to Moses to write is one of the reasons tradition holds him as the author of much of the Pentateuch. "Recite it in the ears of Joshua." The future leader must remember who the enemy is.
  • "I will utterly blot out the memory of Amalek from under heaven." This judgment is hard for modern readers. It is aimed at a people who attacked the weak without cause and "did not fear God" (Deut 25:18), and it was not carried out for centuries. God gave Amalek long time. King Saul was later sent to carry out this judgment and failed to obey, which cost him his kingdom (1 Sam 15). Haman, who tried to wipe out the Jews in Persia, is called "the Agagite," which may tie him to Agag, Saul's Amalekite enemy (Esth 3:1). If so, the old enmity runs through the Bible's history. The last Amalekites are struck down in Hezekiah's time (1 Chr 4:43).
  • "Moses built an altar and called the name of it, The LORD Is My Banner" (v15). In Hebrew, Yahweh Nissi. A nes is a standard or signal pole raised high so troops can rally around it. The staff Moses held up was Israel's standard, but the real banner was the LORD himself. Isaiah says the root of Jesse "shall stand as a signal (nes) for the peoples" (Isa 11:10). The same word names the pole on which Moses later lifted the bronze serpent (Num 21:8–9).
  • "A hand upon the throne of the LORD!" (v16). The Hebrew is difficult. The word for "throne" here (kes) is an unusual short form found only in this verse. Some take the line as an oath, with a hand raised toward God's throne, as the ESV does. Others read it as Amalek raising its hand against God's throne, which is why God wars against them. Many scholars think the word was originally nes, "banner," a very small change in Hebrew letters, giving "a hand upon the banner of the LORD," which fits v15 well. All the readings agree on the result: "The LORD will have war with Amalek from generation to generation."
  • Commentators: Henry says Amalek "basely fell upon their rear, and smote those that were faint and feeble." On the raised hands he writes, "Praying work, if done with due intenseness of mind and vigour of affection, will be found hard work," and on Aaron and Hur, "We should not be shy either of asking help from others or giving help to others, for we are members one of another." He sees Joshua, sharing Jesus' name, as a type of Christ. JFB calls Amalek "descendants of Esau" with a "deep-seated grudge" who made a "dastardly, insidious surprise on the rear," and calls this "the earliest notice of a young warrior destined to act a prominent part in the history of Israel." He pictures Moses holding up "the wonder-working rod" as standard-bearer and intercessor. JFB has no comment on Aaron and Hur or the altar's name. Guzik notes that the Moses who once killed an Egyptian with his own hands (2:12) now lets Joshua fight while he prays, and that prayer is real work that needs partners. He reads Amalek, by application, as a picture of the flesh, the sinful nature that wars against the Spirit (Gal 5:17) and must be fought until it is beaten.

Pointing to Christ

Paul writes that Israel "drank from the spiritual Rock that followed them, and the Rock was Christ" (1 Cor 10:4). The rock was struck once, in the sight of witnesses, and water flowed for a thirsty, grumbling people who did not deserve it. Christ was struck once for all (Heb 9:28; Isa 53:4–5), and from him flows living water, the Spirit given to all who come to him and drink (John 7:37–39). In the battle with Amalek, a man named Joshua, "the LORD saves," wins the victory in the valley while Moses intercedes on the hill. The greater Joshua, Jesus, both fights for his people and intercedes for them, and his hands never grow tired: "he always lives to make intercession for them" (Heb 7:25). The LORD is still the banner his people rally to, lifted up on a cross so that all may look and live (John 3:14–15; 12:32).

Reflection

  • Demanding Instead of Trusting (v.2) — Israel's thirst turns into an accusation against Moses rather than a request lifted to God. When you're in need, does your instinct lean toward trusting prayer or toward demanding and blaming?
  • The Rock That Is Struck (v.6) — God tells Moses He will stand on the very rock that gets struck so that water can flow to the people. How does knowing Christ was struck once, for good, change how you come to Him for what you need?
  • The Power of Raised Hands (v.11) — Israel wins the battle only as long as Moses' hands stay lifted in intercession. Who is interceding for you right now, and who might need you to lift your hands for them?
  • Held Up When You're Weary (v.12) — When Moses' arms grow too tired to hold up, Aaron and Hur physically support them until the battle is won. Who has God placed in your life to hold you up when you can't keep going alone?
  • The Lord Is My Banner (v.15) — Moses names the altar after the truth that the victory belonged to God, not to Israel's own strength. What are you tempted to rally around instead of God when the fight gets hard?
