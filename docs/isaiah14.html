1For the LORD will have compassion on Jacob; once again He will choose Israel and settle them in their own land. The foreigner will join them and unite with the house of Jacob.

2The nations will escort Israel and bring it to its homeland. Then the house of Israel will possess the nations as menservants and maidservants in the LORD’s land. They will make captives of their captors and rule over their oppressors.

3On the day that the LORD gives you rest from your pain and torment, and from the hard labor into which you were forced,

4you will sing this song of contempt against the king of Babylon: How the oppressor has ceased, and how his fury has ended!

5The LORD has broken the staff of the wicked, the scepter of the rulers.

6It struck the peoples in anger with unceasing blows; it subdued the nations in rage with relentless persecution.

7All the earth is at peace and at rest; they break out in song.

8Even the cypresses and cedars of Lebanon exult over you: “Since you have been laid low, no woodcutter comes against us.”

9Sheol beneath is eager to meet you upon your arrival. It stirs the spirits of the dead to greet you—all the rulers of the earth. It makes all the kings of the nations rise from their thrones.

10They will all respond to you, saying, “You too have become weak, as we are; you have become like us!”

11Your pomp has been brought down to Sheol, along with the music of your harps. Maggots are your bed and worms your blanket.

12How you have fallen from heaven, O day star, son of the dawn! You have been cut down to the ground, O destroyer of nations.

13You said in your heart: “I will ascend to the heavens; I will raise my throne above the stars of God. I will sit on the mount of assembly, in the far reaches of the north.

14I will ascend above the tops of the clouds; I will make myself like the Most High.”

15But you will be brought down to Sheol, to the lowest depths of the Pit.

16Those who see you will stare; they will ponder your fate: “Is this the man who shook the earth and made the kingdoms tremble,

17who turned the world into a desert and destroyed its cities, who refused to let the captives return to their homes?”

18All the kings of the nations lie in state, each in his own tomb.

19But you are cast out of your grave like a rejected branch, covered by those slain with the sword, and dumped into a rocky pit like a carcass trampled underfoot.

20You will not join them in burial, since you have destroyed your land and slaughtered your own people. The offspring of the wicked will never again be mentioned.

21Prepare a place to slaughter his sons for the iniquities of their forefathers. They will never rise up to possess a land or cover the earth with their cities.

22“I will rise up against them,” declares the LORD of Hosts. “I will cut off from Babylon her name and her remnant, her offspring and her posterity,” declares the LORD.

23“I will make her a place for owls and for swamplands; I will sweep her away with the broom of destruction,” declares the LORD of Hosts.

24The LORD of Hosts has sworn: “Surely, as I have planned, so will it be; as I have purposed, so will it stand.

25I will break Assyria in My land; I will trample him on My mountain. His yoke will be taken off My people, and his burden removed from their shoulders.”

26This is the plan devised for the whole earth, and this is the hand stretched out over all the nations.

27The LORD of Hosts has purposed, and who can thwart Him? His hand is outstretched, so who can turn it back?

28In the year that King Ahaz died, this burden was received:

29Do not rejoice, all you Philistines, that the rod that struck you is broken. For a viper will spring from the root of the snake, and a flying serpent from its egg.

30Then the firstborn of the poor will find pasture, and the needy will lie down in safety, but I will kill your root by famine, and your remnant will be slain.

31Wail, O gate! Cry out, O city! Melt away, all you Philistines! For a cloud of smoke comes from the north, and there are no stragglers in its ranks.

Summary

Israel's coming restoration opens the chapter: God will again choose Jacob, settle His people in their own land, and give them rest from bondage, with other nations even joining and serving them (vv. 1-2). Verses 3-23 form a taunt song against the fallen king of Babylon, mocking his descent from proud self-exaltation — boasting he would rise above the stars and rival the Most High — down to humiliation in Sheol, stripped of splendor and denied burial. A brief oracle against Assyria follows, declaring God's purpose to break its yoke (vv. 24-27), and the chapter closes with a warning to Philistia not to celebrate a fallen enemy, since a greater threat is still coming (vv. 28-32).

Authorship & Background

Author: Isaiah son of Amoz (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Taunt-Song and Oracles
Key Themes: Israel's future restoration, the king of Babylon's fall, the "Lucifer" passage (pride aspiring to God's throne and cast down to Sheol), God's purpose against Assyria, Philistia warned
Historical Context: Chapter 14 continues the Babylon oracle begun in chapter 13 but shifts from God's direct judgment to Israel's taunt-song over Babylon's fallen king (vv.4-23). The passage vv.12-15 ("How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer") has been interpreted on multiple levels: (1) the literal king of Babylon's fall from power; (2) a veiled description of Satan's original rebellion and expulsion from heaven; (3) both — the human king embodying the spiritual reality behind all tyrannical pride. The five "I will" statements (vv.13-14) represent the quintessence of pride: self-exaltation to God's position. After the Babylon song, brief oracles address Assyria (vv.24-27) and Philistia (vv.28-32).
The LORD Will Have Mercy on Jacob (vv.1-2): Before the taunt-song begins, two verses establish why it is being sung. For the LORD will have mercy on Jacob, and will yet choose Israel, and set them in their own land. Then a clause that is easy to read past, and the strangers shall be joined with them, and they shall cleave to the house of Jacob. The restoration is not exclusive even here, in the middle of the harshest oracles in the book.
The Taunt-Song Against the King of Babylon (vv.3-11): Thou shalt take up this proverb against the king of Babylon, and the word rendered proverb is mashal, which here means a taunt-song. It opens with a question rather than a curse, how hath the oppressor ceased, the golden city ceased. Then the reaction of the natural world, which is relief, the whole earth is at rest, and is quiet, they break forth into singing, yea, the fir trees rejoice at thee. And then the arrival in the underworld, which is the most theatrical scene in the book: hell from beneath is moved for thee, it stirreth up the dead for thee, and the kings of the nations rise from their thrones to greet him with one line, art thou also become weak as we, art thou become like unto us. And the last detail is bedding, the worm is spread under thee, and the worms cover thee.
How Art Thou Fallen from Heaven (vv.12-15): How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning. The name is not in the Hebrew. The phrase there is helel ben shachar, shining one, son of the dawn, and Lucifer is the Latin the Vulgate used for it, light-bearer, which was the ordinary Latin word for the morning star. What follows is five sentences all beginning I will, I will ascend into heaven, I will exalt my throne above the stars of God, I will sit also upon the mount of the congregation, I will ascend above the heights of the clouds, I will be like the most High. And the reply is one line, yet thou shalt be brought down to hell. From the church fathers onward this has been read as an account of Satan's fall, partly because Jesus speaks of Satan falling as lightning from heaven in Luke 10. What the passage itself is addressed to, at verse 4, is the king of Babylon.
The King's Shameful End (vv.16-23): The scene returns to the surface and to onlookers examining a corpse, they that see thee shall narrowly look upon thee, and say, Is this the man that made the earth to tremble. The specific indignity is burial, and it is stated by contrast, all the kings of the nations lie in glory, every one in his own house, but thou art cast out of thy grave like an abominable branch. Then the sentence on the dynasty rather than the man, I will cut off from Babylon the name, and remnant, and son, and nephew. And the city is left to the water, I will make it a possession for the bittern, and pools of water.
Oracle Against Assyria (vv.24-27): Four verses, and they are the ones that mattered most to the original audience, since Assyria was the power actually at the door. The LORD of hosts hath sworn, saying, Surely as I have thought, so shall it come to pass. I will break the Assyrian in my land, and upon my mountains tread him under foot. And the closing question makes the point of the whole chapter, for the LORD of hosts hath purposed, and who shall disannul it, and his hand is stretched out, and who shall turn it back.
Oracle Against Philistia (vv.28-32): Dated in the year that king Ahaz died. Rejoice not thou, whole Philistia, because the rod of him that smote thee is broken, which warns against celebrating the death of an oppressor on the grounds that the replacement will be worse, out of the serpent's root shall come forth a cockatrice. And the chapter closes with a question and an answer that turn the whole block of oracles back toward Judah, what shall one answer the messengers of the nations, that the LORD hath founded Zion, and the poor of his people shall trust in it.

Setting: Chapter 13 announced the fall of Babylon, the proud empire that would one day destroy Jerusalem and carry Judah into exile (586 BC), and that fell to the Medes and Persians in 539 BC. Chapter 14 continues the same oracle. It opens with a short promise that God's people will come home, then gives the song the freed exiles will sing over the dead king of Babylon. That song includes the famous "Day Star, son of Dawn" passage that Christian tradition has long read as a picture of Satan's fall. Two short oracles follow, both from Isaiah's own time: one on Assyria, the superpower of his day, and one on Philistia, dated "in the year that King Ahaz died" (about 715 BC on one common dating, about 727/726 BC on another).

The Restoration of Jacob (vv1–2)

  • "For the LORD will have compassion on Jacob" (v1). "For" ties this to chapter 13: Babylon falls because God means to set his people free. "Compassion" (racham) is related to the word for womb, a mother's tender love for her child (49:15). Babylon showed "no mercy" (13:18). God does.
  • "And will again choose Israel" (v1). God chose Israel first in Abraham (41:8; Gen 12:1–3). The exile looked like that choice was cancelled. "Again" says it was not. The return began in 538 BC, when Cyrus allowed the exiles to go home and rebuild the temple (Ezra 1:1–4).
  • "Sojourners will join them" (v1). "Sojourners" are foreigners living among Israel. Even in the return from exile, outsiders attach themselves to God's people, a thread that runs through Isaiah (2:2–4; 56:3–8). Ruth and Rahab had done this long before.
  • "They will take captive those who were their captors" (v2). The tables turn. The slaves become masters. The Tyndale notes read this as the nations coming to submit to the Lord through Israel (45:14; 60:14), and JFB takes it as conquest "not by physical, but by moral might." Christians have generally seen its fullest fulfillment in the nations being brought under Christ's rule through the gospel (Eph 4:8, "he led a host of captives").
  • Commentators: Henry reads vv1–3 as Israel's case being pleaded against Babylon: Babylon's ruin is Israel's release. JFB grounds the restoration in God's election, so it does not depend on Israel's worthiness. Guzik makes the same point simply: judgment on Babylon is mercy to Israel.

Israel's Remnant Taunts Babylon (vv3–23)

  • "When the LORD has given you rest" (v3). "Rest" is a covenant word. God gave Israel rest in the land under Joshua and David (Josh 21:44; 2 Sam 7:1). Exile undid it, and "the hard service" recalls Egyptian slavery (Exod 1:14). This is a second exodus.
  • "You will take up this taunt against the king of Babylon" (v4). "Taunt" is mashal, a proverb or comparison, here a mocking song. The Tyndale notes point out that it is shaped like a funeral lament, with the opening "How…" ('ekh) of a dirge (compare 2 Sam 1:19, "How the mighty have fallen!"). It is a mock funeral song. God's people sing a lament for a tyrant, and every line drips with irony.
  • Which king? No name is given. Nebuchadnezzar is the obvious candidate, since he destroyed Jerusalem. Others think of the last king, Nabonidus, or his son Belshazzar, killed the night Babylon fell (Dan 5:30). Some scholars suggest the song was first shaped around the Assyrian king Sargon II, who also ruled Babylon and died in battle in 705 BC, his body never recovered for burial, which fits vv19–20 strikingly. Most likely the king stands for the whole line of Babylon's proud rulers.
  • "How the oppressor has ceased" (v4). The word "ceased" (shavat) is the root of "Sabbath." The tyrant who gave no rest now has to stop.
  • "The LORD has broken the staff of the wicked" (v5). The oppressor's rod is broken by God, as with Assyria (9:4; 10:5). It struck the peoples "with unceasing blows… with unrelenting persecution" (v6). Now the beating ends.
  • "The whole earth is at rest… they break forth into singing" (v7). A tyrant's death is a world holiday. Creation itself sings when oppression ends (compare 44:23; 55:12; Rom 8:21).
  • "The cypresses rejoice… the cedars of Lebanon" (v8). Mesopotamian kings boasted of cutting cedars from Lebanon for their palaces and temples. Inscriptions of Nebuchadnezzar carved in the Lebanon mountains record his logging there, and Isaiah quotes Sennacherib's similar boast (37:24). Now the trees can relax: "no woodcutter comes up against us."
  • "Sheol beneath is stirred up to meet you" (v9). Sheol is the Old Testament's name for the realm of the dead, the grave seen as a place. "The shades" (repha'im) are the dead, pictured as weak, shadowy figures. "Leaders of the earth" is literally "he-goats," the lead animals of the flock, as JFB notes. The picture is poetic and dramatic, like a play. Dead kings rise from their thrones as if to welcome a great new arrival. The Tyndale notes observe that this matches Babylonian ideas of the dead keeping their earthly rank. But the welcome is mockery.
  • "You too have become as weak as we!" (v10). Death is the great equalizer. The man who seemed to have no weakness is now one of the shades.
  • "Maggots are laid as a bed beneath you, and worms are your covers" (v11). The palace couch and royal blankets are replaced by maggots and worms. "The sound of your harps" is silenced (compare Rev 18:22).
  • "How you are fallen from heaven, O Day Star, son of Dawn!" (v12). "Day Star" is helel, "shining one," used only here. It is the morning star, the planet Venus, which blazes brightly before dawn and then fades as the sun rises. The lexicon links it to an Assyrian epithet for the morning star. The Latin Vulgate translated it lucifer, "light-bearer," the Latin name for Venus, and the King James Version kept "Lucifer." That is how the word became a name for Satan in English.
  • Canaanite background. The Tyndale notes explain that vv12–14 echo a Canaanite story of a lesser god, the morning star, who tried to rise above the chief god El and was thrown down. Isaiah borrows a story his hearers knew, the way a preacher might use a well-known fable, and applies it to the king's pride.
  • "I will ascend to heaven… I will make myself like the Most High" (vv13–14). Count the boasts: five times "I will." Ascend to heaven, set my throne above the stars of God, sit on the mount of assembly, ascend above the clouds, be like the Most High. This is the sin of Babel again: "let us build ourselves a city and a tower with its top in the heavens" (Gen 11:4). It is also the serpent's lie in Eden: "you will be like God" (Gen 3:5).
  • "The mount of assembly in the far reaches of the north" (v13). The "far north" is tsaphon, also the name of Mount Zaphon in Syria, where Canaanite texts from Ugarit placed the home of their gods, much like Greek Olympus. Psalm 48:2 boldly calls Mount Zion "the far north," claiming that the true seat of God is in Jerusalem, not on the pagan mountain.
  • "But you are brought down to Sheol, to the far reaches of the pit" (v15). The answer to five "I will"s is one "but." He wanted the heights of the north; he gets the depths of the pit. Jesus used this pattern against proud Capernaum: "will you be exalted to heaven? You will be brought down to Hades" (Matt 11:23).
  • Is this Satan? Christians have read the passage in two main ways. Many early church writers, such as Tertullian and Origen, and many readers since, took vv12–15 as describing Satan's original fall, alongside Ezekiel 28 (the king of Tyre) and Jesus' words "I saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven" (Luke 10:18; compare Rev 12:9). Reformers like Luther and Calvin rejected that reading and held it describes the king of Babylon alone, which the text plainly says (v4) and v16 confirms: "Is this the man?" The Tyndale notes agree that Isaiah was thinking of the historical king. A third, middle view, held by many evangelicals, says the king is the subject, but his pride is the same pride that drives the devil, so the passage is a window onto the spirit behind proud empires. Either way, Scripture elsewhere does teach Satan's pride and fall (1 Tim 3:6), and the name "Lucifer" for Satan comes from tradition, not from the Hebrew.
  • A title stolen. Revelation gives the true "bright morning star" title to Jesus (Rev 22:16; compare 2 Pet 1:19). The king grasped for light and glory and fell. Christ "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped," humbled himself, and was exalted (Phil 2:6–9). JFB notes that the title "truly belong[s] to Christ."
  • "Is this the man who made the earth tremble?" (v16). People "stare" and "ponder," squinting at the corpse, barely recognizing him. He "did not let his prisoners go home" (v17), exactly what Babylon did to Judah. Cyrus did let them go (Ezra 1).
  • "Cast out, away from your grave, like a loathed branch" (v19). For ancient kings, an honored tomb was everything. Kings built magnificent burial places, and to be left unburied was the worst disgrace (Jer 22:19). Other kings "lie in glory, each in his own tomb" (v18), but this one is tossed out among battlefield corpses. "Branch" here is netser, the very word used for the Messiah in 11:1, "a branch from his roots shall bear fruit." The contrast is pointed: one branch is loathed and thrown away; the other bears fruit forever.
  • "Because you have destroyed your land, you have slain your people" (v20). A king who wrecks his own land and people for glory forfeits the honor of kings.
  • "Prepare slaughter for his sons" (v21). The dynasty ends. The reason given is "lest they rise and possess the earth, and fill the face of the world with cities," another echo of Babel's city-building. The Tyndale notes point out that the Bible sees individuals in relation to their families and peoples; the sons share in the guilt of the dynasty (Exod 20:5). Babylon's royal line did indeed end with Belshazzar.
  • "I will cut off from Babylon name and remnant" (v22). The builders of Babel wanted to "make a name" (Gen 11:4). God removes Babylon's name and remnant, while he keeps a remnant for Israel (10:20–22).
  • "A possession of the hedgehog, and pools of water… the broom of destruction" (v23). After Persian times, the irrigation canals around Babylon fell into neglect and much of the area became marsh. God sweeps the city clean like a floor.
  • Commentators: Henry has the dead greet the king with "Hast thou also become weak as we?" He does not read v12 as Satan; he takes the "Lucifer" language as the king's own vainglory, "borrowed perhaps from that of the angels who fell." On v13 he observes that worldly glory "is the heaven of a carnal heart." JFB also does not apply v12 to Satan directly, and instead sees the morning star title as rightly Christ's, one an Antichrist figure will try to claim. Guzik takes the double reference view: the king of Babylon first, then Satan, while noting that Calvin and Clarke reject any reference to Satan. He sees the five "I will"s as the heart of pride and contrasts them with Christ's humility in Philippians 2. Guzik also suggests Satan's sin was resenting God's plan to exalt humanity, which is a speculation, not something the text says. The Tyndale notes are more cautious than Guzik: they see Canaanite myth used to describe a human king.

An Oracle Concerning Assyria (vv24–27)

  • "The LORD of hosts has sworn" (v24). God swearing an oath means his word is unbreakable (Heb 6:17–18). "As I have planned, so shall it be."
  • "I will break the Assyrian in my land, and on my mountains trample him" (v25). In Isaiah's day Assyria was the threat. This promise came true in 701 BC, when Sennacherib's army surrounded Jerusalem and "the angel of the LORD" struck 185,000 of them in one night (37:36). Assyria was broken on God's own mountains, around Jerusalem.
  • "His yoke shall depart from them, and his burden from their shoulder" (v25). This repeats almost exactly 10:27 and echoes 9:4, the promise at the birth of the child-king.
  • Why here? Why put an Assyria oracle right after Babylon's? The Tyndale notes suggest Assyria and Babylon are closely linked: Babylon would inherit Assyria's empire. JFB and Henry both see the near fulfillment with Assyria, which Isaiah's hearers saw in their lifetime, as a pledge that the far-off fall of Babylon would come too. If God kept his word about Sennacherib, he will keep it about Babylon.
  • "This is the purpose… concerning the whole earth… the hand that is stretched out over all the nations" (v26). In chapters 5 and 9–10, "his hand is stretched out still" was a refrain of judgment on Israel (5:25; 9:12). Now the same stretched-out hand is over all nations.
  • "Who will annul it?… who will turn it back?" (v27). Two rhetorical questions with the same answer: no one (compare Dan 4:35; Rom 8:31).
  • Commentators: Henry quotes the oath, "As I have thought, so shall it come to pass," and stresses that God himself, not human armies, does the breaking. Guzik points to the 185,000 struck in one night (2 Kgs 19:35) as the fulfillment. JFB calls this fragment a pledge to assure the later captives in Babylon that God would overthrow Babylon too.

An Oracle Concerning Philistia (vv28–32)

  • "In the year that King Ahaz died" (v28). This matches "In the year that King Uzziah died" (6:1). Ahaz was the king who refused to trust God (ch 7). The date of his death is disputed: about 727/726 BC or about 715 BC.
  • Philistia. The Philistines lived on the coastal plain southwest of Judah in five main cities: Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Ekron and Gath. They were longtime enemies of Israel from the days of Samson and David. Under Ahaz they raided and captured towns in Judah (2 Chr 28:18).
  • "Rejoice not, O Philistia… that the rod that struck you is broken" (v29). Who is the rod? There are two main views. (1) A king of Judah: Uzziah had crushed the Philistines (2 Chr 26:6), and the "serpent's root" producing a worse "adder" would be David's line producing Hezekiah, who did strike Philistia "as far as Gaza" (2 Kgs 18:8). Henry and JFB take this view. (2) An Assyrian king: Tiglath-pileser III, who devastated Philistia in 734 BC, died in 727 BC, close to Ahaz's death, and the Philistines rejoiced. But worse Assyrian kings followed: Sargon II crushed Ashdod in 711 BC (20:1), and Sennacherib ravaged the coast in 701 BC. The Tyndale notes lean to an Assyrian reading for the "poisonous snake," and many modern scholars take that view. Either way the message is the same: don't celebrate too early.
  • "An adder… a flying fiery serpent" (v29). The "fiery serpent" is saraph, the same word as the "fiery serpents" in the wilderness (Num 21:6), whose bite burned. "Flying" probably pictures a snake that strikes with lightning speed. The word saraph is the same root as the seraphim of 6:2, the "burning ones."
  • "The firstborn of the poor will graze, and the needy lie down in safety" (v30). "Firstborn of the poor" is a Hebrew way of saying "the poorest of the poor." God's afflicted people will be safe like sheep at pasture, while Philistia's "root" dies of famine. The root that dies is set against the "root of Jesse" that lives (11:10).
  • "Wail, O gate… For smoke comes out of the north" (v31). Invaders from Mesopotamia came down the coast from the north. The "smoke" is probably the dust cloud of an advancing army or the smoke of burning towns. "No straggler in his ranks" means a disciplined army, no one lagging behind (compare 5:27).
  • "What will one answer the messengers of the nation?" (v32). Philistine envoys were evidently in Jerusalem, likely seeking an alliance against Assyria. The Tyndale notes explain that God did not want Judah to make such alliances. Isaiah's answer: "The LORD has founded Zion, and in her the afflicted of his people find refuge." Judah's security is not a coalition but God's foundation (compare 28:16, "Behold, I am the one who has laid as a foundation in Zion, a stone").
  • Commentators: Henry reads the oracle as foretelling Hezekiah's victories: "out of the root of Uzziah should come a cockatrice," with Hezekiah as the "fiery flying serpent." He draws the comfort that "the Lord has founded Zion, and therefore the Philistines must fall," and links Zion's foundation to Christ the rock. JFB likewise names Uzziah and Hezekiah and ties the root to "the stock of Jesse." The Tyndale notes, by contrast, see an Assyrian king behind the serpent imagery. Guzik notes the likely Philistine embassy and Isaiah's consistent call to trust God, not alliances.

Pointing to Christ

The chapter sets two figures side by side. One is the king who said "I will ascend… I will make myself like the Most High," who ended as a loathed branch, unburied and forgotten. The other is the true "bright morning star" (Rev 22:16), the Branch from Jesse's root, who was in the form of God and yet "made himself nothing" and went down into death and the grave for his people. He was raised and given "the name that is above every name" (Phil 2:9). The proud are brought down; the humble Son is lifted up. And the question at the end of the chapter still has the same answer: the Lord has founded Zion on that cornerstone, and the afflicted find refuge there.

Reflection

  • Promised Rest (v.3) — God promises Israel rest from the sorrow, fear, and hard bondage they have endured. What oppressive season are you carrying right now that you need to trust has an end date in God's timing?
  • Five Declarations of "I Will" (vv.13-14) — The king's downfall traces back to a string of self-exalting ambitions to rise above God Himself. How many of your own decisions begin with what you will do rather than what God is calling you to do?
  • A Fall to Match the Reach (v.15) — The one who aimed for the heights of heaven is instead brought down to the depths of the pit. What are you reaching for right now that was never meant to be yours to grasp?
  • The Tyrant Made Small (v.16) — Those who once trembled before this king now stare and ask if this pitiful figure is really the one who shook the earth. What power or person do you fear today whose influence is actually far more temporary than it feels?
  • A Purpose That Cannot Be Overturned (v.24) — God declares that what He has purposed will stand, regardless of Assyria's strength or ambition. Do you trust that God's plans for your life are just as unshakeable, even when circumstances suggest otherwise?
