1At that time some of those present told Jesus about the Galileans whose blood Pilate had mixed with their sacrifices.

2To this He replied, “Do you think that these Galileans were worse sinners than all the other Galileans, because they suffered this way?

3No, I tell you. But unless you repent, you too will all perish.

4Or those eighteen who were killed when the tower of Siloam collapsed on them: Do you think that they were more sinful than all the others living in Jerusalem?

5No, I tell you. But unless you repent, you too will all perish.”

6Then Jesus told this parable: “A man had a fig tree that was planted in his vineyard. He went to look for fruit on it but did not find any.

7So he said to the keeper of the vineyard, ‘Look, for the past three years I have come to search for fruit on this fig tree and haven’t found any. Therefore cut it down! Why should it use up the soil?’

8‘Sir,’ the man replied, ‘leave it alone again this year, until I dig around it and fertilize it.

9If it bears fruit next year, fine. But if not, you can cut it down.’”

10One Sabbath Jesus was teaching in one of the synagogues,

11and a woman there had been disabled by a spirit for eighteen years. She was hunched over and could not stand up straight.

12When Jesus saw her, He called her over and said, “Woman, you are set free from your disability.”

13Then He placed His hands on her, and immediately she straightened up and began to glorify God.

14But the synagogue leader was indignant that Jesus had healed on the Sabbath. “There are six days for work,” he told the crowd. “So come and be healed on those days and not on the Sabbath.”

15“You hypocrites!” the Lord replied. “Does not each of you on the Sabbath untie his ox or donkey from the stall and lead it to water?

16Then should not this daughter of Abraham, whom Satan has kept bound for eighteen long years, be released from her bondage on the Sabbath day?”

17When Jesus said this, all His adversaries were humiliated. And the whole crowd rejoiced at all the glorious things He was doing.

18Then Jesus asked, “What is the kingdom of God like? To what can I compare it?

19It is like a mustard seed that a man tossed into his garden. It grew and became a tree, and the birds of the air nested in its branches.”

20Again He asked, “To what can I compare the kingdom of God?

21It is like leaven that a woman took and mixed into three measures of flour, until all of it was leavened.”

22Then Jesus traveled throughout the towns and villages, teaching as He made His way toward Jerusalem.

23“Lord,” someone asked Him, “will only a few people be saved?” Jesus answered,

24“Make every effort to enter through the narrow door. For many, I tell you, will try to enter and will not be able.

25After the master of the house gets up and shuts the door, you will stand outside knocking and saying, ‘Lord, open the door for us.’ But he will reply, ‘I do not know where you are from.’

26Then you will say, ‘We ate and drank with you, and you taught in our streets.’

27And he will answer, ‘I tell you, I do not know where you are from. Depart from me, all you evildoers.’

28There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth when you see Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and all the prophets in the kingdom of God, but you yourselves are thrown out.

29People will come from east and west and north and south, and will recline at the table in the kingdom of God.

30And indeed, some who are last will be first, and some who are first will be last.”

31At that very hour, some Pharisees came to Jesus and told Him, “Leave this place and get away, because Herod wants to kill You.”

32But Jesus replied, “Go tell that fox, ‘Look, I will keep driving out demons and healing people today and tomorrow, and on the third day I will reach My goal.’

33Nevertheless, I must keep going today and tomorrow and the next day, for it is not admissible for a prophet to perish outside of Jerusalem.

34O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, who kills the prophets and stones those sent to her, how often I have longed to gather your children together as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, but you were unwilling!

35Look, your house is left to you desolate. And I tell you that you will not see Me again until you say, ‘Blessed is He who comes in the name of the Lord.’”

Summary

Jesus responds to reports of Galileans killed by Pilate and others crushed by a falling tower by insisting tragedy is no proof of greater sin, and calls everyone to repent or perish likewise (vv. 1-5), then tells the parable of a barren fig tree given one more year to bear fruit before being cut down (vv. 6-9). Healing a woman bent double for eighteen years on the Sabbath, He answers the indignant synagogue ruler by noting that even animals are watered on the Sabbath (vv. 10-17). He compares the kingdom of God to a mustard seed that grows into a large tree and to leaven that works through dough (vv. 18-21). Journeying toward Jerusalem, He warns that entering the kingdom requires striving through a narrow door, since many who assume familiarity with Him will be shut out while people from every direction feast in God's kingdom (vv. 22-30). Told that Herod wants to kill Him, He presses on toward Jerusalem, since prophets die there, and grieves over the city's repeated rejection of God's messengers, longing to gather her children as a hen gathers her chicks (vv. 31-35).

Authorship & Background

Author: Luke (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 13 addresses the urgent need for repentance (vv.1-9), records a Sabbath healing (vv.10-17), presents two kingdom parables (vv.18-21), teaches about the narrow door (vv.22-30), and ends with Jesus' lament over Jerusalem (vv.34-35).
Classification: Gospel — Narrative and Discourse
Key Themes: two recent tragedies, Galileans killed by Pilate and eighteen crushed by a falling tower, used not to explain suffering but to press the urgency of repentance, a barren fig tree granted one more year of grace by an interceding vinedresser before judgment falls, a woman bent double for eighteen years healed unasked, called to him rather than begging, a synagogue ruler's objection answered by the congregation's own Sabbath practice of watering their animals, two small parables of a mustard seed and hidden leaven kept moving steadily toward Jerusalem, and a lament addressed to that very city by name, O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, which killest the prophets, how often would I have gathered thy children together, and ye would not
The Galileans and the Tower of Siloam (vv.1-5): Two recent tragedies — Pilate killing Galilean worshippers and a tower collapse killing 18 people. The crowd assumed these victims were worse sinners. Jesus corrects this: tragedy is not proof of greater guilt. But He uses both events to press the urgency of repentance — "except ye repent, ye shall all likewise perish."
The Barren Fig Tree (vv.6-9): A parable of God's patience and coming judgment. The tree has had three years to produce fruit (paralleling Jesus' three-year ministry to Israel). The vinedresser (Christ) intercedes for one more year of grace. But patience has a limit — if no fruit comes, judgment follows.
The Woman Bowed Eighteen Years (vv.10-17): Luke describes the condition precisely, a spirit of infirmity eighteen years, and was bowed together, and could in no wise lift up herself. The healing is unasked for, he called her to him, which is unusual. Then the objection from the ruler of the synagogue, who does not address Jesus at all but tells the congregation there are six other days. The answer uses their own practice, doth not each one of you on the sabbath loose his ox or his ass from the stall, and lead him away to watering? and then the comparison that closes it, ought not this woman, being a daughter of Abraham, be loosed from this bond on the sabbath day? Luke records both reactions, his adversaries were ashamed, and all the people rejoiced.
The Mustard Seed and the Leaven (vv.18-22): Two short parables about small beginnings, and both are domestic. A grain of mustard seed cast into a garden becomes a great tree with birds lodging in it. Leaven is hid in three measures of meal till the whole is leavened, and the verb is hid rather than mixed. Luke closes the section with a travel note that keeps the destination in view, and he went through the cities and villages, teaching, and journeying toward Jerusalem.
The Narrow Door (vv.23-30): Someone asks "Are there few that be saved?" Jesus doesn't answer the theoretical question — He makes it personal: "Strive to enter in at the strait gate." The door is narrow, and it will be shut. Casual association with Jesus ("we ate and drank in your presence") is not enough.
Herod, and O Jerusalem (vv.31-35): Pharisees warn him that Herod means to kill him, and the reply is contemptuous and precise, go ye, and tell that fox, Behold, I cast out devils, and I do cures today and tomorrow, and the third day I shall be perfected. He adds a reason for continuing anyway that is grimly geographical, it cannot be that a prophet perish out of Jerusalem. Then the lament, and it is the only place in Luke he addresses the city directly, O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, which killest the prophets, how often would I have gathered thy children together, as a hen doth gather her brood under her wings, and ye would not.

  • A synagogue (v.10): Jesus healed the crippled woman in a synagogue — likely on the route through Perea or Judea.

Setting: About AD 29, still on the journey toward Jerusalem, probably in Galilee or Perea, the region east of the Jordan ruled by Herod Antipas. Chapter 12 ended with a warning to the crowds: they could read the weather but not "the present time," and they should settle with God before they reached the judge (12:54–59). Chapter 13 presses the same urgency. Two recent tragedies become a call to repent, a fig tree gets one more year, a bent woman is set free on the Sabbath, the kingdom is pictured as a seed and as leaven, and someone asks if only a few will be saved. The chapter ends with a threat from Herod and Jesus' lament over Jerusalem, the city where he is headed to die.

Repent or Perish (vv1–5)

  • "The Galileans whose blood Pilate had mingled with their sacrifices" (v1). Pontius Pilate governed Judea from AD 26 to 36. No other source records this incident, but it fits what Josephus tells of him: he provoked crowds and sent soldiers in among them, and once used temple funds for an aqueduct and had protesters beaten. Ordinary Jews could slaughter their own Passover lambs in the temple court, so these Galileans were probably cut down at the altar while they worshiped. Galileans had a reputation for unrest, and Pilate may have suspected a revolt.
  • "Do you think that these Galileans were worse sinners?" (v2). Many assumed suffering proved special guilt. Job's friends argued this way (Job 4:7), and the disciples did too (John 9:2). Jesus says no. Disaster does not sort people into worse and better sinners.
  • "Unless you repent, you will all likewise perish" (v3). Jesus turns the question around. The question is not why it happened to them but what it means for those listening. All are sinners, and all need to repent. "Likewise" may carry a warning that came true: in AD 70 many Jews died violently in Jerusalem, some in the temple courts and some under falling walls and towers.
  • "Those eighteen on whom the tower in Siloam fell" (v4). Siloam was in the southeast corner of Jerusalem, where Hezekiah's tunnel fed a pool (John 9:7). The tower was likely part of the city wall nearby. This was an accident, not an atrocity. Whether death comes by human cruelty or a falling stone, it does not mark the victims as worse than anyone else.
  • "Worse offenders" (v4). The word is literally "debtors," the same picture of sin as a debt seen in the Lord's Prayer (11:4).
  • Commentators: Henry: "The judgments of God upon others are loud calls to us to repent," and "if we will be judging, we have enough to do to judge ourselves." JFB suggests the Galileans may have been followers of the rebel Judas of Galilee (Acts 5:37), and says "of its fall nothing is known" regarding the tower. Guzik makes the point that tragedy should turn the question from why it happened to what it means for the one asking.

The Parable of the Barren Fig Tree (vv6–9)

  • "A fig tree planted in his vineyard" (v6). Fig trees were often planted in vineyards, where the soil was good. Israel is pictured as God's vineyard (Isa 5:1–7) and as a fig tree from which God looks for fruit (Jer 8:13; Hos 9:10; Mic 7:1).
  • "For three years now I have come seeking fruit" (v7). Under the law, fruit was not eaten for the first three years after planting (Lev 19:23–25). So this tree may have had six years or more to bear. Some link the three years to the length of Jesus' ministry, though that may be pressing the detail.
  • "Why should it use up the ground?" (v7). A barren fig tree draws water and nutrients that could feed something fruitful. It is not only useless. It takes the place of something better.
  • "Let it alone this year also, until I dig around it and put on manure" (v8). The vinedresser pleads for mercy and offers extra care. This is a picture of God's patience, giving time and help to repent (Rom 2:4; 2 Pet 3:9).
  • "If not, you can cut it down" (v9). The parable stops without telling what happened. The ending is left open for the hearers to write. John the Baptist had warned, "Even now the axe is laid to the root of the trees" (3:9). In Mark, Jesus curses a fig tree outside Jerusalem just before he clears the temple (Mark 11:12–21).
  • Commentators: JFB reads the tree as Israel and also the visible church, and offers hope: "Genuine repentance, however late, avails to save." Henry: "It is sad to think how many enjoy the privileges of the gospel, and yet do nothing at all to the honour of God." Guzik draws three points, that God looks for fruit, is patient and finally judges, and he suggests that the digging and manure may feel like hardship to the tree even though it is meant for its good.

A Woman with a Disabling Spirit (vv10–17)

  • "Teaching in one of the synagogues on the Sabbath" (v10). This is the last time in Luke that Jesus is in a synagogue. Visiting teachers were often invited to speak after the Scripture readings (4:16–21).
  • "A disabling spirit for eighteen years… bent over and could not fully straighten herself" (v11). Luke the doctor describes the symptoms, which sound like a spinal deformity. He also says the cause was a "spirit of weakness." Jesus later says Satan had bound her (v16). The text does not describe her as possessed in the way of the Gerasene man (8:27). Not every illness in the Gospels is linked to a spirit, but this one is.
  • "Woman, you are freed from your disability" (v12). She did not ask. Jesus saw her and called her over. "You are freed" is in the perfect tense, as if it were already done.
  • "He laid his hands on her, and immediately she was made straight, and she glorified God" (v13). After eighteen years of looking at the ground, she stands up straight and praises God. The verb "made straight" is used in the Greek Old Testament for God lifting up those who are bowed down (Ps 146:8).
  • "There are six days in which work ought to be done. Come on those days" (v14). The synagogue ruler does not dare rebuke Jesus directly. He scolds the crowd. He is quoting the commandment (Exod 20:9), but he treats healing as work and makes the woman wait one more day after eighteen years.
  • "Does not each of you on the Sabbath untie his ox or his donkey… and lead it away to water it?" (v15). Jewish tradition allowed watering animals on the Sabbath. If they would untie an animal for a few hours' thirst, how much more should a woman be freed after eighteen years.
  • "A daughter of Abraham whom Satan bound for eighteen years, be loosed from this bond" (v16). Jesus uses the same verb, "untie" (lyō), for the animal and for the woman. "Daughter of Abraham" is a rare phrase that gives her full standing among God's people, as Zacchaeus is later called "a son of Abraham" (19:9). "Ought" is dei, "must." The Sabbath, a day remembering God's rescue from slavery in Egypt (Deut 5:15), is exactly the right day for setting a captive free.
  • "All his adversaries were put to shame, and all the people rejoiced" (v17). The crowd sees the glory of God. The officials see a broken rule. Luke records the split.
  • Commentators: JFB says the ruler's anger was aimed "not so much at the sabbath violation as at the glorification of Christ," and suggests the spirit of infirmity was a milder form of possession. Henry draws the lesson that bodily infirmities, unless very grievous, "should not keep us from public worship," since she was there. Guzik sees Jesus arguing from the lesser to the greater, and notes that the word translated ought is the stronger word must. He cautions against assuming every illness has a spiritual cause.

The Mustard Seed and the Leaven (vv18–21)

  • "What is the kingdom of God like?" (v18). Luke places these two parables right after the healing, which is a small sign of the kingdom's power. Matthew and Mark put them with the parable of the sower.
  • "A grain of mustard seed… became a tree, and the birds of the air made nests in its branches" (v19). Black mustard seeds are tiny, and the plant can grow to ten feet or more in a season. The kingdom began with one man and a few disciples in a corner of the empire. "Birds… in its branches" echoes the Old Testament picture of a great kingdom as a tree sheltering the birds (Ezek 17:23; 31:6; Dan 4:12). In Ezekiel 17:23, God plants a sprig that grows into a cedar where "birds of every sort will nest." Many readers take the birds to stand for the nations finding shelter in God's kingdom.
  • "Leaven that a woman took and hid in three measures of flour" (v21). Three measures (sata) was about forty to fifty pounds of flour, enough bread for over a hundred people. Sarah made cakes from three measures of flour for the Lord's visit (Gen 18:6). "Hid" is unusual. The yeast is invisible once mixed in, but it works through everything. The kingdom spreads quietly from within.
  • Is the growth good or bad? Most interpreters, including the Tyndale notes and JFB, read both parables as pictures of the kingdom's growth, outward in the mustard and inward in the leaven. A minority, including Guzik and some dispensational teachers, read them as warnings. They point out that leaven usually stands for sin (12:1; 1 Cor 5:6), and that the birds in the sower parable represent the devil (8:5, 12). On that view, the mustard plant's unnatural size and the spreading leaven picture corruption entering the visible church. The majority answer that Scripture can use the same image in different ways, and that "the kingdom of God is like" points to the kingdom itself, not its counterfeit.
  • Commentators: JFB reads the mustard seed as outward growth and the leaven as inward working, showing the gospel's "all-penetrating" and "assimilating" power. Guzik takes the minority view: he links the overgrown tree to the proud tree of Nebuchadnezzar's dream (Dan 4) and the church's later entanglement with worldly power, and reads the leaven as sin, as it is in the Passover laws. Henry treats both as the kingdom's growth from small beginnings.

The Narrow Door (vv22–30)

  • "Teaching and journeying toward Jerusalem" (v22). Luke reminds his readers again where the journey is headed (9:51).
  • "Lord, will those who are saved be few?" (v23). This was a real debate. Some Jewish writings expected that only a few would be saved, while a common saying held that "all Israel has a share in the world to come." Jesus does not answer the curiosity. He makes it personal.
  • "Strive to enter through the narrow door" (v24). "Strive" is agōnizomai, the word for an athlete straining in a contest (1 Cor 9:25). The effort is not earning salvation. It is the seriousness of a person who knows the door will not stay open forever and will not let anything hold them back. The door is narrow because there is only one, Jesus himself (John 10:9; 14:6).
  • "Many… will seek to enter and will not be able" (v24). The problem is timing. They seek when it is too late.
  • "When once the master of the house has risen and shut the door" (v25). The picture is of a banquet. Once the guests are in, the host closes the door. Those outside knock and call, "Lord, open to us," echoing the "knock, and it will be opened" of 11:9. That promise holds now, not after the door is shut. Matthew 25:10–12 tells a similar story of the shut door.
  • "I do not know where you come from" (v25). "Know" in Scripture often means a personal relationship (Amos 3:2; Matt 7:23). The master does not recognize them as his.
  • "We ate and drank in your presence, and you taught in our streets" (v26). Their claim is that they were nearby. They had seen Jesus, eaten where he ate and heard him teach. Being close to Jesus is not the same as knowing him.
  • "Depart from me, all you workers of evil!" (v27). This quotes Psalm 6:8. The words are the Psalmist's to his enemies, and now the Lord says them at the judgment.
  • "Weeping and gnashing of teeth" (v28). A picture of grief and bitter anger. It is made worse by seeing Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and the prophets at the feast, while they, who assumed they belonged, are outside.
  • "People will come from east and west, and from north and south" (v29). The prophets foresaw the nations streaming to God's feast (Isa 25:6; 49:12; Mal 1:11). Gentiles who were "last" will sit down at the table.
  • "Some are last who will be first, and some are first who will be last" (v30). Notice "some." Not every Israelite is excluded, and not every Gentile is included. But the guest list will surprise people.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Many are more inquisitive respecting who shall be saved, and who not, than respecting what they shall do to be saved," and "many are ruined by an ill-grounded hope of heaven, which they never distrusted." JFB calls the striving "a life-and-death struggle," the shut door "awfully sublime and vivid," and says "no nearness of external communion" will avail. Guzik notes the athletic sense of agōnizomai, that the door is Christ rather than works, and that the word some keeps the last-first saying from being a universal rule.

Lament over Jerusalem (vv31–35)

  • "Some Pharisees came and said to him, 'Get away from here, for Herod wants to kill you'" (v31). Not all Pharisees were enemies (Acts 5:34). These may have been friendly, or they may have wanted him out of their area. Herod Antipas had already killed John (9:9) and wanted to see Jesus (9:9; 23:8).
  • "Go and tell that fox" (v32). In Jewish usage the fox stood for a sly, destructive creature of little real weight, the opposite of a lion. Jesus is not intimidated.
  • "Today and tomorrow, and the third day I finish my course" (v32). The phrase means a short, set period, in God's timing, not Herod's. "Finish my course" is teleioumai, "I am made complete" or "I reach my goal." It hints at his death and resurrection on the third day.
  • "It cannot be that a prophet should perish away from Jerusalem" (v33). This is bitter irony. Not every prophet died in Jerusalem, but the city had a long record of killing them (Jer 26:20–23; 2 Chr 24:20–22). Herod cannot touch Jesus in Galilee, because the place of his death is Jerusalem.
  • "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets" (v34). The doubled name is the sound of grief, like David's "O Absalom, my son" (2 Sam 18:33). Matthew places this lament in the temple during the last week (Matt 23:37–39). Jesus may have spoken it more than once.
  • "How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings" (v34). The Old Testament pictures God sheltering his people under his wings (Deut 32:11; Ps 91:4; Ruth 2:12). Jesus speaks as God speaks. A hen gathers her chicks to shield them from danger, even at the cost of her own life. "How often" suggests repeated visits and appeals, which John's Gospel records.
  • "And you were not willing!" (v34). The tragedy is not God's unwillingness but theirs. His will to save and their refusal stand side by side.
  • "Your house is forsaken" (v35). "House" may mean the temple, the city or the nation. It recalls Jeremiah's warning that God would abandon his house (Jer 12:7; 22:5). The temple was destroyed in AD 70.
  • "You will not see me until you say, 'Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!'" (v35). This is Psalm 118:26, sung by pilgrims at the great feasts. The crowds sing it when Jesus enters Jerusalem (19:38), which is a partial fulfillment. Many interpreters also see a final fulfillment at his return, when Israel will welcome him (Rom 11:26; Zech 12:10).
  • Commentators: JFB calls Herod "that crafty, cruel enemy of God's innocent servants," "haunted by guilty fears," and reads v33 with its irony: "Jerusalem is the prophets' slaughter-house." Henry: "Christ's willingness aggravates sinners' unwillingness, and leaves their blood upon their own heads." Guzik suggests the Pharisees' warning may have been sincere, links the hen image to Psalm 91 and Isaiah 31, and sees the final verse pointing to a future national turning of Israel to Christ.

Pointing to Christ

Luke 13 shows Jesus as the one who calls everyone to repent, and the patient vinedresser who pleads for one more year. He sees a bent woman no one else noticed, lays his hands on her and sets a daughter of Abraham free on the Sabbath. He is the narrow door, open now and one day shut. He will not be hurried by a fox like Herod, because he is going to Jerusalem on his Father's schedule, to finish his course on the third day. And he weeps over the city that will kill him, a hen longing to gather her chicks under her wings, ready to take on herself the danger that threatens them.

Reflection

  • Redirected to Personal Repentance (v.3) — Rather than settling the debate about why others suffered, Jesus turns the question back to personal responsibility. Instead of comparing yourself to others, is there unrepented sin in your own life right now?
  • One More Year of Grace (v.8) — The vinedresser intercedes for the barren tree, buying it more time to bear fruit. If you've been spiritually fruitless lately, what would it look like to actually use this season of grace rather than coast through it?
  • Rules Over a Suffering Woman (v.15) — Jesus exposes the hypocrisy of religious leaders who cared more about Sabbath rules than the woman bent double in front of them. Where might you be prioritizing being right over showing compassion to someone hurting?
  • Striving to Enter (v.24) — Jesus describes entering the kingdom with real effort and urgency, not casual interest. Does your pursuit of Christ look more like striving or coasting?
  • Known by Him, Not Just Familiar With Him (vv.25-27) — Some who claim familiarity with Jesus are told He never knew them. What's the difference in your own life between knowing facts about Jesus and being genuinely known by Him?
  • Wept Over a City That Wouldn't (v.34) — Jesus grieves Jerusalem's refusal of His gathering love rather than forcing it on them. Is there an area where you're resisting God's pursuit of you, saying "I will not" to what He's offering?
