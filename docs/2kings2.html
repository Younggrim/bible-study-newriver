1Shortly before the LORD took Elijah up to heaven in a whirlwind, Elijah and Elisha were on their way from Gilgal,

2and Elijah said to Elisha, “Please stay here, for the LORD has sent me on to Bethel.” But Elisha replied, “As surely as the LORD lives and as you yourself live, I will not leave you.” So they went down to Bethel.

3Then the sons of the prophets at Bethel came out to Elisha and said, “Do you know that the LORD will take your master away from you today?” “Yes, I know,” he replied. “Do not speak of it.”

4And Elijah said to Elisha, “Please stay here, for the LORD has sent me on to Jericho.” But Elisha replied, “As surely as the LORD lives and as you yourself live, I will not leave you.” So they went to Jericho.

5Then the sons of the prophets at Jericho came up to Elisha and said, “Do you know that the LORD will take your master away from you today?” “Yes, I know,” he replied. “Do not speak of it.”

6And Elijah said to Elisha, “Please stay here, for the LORD has sent me on to the Jordan.” But Elisha replied, “As surely as the LORD lives and as you yourself live, I will not leave you.” So the two of them went on.

7Then a company of fifty of the sons of the prophets went and stood at a distance, facing Elijah and Elisha as the two of them stood by the Jordan.

8And Elijah took his cloak, rolled it up, and struck the waters, which parted to the right and to the left, so that the two of them crossed over on dry ground.

9After they had crossed over, Elijah said to Elisha, “Tell me, what can I do for you before I am taken away from you?” “Please, let me inherit a double portion of your spirit,” Elisha replied.

10“You have requested a difficult thing,” said Elijah. “Nevertheless, if you see me as I am taken from you, it will be yours. But if not, then it will not be so.”

11As they were walking along and talking together, suddenly a chariot of fire with horses of fire appeared and separated the two of them, and Elijah went up into heaven in a whirlwind.

12As Elisha watched, he cried out, “My father, my father, the chariots and horsemen of Israel!” And he saw Elijah no more. So taking hold of his own clothes, he tore them in two.

13Elisha also picked up the cloak that had fallen from Elijah, and he went back and stood on the bank of the Jordan.

14Then he took the cloak of Elijah that had fallen from him and struck the waters. “Where now is the LORD, the God of Elijah?” he asked. And when he had struck the waters, they parted to the right and to the left, and Elisha crossed over.

15When the sons of the prophets who were watching him from Jericho saw what had happened, they said, “The spirit of Elijah rests on Elisha.” And they went to meet him and bowed down to the ground before him.

16“Look now,” they said to Elisha, “we your servants have fifty valiant men. Please let them go and search for your master. Perhaps the Spirit of the LORD has taken him up and put him on one of the mountains or in one of the valleys.” “Do not send them,” Elisha replied.

17But when they pressed him to the point of embarrassment, he said, “Send them.” And they sent fifty men, who searched for three days but did not find Elijah.

18When they returned to Elisha, who was staying in Jericho, he said to them, “Didn’t I tell you not to go?”

19Then the men of the city said to Elisha, “Please note, our lord, that the city’s location is good, as you can see. But the water is bad and the land is unfruitful.”

20“Bring me a new bowl,” he replied, “and put some salt in it.” So they brought it to him,

21and Elisha went out to the spring, cast the salt into it, and said, “This is what the LORD says: ‘I have healed this water. No longer will it cause death or unfruitfulness.’”

22And the waters there have been healthy to this day, according to the word spoken by Elisha.

23From there, Elisha went up to Bethel, and as he was walking up the road, a group of boys came out of the city and jeered at him, chanting, “Go up, you baldhead! Go up, you baldhead!”

24Then he turned around, looked at them, and called down a curse on them in the name of the LORD. Suddenly two female bears came out of the woods and mauled forty-two of the boys.

25And Elisha went on to Mount Carmel, and from there he returned to Samaria.

Summary

Elijah and Elisha travel from Gilgal to Bethel, Jericho, and the Jordan, where Elijah parts the water with his mantle so they cross on dry ground (vv. 1-8). Before being taken up, Elijah asks what he can do for Elisha, who requests a double portion of his spirit; Elijah says this will be granted only if Elisha witnesses his departure (vv. 9-10). A chariot and horses of fire appear in a whirlwind and carry Elijah to heaven, and the grieving Elisha tears his clothes and picks up the fallen mantle (vv. 11-13). Elisha strikes the Jordan with it, the water parts as it had for Elijah, and the watching sons of the prophets recognize that Elijah's spirit now rests on him (vv. 14-15). Elisha then purifies Jericho's water with salt (vv. 19-22), and when youths at Bethel mock him, two bears maul forty-two of them as a sign of God's judgment (vv. 23-24).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Jeremiah or a prophetic school. Originally one book with 1 Kings. Covers approximately 250 years (850-586 BC) from Elijah's translation through the Babylonian exile.
Historical Context: Chapter 2 records one of the most extraordinary events in all of Scripture — Elijah's translation to heaven without death. Only Enoch shares this distinction (Genesis 5:24). This chapter marks the transition from Elijah's ministry to Elisha's — from the prophet of fire and confrontation to the prophet of grace and miracles. The journey from Gilgal to Bethel to Jericho to the Jordan retraces Israel's conquest route in reverse, as if Elijah is walking backward through Israel's history before departing. The double portion Elisha requests is not "twice as much power" but the firstborn's inheritance share (Deuteronomy 21:17) — Elisha asks to be recognized as Elijah's spiritual heir. The chapter establishes Elisha's credentials through three immediate acts: parting the Jordan, healing Jericho's water, and pronouncing judgment on the mockers at Bethel. The prophetic office continues; God's work does not end when one servant departs.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: A journey retracing Israel's conquest route in reverse as though Elijah is walking backward through its history, three refusals to be left behind answered each time with as the LORD liveth and as thy soul liveth, a double portion requested as a firstborn's inheritance rather than as raw power, a mantle picked up and a question, where is the LORD God of Elijah, that is invocation rather than doubt, and water healed with salt from a new bowl standing permanently unto this day
The Final Journey (vv.1-8): Elijah's route — Gilgal, Bethel, Jericho, Jordan — traces a deliberate path. Three times Elijah tells Elisha to stay behind; three times Elisha refuses: "As the LORD liveth, and as thy soul liveth, I will not leave thee" (vv.2, 4, 6). This is not stubbornness but devotion — Elisha will not be separated from his master in the final hours. The sons of the prophets at Bethel and Jericho both ask, "Knowest thou that the LORD will take away thy master from thy head to day?" — the event was divinely revealed to the prophetic community. Elisha's response — "Yea, I know it; hold ye your peace" — reveals a man processing grief privately, unwilling to discuss what he cannot prevent. At the Jordan, Elijah parts the water with his mantle — echoing Moses at the Red Sea and Joshua at this same river. The mantle is the symbol of prophetic authority; its power comes not from the cloth but from the God who honors it.
The Translation and the Double Portion (vv.9-12): After crossing, Elijah offers Elisha a final gift: "Ask what I shall do for thee." Elisha's request — "let a double portion of thy spirit be upon me" — is the request of a firstborn son claiming his inheritance. It is bold but not presumptuous; it reveals a man who values spiritual power above all earthly gifts. Elijah calls it "a hard thing" — not because God lacks power but because it is not Elijah's to give. The condition — "if thou see me when I am taken" — makes the gift dependent on Elisha's attentiveness and God's sovereign choice. Then the moment comes: a chariot of fire and horses of fire separate them, and Elijah ascends in a whirlwind. Elisha sees it and cries, "My father, my father, the chariot of Israel, and the horsemen thereof!" — Elijah was Israel's true defense, more valuable than any army. Elisha tears his clothes in grief — the old era has ended.
Elisha Takes Up the Mantle (vv.13-18): Elijah's mantle falls — the symbol of authority transferred. Elisha picks it up and returns to the Jordan. His question — "Where is the LORD God of Elijah?" — is not doubt but invocation. He strikes the water; it parts. The same God who empowered Elijah now empowers Elisha. The sons of the prophets witness it and declare, "The spirit of Elijah doth rest on Elisha" — his authority is publicly confirmed. They bow before him. Their insistence on searching for Elijah (vv.16-18) reveals human inability to accept the supernatural — they cannot believe God simply took him. Elisha allows the search to satisfy them; after three fruitless days, they accept what Elisha already knew.
Healing Jericho's Water (vv.19-22): Elisha's first miracle of restoration. Jericho's water was "naught" (bad) — causing death and barrenness. Elisha uses salt in a new bowl — salt preserves and purifies, the new vessel represents a fresh start. But the healing is God's: "Thus saith the LORD, I have healed these waters." The waters remain healed "unto this day" — a permanent miracle. Elisha's ministry begins with life-giving restoration, contrasting with Elijah's ministry that often began with judgment.
The Bears at Bethel (vv.23-25): This difficult passage records youths from Bethel — a center of calf-worship — mocking Elisha: "Go up, thou bald head!" The taunt "go up" likely mocks Elijah's ascension: "Go up like your master — disappear!" The Hebrew "ne'arim qetannim" can mean "young men" or "youths" rather than small children — these are likely adolescents or young men from an idolatrous city deliberately insulting God's new prophet. Elisha curses them "in the name of the LORD" — this is not personal revenge but prophetic judgment. Two bears maul forty-two of them. The severity establishes that mocking God's prophet is mocking God Himself. At the very beginning of Elisha's ministry, God makes clear that His servant carries divine authority.

Map & Geography

  • Samaria — capital of the northern kingdom (Israel); falls to Assyria in 722 BC (ch.17). Jerusalem — capital of Judah; falls to Babylon in 586 BC (ch.25).
  • Key locations: Jordan River (Elisha's miracles, chs.2, 5), Damascus (Syria/Aram), Nineveh (Assyrian capital), Babylon (where Judah is exiled).
  • Sennacherib's invasion route threatens Jerusalem (chs.18-19). Exile destinations: Assyria (for Israel) and Babylon (for Judah).

Setting: About 850 BC, early in the reign of Jehoram (Joram), Ahab's second son, over Israel. Elijah's public work is done. He has confronted three kings of Ahab's house and called Elisha, the farmer from Abel-meholah, to follow him (1 Kgs 19:19–21). Elisha has served him for some years since. Now the LORD is about to take Elijah, and the chapter is about succession. Will the prophetic work go on after the great prophet is gone? The story is told with deliberate echoes of Moses and Joshua: a journey to the Jordan, the water parting, a leader taken by God east of the river, and his successor crossing back into the land to begin his work at Jericho.

Elijah Taken to Heaven (vv1–14)

  • "When the LORD was about to take Elijah up to heaven by a whirlwind" (v1). The narrator tells us the ending first. The suspense is not about what will happen but about whether Elisha will be there. "Whirlwind" (se'arah) is a storm wind, the kind of wind from which God spoke to Job (Job 38:1).
  • "From Gilgal" (v1). There were several places called Gilgal, "circle of stones." The famous one was near Jericho, where Israel first camped after crossing the Jordan (Josh 4:19). But here they "went down" to Bethel (v2), and Bethel sits high in the hills, so this is probably a different Gilgal on the ridge north of Bethel. JFB places it "near Ebal and Gerizim." Either way, the route runs Gilgal, Bethel, Jericho, the Jordan, retracing in reverse the path by which Israel entered the land under Joshua.
  • "Please stay here, for the LORD has sent me as far as Bethel" (v2). Three times Elijah tells Elisha to stay behind (vv2, 4, 6). It may be a test of Elisha's commitment, or Elijah's wish to go alone, or simply kindness, sparing him the parting. Three times Elisha answers with an oath: "As the LORD lives, and as you yourself live, I will not leave you." It echoes Ruth's refusal to leave Naomi (Ruth 1:16–18). Elisha will not be talked out of following to the end.
  • "The sons of the prophets who were in Bethel" (v3). These were communities of prophets, followers or disciples of the great prophets, found at Bethel, Jericho and elsewhere (2 Kgs 4:38; 6:1). Bethel was the site of Jeroboam's golden calf shrine (1 Kgs 12:29), so a group of true prophets there was a faithful witness at the heart of false worship. Elijah visits them one last time. "Do you know that today the LORD will take away your master from over you?" They know too. The phrase "from over you" is literally "from over your head," the place of a master over a disciple.
  • "Yes, I know it; keep quiet" (v3). Elisha does not want to talk about it. Grief, perhaps, or the wish to keep his attention on Elijah to the last moment.
  • Jericho and the Jordan (vv4–7). Jericho, rebuilt in Ahab's day at the cost of the builder's sons as Joshua had warned (1 Kgs 16:34; Josh 6:26), now has a community of prophets too. Fifty of them follow at a distance to watch (v7). They will be witnesses that the two went over the Jordan and only one came back.
  • "Elijah took his cloak and rolled it up and struck the water" (v8). The cloak ('adderet) is the same one Elijah threw over Elisha when he called him (1 Kgs 19:19). The water divides "to the one side and to the other," and the two cross "on dry ground." The echoes are unmistakable: Moses stretched out his staff and the Red Sea parted (Exod 14:21–22). Joshua led Israel across the Jordan on dry ground (Josh 3:17). Elijah is a new Moses, and he is about to be taken in the same region where Moses died, on the east side of the Jordan, near Mount Nebo (Deut 34:1–6). God buried Moses where no one could find him. Elijah is not buried at all.
  • "Ask what I shall do for you, before I am taken from you" (v9). It is like a father's last blessing on his son.
  • "Please let there be a double portion of your spirit on me" (v9). This does not mean Elisha wants twice Elijah's power, though tradition loves to point out that Scripture records roughly twice as many miracles by Elisha. The phrase "double portion" (pi shenayim, literally "a mouth of two") is the inheritance law: the firstborn son received a double share of his father's property (Deut 21:17). Elisha is asking to be Elijah's heir, his firstborn among the prophets, the one who carries on his work. JFB: he asked to be "heir to the prophetic office and gifts of his master." By "your spirit" he means the Spirit of the LORD who rested on Elijah, the same way the Spirit on Moses was shared with the seventy elders (Num 11:17, 25).
  • "You have asked a hard thing" (v10). Hard because it was not Elijah's to give. Only God gives his Spirit. "If you see me as I am being taken from you, it shall be so." Seeing the unseen world is a mark of a prophet (2 Kgs 6:17). If God lets Elisha see, that will be the sign that God has granted the request.
  • "Chariots of fire and horses of fire separated the two of them" (v11). Fire is again the sign of God's presence (Exod 3:2; 19:18; 1 Kgs 18:38). The chariots come between the two men, and then "Elijah went up by a whirlwind into heaven." Notice the text: the whirlwind takes him up. The chariots part the two men. The popular picture of Elijah riding a fiery chariot into the sky combines them, which is fair but not exactly what the verse says. Only Enoch, who "walked with God, and he was not, for God took him" (Gen 5:24), left the world without dying in the same way. JFB thinks the fiery horses were "some bright effulgence" that looked like chariots to those who saw it. Most readers take the vision more plainly.
  • "My father, my father! The chariots of Israel and its horsemen!" (v12). "Father" was what disciples called their master (2 Kgs 6:21; 8:9). "The chariots of Israel and its horsemen" means Elijah himself had been Israel's real defense, worth more than all its armies. Kings boasted of chariots. Israel's true strength was a prophet who stood before God. When Elisha himself dies, the king of Israel cries out the same words over him (2 Kgs 13:14). "And he saw him no more." Elisha saw, so the request is granted.
  • "He took hold of his own clothes and tore them in two pieces" (v12). Tearing one's clothes was the standard sign of mourning (Gen 37:34; 2 Sam 1:11). He tears them right through, in two. Then he picks up what has replaced them.
  • "He took up the cloak of Elijah that had fallen from him" (v13). At his call, Elijah threw this cloak on Elisha. Now it falls to him for good. It is the sign that the office has passed. He goes back to the riverbank, alone.
  • "Where is the LORD, the God of Elijah?" (v14). This is not doubt but a prayer, maybe even a challenge of faith: the God who worked through Elijah, is he with me now? Elisha strikes the water as Elijah did, and the water divides. The power was never in the cloak. It was in the LORD, and the LORD is still here. Elijah is gone, but the God of Elijah is not.
  • Commentators: All three read the "double portion" as the firstborn's inheritance, not twice Elijah's power. Guzik adds that Elisha was asking for what he needed to fulfill a call he had already received. Henry says Elijah's departure was "a holy prophet leaving it in honour," and that "This revolution in prophecy makes a greater figure than the revolution of a kingdom." JFB reads "my father" as "spiritual father," and says the cry means one prophet did more for Israel than its chariots and horsemen. Guzik draws on Meyer's observation that Elijah, who might have wanted a great public farewell, chose to go quietly. On v14 he notes that the cloak was only a sign: the power was God's presence.

Elisha Succeeds Elijah (vv15–25)

  • "The spirit of Elijah rests on Elisha" (v15). The fifty prophets at Jericho saw the Jordan part. They recognize the succession at once, and bowed to the ground before him. Elisha has crossed back into the land at Jericho, exactly as Joshua did when he took up Moses' work. The names fit too: Joshua means "the LORD saves," and Elisha means "God saves." Jesus' own name is the Greek form of Joshua.
  • "It may be that the Spirit of the LORD has caught him up and cast him upon some mountain" (v16). The prophets knew Elijah's habit of appearing and vanishing (1 Kgs 18:12). They want to search. Elisha knows better: "You shall not send."
  • "They urged him till he was ashamed" (v17). Elisha gives in, perhaps to settle the matter for them. Fifty men search for three days and find nothing. "Did I not say to you, 'Do not go'?" (v18). The empty search confirms what happened. Elijah is not dead in a ravine. He is with God. There is a faint echo of a later empty search: the women at the tomb who came looking for a body that was not there (Luke 24:5).
  • "The situation of this city is pleasant... but the water is bad" (v19). Jericho is an oasis in the Jordan Valley, warm and fertile, with palm trees. Its main spring, now called Elisha's Spring (Ein es-Sultan), still flows. But the water had become bad and the land "unfruitful," or literally "causing miscarriage," affecting crops, flocks and perhaps people (v21). Joshua had laid a curse on anyone who rebuilt Jericho (Josh 6:26), and it had recently been rebuilt (1 Kgs 16:34). Some see a link between that curse and the bad water, though the text does not draw it.
  • "Bring me a new bowl, and put salt in it" (v20). A new bowl, unused and clean, fit for a holy purpose. Salt does not purify water. In fact, salty water would be worse. JFB calls it "a symbolic act." Salt was used in sacrifices as a sign of the covenant (Lev 2:13; Num 18:19), and stood for preservation and purity.
  • "Thus says the LORD, I have healed this water" (v21). Elisha is careful to say who did it. "Neither death nor miscarriage shall come from it." His first miracle in the land is healing, a curse reversed. "So the water has been healed to this day" (v22): it was still good when Kings was written, and the spring still provides Jericho's water today.
  • "He went up from there to Bethel" (v23). Bethel was the center of the calf worship, the official religion of the northern kingdom. A prophet of the LORD walking in from the healing at Jericho would have been unwelcome.
  • "Some small boys came out of the city and jeered at him" (v23). This scene troubles many readers, and the details matter. "Small boys" is ne'arim qetannim. Na'ar covers a wide range of ages, from a child to a young man of fighting age or a servant. It is used of Isaac at about twelve or older (Gen 22:5), of Joseph at seventeen (Gen 37:2), of the young soldiers in 1 Kings 20:14, and of Solomon on becoming king (1 Kgs 3:7). Qatan means "young" or "small." So these were probably youths or young men, not toddlers, and a gang of more than forty-two of them. The Tyndale notes say they may have been young adults.
  • "Go up, you baldhead!" (v23). Two insults in one. "Baldhead" was contempt, whether or not Elisha was actually bald (Elijah, by contrast, was "a hairy man," 1:8). And "go up" almost certainly mocks Elijah's ascension: "Go on, go up like your master did, if you can," or simply "get out of here." The mockery is aimed at the LORD's prophet and the LORD's work, at the very place where Israel's false worship was centered. JFB calls them "idolatrous, or infidel young men of the place."
  • "He cursed them in the name of the LORD. And two she-bears came out of the woods" (v24). Elisha does not attack them. He pronounces a curse in God's name and leaves it to God. Bears lived in the hills of Israel (1 Sam 17:34), and a mother bear was proverbially dangerous (2 Sam 17:8; Prov 17:12). "Tore forty-two of the boys." The word means "mauled" or "tore open." It does not necessarily say all of them died. The scene is a warning, early in Elisha's ministry, that mocking God's word and God's messenger is not a small thing. The covenant had warned that wild animals would come on a rebellious people (Lev 26:21–22). Readers have long wrestled with the severity, and it is honest to admit the difficulty. The text presents it as God's judgment, not as Elisha's temper.
  • "From there he went on to Mount Carmel, and from there he returned to Samaria" (v25). Carmel, the place of Elijah's great victory, and then Samaria, the royal capital. Elisha's ministry will be much closer to the court and the people than Elijah's was.
  • Commentators: Guzik and the Tyndale notes both stress that the Hebrew for "boys" has a broad range, and Guzik passes on Poole's reading of "go up" as a taunt about Elijah's ascension. Guzik also notes Dilday's point that the word for "tore" may mean the youths were mauled rather than all killed, and Wiseman's that bears lived in those hills into the Middle Ages. JFB says "bald head" was a term of contempt in the East whatever a man's hair, and reads the bears as God's defense of his new prophet. On Jericho, Guzik and JFB both stress that the healing was God's word, not the salt. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's encouragement that grief should give way to work: Elisha mourned, then picked up the cloak.

Pointing to Christ

Elijah's departure points to Christ's in many ways. Jesus too led his followers out to the place where he would be taken from them, and "as they were looking on, he was lifted up, and a cloud took him out of their sight" (Acts 1:9). Like Elisha, the disciples were told to wait, and then received the Spirit of their Master, not a double portion but the Spirit himself, poured out on all his people (Acts 2:33). They did "greater works" in reach and number, as Jesus promised (John 14:12), because he went to the Father. The question "Where is the LORD, the God of Elijah?" has its answer in the risen Christ, who said, "I am with you always" (Matt 28:20). And the healing of Jericho's water, a curse reversed by the word of God, is a small sign of what Jesus does for the whole creation: "No longer will there be anything accursed" (Rev 22:3).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes that Elisha's persistence in following Elijah was the key to receiving the double portion. Those who are easily discouraged or easily turned aside will not receive the fullness of God's blessing. The three tests — "stay here" — were not rejections but invitations to prove devotion. Guzik also notes that the youths at Bethel were not innocent children but young people from an idolatrous city who represented organized opposition to God's prophet at the very start of his ministry.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Refusing to Be Left Behind (vv.2,4,6) — Three times Elijah tells Elisha to stay behind, and three times Elisha insists on staying with him to the end. What are you tempted to walk away from too soon, when staying close would bring the greater blessing?
  • Asking Boldly (v.9) — Given one final request, Elisha asks not for comfort or wealth but for a double portion of Elijah's spirit, the inheritance of a firstborn son. If God asked what you wanted most, would your answer reveal spiritual hunger or something smaller?
  • The Same God Still Answers (vv.13-14) — Elisha picks up the fallen mantle, strikes the Jordan, and asks where the God of Elijah is, and the water parts just as it did before. When a season or a leader you relied on ends, do you trust that God's power hasn't ended with them?
  • A Ministry That Begins With Healing (vv.20-21) — Elisha's first act as prophet is not judgment but restoring Jericho's poisoned water through a new bowl and salt. Does your life show more of God's capacity to heal and restore, or just to correct and confront?
  • Contempt for God's Servant (vv.23-24) — Youths from an idol-worshiping town mock Elisha's calling, and the severity of God's response shows how seriously He takes contempt for His messengers. Where might you be dismissing what God is saying because of who is saying it?
