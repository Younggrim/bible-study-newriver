2There He was transfigured before them. His face shone like the sun, and His clothes became as white as the light.

3Suddenly Moses and Elijah appeared before them, talking with Jesus.

4Peter said to Jesus, “Lord, it is good for us to be here. If You wish, I will put up three shelters—one for You, one for Moses, and one for Elijah.”

5While Peter was still speaking, a bright cloud enveloped them, and a voice from the cloud said, “This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. Listen to Him!”

6When the disciples heard this, they fell facedown in terror.

7Then Jesus came over and touched them. “Get up,” He said. “Do not be afraid.”

8And when they looked up, they saw no one except Jesus.

9As they were coming down the mountain, Jesus commanded them, “Do not tell anyone about this vision until the Son of Man has been raised from the dead.”

10The disciples asked Him, “Why then do the scribes say that Elijah must come first?”

11Jesus replied, “Elijah does indeed come, and he will restore all things.

12But I tell you that Elijah has already come, and they did not recognize him, but have done to him whatever they wished. In the same way, the Son of Man will suffer at their hands.”

13Then the disciples understood that He was speaking to them about John the Baptist.

14When they came to the crowd, a man came up to Jesus and knelt before Him.

15“Lord, have mercy on my son,” he said. “He has seizures and is suffering terribly. He often falls into the fire or into the water.

16I brought him to Your disciples, but they could not heal him.”

18Then Jesus rebuked the demon, and it came out of the boy, and he was healed from that moment.

19Afterward the disciples came to Jesus privately and asked, “Why couldn’t we drive it out?”

20“Because you have so little faith,” He answered. “For truly I tell you, if you have faith the size of a mustard seed, you can say to this mountain, ‘Move from here to there,’ and it will move. Nothing will be impossible for you.”

Summary

Jesus takes Peter, James, and John up a high mountain, where he is transfigured before them, his face shining like the sun and his clothes turning white as light, as Moses and Elijah appear speaking with him and a voice from heaven again declares him God's beloved Son (vv. 1-8). As they come down, Jesus tells them not to speak of it until after his resurrection and explains that John the Baptist already fulfilled the coming of Elijah (vv. 9-13). At the foot of the mountain he heals a boy tormented by a demon that the disciples had been unable to cast out, attributing their failure to little faith and saying that faith even as small as a mustard seed can move mountains (vv. 14-21). Jesus again predicts to his disciples that he will be betrayed, killed, and raised on the third day, filling them with grief (vv. 22-23). The chapter closes with Jesus, to avoid giving offense over the temple tax, miraculously providing the coin from a fish's mouth for Peter to pay it for both of them (vv. 24-27).

Authorship & Background

Author: Matthew (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 17 records three events: the Transfiguration (vv.1-13), the healing of a demon-possessed boy (vv.14-21), and the second prediction of Jesus' death (vv.22-23), followed by the Temple tax incident (vv.24-27).
The Transfiguration: Six days after Peter's confession at Caesarea Philippi, Jesus takes Peter, James, and John up a high mountain (likely Mount Hermon, 9,200 feet). There His divine glory breaks through His human form — His face shines like the sun, His clothes become white as light. Moses (representing the Law) and Elijah (representing the Prophets) appear with Him, confirming that Jesus is the fulfillment of both.
Classification: Gospel — Narrative
Key Themes: three disciples taken up a mountain and shown a glory breaking through ordinary human form, his face did shine as the sun, Moses and Elijah appearing beside him before a voice repeats the words from the baptism with one addition, hear ye him, a boy the disciples cannot heal met first with exasperation and then with an explanation of size, faith as a grain of mustard seed, a second passion prediction answered in four words, they were exceeding sorry, and an episode found only in this Gospel, a temple tax paid though Jesus argues the sons of the king are exempt from it, the coin pulled from a fish's mouth
The Transfiguration (vv.1-13): Six days after, and the three taken up are the same three who will be taken into Gethsemane. Matthew describes the change in two comparisons, his face did shine as the sun, and his raiment was white as the light. Moses and Elijah appear, Peter offers to build three tabernacles, and the voice from the cloud repeats the words from the baptism with three added, hear ye him. The disciples fall on their faces and are touched and told to rise, and Matthew's closing note is quiet, they lifted up their eyes, they saw no man, save Jesus only. Coming down, the question about Elijah is answered by identifying him with a man already dead.
The Boy the Disciples Could Not Heal (vv.14-21): The father's complaint is about the disciples rather than the illness, I brought him to thy disciples, and they could not cure him, and the first response is exasperated, O faithless and perverse generation, how long shall I be with you? The healing is one clause. Then the private question, why could not we cast him out? and the answer is about size, because of your unbelief, for verily I say unto you, if ye have faith as a grain of mustard seed, ye shall say unto this mountain, Remove hence to yonder place, and it shall remove.
The Father's Voice: "This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased; hear ye him" (v.5). The same declaration as at the baptism (3:17) with one addition: "hear ye him." The disciples are commanded to listen to Jesus above Moses and Elijah — He is greater than both.
The Demon-Possessed Boy: The disciples failed to cast out the demon. Jesus attributes their failure to unbelief (v.20). This is a picture of the church's powerlessness when faith is weak — and a reminder that spiritual authority flows from dependent faith, not technique.
The Tribute Money (vv.22-27): The second passion prediction is given in two verses and Matthew records the reaction in four words, and they were exceeding sorry. Then an episode found only in this Gospel, and it is about a temple tax. Peter answers for his master before asking him, and the argument put to him afterwards is about family, do the kings of the earth take custom of their own children, or of strangers? The conclusion is that they are exempt, and the tax is paid anyway, notwithstanding, lest we should offend them. The coin comes out of a fish's mouth, and it is enough for two.

Map & Geography

  • "A high mountain" (v.1): Traditionally identified as Mount Tabor in Galilee, though Mount Hermon (near Caesarea Philippi, the setting of ch.16) is more likely geographically — it is the highest peak in the region (~9,200 ft).
  • Capernaum (v.24): Jesus returned to Capernaum, where the Temple tax collectors approached Peter. This was Jesus' home base in Galilee.

Setting: Northern Galilee, probably late summer or autumn of AD 29, some six months before the crucifixion. Chapter 16 was the turning point of the Gospel: at Caesarea Philippi Peter confessed Jesus as "the Christ, the Son of the living God," and from then on Jesus began to tell his disciples plainly that he must suffer, die and be raised. He added that some standing there would "see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom" (16:28). Chapter 17 opens with a glimpse of exactly that: on a mountain, three disciples see his glory. Then the story comes down into a valley of failure and need, a second warning of the cross, and a quiet scene about a tax in Capernaum. Glory and humility sit side by side all through the chapter.

The Transfiguration (vv1–13)

  • "After six days" (v1). Matthew and Mark count six days from Peter's confession; Luke says "about eight days" (Luke 9:28), probably counting inclusively. The link to 16:28 is deliberate. The promise that some would see the kingdom before they died is kept, in part, within a week.
  • Peter, James and John (v1). The inner circle. The same three were with Jesus when he raised Jairus's daughter (Mark 5:37) and will be closest to him in Gethsemane (26:37). They see his glory here and his agony there. Peter later writes, "we were eyewitnesses of his majesty… when we were with him on the holy mountain" (2 Pet 1:16–18).
  • "A high mountain by themselves" (v1). The text does not name it. Tradition, from the fourth century on, points to Mount Tabor in the Jezreel Valley, but it is about 1,900 feet high and had a fortified settlement on top in Jesus' day. Many prefer Mount Hermon (over 9,000 feet) near Caesarea Philippi. The Tyndale notes favor Mount Meron, between Caesarea Philippi and Capernaum. No one knows for sure, and the Gospels show no interest in the place itself.
  • "He was transfigured before them" (v2). The verb is metamorphoō, to transform, the root of English "metamorphosis." It appears only four times in the New Testament: twice of this event (here and Mark 9:2), and twice of believers being "transformed" (Rom 12:2; 2 Cor 3:18). The glory Jesus shows openly here is the glory his people are slowly being changed into.
  • "His face shone like the sun" (v2). Moses' face shone after speaking with God on Sinai (Exod 34:29–35), but that light was reflected and faded. Jesus' light comes from within. Matthew alone adds "like the sun," the same picture John uses for the risen Christ (Rev 1:16). Jesus had said the righteous "will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father" (13:43).
  • "Moses and Elijah, talking with him" (v3). Moses stands for the Law and Elijah for the Prophets, the two parts of Israel's Scriptures Jesus came to fulfill (5:17). Both met God on a mountain (Exod 24; 1 Kgs 19:8–18). Both had unusual endings: God buried Moses himself (Deut 34:5–6), and Elijah was taken up in a whirlwind (2 Kgs 2:11). Luke tells what they talked about: his "departure" (literally his "exodus") that he was about to accomplish at Jerusalem (Luke 9:31).
  • "It is good that we are here… three tents" (v4). The word is skēnē, a tent or booth, the word used for the tabernacle. Peter may have been thinking of the Feast of Booths, when Israel lived in shelters and looked ahead to God's final kingdom (Zech 14:16). He wants to hold onto the moment, and in doing so he puts Jesus on a level with Moses and Elijah. Mark adds kindly, "he did not know what to say, for they were terrified" (Mark 9:6).
  • "A bright cloud overshadowed them" (v5). This is the cloud of God's presence, the glory that covered Sinai (Exod 24:15–18) and filled the tabernacle so that even Moses could not enter (Exod 40:34–35). Later Jewish writers called that presence the Shekinah. The word "overshadowed" is also used of the Spirit coming upon Mary (Luke 1:35).
  • "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased; listen to him" (v5). The Father repeats his words from Jesus' baptism (3:17), which echo Psalm 2:7 (the royal Son) and Isaiah 42:1 (the Servant in whom God delights). Then he adds "listen to him," which recalls Deuteronomy 18:15, where Moses promised that God would raise up a prophet like him: "it is to him you shall listen." Peter had just tried to correct Jesus about the cross (16:22). The Father's word answers him: do not speak over the Son; listen to him.
  • "They fell on their faces and were terrified" (v6). Matthew alone records this. The voice, more than the sight, drives them to the ground, the common reaction of people who meet God's holiness (Isa 6:5; Dan 10:9; Rev 1:17).
  • "Jesus came and touched them… 'Rise, and have no fear'" (v7). Another Matthew detail. The one whose face blazed like the sun is the one who reaches out and touches frightened men. Glory and gentleness are in the same person.
  • "They saw no one but Jesus only" (v8). Moses and Elijah are gone. The Law and the Prophets have done their work of witness, and the Son remains. The point of the scene is in those last words.
  • "Tell no one the vision, until the Son of Man is raised" (v9). "Vision" (horama) here means something seen, not a dream, as the three are fully awake. The command to secrecy fits Matthew's pattern (16:20). Talk of a glorious Messiah before the cross would only feed political hopes. After the resurrection the glory and the suffering could be told together.
  • "Why do the scribes say that first Elijah must come?" (v10). Malachi, the last prophet in the Old Testament, promised that God would send "Elijah the prophet before the great and awesome day of the LORD" (Mal 4:5–6). If Jesus is the Messiah, and the disciples have just seen Elijah leave again, where is the promised forerunner?
  • "Elijah does come… But I tell you that Elijah has already come" (vv11–12). Jesus affirms Malachi's promise and then says it has been fulfilled in a way no one recognized. John the Baptist came "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17), dressed like him (3:4; 2 Kgs 1:8), and confronted a king and his wicked wife as Elijah confronted Ahab and Jezebel. John said he was not Elijah in person (John 1:21), and Jesus does not say he was. He was the Elijah of Malachi's promise. Some interpreters, especially in dispensational circles, also expect a future Elijah-like figure before Christ's return (often linked with the two witnesses of Rev 11). Others think "will restore all things" simply describes John's work as Malachi pictured it.
  • "So also the Son of Man will certainly suffer at their hands" (v12). They "did to him whatever they pleased": Herod had John beheaded (14:1–12). The forerunner's fate previews the Messiah's.
  • Commentators: Guzik and the Tyndale notes both weigh the mountain candidates, and both lean toward Meron as fitting the route from Caesarea Philippi to Capernaum. Guzik says metamorphoō points to a change from within, not a surface glow, which fits the lexicon's "transform" and its use for inner change in Romans 12:2. His striking point is that the greater wonder is not that Jesus' glory showed for a moment, but that he kept it veiled the rest of the time. He also notes that the disciples fell down at the voice, not the light. Henry says Jesus took three because that was "a competent number to testify what they should see," since the law required two or three witnesses (Deut 19:15). Guzik ties the future Elijah to the two witnesses of Revelation 11, a view Henry and JFB do not share. JFB gives no comment on these verses in Matthew and sends readers to its notes on Luke 9.

Jesus Heals a Boy with a Demon (vv14–21)

  • From the mountain to the crowd (v14). Raphael's famous last painting, The Transfiguration, puts both scenes on one canvas: Christ in glory above and the convulsing boy and helpless disciples below. Matthew's order makes the same point. The glory on the mountain does not stay there; it comes down to meet human need.
  • "Lord, have mercy on my son" (v15). The father kneels, the posture of a man who has run out of options. Mark gives a much longer account, including his famous cry, "I believe; help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:14–29). Luke adds that this was his only son (Luke 9:38).
  • "He has seizures" (v15). The Greek word is selēniazomai, literally "to be moonstruck." The ancient world linked epilepsy with the moon, and the old English word "lunatic" (KJV) comes from the Latin for moon. The word appears only here and in 4:24. Matthew describes symptoms that look like epilepsy, and v18 says a demon was the cause. Matthew 4:24 lists the demon-possessed and those with seizures as separate groups, so the Gospels do not treat every seizure as demonic. Here the two were bound together.
  • "Often he falls into the fire, and often into the water" (v15). In a home with an open cooking fire and in a land with wells, cisterns and the lake, the boy was in constant danger. Mark says the spirit tried to destroy him (Mark 9:22).
  • "I brought him to your disciples, and they could not heal him" (v16). The nine left below had been given authority over unclean spirits (10:1, 8) and had used it before. Now, in front of a crowd and, Mark says, arguing scribes (Mark 9:14), they failed.
  • "O faithless and twisted generation" (v17). The words echo Moses' song about a rebellious Israel, "a crooked and twisted generation" (Deut 32:5). Jesus' frustration takes in the whole scene: the crowd, the scribes, and his own disciples. "How long am I to be with you?" is the sigh of someone who knows his time with them is short.
  • "Bring him here to me" (v17). Whatever the failure of his followers, the way to Jesus himself is still open. "The boy was healed instantly" (v18). Matthew tells it briefly. The demon has no answer to Jesus' rebuke.
  • "Why could we not cast it out?" (v19). They ask privately, which is to their credit. "Because of your little faith" (v20). The word is oligopistia, "littleness of faith," found only here in the New Testament, though Matthew often uses the related "you of little faith" (6:30; 8:26; 14:31; 16:8). The problem was not that their faith was small in size, as the next line shows, but that it had shifted from God to themselves, as if their authority were a power they owned.
  • "Faith like a grain of mustard seed" (v20). The mustard seed was proverbially the smallest seed a Galilean farmer sowed (13:31–32). Tiny faith in a great God is enough. "Move this mountain" was a known Jewish figure of speech for overcoming what seems impossible; the rabbis called a great teacher an "uprooter of mountains." Jesus may have pointed to the very mountain they had just come down. He is not promising a magic trick but saying that nothing God wills is out of reach for those who trust him.
  • Verse 21 is missing. The ESV jumps from v20 to v22 and gives v21 in a footnote: "But this kind never comes out except by prayer and fasting." The verse is absent from the earliest and best manuscripts of Matthew, including Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, and most scholars think scribes copied it in from Mark 9:29. Mark's own text says "by prayer," and some later manuscripts of Mark add "and fasting." The KJV includes the verse because it followed later manuscripts. Nothing in Christian teaching depends on it, and Mark's version of the saying is secure.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Sense of misery will bring people to their knees," and he comforts readers that "the failures of instruments shall not hinder the operations of his grace." He also writes, "It is because of our unbelief, that we bring so little to pass in religion." Guzik sees the descent from glory into a scene of failure as a deliberate contrast, and he notes the "moonstruck" sense of the word, which the lexicon confirms. He stresses that the size of faith matters far less than what it rests on. On the prayer-and-fasting saying Henry comments that "fasting is of use to put an edge upon prayer," and Guzik treats both as signs of dependence on God, though neither of them discusses the manuscript question. JFB again sends readers to Mark 9.

Jesus Again Foretells Death and Resurrection (vv22–23)

  • "As they were gathering in Galilee" (v22). Mark says Jesus did not want anyone to know where they were, because he was teaching his disciples (Mark 9:30–31). This was a private journey, not a preaching tour.
  • "The Son of Man is about to be delivered into the hands of men" (v22). This is the second of three plain predictions in Matthew (16:21; 20:18–19). "Delivered" (paradidōmi) is the word used later for Judas betraying him (26:15–16), for the leaders handing him to Pilate (27:2) and for Pilate handing him over to be crucified (27:26). Behind all of them, Paul says, God "gave him up for us all" (Rom 8:32), using the same verb. There is wordplay too: the Son of Man is handed over into the hands of men.
  • "And he will be raised on the third day" (v23). Each prediction ends with resurrection. The disciples hear the first half and miss the second.
  • "They were greatly distressed" (v23). This is progress of a kind. In chapter 16 Peter rebuked Jesus for such talk. Now they believe him enough to grieve. Luke adds that they did not understand and were afraid to ask (Luke 9:45).
  • Commentators: JFB, quoting Mark, calls this "not a preaching, but a private, journey through Galilee," and thinks their sorrow was deeper than at the first announcement, because it was now sinking in. Guzik notes how often the disciples heard these predictions and still grieved instead of hoping, because they did not take in the promise of resurrection. Henry observes that Christ fully knew what would happen to him and took up the work of redemption anyway.

The Temple Tax (vv24–27)

  • "The collectors of the two-drachma tax" (v24). This was not a Roman tax. It was the annual temple tax that every Jewish man over twenty paid for the upkeep of the temple. It went back to the half-shekel "atonement money" of Exodus 30:11–16, made a yearly payment in later times (2 Chr 24:6–9; Neh 10:32). In Jesus' day the half-shekel was reckoned as two drachmas, about two days' wages. Collectors went round in the month of Adar (February–March), before Passover. Only Matthew, the former tax collector, tells this story.
  • "Does your teacher not pay the tax?" (v24). The question may be a trap or a simple inquiry. Priests claimed exemption, and some groups disputed the tax. The Qumran community thought it should be paid only once in a lifetime. "Yes" (v25). Peter answers for Jesus without asking him, which sets up the lesson.
  • "Jesus spoke to him first" (v25). Before Peter could raise it, Jesus already knew. He asks a question from everyday politics: do kings tax their own families or their subjects? Subjects, of course.
  • "Then the sons are free" (v26). The temple is his Father's house (21:13; John 2:16). The King's Son does not pay tax to his Father's house. Jesus has already said "something greater than the temple is here" (12:6). Interpreters differ on whether "sons" (plural) means only Jesus or includes his disciples as children of the Father too. JFB insists it means Jesus himself. Others note that Jesus includes Peter in the payment and see a hint that his followers share his freedom as sons.
  • "However, not to give offense to them" (v27). "Offense" is skandalizō, to cause someone to stumble. Jesus has the right not to pay, but he will not let a needless quarrel over a tax turn people away from the temple or from him. He sets aside a right for others' sake, which is exactly what Paul later urges the church to do (1 Cor 8:13; 9:12).
  • "Take the first fish that comes up… you will find a shekel" (v27). The coin is a statēr, a silver coin worth four drachmas, exactly the tax for two men. Peter, a net fisherman, is sent out with a single hook. Matthew does not actually narrate Peter catching the fish. He simply leaves Jesus' word standing, as if its outcome is beyond doubt. Some have tried to explain the miracle away. The text gives it as a sign of the Son's authority over creation, and of his care in supplying what Peter could not.
  • "For me and for yourself" (v27). Jesus does not say "for us." He keeps the difference between the free Son and the disciple who owes. Yet one coin covers both. The Son pays what he does not owe, and pays for Peter too.
  • Commentators: JFB calls the tax "an ecclesiastical tax," not a civil one, and on v26 has Jesus saying in effect, "As His Son, then, that tax is not due by Me—I AM FREE." It also notes the careful wording "for Me and thee." Guzik draws out that Jesus went beyond what he owed to avoid needless offense, and sees in one payment covering both men a picture of redemption. He also follows R. T. France in reading the story as a sign that Matthew wrote before the temple fell in AD 70, since after that the tax went to a pagan temple in Rome. Henry's outline places this scene, with the passion prediction, under Christ's "humiliation."

Pointing to Christ

The chapter shows who Jesus is from two sides. On the mountain the Father names him the beloved Son, Moses and Elijah fade, and only Jesus is left standing. In Capernaum the same Son quietly pays a tax he does not owe, so as not to put a stumbling block in anyone's way, and his payment covers Peter's debt as well as his own. Between those two scenes lies a boy delivered by his word and a second warning that he will be handed over to die and rise. The glory and the cross belong together. The one whose face shone like the sun is the one who touched terrified men and said, "Rise, and have no fear."

Reflection

  • Listen to Him Above All (v.5) — The Father's voice from the cloud commands the disciples to listen to Jesus, not to any other voice, however good. What competing voices are you listening to more than Christ's?
  • Jesus Alone Remains (v.8) — When the vision fades, Moses and Elijah are gone and only Jesus is left standing. What traditions, teachers, or past experiences do you need to let fade so Christ alone has the final word?
  • Faith Over Formula (v.20) — The disciples fail to heal the boy because of unbelief, and Jesus says even faith the size of a mustard seed can move mountains. Are you relying on method and routine instead of genuine trust in God's power?
  • Deeper Battles Need Deeper Preparation (v.21) — Jesus points to prayer and fasting as the path through certain strongholds that don't yield easily. What spiritual discipline are you neglecting that might be the key to breakthrough where you feel stuck?
  • Freedom Surrendered for Others (v.27) — Though exempt, Jesus pays the Temple tax so as not to needlessly offend. Where might you need to set aside your own rights for the sake of others — not because you're wrong, but because love chooses not to cause offense?
