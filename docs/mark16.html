15And He said to them, “Go into all the world and preach the gospel to every creature.

Summary

Early on the first day of the week, Mary Magdalene and other women come to the tomb with spices, wondering who will roll away the stone, only to find it already open and empty (vv. 1-4). A young man dressed in white tells them Jesus has risen and instructs them to tell the disciples and Peter that He is going ahead of them to Galilee, but the women flee in fear and say nothing to anyone (vv. 5-8). In the passage traditionally included as the longer ending, the risen Jesus appears to Mary Magdalene, to two disciples on the road, and to the Eleven, rebuking their unbelief before commissioning them to go into all the world and preach the gospel to every creature, and He is then taken up into heaven (vv. 9-20).

Authorship & Background

Author: John Mark (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 16 records the resurrection of Jesus — the event that validates everything He claimed and everything He accomplished. Mark's resurrection account is the shortest of the four Gospels.
Classification: Gospel — Narrative
Key Themes: Spices bought and a stone already rolled away before the question about it is finished, an errand that names one disciple specifically among the rest, the earliest manuscripts ending on fear and silence rather than resolution, a longer ending debated for centuries as possibly not Mark's own hand, and a Gospel that leaves its reader to decide whether they too will tell
The Stone Rolled Away (vv.1-8): The women buy spices when the sabbath is past and set out at sunrise, and the problem they discuss on the way is practical, who shall roll us away the stone from the door of the sepulchre? It is already done. Inside, a young man in a long white garment tells them he is not there, behold the place where they laid him, and gives them an errand that names one disciple specifically, go your way, tell his disciples and Peter. Then the sentence on which the earliest manuscripts of this Gospel end: they went out quickly, and fled from the sepulchre, for they trembled and were amazed, neither said they any thing to any man, for they were afraid.
The Textual Question (vv.9-20): The most significant textual issue in the New Testament. The two oldest Greek manuscripts (Sinaiticus and Vaticanus) end at verse 8. Most scholars believe Mark's original ending was either lost or that he intentionally ended at v.8 — with the women fleeing in fear and amazement. Verses 9-20 (the "Longer Ending") were likely added later to provide a more complete conclusion. They are consistent with the other Gospels but may not be from Mark's hand.
Mark's Ending at Verse 8: If Mark intentionally ended here, it is a masterful literary choice. The women are told to go tell the disciples — but "they said nothing to any man; for they were afraid" (v.8). The reader is left with a question: will you tell? Will you overcome your fear and proclaim the risen Christ? The open ending makes every reader a participant.

Map & Geography

  • The tomb (vv.1-6): The rock-hewn tomb near Golgotha in Jerusalem where Jesus was buried. The women came at sunrise on the first day of the week.
  • Galilee (v.7): The angel directed the disciples to meet Jesus in Galilee — back where it all began.
  • "All the world" (v.15): The commission extends the geographic scope from Israel to every creature in every place.

Deep Dive

Setting: Jerusalem, early Sunday morning, the third day after the crucifixion, around AD 30. Jesus died on Friday afternoon and was hurriedly buried before sunset in a rock tomb belonging to Joseph of Arimathea. Two of the women watched where he was laid (15:47). Saturday was the Sabbath, a day of enforced stillness. Now the women return to finish the burial and find the tomb open and empty. Mark's account of the resurrection is the shortest in the Gospels, and it ends at v8 with the women running from the tomb in fear. Verses 9–20, set in double brackets in the ESV, are missing from the two oldest complete manuscripts of Mark, and most scholars think they were added later. This study looks at both parts, and at why the question matters less than it may first seem.

The Resurrection (vv1–8)

  • "When the Sabbath was past… bought spices" (v1). The Sabbath ended at sunset on Saturday, when shops could open again. The burial on Friday had been rushed. The women bought aromatic oils to pour on the body, a final act of love and a way of covering the smell of decay. They were not embalming, which Jews did not practice. They plainly expected to find a corpse.
  • The women (v1). Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James and Salome were at the cross (15:40) and at the burial (15:47). They are the only people in Mark who see the death, the tomb and the empty tomb. In that culture women were not usually accepted as legal witnesses. If the early church had invented the story, it would hardly have made women its first witnesses. Their presence is one of the marks of a true report.
  • "Very early on the first day of the week, when the sun had risen" (v2). Sunday. Christians began meeting on "the first day of the week" because of this morning (Acts 20:7; 1 Cor 16:2), and by the end of the first century called it "the Lord's day" (Rev 1:10).
  • "Who will roll away the stone for us?" (v3). Rich families' tombs were closed with a large disk-shaped stone rolled in a sloping channel. Rolling it down into place was easy. Rolling it back up was hard work for several men. The women set out without a plan for it. Matthew adds that the tomb had been sealed and guarded (Matt 27:62–66).
  • "The stone had been rolled back—it was very large" (v4). The stone was not moved to let Jesus out. He was already gone. It was moved to let the witnesses in. Matthew says an angel rolled it away (Matt 28:2).
  • "A young man sitting on the right side, dressed in a white robe" (v5). Mark calls him a young man; Matthew calls him an angel (Matt 28:5). White robes in the Bible mark heavenly beings (Dan 7:9; Acts 1:10; Rev 7:9). Luke and John mention two (Luke 24:4; John 20:12). The accounts differ in how many they mention, not in what was said. Some link this "young man" with the one who fled naked from Gethsemane (14:51–52), the same Greek word, now clothed in white. That is an attractive literary echo, but the text does not make it explicit.
  • "Do not be alarmed" (v6). "Alarmed" is the strong word Mark used of Jesus in Gethsemane (14:33).
  • "You seek Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified. He has risen; he is not here. See the place where they laid him" (v6). Every word counts. "Jesus of Nazareth": the same man, not a different one. "Who was crucified": the Greek perfect tense means he remains the crucified one; the risen Lord still bears his wounds (John 20:27). "He has risen" is literally "he was raised," God's act (Acts 2:24). "See the place": the empty tomb is evidence, and the women are invited to look.
  • "Go, tell his disciples and Peter" (v7). Only Mark includes "and Peter." The man who swore he did not know Jesus (14:71) is named. He has not been written off. If early tradition is right that Mark wrote down Peter's preaching, then Peter himself included these two words every time he told the story.
  • "He is going before you to Galilee. There you will see him, just as he told you" (v7). Jesus promised this the night of his arrest (14:28). Galilee is where it all began (1:14–20), and he will regather his scattered followers there. Matthew tells of the meeting on a mountain in Galilee (Matt 28:16–20), and John of breakfast by the lake (John 21).
  • "They went out and fled… trembling and astonishment… they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid" (v8). Throughout Mark, people react to God's power with fear and awe: the disciples after the storm (4:41), the people of Gerasa (5:15), the three on the mountain (9:6). This is not mere fright but the overwhelming dread of meeting God's work. "Said nothing to anyone" probably means they spoke to no one on the way, not that they never told the disciples. Matthew says they ran "with fear and great joy… to tell his disciples" (Matt 28:8), and Luke that they reported it to the eleven (Luke 24:9).
  • Did Mark end here? The oldest manuscripts end at v8, with the Greek word gar, "for": "they were afraid, for." Some scholars think Mark ended deliberately on this unsettling note, leaving readers to answer the question the women faced. Others, including the Tyndale notes, find that hard to believe. A Gospel that opens with "the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (1:1) and fulfills every one of Jesus' predictions would be unlikely to leave the promised meeting in Galilee untold. They suggest the original last page was lost or never finished. Either way, the resurrection itself is plain in v6: "He has risen."
  • Commentators: Guzik notes that the women went expecting a body and worried about the stone, which shows they were not inventing a story they hoped for. He takes "and Peter" as a special word of grace to the one who failed worst. Henry agrees that the angel named Peter because he was a penitent who might fear he was shut out: "They must be sure to tell Peter." JFB, believing Mark drew mainly on Peter's preaching, calls the phrase "deeply affecting." Guzik and the Tyndale notes both read the women's silence as silence on the road, not failure to report.

Jesus Appears to Mary Magdalene (vv9–11)

  • The longer ending. Verses 9–20 are missing from Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, the two oldest complete Greek manuscripts (both from the 300s). Early writers Eusebius and Jerome said the most accurate copies they knew ended at v8. Some manuscripts mark the verses as doubtful, and one adds a different, shorter ending of a single sentence. The vocabulary and style differ from the rest of Mark, and the move from v8 to v9 is abrupt: Mary Magdalene is introduced again as if not just named, and the other women vanish. On the other hand, the longer ending is in the vast majority of manuscripts, and Irenaeus quotes v19 as Mark's around AD 180, so it was known very early. Most scholars conclude that it was written in the early 100s to round off a Gospel that seemed to stop too soon, drawing on what the other Gospels and Acts record. It has been read in the church for most of its history.
  • What does that mean for readers? The ESV keeps the verses in the text with brackets, so readers can see both the evidence and the passage. Nothing in vv9–20 teaches anything not also found elsewhere in the New Testament. Most of it is a summary of appearances told more fully in Luke, John and Acts. Where it says something unusual, such as picking up serpents (v18), it is wise not to build anything on it that the rest of Scripture does not support.
  • "He appeared first to Mary Magdalene, from whom he had cast out seven demons" (v9). This matches John 20:11–18, where Mary weeps at the tomb and meets Jesus, thinking he is the gardener. Luke 8:2 mentions the seven demons. The one who had been most deeply bound becomes the first witness of the risen Lord.
  • "As they mourned and wept… they would not believe it" (vv10–11). Luke says the apostles thought the women's words "an idle tale" (Luke 24:11). The disciples were not ready to believe. Their resistance is another sign that the resurrection was not something they made up because they expected it.
  • Commentators: Guzik sets out the case on both sides: the oldest manuscripts and the differences in style against the verses, and the great majority of manuscripts and early citations for them. He concludes that Mark probably did not mean to stop at v8 and that the original ending may have been lost early. JFB comments on these verses as part of Mark without raising the question. The Tyndale notes say nearly all scholars agree Mark did not write either added ending.

Jesus Appears to Two Disciples (vv12–13)

  • "In another form to two of them, as they were walking into the country" (v12). This is a summary of the Emmaus story told in full in Luke 24:13–35. "In another form" explains why they did not recognize him at first (Luke 24:16). His risen body was truly his own, but changed.
  • "They did not believe them" (v13). Here the longer ending differs a little from Luke, who says that by the time the two returned, the eleven were already saying, "The Lord has risen indeed, and has appeared to Simon!" (Luke 24:34). Probably belief and doubt were mixed that evening. Luke admits that even when Jesus stood among them, "they still disbelieved for joy" (Luke 24:41).
  • Commentators: Guzik reads the repeated unbelief as evidence that the disciples were not quick to believe something they wanted to be true. JFB links v12 to the Emmaus account in Luke.

The Great Commission (vv14–20)

  • "He appeared to the eleven… and he rebuked them for their unbelief and hardness of heart" (v14). "The eleven," because Judas was gone. "Hardness of heart" is the very charge Mark has made against the disciples before (6:52; 8:17). Even after the resurrection, Jesus' first word to his followers in this passage is a rebuke, and his next is a commission. He sends the same people who doubted.
  • "Go into all the world and proclaim the gospel to the whole creation" (v15). Compare Matthew 28:19–20 and Acts 1:8. Jesus said the gospel must be preached to all nations before the end (13:10). "The whole creation" stresses that no people or place is outside the reach of the good news.
  • "Whoever believes and is baptized will be saved, but whoever does not believe will be condemned" (v16). Notice that condemnation is tied to unbelief alone, not to lack of baptism. In the New Testament, faith and baptism normally go together, as the outward sign follows the inner trust (Acts 2:38, 41; Rom 6:3–4). Most Christian traditions take this verse to describe that normal pattern rather than to teach that baptism itself saves. The thief on the cross was promised paradise without baptism (Luke 23:43). Traditions that hold baptism to be a means of grace (Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran) and those that see it as an act of obedience and testimony (Baptist and many evangelicals) both affirm that salvation rests on Christ received by faith.
  • "These signs will accompany those who believe" (vv17–18). Most of the list matches what Acts records: demons cast out (Acts 16:18), new tongues (Acts 2:4; 10:46), the sick healed by laying on of hands (Acts 28:8). "They will pick up serpents" recalls Paul shaking off a viper on Malta unharmed (Acts 28:3–6). Drinking poison has no New Testament example, though Papias, an early writer quoted by Eusebius, tells of a Christian named Justus who drank poison and survived. These are promises of God's protection for his messengers in danger, not tests to stage. Jesus himself refused to throw himself from the temple to test God's protection (Matt 4:5–7). The few modern groups who handle snakes in worship build on a disputed passage what no other Scripture teaches.
  • Do these signs continue? Christians differ. Some hold that miraculous gifts were mainly for the founding of the church and have largely ceased. Others hold that all the gifts continue. Many stand between, believing God still heals and works wonders as he wills, without promising them on demand. Verse 20 itself ties the signs to the message: they "confirmed the message."
  • "The Lord Jesus… was taken up into heaven and sat down at the right hand of God" (v19). This summarizes the ascension (Luke 24:50–51; Acts 1:9–11). "Sat down at the right hand of God" is Psalm 110:1, the verse Jesus quoted in the temple (12:36) and before the high priest (14:62). The man condemned for claiming that seat now holds it. Irenaeus quotes this verse as Mark's around AD 180.
  • "They went out and preached everywhere, while the Lord worked with them" (v20). The book of Acts in one sentence. The disciples who ran from Gethsemane and hid after the cross now go everywhere. They do not go alone. The risen Lord "worked with them," as he promised, "I am with you always" (Matt 28:20).
  • Commentators: Guzik notes that condemnation in v16 rests on unbelief, and that baptism is obedience rather than a condition of salvation. He warns that handling snakes or drinking poison was never meant as a test of faith, and points to Paul on Malta. JFB calls baptism here "the external signature of the inner faith of the heart" and observes that "the Lord" in v19 is a title used this way nowhere else in Mark, though it is frequent in Luke, another small sign the verses come from a different hand. JFB also ties v20 to Matthew's "Lo, I am with you alway."

Pointing to Christ

Mark began with "the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" and has shown a Messiah who suffers, serves and gives his life as a ransom. Here is the end that Jesus kept predicting: "after three days he will rise." The tomb is open, the body is gone, and the message is simple: "He has risen; he is not here." The first word sent to the disciples singles out the one who failed worst, "and Peter." Whether Mark's own pen stopped at the women's fear or went on, the story does not end there, because the risen Lord goes ahead of his people, meets them, forgives them and sends them into the whole world with him working alongside.

Reflection

  • The Empty Tomb Changes Everything (v.6) — The announcement that Jesus is risen and no longer in the tomb is the hinge the entire Christian faith turns on. Do you live as though the resurrection is actually true, or treat it as a doctrine you simply affirm?
  • Grace That Names the Failure (v.7) — The angel singles out Peter by name, the disciple who had denied Jesus three times, making sure he knows he is still called. Where do you need to believe that your worst failure hasn't disqualified you from God's grace?
  • Fear That Almost Silences Good News (v.8) — The women leave the tomb trembling and afraid, and the earliest manuscripts suggest Mark's account may have originally ended right there, on a note of silence. What fear is currently keeping you from telling someone what God has done?
  • Doubt Confronted, Not Excused (v.14) — Jesus rebukes the disciples for their unbelief and hardness of heart even after hearing reports that He was alive. What doubts are you avoiding instead of bringing honestly to Him?
  • A Commission for Every Believer (v.15) — Jesus sends His followers to carry the gospel to the whole world, not just to a select few. What would obeying that command look like in your specific daily life?
  • Not Sent Alone (v.20) — As the disciples go out preaching, the Lord works alongside them, confirming their message. How does knowing Christ is actively working with you change how you approach opportunities to share your faith?
