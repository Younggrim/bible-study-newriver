1There was a man in the land of Uz whose name was Job. And this man was blameless and upright, fearing God and shunning evil.

2He had seven sons and three daughters,

3and he owned 7,000 sheep, 3,000 camels, 500 yoke of oxen, 500 female donkeys, and a very large number of servants. Job was the greatest man of all the people of the East.

4Job’s sons would take turns holding feasts in their homes, and they would invite their three sisters to eat and drink with them.

5And when the days of feasting were over, Job would send for his children to purify them, rising early in the morning to offer burnt offerings for all of them. For Job thought, “Perhaps my children have sinned and cursed God in their hearts.” This was Job’s regular practice.

6One day the sons of God came to present themselves before the LORD, and Satan also came with them.

7“Where have you come from?” said the LORD to Satan. “From roaming through the earth,” he replied, “and walking back and forth in it.”

8Then the LORD said to Satan, “Have you considered My servant Job? For there is no one on earth like him, a man who is blameless and upright, who fears God and shuns evil.”

9Satan answered the LORD, “Does Job fear God for nothing?

10Have You not placed a hedge on every side around him and his household and all that he owns? You have blessed the work of his hands, and his possessions have increased in the land.

11But stretch out Your hand and strike all that he has, and he will surely curse You to Your face.”

12“Very well,” said the LORD to Satan. “Everything he has is in your hands, but you must not lay a hand on the man himself.” Then Satan went out from the presence of the LORD.

13One day, while Job’s sons and daughters were eating and drinking wine in their oldest brother’s house,

14a messenger came and reported to Job: “While the oxen were plowing and the donkeys were grazing nearby,

17While he was still speaking, another messenger came and reported: “The Chaldeans formed three bands, raided the camels, and took them away. They put the servants to the sword, and I alone have escaped to tell you!”

Summary

The narrator introduces Job, a man from the land of Uz who is blameless, upright, and extraordinarily wealthy, with seven sons, three daughters, and vast flocks and herds (vv. 1-3). His sons take turns hosting feasts, and Job regularly offers sacrifices afterward in case they sinned in their hearts (vv. 4-5). In a heavenly scene, God points out Job's righteousness to Satan, who counters that Job only serves God because of his blessings and asks permission to test him (vv. 6-11). God allows Satan to strike everything Job owns, but not Job himself (v. 12). Four messengers arrive in rapid succession reporting the loss of his oxen, donkeys, sheep, camels, and finally all ten of his children in a collapsing house (vv. 13-19). Job tears his robe, shaves his head, and falls down worshiping, blessing the Lord's name rather than cursing him, and does not sin in all of this (vv. 20-22).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown. Candidates include Job himself, Moses, Solomon, or an anonymous sage. Likely the oldest book in the Bible by setting (patriarchal era, approximately 2000-1800 BC), though the date of composition is debated. The book addresses the problem of innocent suffering and God's sovereignty.
Historical Context: Chapter 1 introduces Job and establishes the premise of the entire book. Job lives in Uz (likely in Edom or northern Arabia), placing him outside Israel — this is a universal story about human suffering, not a narrowly Israelite one. The patriarchal setting is evident: Job acts as priest for his family (v.5), wealth is measured in livestock, and there is no reference to the Law, the tabernacle, or Israel's history. The heavenly council scene (vv.6-12) pulls back the curtain on the unseen realm, revealing that human suffering may have causes entirely hidden from the sufferer. Satan ("the adversary") appears not as an independent evil power but as one who operates within God's permission. The theological question at the heart of the book is posed by Satan himself: "Doth Job fear God for nought?" — Is disinterested righteousness possible? Can a man love God for who He is, not merely for what He gives?
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: four descriptive words, perfect, upright, God-fearing and evil-avoiding, applied at once to introduce a man before a single event happens to him, a heavenly council convened where God himself raises Job's name to Satan rather than the reverse, an accusation reduced to a single transactional question, doth Job fear God for nought, four catastrophes arriving in such rapid succession that each messenger's report overlaps the next, and a response of worship rather than curse, the LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away, blessed be the name of the LORD
Job's Character and Wealth (vv.1-5): Job is described with four characteristics: perfect (blameless/complete), upright (straight/ just), fears God, and eschews (turns away from) evil. He is the greatest man "of all the men of the east" — wealthy beyond measure with 7,000 sheep, 3,000 camels, 500 yoke of oxen, 500 donkeys, and a very great household. Seven sons and three daughters complete the picture of a blessed life. Yet Job is not complacent — he rises early to offer burnt offerings for his children, concerned they may have "cursed God in their hearts" (v.5). This continual priestly intercession reveals a man deeply aware of God's holiness and human frailty.
The Heavenly Council (vv.6-12): The scene shifts to heaven. "The sons of God" (angelic beings) present themselves before the LORD, and Satan comes among them. God initiates the conversation about Job — not Satan. God boasts of Job: "there is none like him in the earth." Satan's challenge is devastating in its logic: Job serves God because it pays well. Remove the hedge of blessing, and Job will curse God to His face. God permits the test with one restriction: Satan cannot touch Job's person. This reveals that Satan is not omnipotent, not sovereign, and operates only within divinely set boundaries.
The Four Catastrophes (vv.13-19): The disasters come in rapid succession — "while he was yet speaking" is repeated three times, emphasizing the overwhelming speed of loss. Sabeans take oxen and donkeys. Fire from heaven burns sheep. Chaldeans raid camels. A great wind kills all ten children. The disasters alternate between human evil (Sabeans, Chaldeans) and natural catastrophe (fire, wind), showing that Satan uses both human and natural agents. Each messenger ends with the same haunting phrase: "I only am escaped alone to tell thee."
Job's Response (vv.20-22): Job's response is worship. He tears his robe, shaves his head (signs of mourning), falls to the ground — and worships. His declaration is among the most profound in Scripture: "Naked came I out of my mother's womb, and naked shall I return thither: the LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD." The narrator's verdict: "In all this Job sinned not, nor charged God foolishly."

Deep Dive

Setting: Job is one of the Bible's wisdom books, alongside Proverbs and Ecclesiastes, and it asks the hardest question they raise: why do righteous people suffer? Nobody knows who wrote it or when. Guesses range from the age of Solomon to the time after the exile. The story itself, though, feels very old. Job measures wealth in livestock, offers sacrifices as the priest of his own family, lives about 140 years after his trials (42:16), and never mentions Israel, the law of Moses or the temple. Many readers place him in the time of the patriarchs, around Abraham. Ezekiel names him with Noah and Daniel as a byword for righteousness (Ezek 14:14, 20), and James points to "the steadfastness of Job" (Jas 5:11), so Scripture treats him as a real man. The book has a frame and a center. Chapters 1–2 and the end of chapter 42 are prose narrative. Everything in between is poetry: Job's lament, a long debate with three friends, a fourth speaker, and finally God's own voice from the whirlwind. Chapter 1 shows the reader something Job never sees, a scene in heaven that explains why his world is about to fall apart.

Job's Character and Wealth (vv1–5)

  • "There was a man in the land of Uz whose name was Job" (v1). The opening sounds like the start of a story told around a fire, and the Hebrew is the same pattern used for real people in 1 Samuel 1:1. Uz is somewhere east of the Jordan. Lamentations links it with Edom in the south (Lam 4:21), and Genesis lists an Uz among Aram's sons in the north (Gen 10:23). JFB places it toward the Euphrates. Either way Job is not an Israelite. The book's greatest example of faithfulness is a man from outside God's chosen people. The meaning of the name Job (Iyyob) is unknown. Suggestions include "object of enmity" and "one who turns back to God," but the lexicon calls them all doubtful.
  • "Blameless and upright, one who feared God and turned away from evil" (v1). Four marks of character, and God repeats them word for word in v8 and 2:3. "Blameless" is tam: complete, whole, a person of integrity, the same word used of Jacob as a "quiet" man (Gen 25:27). It doesn't mean sinless. Job himself later admits sin (7:21; 13:26). It means a person whose life is all of a piece, with no hidden double life. "Feared God" is the Old Testament's root of wisdom (Prov 1:7; Job 28:28). This verse is vital for the whole book. The narrator tells us, before anything happens, that Job's suffering is not a punishment. The three friends will spend thirty chapters arguing it must be.
  • Seven sons, three daughters, 7,000 sheep, 3,000 camels (vv2–3). The numbers are round and full: seven and three, ten children, five hundred and five hundred. They describe a life blessed in every way the ancient world could measure. Camels were the luxury vehicles of the desert. "The greatest of all the people of the east" sets him among the wise and wealthy of the eastern lands (compare 1 Kgs 4:30). At the end of the book God gives back exactly double the livestock (42:12).
  • "His sons used to go and hold a feast… on his day" (v4). "His day" probably means each son's birthday, or a set day in turn. The scene is happy: grown siblings who enjoy each other and include their sisters. Nothing in the text suggests the feasts were wild.
  • "Job would send and consecrate them… and offer burnt offerings according to the number of them all" (v5). Job acts as the priest of his household, as Noah and Abraham did, before there was a priesthood. "Consecrate" probably means calling them to wash and prepare, as Israel did before meeting God (Exod 19:10). He rises "early in the morning" to do it, and he does it "continually." His fear is not that they had done something openly wrong but that they had "cursed God in their hearts." Job cares about the heart, not just behavior.
  • "Cursed God" (v5). The Hebrew word here is actually barak, "bless." Writing "curse God" was so offensive that the writer uses "bless" as a polite substitute, as in 1 Kings 21:10, 13. The same word returns in 1:11, 2:5 and 2:9, and in 1:21 Job uses it in its normal sense: "blessed be the name of the LORD." The whole chapter turns on that one word. Satan says Job will "bless" (curse) God. Job blesses God.
  • Commentators: Henry's outline moves from Job's piety in general (v1) to a particular instance of it (v5), and he calls Job an example of suffering "from which no prosperity can secure us, but through which integrity and uprightness will preserve us." Guzik and JFB both stress that "blameless" means sincerity and consistency, not sinless perfection, and Guzik notes that a man who offers sacrifices for sin knows he isn't perfect. JFB reads barak in v5 as "bid farewell to" God rather than curse him. Guzik passes on the older view of E. W. Bullinger that scribes changed an original word for "curse" into "bless" out of reverence. Most scholars treat it as a polite substitute the writer chose, not a later change, and the result for the reader is the same.

Satan Allowed to Test Job (vv6–12)

  • "The sons of God came to present themselves before the LORD" (v6). The scene moves to heaven. "Sons of God" here means angels, heavenly beings who serve in God's court (38:7; Ps 89:6–7; 1 Kgs 22:19). They come to report, like officials before a king. The book uses God's covenant name, "the LORD" (Yahweh), in the prose frame, while the poetic speeches mostly use other names for God. Job may be from outside Israel, but the God in this scene is Israel's God.
  • "And Satan also came among them" (v6). In Hebrew it is ha-satan, "the satan," with the article. The lexicon gives the word's basic meaning as "adversary," someone who opposes or accuses, as in a courtroom (Ps 109:6; Zech 3:1). In Job it works almost like a job title: "the Accuser." Some scholars argue that this figure is only a heavenly prosecutor, not yet the devil of the New Testament. Most Christian readers identify him with Satan, "the accuser of our brothers" who "accuses them day and night before our God" (Rev 12:10). The way he acts here, cynical about faith and eager to destroy, fits that identification. Either way, he is a creature. He comes when summoned, answers questions, and needs permission.
  • "From going to and fro on the earth, and from walking up and down on it" (v7). The verb "going to and fro" (shut) is the same one used of the LORD's eyes that "run to and fro throughout the whole earth" to support the faithful (2 Chr 16:9; Zech 4:10). Satan roams too, but to find fault. Peter uses the same picture: the devil "prowls around like a roaring lion" (1 Pet 5:8).
  • "Have you considered my servant Job?" (v8). God, not Satan, raises Job's name. "My servant" is an honor given to Abraham, Moses, David and the prophets. God's boast in Job is the real starting point of the story. This is hard for readers, and the book doesn't explain it away. But it does show that God is in charge from the first line, and that Job's suffering begins with God's confidence in him, not God's anger at him.
  • "Does Job fear God for no reason?" (v9). This is the question the whole book tests. "For no reason" is chinnam, "for nothing, free of charge," from the root for "grace" or "favor." Satan's accusation is that Job's faith is a business deal: God pays, Job worships. Take away the pay, and the worship will stop. If that is true, there is no such thing as loving God for his own sake. In 2:3 God uses the same word back at Satan: "you incited me against him to destroy him without reason."
  • "Have you not put a hedge around him?" (v10). The hedge is God's protection and blessing (Ps 34:7). Satan admits he can't get through it. Later Job, not knowing about this conversation, will complain that God has "hedged in" his way (3:23), a related word turned bitter.
  • "Stretch out your hand and touch all that he has, and he will curse you to your face" (v11). Satan asks God to do it. The Tyndale notes observe that through the story the hand is both God's and Satan's: God permits, Satan strikes, and Job will rightly say the LORD has taken away.
  • "All that he has is in your hand. Only against him do not stretch out your hand" (v12). God sets the limit. Satan can touch Job's possessions and family but not his body. In chapter 2 the limit moves, but it is still God who sets it (2:6). The New Testament has the same pattern: "Satan demanded to have you, that he might sift you like wheat, but I have prayed for you" (Luke 22:31–32), and God "will not let you be tempted beyond your ability" (1 Cor 10:13).
  • The reader knows what Job doesn't. Job never hears about this scene, even at the end of the book. The friends argue without it. The reader is given a seat in heaven to learn one thing: suffering can have a purpose that the sufferer cannot see. The book does not say this is the reason for every trial. It says there can be reasons we are not told.
  • Commentators: Henry says Satan "thrust himself into an assembly of the sons of God." On v9 he grants that "It was a great truth that Job did not fear God for nought; he got much by it," but the suggestion that Job would have dropped God without reward was "a falsehood." He explains the hedge: "divine grace makes a hedge about their spiritual life, and divine providence about their natural life." On v12, "It is a limited power that the devil has." Guzik agrees that Satan needed permission and acts only within God's limits. He suggests the trial was meant partly to display God's wisdom to the angels (Eph 3:10), which is a theological inference, not something Job 1 says. JFB says the name Satan means "one who lies in wait." The lexicon gives only "adversary," so that sense should not be pressed. JFB rightly contrasts Satan the accuser with Christ, the believer's Advocate (1 John 2:1).

Satan Takes Job's Property and Children (vv13–22)

  • "Now there was a day when his sons and daughters were eating and drinking wine" (v13). The timing is cruel. Disaster strikes on a feast day, when the family is gathered and happy, and at the eldest brother's house, where the feasting began.
  • Four messengers (vv14–19). The account is built to feel like hammer blows. "While he was yet speaking, there came another" repeats three times, and every report ends with "I alone have escaped to tell you." The disasters alternate between human attacks and natural ones: Sabean raiders from the south (v15), "the fire of God" (v16), Chaldean raiders from the north (v17), and a great wind from the wilderness (v19). Enemies on every side and the sky itself turn against Job, and everything listed in vv2–3 is undone in reverse order, ending with the children.
  • "The Sabeans… the Chaldeans" (vv15, 17). These are raiding tribes of the desert. The Sabeans came from Arabia (6:19). The Chaldeans here are probably early nomadic tribes, long before they ruled Babylon. "Formed three groups" is the classic raiding tactic, the same one Gideon and Saul used (Judg 7:16; 1 Sam 11:11).
  • "The fire of God fell from heaven" (v16). To the servant it looked like God's own judgment, probably lightning or a firestorm. Satan's attack is designed to look like it came from God, and that is exactly how Job will experience it in the chapters ahead. Satan is allowed real power over nature here, but only within the limits God has set.
  • "A great wind came across the wilderness and struck the four corners of the house" (v19). A desert storm, perhaps a whirlwind, hitting all four corners at once. At the end of the book God speaks to Job "out of the whirlwind" (38:1). The storm that took his children is not the last storm in Job's story.
  • "Job arose and tore his robe and shaved his head and fell on the ground" (v20). These are the normal signs of deep mourning in the ancient world (Gen 37:34; Jer 41:5). Job doesn't pretend he isn't hurt. Faith in this chapter is not stoicism. Then comes the surprise: he "worshiped." The word means to bow down low, face to the ground. His first act after losing everything is to bow before God.
  • "Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked shall I return" (v21). "Return" there probably means to the earth, pictured as a mother (Ps 139:15; Eccl 5:15). Job treats everything he had, even his children, as a gift he never owned. Paul says much the same: "we brought nothing into the world, and we cannot take anything out of the world" (1 Tim 6:7).
  • "The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD" (v21). Job doesn't blame the Sabeans, the Chaldeans, the weather or Satan, whom he knows nothing about. He traces everything back to God, both the giving and the taking, and he blesses him. This is the answer to Satan's charge. Job does fear God "for nothing." Notice the irony of barak: Satan said Job would "bless" (curse) God. He blesses him.
  • "In all this Job did not sin or charge God with wrong" (v22). "Wrong" is tiphlah, something unseemly or unfitting. Job doesn't accuse God of acting improperly. Round one goes to Job, and Satan's theory is proved false. But chapter 2 brings a second test, and the long poetic chapters will show that Job's faith, while it holds, does not stay calm.
  • Commentators: Henry notes that "He did not passionately tear his hair, but deliberately shaved his head," grief expressed with control, and that "Afflictions must not divert us from, but quicken us to, the exercises of religion." On v22: "Discontent and impatience do in effect charge God with folly." Guzik sees Job's first reaction, worship, as revealing his heart. He also notes that Job traces the loss to God even though people and storms were the direct cause, and he points out that Job's mourning avoided the cutting of the body that pagan grief often involved (Lev 19:28). JFB suggests "fire of God" may be a Hebrew way of saying a very great fire, probably a burning desert wind, and reads the three Chaldean bands as aimed at three herds of camels. JFB reads "charged God foolishly" as Job allowing himself "no folly against God."

Pointing to Christ

Satan's question, "Does Job fear God for no reason?", is finally answered in Jesus. Job was blameless but not sinless. Jesus was truly without sin, and he was stripped of everything, his friends, his clothes and his life, while he was mocked with the same kind of charge: "He trusts in God; let God deliver him now" (Matt 27:43). He trusted his Father anyway, for nothing, and said, "Into your hands I commit my spirit" (Luke 23:46). Job never learned about the heavenly courtroom, but Christians know the Accuser has been "thrown down" by "the blood of the Lamb" (Rev 12:10–11), and that we now have an Advocate before the Father (1 John 2:1). Job's worship in the ashes is a small picture of the faith Jesus perfected, and the faith he gives to everyone who belongs to him.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik notes that God initiated the conversation about Job — He brought Job to Satan's attention, not the reverse. This reveals that God was confident in Job's faith and willing to put it on display. The "hedge" Satan mentions (v.10) acknowledges that God actively protects His people. Satan cannot penetrate God's protection without permission. Guzik emphasizes that Job's worship in v.20 is the answer to Satan's question: yes, Job does fear God "for nought" — his worship survives the removal of every blessing.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Reasons for Suffering May Stay Hidden From You (vv.6-12) — Job never learns about the heavenly conversation that set his trial in motion. Can you trust that God has reasons for your hardship even when you're never shown what they are?
  • Every Trial Has a Boundary God Sets (v.12) — Satan is permitted to test Job but only within limits God defines. When you're in the middle of a trial, does it help to remember that it isn't limitless, that God has already set its bounds?
  • Worship Can Be a Decision, Not Just a Feeling (v.20) — In his worst moment, Job tears his robe in grief and then falls down to worship. When circumstances crush you, are you able to choose worship even when you don't feel like it?
  • Loss Doesn't Have to Shake What You Believe About God (vv.21-22) — Job acknowledges that everything he had came from God and was God's to take, and he does not blame God for it. When you lose something precious, does your view of God's goodness survive the loss?
