1Paul, called to be an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God, and our brother Sosthenes,

2To the church of God in Corinth, to those sanctified in Christ Jesus and called to be holy, together with all those everywhere who call on the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, their Lord and ours:

3Grace and peace to you from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

4I always thank my God for you because of the grace He has given you in Christ Jesus.

5For in Him you have been enriched in every way, in all speech and all knowledge,

6because our testimony about Christ was confirmed in you.

7Therefore you do not lack any spiritual gift as you eagerly await the revelation of our Lord Jesus Christ.

8He will sustain you to the end, so that you will be blameless on the day of our Lord Jesus Christ.

9God, who has called you into fellowship with His Son Jesus Christ our Lord, is faithful.

10I appeal to you, brothers, in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that all of you agree together, so that there may be no divisions among you and that you may be united in mind and conviction.

11My brothers, some from Chloe’s household have informed me that there are quarrels among you.

12What I mean is this: Individuals among you are saying, “I follow Paul,” “I follow Apollos,” “I follow Cephas,” or “I follow Christ.”

13Is Christ divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Were you baptized into the name of Paul?

14I thank God that I did not baptize any of you except Crispus and Gaius,

15so no one can say that you were baptized into my name.

16Yes, I also baptized the household of Stephanas; beyond that I do not remember if I baptized anyone else.

17For Christ did not send me to baptize, but to preach the gospel, not with words of wisdom, lest the cross of Christ be emptied of its power.

18For the message of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God.

19For it is written: “I will destroy the wisdom of the wise; the intelligence of the intelligent I will frustrate.”

20Where is the wise man? Where is the scribe? Where is the philosopher of this age? Has not God made foolish the wisdom of the world?

21For since in the wisdom of God the world through its wisdom did not know Him, God was pleased through the foolishness of what was preached to save those who believe.

22Jews demand signs and Greeks search for wisdom,

23but we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles,

24but to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God.

25For the foolishness of God is wiser than man’s wisdom, and the weakness of God is stronger than man’s strength.

26Brothers, consider the time of your calling: Not many of you were wise by human standards; not many were powerful; not many were of noble birth.

27But God chose the foolish things of the world to shame the wise; God chose the weak things of the world to shame the strong.

28He chose the lowly and despised things of the world, and the things that are not, to nullify the things that are,

29so that no one may boast in His presence.

30It is because of Him that you are in Christ Jesus, who has become for us wisdom from God: our righteousness, holiness, and redemption.

31Therefore, as it is written: “Let him who boasts boast in the Lord.”

Summary

Paul greets the church at Corinth as those sanctified and called to be saints, thanking God for the grace and spiritual gifts they have received in Christ (vv. 1-9). He then appeals for unity, having heard of quarrels forming around Paul, Apollos, Cephas, and Christ himself (vv. 10-13). He recalls that his calling was to preach the gospel, not to impress with eloquent wisdom, since the message of the cross seems foolish to those who are perishing but is God's power to those being saved (vv. 14-18). God has overturned worldly wisdom, choosing what is foolish, weak, and lowly in the world's eyes to shame the wise and strong (vv. 19-29). Believers' standing comes from God alone, who made Christ their wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, and redemption, so that anyone who boasts should boast only in the Lord (vv. 30-31).

Authorship & Background

Author: The Apostle Paul, writing from Ephesus (16:8) during his third missionary journey, approximately AD 55.
Audience: The church at Corinth — a church Paul founded (Acts 18) but which had developed serious problems: divisions, immorality, lawsuits, abuse of spiritual gifts, and doctrinal confusion.
Corinth: A wealthy, cosmopolitan, morally corrupt city. "To live like a Corinthian" was a Greek idiom for sexual immorality. The church reflected its culture — gifted but immature, free but undisciplined.
Purpose: To address specific problems reported to Paul (by Chloe's household, v.11, and by letter, 7:1) and to call the church back to the centrality of the cross, unity in Christ, and holy living.
Chapter 1 Content: After greeting (vv.1-9), Paul addresses the first problem: divisions (vv.10-17). The church was splitting into factions around human leaders. Paul's response: the cross makes all human wisdom and boasting irrelevant (vv.18-31).
Classification: Epistle — Pauline
Key Themes: Called saints in a divided church, factions named after their favorite teacher, baptism as a side issue, the cross as folly to those perishing, and God choosing what the world counts as nothing
Greeting: Called to Be Saints (vv.1-3): Paul addresses a church he is about to spend sixteen chapters correcting as “the church of God which is at Corinth”, sanctified and called to be saints. The status is stated before any of the problems, and it is not presented as flattery or as something they have earned. He also links them with all who call on the name of Jesus in every place, which quietly answers a congregation inclined to think of itself as its own center.
Thanksgiving: Enriched in Everything (vv.4-9): The thanksgiving is genuine and specific: they come behind in no gift, they are enriched in all utterance and knowledge. Given what chapters 12 to 14 will say about how those gifts were being used, the commendation is striking — Paul does not dispute that they are gifted. Verse 9 grounds their standing in God's faithfulness rather than their own, which is the assumption the rest of the letter argues from.
The Divisions: I Am of Paul (vv.10-17): The first problem, reported by Chloe's household: factions naming themselves after Paul, Apollos, Cephas and Christ. Paul's questions in v.13 refuse to take the flattery — is Christ divided, was Paul crucified for you, were you baptized in Paul's name? He is glad he baptized almost none of them, which is a deliberate deflation of a status contest. Verse 17 states his commission as preaching rather than baptizing, and “not with wisdom of words” opens the argument that follows.
The Preaching of the Cross Is Foolishness (vv.18-21): The cross divides its hearers into two groups by their response to it: foolishness to them that perish, the power of God to those being saved. Paul quotes Isaiah on destroying the wisdom of the wise, then asks where the scribe and the disputer are now. Verse 21's claim is that God chose the method deliberately — since the world by wisdom did not know Him, it pleased God to save by what preaching looks like from outside, which is foolishness.
Christ Crucified: Stumbling Block and Foolishness (vv.22-25): The two audiences are named with what each wants: Jews require a sign, Greeks seek wisdom. A crucified messiah fails both tests at once, being an offense to the first and absurd to the second. To those called it is the power and wisdom of God. Verse 25's “the foolishness of God is wiser than men” is rhetorical rather than literal — there is no foolishness in God, and the comparison concedes the world's terms in order to overturn them.
God Chooses the Weak Things (vv.26-29): “Behold your calling” is an argument from the congregation's own membership: not many wise, not many mighty, not many noble. Paul is pointing at the room. God chose the foolish, the weak, the base and despised, and things which are not, to bring to nothing the things that are. The stated purpose in v.29 is that no flesh should glory in His presence, which returns to the factions the chapter began with.
Christ Made unto Us Wisdom (vv.30-31): The chapter closes by relocating everything the Corinthians had been competing over: Christ is made unto us wisdom, righteousness, sanctification and redemption. All four are received rather than achieved. The quotation that ends it — he that glorieth, let him glory in the Lord — leaves the boasting instinct intact while changing its object.

Deep Dive

Setting: Paul writes from Ephesus around AD 53–55, during the long stay of Acts 19 ("I will stay in Ephesus until Pentecost," 16:8). He had planted the church in Corinth a few years earlier, staying a year and a half (Acts 18:1–18). Corinth sat on the narrow isthmus joining mainland Greece to the Peloponnese, with a port on each side, and ships and cargo were hauled across the land bridge rather than risk the stormy sail around the southern cape. The old Greek city had been razed by Rome in 146 BC and refounded by Julius Caesar in 44 BC as a Roman colony, so it was newly rich, mixed, ambitious and status-hungry. It hosted the Isthmian Games and was crowded with temples. The church was a cross-section of that city: a few well-off members, many ordinary people and slaves, Jews and Gentiles. Paul had already written them one letter, now lost (5:9). Since then reports had reached him of quarrels, and the church had sent him questions (7:1). Chapter 1 opens the whole letter: a greeting, a thanksgiving, and then the first and deepest problem, a church splitting into fan clubs, which Paul answers by taking them back to the cross.

Greeting (vv1–3)

  • "Called by the will of God to be an apostle" (v1). Paul leads with his calling because some in Corinth were weighing him against other teachers and finding him wanting (4:3; 9:1–3). An apostle (apostolos) is one sent with a commission. Paul did not volunteer for the job or win it by vote. God put him there.
  • "Our brother Sosthenes" (v1). Possibly the synagogue ruler of Acts 18:17, beaten before the governor Gallio's court when the case against Paul collapsed. If so, the man who once led the synagogue that opposed Paul is now co-signing his letter. The name was common, so this is a reasonable guess, not a certainty.
  • "The church of God that is in Corinth" (v2). The church belongs to God, not to Paul or Apollos, a quiet first answer to the factions of v12. Ekklēsia was the ordinary word for a city's citizen assembly, and the Greek Old Testament used it for Israel gathered before the LORD. The Corinthian believers are God's assembly set down in a pagan city.
  • "Sanctified… called to be saints" (v2). Both words come from the same root, holy. The words "to be" are supplied by translators. Paul literally says "called saints." They are already holy by God's call and Christ's work, and the rest of the letter will urge them to live like it. A church with this many problems is still addressed as "saints."
  • "Together with all those who in every place call upon the name of our Lord" (v2). From the first sentence Paul reminds a proud church that it is one congregation among many (4:17; 7:17; 11:16; 14:33). "Call upon the name of the LORD" is Old Testament language for worshiping Israel's God (Joel 2:32), and here it is directed to Jesus.
  • "Grace to you and peace" (v3). Paul's usual greeting, joining the Greek greeting ("grace") with the Hebrew shalom, and tracing both to the Father and the Lord Jesus together.
  • Commentators: Guzik and JFB both note that "to be" is not in the Greek, so the Corinthians are saints by calling, not by achievement. JFB adds that Paul may name Sosthenes "in modesty," or because he was "a 'brother' of note in Corinth" whose name would carry weight. Guzik leans toward identifying him with the man of Acts 18:17 and suggests he may also have been Paul's scribe, while treating both as possibilities.

Thanksgiving (vv4–9)

  • "I give thanks to my God always for you" (v4). Before a single rebuke, Paul gives thanks. He is about to confront factions, incest, lawsuits, drunkenness at the Lord's Supper and denial of the resurrection, yet he can honestly thank God for this church, because what he thanks God for is "the grace of God that was given you." His confidence is in what God has done, not in their record.
  • "Enriched in him in all speech and all knowledge" (v5). These are the very gifts the Corinthians were proudest of. "Speech" (logos) and "knowledge" (gnōsis) return again and again in the letter: knowledge that "puffs up" (8:1), tongues without love (13:1). Paul thanks God for the gifts here, and later corrects how they were being used.
  • "Not lacking in any gift" (v7). "Gift" is charisma, a gift of grace, the word used for spiritual gifts in chapters 12–14. They had plenty of gifts. What they lacked was humility and love.
  • "As you wait for the revealing of our Lord Jesus Christ" (v7). The gifts are for the meantime. "Revealing" (apokalypsis) points to Christ's return. Some in Corinth acted as if they had already arrived (4:8). Paul keeps their eyes on a future that has not yet come.
  • "Who will sustain you to the end, guiltless" (v8). "Guiltless" (anenklētos) means not open to any charge, unaccusable. It is a courtroom word. "The day of our Lord" takes up the Old Testament "day of the LORD," now named for Jesus. Believers will still give account for their work (3:12–15; 2 Cor 5:10), but no charge will stand that could separate them from God (Rom 8:33–34).
  • "God is faithful" (v9). The ground of all this confidence. The same words come back in the promise about temptation (10:13).
  • "Called into the fellowship of his Son" (v9). "Fellowship" is koinōnia, a shared life, partnership, participation. It is the word Paul uses for sharing in Christ's body and blood at the Lord's Supper (10:16). That is the bridge to what follows: people who share in one Son cannot be split into rival camps. Notice how often Jesus is named in these nine verses. Paul mentions him in nearly every one.
  • Commentators: Guzik stresses that Paul thanks God for grace given, not for the Corinthians' achievements, and that God's faithfulness is the only reason to expect they will stand blameless. Henry, in his concise commentary: "Those that wait for the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, will be kept by him to the end." On v7 JFB is careful: "not that all had all gifts, but different persons among them had different gifts," a point Paul develops in chapter 12.

Divisions in the Church (vv10–17)

  • "I appeal to you, brothers, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" (v10). He appeals not by his own name, which some were rallying behind, but by Christ's. "Brothers" (adelphoi) includes the sisters, as the next verse shows.
  • "No divisions among you" (v10). "Divisions" is schismata, from a verb meaning to split or tear. The same word is used of the tear in an old garment patched with new cloth (Mark 2:21). The church is not yet split into separate congregations. It is torn while still meeting together (11:18).
  • "United in the same mind" (v10). "United" (katartizō) means to put in order, repair or restore. It is the word for James and John "mending their nets" (Mark 1:19), and in medical writers it was used of setting a broken bone. The goal is not grudging coexistence but a torn thing made whole.
  • "Chloe's people" (v11). Chloe is mentioned only here. "Her people" were probably members of her household, perhaps slaves or employees, who traveled between Corinth and Ephesus, possibly on business. Paul names his source openly rather than passing on anonymous complaints. "Quarreling" is eris, strife or wrangling, which Paul lists among the works of the flesh (Gal 5:20).
  • The four slogans (v12). "I follow Paul": loyalty to the founder. "I follow Apollos": the eloquent Alexandrian teacher who had worked in Corinth after Paul left (Acts 18:24–19:1). "I follow Cephas": Peter, using his Aramaic name, perhaps favored by Jewish believers or by people impressed that he had been with Jesus from the start. "I follow Christ": either the right answer said in the wrong spirit, a superior group above all human teachers, or Paul's own sarcastic way of finishing the list. Interpreters are divided. There is no sign that Paul, Apollos or Peter encouraged any of this. Paul speaks warmly of Apollos (3:6; 16:12).
  • Who really led the parties? Some scholars think the groups formed around local leaders, and that Paul uses his own name and Apollos's as stand-ins to avoid naming people. He says as much in 4:6, "I have applied all these things to myself and Apollos for your benefit." Either way, the problem is the same: Christians ranking teachers the way Corinth ranked orators and athletes, and gaining status from the one they backed.
  • "Is Christ divided?" (v13). Three questions, each expecting "No!" Christ cannot be cut up and handed out in portions. "Was Paul crucified for you?" puts the cross at the center of the argument for the first time. "Were you baptized in the name of Paul?" Baptism in Jesus' name marks a person as belonging to Jesus, not to whoever did the baptizing.
  • Crispus and Gaius (v14). Crispus had been ruler of the Corinthian synagogue and believed with his whole household (Acts 18:8). Gaius later hosted Paul and the whole church (Rom 16:23). Some scholars identify him with Titius Justus, whose house stood next door to the synagogue (Acts 18:7), which would give him the full Roman name Gaius Titius Justus. That is a plausible reconstruction, not a stated fact.
  • "I do not know whether I baptized anyone else" (v16). An honest correction mid-sentence, as if dictating: Paul remembers Stephanas's household, the first converts in Achaia (16:15), who may have been the very people carrying this letter (16:17). It shows how little Paul cared about keeping a tally.
  • "Christ did not send me to baptize but to preach the gospel" (v17). Paul is not downplaying baptism. He commands it and ties it closely to faith elsewhere (Rom 6:3–4; Gal 3:27). He is saying that his particular commission was to preach, and that others could do the baptizing. His point is that no one has a claim to rank because of who baptized them.
  • "Not with words of eloquent wisdom, lest the cross of Christ be emptied of its power" (v17). Literally "not in wisdom of word." Corinth loved polished speakers. Traveling orators drew crowds and fees, and audiences judged them on style. Paul refused to win people that way. "Emptied" (kenoō) means made empty or of no effect. If people were won by the speaker's cleverness, their faith would rest on the speaker, and the cross would be robbed of its power. This verse is the hinge into the rest of the chapter.
  • Commentators: JFB calls "perfectly joined together" "the opposite word to 'divisions,'" a word for "healing a wound, or making whole a rent." Guzik, following Barclay, calls it a medical word for setting a broken bone. The lexicon's main senses are "mend, repair, restore," with "set" among its classical senses, so the picture is fair, though mending nets is just as close. Henry: "Agreement in the greater things should extinguish divisions about the lesser." JFB says of v17 that "Paul says this not to depreciate baptism; for he exalts it most highly." Guzik argues from v17 that baptism is not the thing that saves. Traditions that hold a higher view of baptism read the verse more narrowly, as being about Paul's calling and the Corinthians' boasting, not about baptism's meaning.

Christ the Wisdom and Power of God (vv18–31)

  • "The word of the cross" (v18). The gospel in one phrase. In the first century the cross was not a piece of jewelry. It was the most degrading execution Rome had, reserved for slaves, rebels and the worst criminals, and polite people avoided even naming it. An ancient graffito from Rome shows a man worshiping a crucified figure with a donkey's head, mocking a Christian: "Alexamenos worships his god." That is what "folly" meant.
  • "Those who are perishing… us who are being saved" (v18). Both are present tense, ongoing. Humanity is not divided by intelligence but by direction. To one group the cross is "folly" (mōria, from which English gets "moron"). To the other it is "the power of God," the same thing Paul says of the gospel in Romans 1:16.
  • "I will destroy the wisdom of the wise" (v19). From Isaiah 29:14, in the Greek version. In Isaiah, Judah's leaders were trusting their own clever foreign policy, an alliance with Egypt, instead of trusting the LORD, and God promised to bring their wisdom to nothing. Paul sees the same pattern on a larger scale: God's saving act makes nonsense of human cleverness.
  • "Where is the one who is wise? Where is the scribe? Where is the debater of this age?" (v20). The wise man is the Greek philosopher. The scribe is the Jewish expert in the law. The debater (syzētētēs, a disputant) is the professional arguer. The questions echo Isaiah 19:12 and 33:18, where God asks where the experts have gone. None of them saw the cross coming, and none of them can explain it away.
  • "In the wisdom of God, the world did not know God through wisdom" (v21). God arranged it so that human wisdom could not reach him on its own terms. "It pleased God" means it was his good choice. "The folly of what we preach" is not the folly of the act of preaching but of its content. The word is kērygma, a herald's proclamation, the message itself. God saves through a message the world finds ridiculous, and he saves "those who believe," not those who figure it out.
  • "Jews demand signs and Greeks seek wisdom" (v22). Each side set its own terms. Many Jews wanted proof by power, a sign from heaven like the Exodus (Matt 12:38; John 6:30). Many Greeks wanted a system that satisfied the mind. God refused both sets of terms.
  • "Christ crucified" (v23). In Greek the participle means "Christ as the crucified one," not only a past event but a lasting identity. The risen Christ still bears the wounds (John 20:27). To Jews this was a "stumbling block" (skandalon, originally the bait-stick of a trap, then a snare or obstacle). A crucified Messiah sounded like a contradiction, since "a hanged man is cursed by God" (Deut 21:23; Gal 3:13). Paul knew that offense from the inside. He once persecuted the church over it. To Gentiles it was simply absurd: a god who dies as a criminal.
  • "To those who are called… Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God" (v24). God's call overcomes both objections. The Jew who wanted power finds it in the cross, and the Greek who wanted wisdom finds it there too. "Called" links back to v2 and v9.
  • "The foolishness of God is wiser than men" (v25). Paul is not admitting God is foolish. He is saying that the thing that looks most foolish and weak about God, a crucified Messiah, outstrips the best of human wisdom and strength. It dealt with sin and death, which no philosopher could touch.
  • "Consider your calling, brothers" (v26). "Look around the room." Not many were "wise" (educated), "powerful" (holding office or influence) or "of noble birth" (from the old families). Not many, but some: Crispus the synagogue ruler, Gaius who could host the whole church, and Erastus the city treasurer (Rom 16:23). A paving inscription found near Corinth's theater names an Erastus who laid the pavement at his own expense as aedile, a city official. Many scholars think he is the same man, though that is debated. Most of the church, though, were ordinary tradespeople, freed slaves and slaves.
  • "God chose what is foolish… weak… low and despised… even things that are not" (vv27–28). "Chose" is repeated three times. "Things that are not" means people the world counts as nothing. God picked people Corinth would not have looked at twice, and through them he shames and "brings to nothing" what the world prizes. This is not praise of ignorance. It is the cross pattern repeated in the makeup of the church.
  • "So that no human being might boast in the presence of God" (v29). The point of it all. "Boast" (kauchaomai) can be glad praise or self-promotion, depending on what is being praised. Paul uses the word family more in his letters to Corinth than anywhere else, because boasting was the city's way of life and the church's disease.
  • "Because of him you are in Christ Jesus" (v30). Their standing is God's doing, not their own discovery. Christ "became to us wisdom from God," and that wisdom is then spelled out as three gifts. Righteousness: right standing with God, credited to believers (Rom 3:21–26; 2 Cor 5:21). Sanctification: being made holy, set apart for God (1:2; 6:11). Redemption: release by payment, the language of buying a slave's freedom (6:20; 7:23). The wisdom the Corinthians were chasing turns out to be a person, and he gives them everything they need.
  • "Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord" (v31). A shortened form of Jeremiah 9:23–24: "let not the wise man boast in his wisdom… but let him who boasts boast in this, that he understands and knows me." Jeremiah names the wise, the mighty and the rich, the same three groups as v26. In Jeremiah "the LORD" is Yahweh. Paul applies it to the Lord Jesus. Boasting is not banned, only redirected.
  • Commentators: Henry: "All the boasted science of the heathen world did not, could not, effectually bring home the world to God." On vv26–29 he adds that God "is a better judge than we what instruments and measures will best serve the purposes of his glory," pointing to the fishermen Jesus chose as his first preachers. On v30 he holds the gifts together: "Where Christ is made righteousness to any soul, he is also made sanctification." JFB makes the same point with a neat contrast: justifying righteousness is "perfect, but not inherent," while sanctification is "inherent, but not perfect." JFB takes the scribe as the Jewish expert and the debater as the Greek one, and on v29: "We are to glory not before Him, but in Him." Guzik argues that both Jewish and Greek demands were ways of making God answer to human terms, and warns against reshaping the message of the cross to fit what a culture finds acceptable. He also tells the story of a countess who said she was saved by the letter "m": the verse says "not many noble," not "not any."

Pointing to Christ

The chapter names Jesus in nearly every verse, and it moves from his name (v2) to his cross (vv17–25) to his person as everything God gives (v30). The Corinthians were looking for wisdom, power and status, and dividing over which teacher supplied them best. Paul's answer is that God has already given all three in a crucified Messiah, in a way designed to leave no one anything to boast about except him. The weakness of the cross is not a stage God passed through on the way to power. It is the shape his power takes.

Reflection

  • Unity Over Human Loyalty (vv.10-13) — The Corinthians were splitting into camps around Paul, Apollos, and Cephas instead of standing united in Christ. Do you find your identity more in a leader, group, or label than in Christ Himself?
  • The Cross as Power, Not Just Doctrine (v.18) — Paul says the message of the cross looks foolish to those perishing but is the power of God to those being saved. Is the cross something you merely believe, or something you experience as power in your daily life?
  • God's Wisdom Confounds the World's (vv.20-21) — God chose to save people through preaching the world calls foolish rather than through impressive human wisdom. Are you ever tempted to soften or dress up the gospel to make it seem more respectable?
  • God's Preference for the Overlooked (vv.26-29) — Paul reminds the Corinthians that not many of them were wise, powerful, or well-born by the world's standards, yet God chose them so no one could boast before Him. Does it encourage or challenge you that God delights in using ordinary, unimpressive people?
  • Christ Is Everything We Need (vv.30-31) — Paul says Christ has become our wisdom, righteousness, holiness, and redemption, so the only rightful boast is in the Lord. What are you tempted to boast in besides Christ — your achievements, knowledge, or reputation?
