1By the seventh month, the Israelites had settled in their towns, and the people assembled as one man in Jerusalem.

2Then Jeshua son of Jozadak and his fellow priests, along with Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel and his associates, began to build the altar of the God of Israel to sacrifice burnt offerings on it, as it is written in the Law of Moses the man of God.

3They set up the altar on its foundation and sacrificed burnt offerings on it to the LORD—both the morning and evening burnt offerings—even though they feared the people of the land.

4They also celebrated the Feast of Tabernacles in accordance with what is written, and they offered burnt offerings daily based on the number prescribed for each day.

5After that, they presented the regular burnt offerings and those for New Moons and for all the appointed sacred feasts of the LORD, as well as all the freewill offerings brought to the LORD.

6On the first day of the seventh month, the Israelites began to offer burnt offerings to the LORD, although the foundation of the temple of the LORD had not been laid.

7They gave money to the masons and carpenters, and food and drink and oil to the people of Sidon and Tyre to bring cedar logs from Lebanon to Joppa by sea, as authorized by Cyrus king of Persia.

8In the second month of the second year after they had arrived at the house of God in Jerusalem, Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel, Jeshua son of Jozadak, and the rest of their associates including the priests, the Levites, and all who had returned to Jerusalem from the captivity, began the work. They appointed Levites twenty years of age or older to supervise the construction of the house of the LORD.

9So Jeshua and his sons and brothers, Kadmiel and his sons (descendants of Yehudah), and the sons of Henadad and their sons and brothers—all Levites—joined together to supervise those working on the house of God.

10When the builders had laid the foundation of the temple of the LORD, the priests in their apparel with trumpets, and the Levites (the sons of Asaph) with cymbals, took their positions to praise the LORD, as David king of Israel had prescribed.

11And they sang responsively with praise and thanksgiving to the LORD: “For He is good; for His loving devotion to Israel endures forever.” Then all the people gave a great shout of praise to the LORD, because the foundation of the house of the LORD had been laid.

Summary

When the seventh month arrives, the returned exiles gather in Jerusalem, and Jeshua and Zerubbabel rebuild the altar to resume burnt offerings despite their fear of the surrounding peoples (vv. 1-3). They keep the Feast of Tabernacles and reinstate the regular offerings, though the Temple's foundation is not yet laid (vv. 4-6). Builders are hired and materials brought from Sidon and Tyre, and in the second year the Levites are appointed to oversee the work (vv. 7-9). When the foundation is finally laid, priests and Levites praise the LORD with trumpets and cymbals as the people shout for joy, while older men who remember Solomon's Temple weep at the smaller sight, mingling grief and celebration (vv. 10-13).

Authorship & Background

Author: Ezra the priest and scribe (likely author/compiler). Written approximately 450-400 BC. Ezra covers two returns from Babylonian exile: (1) Zerubbabel's return and Temple rebuilding (538-516 BC, chapters 1-6); (2) Ezra's return and spiritual reform (458 BC, chapters 7-10).
Classification: Historical Narrative — Post-Exilic
Key Themes: An altar rebuilt before any walls or houses, the festival calendar restored before the building exists, cedar fetched from Lebanon as Solomon had, and weeping and shouting that could not be told apart
Historical Context: Chapter 3 records two foundational acts of worship restoration: the rebuilding of the altar (vv.1-6) and the laying of the Temple foundation (vv.7-13). Both events carry deep theological significance. The altar was rebuilt first — before walls, before the Temple, before homes were fully established — because worship of God takes priority over all other activity. The seventh month (Tishri) was chosen deliberately: it was the month of the Feast of Trumpets, the Day of Atonement, and the Feast of Tabernacles. The returnees reestablished the entire liturgical calendar before the building was complete. The foundation-laying in the second year (536 BC) produced a remarkable scene: younger people shouted with joy at the new beginning, while elderly priests and leaders who remembered Solomon's glorious Temple wept at the comparison. The two sounds merged into one indistinguishable roar heard from far away. This mixture of joy and grief reflects the theological tension of restoration — genuine gratitude for what God is doing, mixed with honest sorrow over what was lost. The cedar procurement from Lebanon echoes Solomon's original Temple construction (1 Kings 5), intentionally connecting this new work to the former glory.
The Altar Before the Temple (vv.1-3): In the seventh month the people gather as one man to Jerusalem and build the altar first — before walls, before the temple, before their own houses are finished. The order is the point. Verse 3 adds that they set it on its bases “for fear was upon them because of the people of those countries”, so the first act of worship is performed by people who are afraid, not by people who feel safe.
Tabernacles Kept, the Calendar Restored (vv.4-6): The seventh month was chosen deliberately: it held the Feast of Trumpets, the Day of Atonement and Tabernacles. They keep the feast, offer the daily burnt offerings, and restore the whole liturgical year. Verse 6 notes plainly that the foundation of the house was not yet laid. The calendar is running before the building exists, which reverses the order anyone would expect.
Cedar from Lebanon, the Foundation Laid (vv.7-11): Masons and carpenters are hired and cedar is fetched from Lebanon by sea to Joppa, the same arrangement Solomon made in 1 Kings 5 — a deliberate link to the first temple. In the second year the foundation is laid, and the ceremony follows the old pattern with priests in their apparel, trumpets, and cymbals. The refrain sung is the one from the Psalms: for he is good, for his mercy endureth for ever.
Weeping and Shouting Indistinguishable (vv.12-13): The scene the chapter is remembered for. The young shout for joy at a beginning, while the old priests and heads of families who had seen Solomon's house weep with a loud voice at the comparison. The two sounds mix so that the people could not distinguish the shout of joy from the noise of the weeping, and it was heard far off. Restoration is recorded as genuinely both things at once, without the text choosing between them.

Deep Dive

Setting: The first wave of exiles has come home from Babylon under Cyrus's decree of 538 BC (ch 1), and Ezra 2 listed them by family and town. They have spent the summer settling into ruined villages around Judah. Jerusalem itself lay largely in rubble. Nebuchadnezzar's army had burned the temple in 586 BC, about fifty years earlier, and foreign peoples had moved into the land in the meantime. The returnees are few, poor and nervous, but they hold a royal permit and the temple vessels. Ezra 3 tells how they did first things first. Before they laid a single stone of the temple, they rebuilt the altar and restored the daily worship of God. Only after that did they begin the temple foundation, and the day ended with a sound no one could quite sort out, joy and weeping mixed together. The events happen around 537–536 BC, nearly eighty years before Ezra himself arrives (ch 7).

Rebuilding the Altar (vv1–7)

  • "When the seventh month came" (v1). The seventh month (Tishri, September–October) was the holiest month of Israel's year. It opened with the Feast of Trumpets on the first day (Lev 23:24), held the Day of Atonement on the tenth (Lev 16) and the Feast of Booths from the fifteenth (Lev 23:34). The returnees time their first gathering by God's calendar, not by their own building needs. The year is not stated, but most scholars take it to be the year they arrived, 537 BC.
  • "The people gathered as one man to Jerusalem" (v1). The same phrase describes Israel at its moments of shared purpose (Judg 20:1; Neh 8:1). A people who had just been counted family by family in chapter 2 now act as one body. No one forced them. They left half-repaired homes and fields to come and worship.
  • Jeshua and Zerubbabel (v2). The two leaders stand for the two offices Israel would need: priest and king. Jeshua son of Jozadak was the high priest (Hag 1:1; Zech 3:1). His father had been carried into exile (1 Chr 6:15). Ezra never gives Jeshua the title "high priest," and the Tyndale notes suggest the formal title may have waited until the temple was finished. Zerubbabel, whose name probably means "seed of Babylon," was the grandson of King Jehoiachin and so of David's royal line (1 Chr 3:17–19). He served as governor under Persia (Hag 1:1). Matthew lists him in the genealogy of Jesus (Matt 1:12). Priest comes first in this verse, which fits the chapter: worship before building.
  • "They built the altar of the God of Israel… as it is written in the Law of Moses" (v2). The returnees did not invent a new kind of worship for a new era. They went back to the written word. "As it is written" recurs (v4), and "according to the directions of David" follows (v10). The whole chapter is about rebuilding according to the pattern.
  • "They set the altar in its place" (v3). Literally "on its foundations" or "on its bases." They found where Solomon's altar had stood and rebuilt on that exact spot. That site went back to David's altar on the threshing floor of Araunah (2 Sam 24:18–25; 2 Chr 3:1), and tradition links it with Mount Moriah, where Abraham offered Isaac (Gen 22:2). Building on the old base said this was the same worship of the same God, not a rival shrine.
  • "For fear was on them because of the peoples of the lands" (v3). The Hebrew can be read two ways, and translations split. Either they built the altar despite their fear, or because of it, turning to God for protection when they felt most exposed. The ESV's "for" leans toward the second reading. Either way, fear did not stop worship. "The peoples of the lands" were the mixed populations around them: descendants of foreigners settled by the Assyrians (2 Kgs 17:24; Ezra 4:2), Edomites who had pushed into southern Judah, and the Judeans who had never been exiled and whose religion had grown mixed. Chapter 4 shows the fear was justified.
  • "Burnt offerings morning and evening" (v3). The daily offering of a lamb at dawn and dusk (Exod 29:38–42; Num 28:3–8) had stopped when the temple fell. Its return meant every day again began and ended under God's covenant. The burnt offering was wholly given to God on the fire, and it dealt with sin and expressed complete devotion.
  • "They kept the Feast of Booths, as it is written" (v4). For seven days Israel lived in shelters made of branches to remember the wilderness years, when God brought them out of Egypt and cared for them on the road (Lev 23:39–43). For people who had just made a four-month journey from Babylon and were living in makeshift housing, the feast cannot have felt like a mere ritual. A second exodus had just happened. "By number according to the rule, as each day required" points to the long schedule in Numbers 29:12–38, where the bulls offered drop from thirteen on the first day to seven on the seventh.
  • Every kind of offering restored (v5). After the feast they kept up the "regular" (daily) offerings, the new moon offerings on the first of each month (Num 28:11–15), the offerings at "all the appointed feasts" and the voluntary "freewill offering" (Hebrew nedabah), which no law required.
  • "From the first day of the seventh month" (v6). That was the day of the Feast of Trumpets, called in Leviticus 23:24 a day of teruah, a blast of horns or a shout. The same word returns at the end of the chapter for the people's "great shout" (vv11, 13). The worship that began with the trumpet day ends with a shout of a different kind.
  • "But the foundation of the temple of the LORD was not yet laid" (v6). The writer is honest. Worship came back before the building did. Sacrifice on an open-air altar among ruins was not ideal, but it was obedience, and it was enough to begin with.
  • Cedar from Lebanon by sea to Joppa (v7). This deliberately echoes Solomon (1 Kgs 5:6–11; 2 Chr 2:8–16). Then as now, the Phoenician cities of Sidon and Tyre (on the coast of modern Lebanon) supplied cedar logs, floated them down the coast in rafts to Joppa (modern Jaffa, near Tel Aviv) and were paid in food and olive oil, since their own narrow coastal strip grew too little food. From Joppa the timber was hauled about 35 miles uphill to Jerusalem. "According to the grant that they had from Cyrus" ties the work to the royal permit of chapter 1 (and its full text in 6:3–5, which promised royal funds).
  • Commentators: Henry draws from v1 that "worldly business" should be "postponed to the business of religion and it will prosper the better," and he reads the fear of v3 as a reason to pray, not to stop worshiping. Guzik gives several reasons the altar came first: it could be done quickly, it was where sin was dealt with and ordinary people met God, and it was plain obedience. He compares the altar's place in their life to the place of the cross for Christians. JFB stresses that the altar was urgent because it gave atonement for sin and sought God's blessing on the work still to come. Henry notes that "Tyre and Sidon must now, as of old," supply the workmen, and Guzik also sees the Gentile help as a repeat of Solomon's day.

Rebuilding the Temple (vv8–13)

  • "In the second year… in the second month" (v8). That is April–May, probably of 536 BC. The second month was the month Solomon began his temple (1 Kgs 6:1), another deliberate echo. The timing also made practical sense: the winter rains were over, the dry season had begun and the timber had had time to arrive.
  • "Made a beginning" (v8). The verb is modest and honest. They began. Chapter 4 explains why they did not finish for another sixteen years, until 516 BC (4:24; 6:15). The prophets Haggai and Zechariah later describe the foundation as being laid in 520 BC (Hag 2:18; Zech 4:9), which has led some scholars to doubt that much was done in 536. A common answer is that work was started in 536, abandoned under pressure and then begun again in 520, so that both could be called a foundation-laying (compare 5:16, where the elders say the work has gone on since Sheshbazzar's day "and it is not yet finished").
  • "The Levites, from twenty years old and upward" (v8). In Moses' day Levites served from age thirty (Num 4:3) or twenty-five (Num 8:24). David lowered the age to twenty when the work changed from carrying the tabernacle to serving at a fixed temple (1 Chr 23:24–27). The returnees followed David's rule. There was also a plain practical reason: only 74 Levites had returned (2:40), so every able one was needed.
  • "To supervise the work" (v8). The verb (Hebrew natsach) means to oversee or direct, and it is related to the word in many psalm titles usually translated "to the choirmaster." The Levites were not the bricklayers. They directed the work so that it followed God's pattern for his house, as Levites had done in the temple repairs of earlier kings (2 Chr 34:12–13).
  • "Jeshua with his sons and his brothers, and Kadmiel" (v9). This is a different Jeshua, the head of a Levite family named in the list of returnees (2:40), not the high priest of v2. The names Jeshua and Kadmiel and the phrase "sons of Judah" match the "sons of Jeshua and Kadmiel, of the sons of Hodaviah" in 2:40. Hodaviah and Judah look alike in Hebrew script, so many scholars think "Judah" here is the same family name. "The sons of Henadad" appear later as wall-builders in Nehemiah's day (Neh 3:18, 24; 10:9).
  • "The priests in their vestments came forward with trumpets" (v10). These were the silver trumpets God commanded Moses to make (Num 10:2, 10), blown by priests at feasts and sacrifices. The "sons of Asaph" were the guild of temple singers going back to David's chief musician (1 Chr 16:4–7; 25:1). 128 of them had come home (2:41). Cymbals were Asaph's instrument (1 Chr 16:5). Everything about the ceremony followed "the directions of David king of Israel," just as the altar followed the Law of Moses (v2).
  • "For he is good, for his steadfast love endures forever toward Israel" (v11). This was Israel's best-known refrain of worship (Ps 106:1; 107:1; 118:1; 136, where it ends all 26 verses). It was sung when David brought the ark to Jerusalem (1 Chr 16:34) and when the glory cloud filled Solomon's temple (2 Chr 5:13; 7:3). "Steadfast love" is hesed, God's loyal covenant love, the love that keeps its promises. Jeremiah had foretold this very song: in the ruined land the voice of those who sing "Give thanks to the LORD of hosts, for the LORD is good, for his steadfast love endures forever" would be heard again (Jer 33:10–11). Here it is, sung over a foundation. The words "toward Israel" are the singers' own addition, and they matter. After the exile, the people who had broken the covenant were confessing that God had not broken his.
  • "They sang responsively" (v11). Literally they "answered," probably with one choir answering another, or a leader singing a line and the people the refrain, the way Psalm 136 is built.
  • "All the people shouted with a great shout" (v11). This is teruah, the word for the trumpet blast of v6 and for the shout of joy that greeted the ark (1 Sam 4:5; 2 Sam 6:15).
  • "Old men who had seen the first house, wept with a loud voice" (v12). Solomon's temple had fallen about fifty years before. A man who saw it as a boy of ten or fifteen would now be in his sixties or seventies. They wept because this foundation was so much smaller and plainer than what they remembered, and probably also because of what would never return. The ark of the covenant had been lost, and the second temple never had it. Haggai later voices the same grief: "Who is left among you who saw this house in its former glory?… Is it not as nothing in your eyes?" (Hag 2:3). God's answer through Haggai was that "the latter glory of this house shall be greater than the former" (Hag 2:9). Their weeping may also have held sorrow for the sins that had brought the city down.
  • "Many shouted aloud for joy" (v12). The younger generation, born in Babylon, had never seen Solomon's temple. To them this was not a reduced version of something lost but the first sign of God's house they had ever seen. Neither group was simply right or wrong.
  • "The people could not distinguish the sound of the joyful shout from the sound of the people's weeping" (v13). Middle Eastern grief and joy are both loud, and from a distance the two sounds blurred into one. The writer does not settle which reaction was correct. He records both, and the closing words, "the sound was heard far away," quietly set up chapter 4, where the neighbors come to see what is happening.
  • Commentators: Henry's lesson from v8 is "Well begun (we say) is half ended," and he notes that the Levites who did not build could still serve "by quickening and encouraging" those who did. He grants the old men some cause for grief but faults them for damping the people's joy, and he hears in the mixed sound a picture of life on earth, where joy and sorrow mingle, unlike heaven's pure song. JFB lists what the second temple lacked: the ark, the shekinah glory and the Urim and Thummim. He finds its greater glory in the coming of "the incarnate Saviour." Guzik, drawing on G. Campbell Morgan, warns against a backward look that despises present work, and like Henry points to the prophets' charge not to despise "the day of small things" (Zech 4:10). Guzik and JFB both note that David had lowered the Levites' age to twenty. JFB rightly identifies the Jeshua of v9 as a Levite, not the high priest.

Pointing to Christ

The returnees rebuilt the altar before the temple because they knew sin had to be dealt with before anything else could stand. The daily lamb, morning and evening, pointed forward to "the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world" (John 1:29), whose one offering ended the need for any other (Heb 10:11–14). Hebrews says plainly, "We have an altar" (Heb 13:10), and it is not in Jerusalem. The song over the foundation praised a God whose steadfast love endures toward a people who had failed him, and that love reached its fullest form in Christ. The old men were right that the second house was smaller than the first. But Haggai's promise that its latter glory would be greater was kept when Jesus himself walked into that rebuilt temple and called it "my Father's house" (John 2:16). He is the foundation laid by God (1 Cor 3:11; 1 Pet 2:6), and in him small and tearful beginnings are not despised.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes that the altar was built before the Temple — showing that access to God through sacrifice is more fundamental than buildings. He notes the "fear" of v.3 actually motivated worship rather than paralysis, and draws attention to the bittersweet mixture of weeping and joy as a model for honest emotional response in worship.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Following the Pattern, Not Inventing One (v.2) — The returnees rebuilt the altar exactly as written in the Law of Moses rather than improvising their own approach to worship. Are you shaping your spiritual life by Scripture, or by what feels convenient or familiar?
  • Worship as the Response to Fear (v.3) — Even while afraid of the hostile peoples around them, the exiles' first move was to build an altar and restore sacrifice, not to fortify themselves. When you're afraid, is your instinct to run toward God or to rely on your own defenses?
  • Starting With What You Have (vv.6-7) — The returnees began the work of rebuilding and gathering materials long before the Temple foundation matched Solomon's former glory. Are you waiting for resources equal to some past standard before you'll start, instead of being faithful with what's in your hands now?
  • Room for Both Grief and Joy (vv.12-13) — At the foundation-laying, the elders who remembered Solomon's Temple wept while the younger generation shouted for joy, the two sounds blending into one. Can you make space to hold genuine grief over what's been lost alongside real gratitude for what God is doing now?
