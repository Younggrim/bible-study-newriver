1Now the first covenant had regulations for worship and also an earthly sanctuary.

2A tabernacle was prepared. In its first room were the lampstand, the table, and the consecrated bread. This was called the Holy Place.

3Behind the second curtain was a room called the Most Holy Place,

4containing the golden altar of incense and the gold-covered ark of the covenant. Inside the ark were the gold jar of manna, Aaron’s staff that had budded, and the stone tablets of the covenant.

5Above the ark were the cherubim of glory, overshadowing the mercy seat. But we cannot discuss these things in detail now.

6When everything had been prepared in this way, the priests entered regularly into the first room to perform their sacred duties.

7But only the high priest entered the second room, and then only once a year, and never without blood, which he offered for himself and for the sins the people had committed in ignorance.

8By this arrangement the Holy Spirit was showing that the way into the Most Holy Place had not yet been disclosed as long as the first tabernacle was still standing.

9It is an illustration for the present time, because the gifts and sacrifices being offered were unable to cleanse the conscience of the worshiper.

10They consist only in food and drink and special washings—external regulations imposed until the time of reform.

11But when Christ came as high priest of the good things that have come, He went through the greater and more perfect tabernacle that is not made by hands and is not a part of this creation.

12He did not enter by the blood of goats and calves, but He entered the Most Holy Place once for all by His own blood, thus securing eternal redemption.

13For if the blood of goats and bulls and the ashes of a heifer sprinkled on those who are ceremonially unclean sanctify them so that their bodies are clean,

14how much more will the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered Himself unblemished to God, purify our consciences from works of death, so that we may serve the living God!

15Therefore Christ is the mediator of a new covenant, so that those who are called may receive the promised eternal inheritance, now that He has died to redeem them from the transgressions committed under the first covenant.

16In the case of a will, it is necessary to establish the death of the one who made it,

17because a will does not take effect until the one who made it has died; it cannot be executed while he is still alive.

18That is why even the first covenant was not put into effect without blood.

19For when Moses had proclaimed every commandment of the law to all the people, he took the blood of calves and goats, along with water, scarlet wool, and hyssop, and sprinkled the scroll and all the people,

20saying, “This is the blood of the covenant, which God has commanded you to keep.”

21In the same way, he sprinkled with blood the tabernacle and all the vessels used in worship.

22According to the law, in fact, nearly everything must be purified with blood, and without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness.

23So it was necessary for the copies of the heavenly things to be purified with these sacrifices, but the heavenly things themselves with better sacrifices than these.

24For Christ did not enter a man-made copy of the true sanctuary, but He entered heaven itself, now to appear on our behalf in the presence of God.

25Nor did He enter heaven to offer Himself again and again, as the high priest enters the Most Holy Place every year with blood that is not his own.

26Otherwise, Christ would have had to suffer repeatedly since the foundation of the world. But now He has appeared once for all at the end of the ages to do away with sin by the sacrifice of Himself.

27Just as man is appointed to die once, and after that to face judgment,

28so also Christ was offered once to bear the sins of many; and He will appear a second time, not to bear sin, but to bring salvation to those who eagerly await Him.

Summary

The author describes the earthly tabernacle's two rooms — the Holy Place and the Holy of Holies behind the veil — which only the high priest could enter, and only once a year with blood for his own sins and the people's (vv. 1-7). This showed that the way into God's true presence was not yet open, since the old system's sacrifices could never perfect the conscience but were only external regulations until a better order came (vv. 8-10). Christ, by contrast, entered the greater heavenly tabernacle once for all through his own blood rather than the blood of goats and calves, securing eternal redemption and cleansing the conscience from dead works to serve the living God (vv. 11-14). He is the mediator of a new covenant, and just as a will only takes effect after death, his death ratifies the covenant and pays for sins committed under the first one (vv. 15-22). He entered heaven itself to appear before God on our behalf, and having appeared once to remove sin through his own sacrifice, he will appear a second time — not to deal with sin, but to bring salvation to those waiting for him (vv. 23-28).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown (see Chapter 1 notes for full discussion).
Historical Context: Chapter 9 compares the old covenant's worship system with Christ's superior sacrifice. The author describes the earthly tabernacle and its limitations (vv.1-10), then shows how Christ entered the heavenly sanctuary with His own blood, obtaining eternal redemption (vv.11-28). This chapter contains some of the most important atonement theology in the NT.
Classification: Epistle — Sermon or Treatise
Key Themes: A Most Holy Place entered only once a year and only with blood, sacrifices unable to perfect the conscience no matter how often repeated, Christ's own blood offered in a greater and more perfect tabernacle, a testament requiring the death of the one who made it, and an appearance promised a second time for salvation rather than to deal with sin again
The Earthly Tabernacle (vv.1-10): Description of the Holy Place and Most Holy Place with their furnishings (vv.1-5). The priests served daily in the first room; the high priest entered the second room only once a year with blood (vv.6-7). The Holy Spirit was signifying that access to God was "not yet made manifest" under the old system (v.8). The old sacrifices could not perfect the conscience (v.9) — they were temporary, "imposed until the time of reformation" (v.10).
Christ's Superior Sacrifice (vv.11-22): Christ entered the greater, heavenly tabernacle (v.11). Not with animal blood but His own blood (v.12). He obtained "eternal redemption" — once for all (v.12). If animal blood purified the flesh, "how much more shall the blood of Christ... purge your conscience from dead works" (vv.13-14). He is mediator of the new testament/covenant (v.15). A testament requires the death of the testator (vv.16-17). "Without shedding of blood is no remission" (v.22).
Christ's Once-for-All Appearance (vv.23-28): Christ entered heaven itself — not a man-made copy (v.24). He doesn't offer Himself repeatedly (v.25). He appeared "once in the end of the world... to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself" (v.26). As men die once then face judgment, Christ was offered once to bear sins — and will appear a second time for salvation (vv.27-28).

Deep Dive

Setting: The sermon to the Hebrews, probably written in the 60s to Jewish Christians tempted to slip back into the old ways, is now in its central argument. Chapters 7 and 8 showed that Jesus is a better high priest, "after the order of Melchizedek," serving in "the true tent that the Lord set up, not man" (8:2), of which the earthly tabernacle was only "a copy and shadow" (8:5). Chapter 8 then quoted God's promise of a new covenant in Jeremiah 31 and ended with the old covenant "becoming obsolete and growing old… ready to vanish away" (8:13). Chapter 9 takes that apart piece by piece. First it walks through the tabernacle and its yearly high point, the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16). Then it shows what Christ did in the true sanctuary: a better place, a better blood, a better covenant, and a sacrifice offered once and never repeated. The writer draws on the tabernacle of the wilderness books, not the temple of his own day, because that is the pattern God gave Moses.

The Earthly Holy Place (vv1–10)

  • "Even the first covenant had regulations for worship" (v1). The writer is not mocking the old system. God gave it, and it had real order and real holiness. His point throughout is that it was good for its purpose and that its purpose was to point beyond itself. "Earthly" is literally "worldly" (kosmikos), belonging to this created world, which sets up the contrast in v11 with a tent "not of this creation."
  • "The first section… the Holy Place" (v2). The tabernacle was a portable tent about 45 feet long and 15 wide, divided by a curtain into two rooms. In the outer room stood the golden lampstand (Exod 25:31–40), the only light inside, and the table with the bread of the Presence, twelve loaves set out fresh each Sabbath before the LORD (Lev 24:5–9). "Section" translates the word for "tent," so the writer speaks of a first tent and a second tent.
  • "Behind the second curtain" (v3). There was a screen at the entrance of the Holy Place and a second, heavier curtain embroidered with cherubim between the two rooms (Exod 26:31–33). Behind it was the Most Holy Place, a perfect cube about 15 feet each way, where God's presence dwelt above the ark.
  • "Having the golden altar of incense" (v4). This is a well-known puzzle. Exodus places the incense altar in the outer room, "in front of the veil that is by the ark" (Exod 30:6). There are two main views. Some, following the KJV ("golden censer"), think the writer means the censer the high priest carried in on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:12–13). The Greek word thymiatērion can mean either, and the lexicon lists both senses. Most modern translations and scholars read "altar of incense," as other Jewish writers of the time used the word. Either way, notice that the writer says the Most Holy Place had the altar, not that it stood inside. The altar belonged to the inner room in function: its incense rose toward the ark, and on the Day of Atonement its horns were smeared with the same blood (Exod 30:10). 1 Kings 6:22 even calls it "the altar that belonged to the inner sanctuary."
  • The ark and its contents (v4). The ark was a wooden chest overlaid with gold, the throne-footstool of God among his people. Inside were three reminders of the wilderness: the golden urn of manna (Exod 16:32–34), God's daily bread; Aaron's staff that budded overnight, settling who was God's chosen priest (Num 17:1–11); and the tablets of the covenant, the Ten Commandments. Exodus and Numbers say the manna and the staff were placed "before the testimony," and by Solomon's day only the tablets were in the ark (1 Kings 8:9). The writer follows the tradition that all three were kept inside it, which may have been true in the early years.
  • "The cherubim of glory overshadowing the mercy seat" (v5). Two golden cherubim faced each other on the lid of the ark with wings spread (Exod 25:18–22). The lid is the "mercy seat," Greek hilastērion, "the place of atonement" or "propitiation." Paul uses the same word of Christ: God "put forward" Jesus "as a propitiation by his blood" (Rom 3:25). The ark held the law Israel broke, and the lid where the blood was sprinkled stood between that law and the God who dwelt above it.
  • "Of these things we cannot now speak in detail" (v5). Every item could fill a sermon, and he knows it. He is not after the symbolism of each piece of furniture. He is after the layout: two rooms, a curtain and a closed door.
  • "The priests go regularly into the first section" (v6). Every day priests trimmed the lamps and burned incense morning and evening (Exod 30:7–8). The present tense ("go," "offers") may suggest the temple was still running when he wrote, which is one reason many date the letter before AD 70, though writers also used the present to describe the Law's instructions.
  • "Into the second only the high priest goes, and he but once a year" (v7). Only one man, on only one day, the Day of Atonement (Lev 16). On that day he actually went in several times: with incense, with the bull's blood for himself and his house, and with the goat's blood for the people. But the door opened on one day a year, and never "without taking blood." Tradition holds he was not to linger, lest the people fear he had been struck down (compare Lev 16:2).
  • "For himself and for the unintentional sins of the people" (v7). First he had to deal with his own sin (Lev 16:6, 11; Heb 5:3; 7:27). "Unintentional sins" translates agnoēmata, "sins of ignorance." The Law drew a sharp line between sins done unknowingly and sins done "with a high hand" in open defiance (Num 15:27–31), and the writer will return to that line in 10:26.
  • "By this the Holy Spirit indicates" (v8). The writer reads the floor plan as the Spirit's message. A shut curtain says that "the way into the holy places is not yet opened." As long as the first section, and the whole first-covenant arrangement it represents, is still standing, ordinary worshipers stay outside.
  • "Which is symbolic for the present age" (v9). "Symbolic" is parabolē, "parable." The tabernacle was an acted-out parable. Its lesson was that animal sacrifices "cannot perfect the conscience of the worshiper." They could make a person ceremonially clean to take part in worship, but they could not touch the inner sense of guilt before God.
  • "Food and drink and various washings… until the time of reformation" (v10). "Reformation" is diorthōsis, a "setting straight" or "putting right," used of setting a broken bone. These rules were "regulations for the body" (literally "of the flesh"), imposed for a time. A deadline was built in from the start.
  • Commentators: Henry says "the Old Testament was never intended to be rested in, but to prepare for the institutions of the gospel," and on v9 that its sacrifices "could not deliver conscience from a dread of the wrath of God." Guzik gives the tabernacle's dimensions and reads the ark's contents as reminders of Israel's failures: grumbling over food, rebellion against God's priest, and the broken law, all covered by blood on the mercy seat. He also notes that parabolē makes the whole system a parable. JFB insists v4 should read "golden censer," not altar, since the altar stood in the outer room. Guzik, like the ESV and the Tyndale notes, takes it as the incense altar, which the lexicon allows. Both readings are held by careful interpreters.

Redemption Through the Blood of Christ (vv11–28)

  • "But when Christ appeared" (v11). This is the turning point of the chapter, and arguably of the whole letter. Everything in vv1–10 was "not yet." Now Christ has come.
  • "A high priest of the good things that have come" (v11). Manuscripts differ here. Some early ones read "the good things that have come" (followed by the ESV and NIV), and others read "the good things to come" (KJV, NKJV). Both are well supported. Both are also true in Hebrews: the blessings of the new covenant have arrived in Christ, and their fullness still lies ahead (10:1; 13:14).
  • "The greater and more perfect tent (not made with hands…)" (v11). Christ did not serve in Herod's temple. The phrase "not made with hands" echoes the charge at Jesus' trial (Mark 14:58) and Stephen's speech (Acts 7:48). Interpreters differ on what the "tent" is. Most take it as the heavenly sanctuary itself, the reality behind the copy (8:2, 5; 9:24). Some early writers took it as Christ's own body, through which he approached God (compare 10:20; John 2:21). The first view fits the chapter's flow best.
  • "Not by means of the blood of goats and calves but by means of his own blood" (v12). On the Day of Atonement the high priest brought a bull and a goat (Lev 16:3, 15). Christ brought nothing but himself. He is priest and sacrifice at once.
  • "Once for all into the holy places… an eternal redemption" (v12). "Once for all" (ephapax) is a key word of Hebrews (7:27; 10:10). The high priest came out and had to go back the next year. Christ went in and the work was finished. "Redemption" means release by payment of a price, the language of freeing slaves and prisoners. "Thus securing" is literally "having found," or obtained. The redemption is "eternal" because it never needs topping up.
  • "The ashes of a heifer" (v13). Numbers 19 describes a red heifer burned whole outside the camp. Its ashes were mixed with water and sprinkled on anyone defiled by touching a dead body. The writer grants that these rites really worked at their own level: they "sanctify for the purification of the flesh." They restored a person's outward fitness to worship.
  • "How much more will the blood of Christ" (v14). This is the classic lesser-to-greater argument, which rabbis called "light and heavy." If animal blood could make the body ceremonially clean, Christ's blood can reach the conscience.
  • "Who through the eternal Spirit offered himself" (v14). Two main readings. Most take "eternal Spirit" as the Holy Spirit, who anointed Jesus for his ministry and sustained him to the cross (Isa 42:1; Luke 4:18). Others take it as Christ's own divine spirit, which gave his offering eternal worth. Either way, the cross was not something that simply happened to Jesus. He "offered himself," willingly, with all three persons of the Trinity at work in it.
  • "Without blemish" (v14). Amōmos is the word for an animal fit for sacrifice, with no defect (Lev 22:19–21). Applied to Jesus it means a sinless life (4:15; 1 Pet 1:19).
  • "Purify our conscience from dead works to serve the living God" (v14). "Dead works" appeared among the foundation stones in 6:1: acts that lead to death or have no life in them. Note the goal. A cleansed conscience is not the end in itself. It sets a person free "to serve," and the word means priestly worship. What only priests did in the tabernacle, every forgiven believer now does.
  • "Therefore he is the mediator of a new covenant" (v15). A mediator stands between two parties to bring them together (8:6; 12:24; 1 Tim 2:5). His death "redeems them from the transgressions committed under the first covenant." That is striking: Christ's death reached backward to cover believers who lived before it, which the animal sacrifices only pointed toward (compare Rom 3:25). "Those who are called" now receive "the promised eternal inheritance" that Abraham and his children looked for (11:13–16).
  • "Where a will is involved" (vv16–17). Here the writer plays on a word. Diathēkē is the Greek Bible's word for "covenant," but in everyday Greek it meant a last will and testament, and the lexicon gives both senses. A will "takes effect only at death," so a death had to happen before the heirs could inherit. Most translations (ESV, NIV, NLT) follow this reading. Some scholars think "covenant" is still meant in vv16–17, because ancient covenants were ratified over the bodies of slain animals that stood in for the covenant-maker, as in Genesis 15 and Jeremiah 34:18–20. On that reading the point is the same: no covenant without death. The ESV's choice is sound, and both views agree that Christ's death is what makes the inheritance ours.
  • "Not even the first covenant was inaugurated without blood" (v18). "Inaugurated" means dedicated or put into service, the same verb used in 10:20 of Christ opening "a new and living way."
  • Moses at Sinai (vv19–20). This recalls Exodus 24:3–8, when Moses read the Book of the Covenant, the people answered "All that the LORD has spoken we will do," and he threw blood on the altar and on the people. Hebrews adds details Exodus does not mention there: goats, water, scarlet wool, hyssop, and the sprinkling of the book itself. Hyssop and scarlet wool belong to other cleansing rites (Lev 14:4–7; Num 19:6), and the writer seems to gather the familiar elements of purification into one picture, possibly drawing on Jewish tradition. The Tyndale notes observe that Hebrews is the only ancient source that mentions the book being sprinkled.
  • "This is the blood of the covenant" (v20). Moses' words in Exodus 24:8. Any Christian hearing them would think of the upper room, where Jesus took the cup and said, "This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins" (Matt 26:28). The writer slightly changes "Behold the blood" to "This is the blood," perhaps with the Lord's Supper in mind.
  • "He sprinkled with the blood both the tent and all the vessels" (v21). Exodus 40 describes the tabernacle being anointed with oil, not blood. But Leviticus has blood applied to the altar and the sanctuary on the Day of Atonement (Lev 8:15; 16:14–19), and Josephus reports a tradition like the one here. The writer's point is general: everything that touched worship had to be cleansed.
  • "Almost everything is purified with blood" (v22). "Almost" is honest. The Law allowed a very poor person to bring flour for a sin offering (Lev 5:11–13), and some things were cleansed with water or fire (Num 31:22–23). But the rule held.
  • "Without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness of sins" (v22). "Shedding of blood" is one word, haimatekchysia, found nowhere before this in surviving Greek, and possibly coined by the writer. It means blood poured out, a life given up. The principle comes from Leviticus 17:11: "the life of the flesh is in the blood… it is the blood that makes atonement by the life." Sin brings death (Rom 6:23), so forgiveness costs a life.
  • "The heavenly things themselves with better sacrifices" (v23). A hard phrase: why would heaven need cleansing? Several answers have been given. Some say what is cleansed is the people who will enter God's presence, their consciences in particular (v14; 10:22). Others say sin had defiled even the relationship between God and humanity, so the place of meeting needed to be made fit, much as the earthly sanctuary was cleansed "because of the uncleannesses of the people of Israel" (Lev 16:16). Others take "purified" here to mean dedicated, as the first covenant was inaugurated in v18. The plural "sacrifices" is a general statement matching "these rites." The writer does not mean Christ offered more than once (v25).
  • "Not into holy places made with hands… but into heaven itself" (v24). The earthly rooms were "copies" (antitypa) of the true things. Christ went straight into the reality.
  • "Now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf" (v24). The high priest went in hidden by a cloud of incense (Lev 16:13). Christ appears openly before God's face, and he does it "on our behalf." This is his ongoing ministry of intercession (7:25; Rom 8:34; 1 John 2:1).
  • "Nor was it to offer himself repeatedly" (vv25–26). The writer argues from the absurd. If Christ's sacrifice worked the way the yearly ones did, "he would have had to suffer repeatedly since the foundation of the world." One death that could not finish the job would require endless deaths. But a single death that does finish it never needs repeating.
  • "Once for all at the end of the ages to put away sin" (v26). "The end of the ages" means the climax of history. The last days began with Christ's first coming (1:2; 1 Cor 10:11). "Put away" is athetēsis, a legal word for annulling or cancelling, the same word used in 7:18 of the old commandment being "set aside." Sin is not just covered over for another year. It is cancelled.
  • "It is appointed for man to die once, and after that comes judgment" (v27). One life, one death, one judgment. The verse leaves no room for reincarnation or a second chance after death. But it is here mainly as a comparison. Humans die once and then face judgment. Christ died once and then comes not with judgment but with salvation for his people. A few people in Scripture did not die (Enoch, Elijah), and some were raised and later died again, but these exceptions do not change the rule.
  • "Offered once to bear the sins of many" (v28). This echoes Isaiah 53:12: "he bore the sin of many." "Many" does not mean "some but not all." It is a Hebrew way of saying a great multitude, set against the one who bears them (compare Mark 10:45).
  • "Will appear a second time… to save those who are eagerly waiting for him" (v28). On the Day of Atonement the people stood outside the sanctuary waiting for the high priest to come back out. His reappearance meant the sacrifice had been accepted (compare Luke 1:21, where the crowd waits for the priest Zechariah). Christ has gone in, and his people wait for him to come out. When he does, it will be "not to deal with sin," since that is finished, but to bring the salvation he secured. Notice the chapter's three appearings: he has appeared once to put away sin (v26), he now appears before God for us (v24), and he will appear a second time (v28). Past, present and future are all in his hands.
  • Commentators: On v14 Henry stresses that it was "Christ's offering up himself to God through the eternal Spirit," and that this blood "is sufficient to purge the conscience from dead works." JFB reads "eternal Spirit" as Christ's own divine nature, while most modern interpreters and the Tyndale notes take it as the Holy Spirit. On vv16–17 Guzik and JFB both explain the will-and-covenant double meaning of diathēkē, and JFB adds that a will "is never availing" while the testator lives. JFB notes candidly that the sprinkling of the book "is not mentioned" in Exodus 24. On v26 both Guzik and JFB draw the conclusion that a once-for-all sacrifice cannot be repeated, and both press it against the Catholic teaching on the Mass. That is a point where traditions differ: Catholic teaching holds that the Mass re-presents the one sacrifice rather than repeating it. On v27 Henry writes, "This is the unalterable decree of God concerning men—they must die, and they must be judged." Guzik agrees that the verse rules out reincarnation and second chances after death. On v28 Henry says, "It is the distinguishing character of true believers that they are looking for Christ," and Guzik wishes that were truer of believers than it is.

Pointing to Christ

The chapter begins with a closed curtain and a single nervous priest, and ends with Christ in the very presence of God, "on our behalf." Every piece of the old system becomes a signpost: the lampstand and the bread, the ark and its mercy seat, the yearly blood, the red heifer's ashes, the blood Moses threw at Sinai. All of it said something true about sin and holiness, and none of it could cleanse a conscience. Jesus is at once the priest who goes in, the sacrifice he brings, and the mediator of the covenant his blood seals. He went in once, and he will come out again. Until then, his people do not stand outside afraid. They wait eagerly.

Reflection

  • A Conscience Purged for Service (v.14) — Christ's blood cleanses the conscience from dead works so believers can serve the living God freely. Are you still carrying guilt for past sin, or trying to earn favor through effort, instead of resting in a cleansed conscience?
  • The Necessity of Blood (v.22) — Nearly everything under the law was cleansed with blood, and without it there is no forgiveness. Do you appreciate what it actually cost for your sins to be forgiven?
  • Sin Put Away, Not Just Covered (v.26) — Christ appeared once at the culmination of the ages to remove sin through His own sacrifice. Do you live as if your sin is truly gone, or do you keep pulling back out what God has already put away?
  • One Death, Then Judgment (v.27) — Just as each person dies once and then faces judgment, life is short and its outcome certain. Is there anything unsettled between you and God that this truth should move you to address?
  • Watching for His Return (v.28) — Christ will appear a second time, not to deal with sin again but to bring salvation to those eagerly waiting for Him. Are you living with real anticipation of His return, or has that hope faded into the background?
