The Foundation

Who God Is

The "attributes of God" are the qualities that make up who He is — not separate parts bolted together, but the one perfect, unified character of the one true God, each quality fully present and fully expressed in every other. When Scripture says God is holy, it does not mean holiness is one ingredient among many; it means everything else about Him — His love, His justice, His power — is holy love, holy justice, holy power. Theologians study the attributes one at a time only because human minds can take in truth a piece at a time, the way a prism splits one beam of light into colors that were always there together.

Theology has long sorted these qualities into two families. The incommunicable attributes are the ones God alone possesses and cannot share with His creatures in any degree — His holiness, sovereignty, omniscience, omnipotence, omnipresence, immutability, and eternality. No creature can be all-knowing, present everywhere, or without beginning; these mark the infinite distance between Creator and creature. The communicable attributes are the ones God shares with us in finite, creaturely measure because we are made in His image — His love, justice, mercy, faithfulness, patience, and righteousness. We cannot love infinitely or judge perfectly as He does, but we were built to reflect these qualities in a real, if limited, way, and growing in Christ means growing more and more into their likeness.

This distinction is not academic hair-splitting. The psalmist writes, "Be still, and know that I am God" (Psalm 46:10) — stillness before Him only makes sense once we know who it is we are being still before. Jeremiah records God's own priority for what a person should boast in: "Let him who boasts boast in this, that he understands and knows me, that I am the LORD who practices steadfast love, justice, and righteousness in the earth" (Jeremiah 9:23-24). True worship, true obedience, and true comfort in suffering all flow from true knowledge of who God actually is — not a vague feeling about Him, and not a god remade in our own preferences, but the God who has told us plainly what He is like.

This page is a close companion to the site's Names of God study, but the two ask different questions. The names are titles — Yahweh, El Shaddai, Jehovah Jireh — each one a window opened onto a facet of who God is in a particular moment or relationship. This page studies the qualities themselves: not what He is called, but what He is actually like, in His own unchanging nature.

An Incommunicable Attribute — Isaiah 6:3

Holiness

Meaning

At its root, holiness means "set apart" — utterly distinct from everything else that exists. When Isaiah is given a vision of the throne room, the seraphim do not call out that God is powerful or wise three times over; they cry "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts" (Isaiah 6:3), the only attribute in Scripture given this triple repetition, as if no single word or even a doubled word could carry the weight of it. God's holiness is both His otherness — He is in a category with nothing else that exists — and His absolute moral purity. Habakkuk says it plainly: God's "eyes are too pure to behold evil" and He "cannot look at wrong" (Habakkuk 1:13). Evil is not simply distasteful to Him; it is categorically incompatible with who He is.

Because holiness is who God is rather than merely what He does, it becomes the standard He sets for His people: "You shall be holy, for I am holy" (Leviticus 11:44-45; 1 Peter 1:15-16). This is not an arbitrary rule but a call to reflect, in creaturely measure, the very nature of the One who made us. And Revelation 4:8 shows that this truth never becomes old news even in heaven — the four living creatures never stop, day or night, singing "holy, holy, holy," because eternity does not exhaust the wonder of who God is.

How We Respond

Rightly understood, God's holiness produces both reverence and relief — reverence because we stand before a God who cannot be casually approached or managed, and relief because the gospel means that very holiness has been made our covering in Christ rather than our condemnation. Isaiah's own response to the vision was to cry "Woe is me! For I am lost" (Isaiah 6:5), and that honest reckoning with our own impurity in the presence of God's purity is where worship starts. From there, holiness calls us not to earn God's favor through moral effort but to pursue a life that increasingly reflects the character of the One who has already made us His own.

An Incommunicable Attribute — Psalm 115:3

Sovereignty

Meaning

God's sovereignty is His supreme, unrivaled rule over all creation — every nation, every ruler, every atom, every event answers finally to Him. "Our God is in the heavens; he does all that he pleases" (Psalm 115:3). This is not a God who hopes things turn out well or who is surprised by headlines; Daniel 4:35 says plainly that "he does according to his will among the host of heaven and among the inhabitants of the earth, and none can stay his hand or say to him, 'What have you done?'" His rule extends over the rise and fall of kings, the weather, and the smallest details of a believer's life.

Sovereignty is not cold determinism; it is the backdrop that makes the rest of Scripture's promises trustworthy. Paul can write that "for those who love God all things work together for good, for those who are called according to his purpose" (Romans 8:28) only because nothing — not evil, not tragedy, not human rebellion — lies outside the reach of God's ultimate purposes. He is working all things, as Ephesians 1:11 says, "according to the counsel of his will."

How We Respond

Because God is sovereign, surrender rather than anxious control becomes the posture of faith. We can release our grip on outcomes we cannot manage, bring our plans to Him in prayer rather than treating them as ours to secure alone, and face suffering and uncertainty without despair, trusting that no circumstance has slipped outside His hand. Sovereignty also humbles us: the God who rules the nations does not need our strategies to accomplish His purposes, only our willing obedience.

An Incommunicable Attribute — Psalm 139:1-4

Omniscience

Meaning

Omniscience means God knows all things — perfectly, simultaneously, and without ever learning or discovering anything new. David marvels at this in deeply personal terms: "O LORD, you have searched me and known me! You know when I sit down and when I rise up... Even before a word is on my tongue, behold, O LORD, you know it altogether" (Psalm 139:1-4). This is not distant, abstract knowledge but intimate awareness of the details of every life. Hebrews 4:13 extends the scope: "No creature is hidden from his sight, but all are naked and exposed to the eyes of him to whom we must give account."

God's knowledge includes what has happened, what is happening, and what will happen — Isaiah 46:10 shows Him "declaring the end from the beginning" — as well as the hidden motives of the human heart. "Whenever our heart condemns us, God is greater than our heart, and he knows everything" (1 John 3:20). Nothing about us, good or bad, is a surprise to Him.

How We Respond

God's omniscience is simultaneously unsettling and comforting. It is unsettling because there is no hiding from Him — no private sin, no secret motive, no hidden grief escapes His notice, which calls us to honesty rather than performance before Him. But it is deeply comforting for the same reason: because He knows everything, we can bring Him our confusion without needing to explain ourselves fully, trust His guidance in decisions we cannot see the outcome of, and rest knowing that He already knows the worst about us and has loved us anyway.

Key Verses

  • Psalm 139:1-4 — "You know when I sit down and when I rise up... you know it altogether." God's knowledge of us is exhaustive and personal.
  • Hebrews 4:13 — "No creature is hidden from his sight, but all are naked and exposed to the eyes of him." Nothing escapes God's awareness.
  • 1 John 3:20 — "God is greater than our heart, and he knows everything." Even our own self-condemnation cannot out-measure His knowledge.
  • Isaiah 46:10 — God, "declaring the end from the beginning." His knowledge spans all of history before it unfolds.
An Incommunicable Attribute — Jeremiah 32:17

Omnipotence

Meaning

Omnipotence means God possesses all power — there is nothing that exceeds His strength or lies beyond His ability to accomplish. Jeremiah prays, "Ah, Lord GOD! It is you who have made the heavens and the earth by your great power and by your outstretched arm! Nothing is too hard for you" (Jeremiah 32:17), and God Himself echoes that exact phrase to Abraham when Sarah laughs at the promise of a son in old age: "Is anything too hard for the LORD?" (Genesis 18:14). Job, after God's own extended testimony to His power in creation, concludes simply, "I know that you can do all things, and that no purpose of yours can be thwarted" (Job 42:2).

This power is not raw force without direction; it is always exercised in harmony with God's other attributes — His wisdom, His holiness, His love. Jesus assures His disciples that "with God all things are possible" (Matthew 19:26) precisely in a conversation about salvation, something no human effort could accomplish on its own. And Revelation 19:6 pictures the end of history with a multitude crying "Hallelujah! For the Lord our God the Almighty reigns."

How We Respond

Omnipotence gives weight to prayer — we are not petitioning a God who merely sympathizes but one who is actually able to act. It gives grounds for hope when a situation looks humanly impossible, since the God who spoke the universe into being is not limited by what we can imagine a solution to look like. And it calls for worship rather than fear, because this limitless power is wielded by a Father who is also perfectly good.

An Incommunicable Attribute — Psalm 139:7-10

Omnipresence

Meaning

Omnipresence means God is fully present everywhere at once — not spread thin across the universe like a diffused gas, but wholly and entirely present in every place simultaneously, in a way no creature could ever be. David asks the question rhetorically and answers it from every direction: "Where shall I go from your Spirit? Or where shall I flee from your presence? If I ascend to heaven, you are there! If I make my bed in Sheol, you are there!" (Psalm 139:7-10). There is no location, however remote or however dark, that falls outside God's presence.

Jeremiah 23:23-24 makes the same point against the false idea that God could be contained in one place or blind to what happens elsewhere: "Do I not fill heaven and earth? declares the LORD." And Paul, preaching to a Gentile audience at the Areopagus, describes this presence as the very environment of existence itself: "In him we live and move and have our being" (Acts 17:27-28).

How We Respond

Omnipresence means there is no circumstance in which we are truly alone — not in a hospital room at 3 a.m., not in grief, not in a foreign country, not in the ordinary stretch of an unremarkable Tuesday. For the believer this is comfort; for the one trying to hide from God, it is a sobering reminder that there is no escape from His presence, only a choice about whether to run toward Him or away. Either way, omnipresence invites us to seek God not as a distant figure we must travel to find, but as the One already here.

An Incommunicable Attribute — Malachi 3:6

Immutability

Meaning

Immutability means God does not change — not in His character, His purposes, His promises, or His essential nature. "For I the LORD do not change; therefore you, O children of Jacob, are not consumed" (Malachi 3:6) — Israel's survival is tied directly to God's unchanging commitment to His covenant. James describes God as "the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shadow due to change" (James 1:17), and Numbers 23:19 puts it bluntly: "God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind."

This is not to say God never acts differently in response to changing circumstances — Scripture is full of God relating dynamically to His people, warning, relenting, and responding to prayer. What never changes is His underlying character, His holiness, His covenant faithfulness, and His purposes. Hebrews 13:8 draws this directly to Christ: "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever."

How We Respond

Immutability is the ground beneath every other promise in Scripture — a promise is only as trustworthy as the one who made it, and a God who might change His mind tomorrow would be no secure foundation for faith today. Because He does not change, we do not need to wonder whether the grace that saved us yesterday will still be there tomorrow, or whether the God who loved us at our worst might grow tired of us. His steadiness becomes an anchor in seasons when everything else in life feels unstable.

A Communicable Attribute — 1 John 4:8, 16

Love

Meaning

Scripture does not merely say that God loves — it says "God is love" (1 John 4:8, 16), identifying love as part of His essential nature rather than simply one action among many that He performs. This love is not primarily a feeling but a self-giving commitment to the good of another, demonstrated most clearly at the cross: "God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). God's love was not a response to our lovability; it moved toward us while we were still His enemies.

This love is also deeply personal and enduring. "I have loved you with an everlasting love; therefore I have continued my faithfulness to you" (Jeremiah 31:3), and John 3:16 grounds the entire scope of salvation in it: "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only Son." God's love is not generic goodwill toward humanity in the abstract — it reaches individuals, by name, at cost to Himself.

How We Respond

Because God's love does not depend on our performance, it becomes the secure foundation from which we can face failure honestly rather than hiding from Him in shame. Receiving this love is the first step; the second is letting it overflow — "we love because he first loved us" (1 John 4:19), and a life genuinely shaped by God's love increasingly loves others the same way: sacrificially, patiently, and without demanding they earn it first.

A Communicable Attribute — Deuteronomy 32:4

Justice

Meaning

God's justice means He is always, perfectly fair — He never judges wrongly, never shows favoritism, and never lets wrongdoing simply slide. "The Rock, his work is perfect, for all his ways are justice. A God of faithfulness and without iniquity, just and upright is he" (Deuteronomy 32:4). Justice and righteousness are not peripheral to who God is but foundational to His rule: "Righteousness and justice are the foundation of your throne" (Psalm 89:14).

Abraham appeals directly to this attribute when pleading for Sodom: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" (Genesis 18:25) — a question that assumes the answer is obviously yes. God's justice is not cold legalism; it is love's commitment to what is actually right, which is why He pairs it with mercy so often in Scripture: "For I the LORD love justice; I hate robbery and wrong" (Isaiah 61:8).

How We Respond

God's justice gives believers confidence that no wrong done to them, and no wrong they themselves have done, will simply be lost or forgotten — every account will finally be settled rightly, whether through the cross or final judgment. This should move us to pursue justice ourselves rather than taking private revenge (Romans 12:19), and it should sober us: the same God who will right every wrong done against us is also the Judge before whom we ourselves stand, which is exactly why the gospel's good news of a Savior who bears that judgment for us is such good news.

A Communicable Attribute — Ephesians 2:4-8

Mercy and Grace

Meaning

Mercy and grace are close companions but not identical: mercy is God withholding the judgment we deserve, while grace is God giving the favor we do not deserve. Paul holds both together in one breath: "God, being rich in mercy, because of the great love with which he loved us... raised us up with him... For by grace you have been saved through faith" (Ephesians 2:4-8). Neither is something we can earn; both flow entirely from who God is rather than from anything in us.

This pairing goes all the way back to God's own self-description at Sinai, the fullest statement of His character in the whole Bible: "The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Exodus 34:6-7). Lamentations draws out just how reliable this mercy is: "His mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning" (Lamentations 3:22-23). And Titus is explicit that salvation itself runs on mercy, not merit: "He saved us, not because of works done by us in righteousness, but according to his own mercy" (Titus 3:5).

How We Respond

Receiving mercy and grace should produce deep gratitude rather than entitlement — we were not owed either one. It should also shape how we treat others: those who have genuinely received undeserved mercy find it harder to withhold mercy from the people who wrong them (Matthew 18:21-35), and grace received becomes grace extended, not grace hoarded.

A Communicable Attribute — Lamentations 3:22-23

Faithfulness

Meaning

God's faithfulness means He always keeps His word and never abandons His covenant commitments, no matter how long it takes or how unfaithful His people prove to be. "The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases; his mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning; great is your faithfulness" (Lamentations 3:22-23) — written, remarkably, in the aftermath of Jerusalem's destruction, when faithfulness might have seemed hardest to believe. Paul states the principle even more starkly: "If we are faithless, he remains faithful — for he cannot deny himself" (2 Timothy 2:13). God's faithfulness does not depend on ours.

This is covenant language through and through. "Know therefore that the LORD your God is God, the faithful God who keeps covenant and steadfast love with those who love him and keep his commandments, to a thousand generations" (Deuteronomy 7:9), and the psalmist pictures the sheer scale of it: "Your steadfast love, O LORD, extends to the heavens, your faithfulness to the clouds" (Psalm 36:5).

How We Respond

God's faithfulness is what makes waiting bearable — when a promise seems delayed or prayers seem unanswered, we are not trusting a God with a track record of giving up, but one who has never once failed to keep His word across the whole of Scripture. This truth also sets the bar for our own faithfulness: in marriage, friendship, church commitments, and daily obedience, we reflect, however imperfectly, the steadiness of the God who never wavers in His commitment to us.

A Communicable Attribute — 2 Peter 3:9

Patience

Meaning

God's patience means He withholds judgment that is fully deserved, giving time and space for repentance rather than acting the instant sin occurs. "The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" (2 Peter 3:9). This patience is woven into His own self-description at Sinai — He is "slow to anger" (Exodus 34:6) — and Paul asks pointedly whether we presume on "the riches of his kindness and forbearance and patience," not realizing that "God's kindness is meant to lead you to repentance" (Romans 2:4).

Patience is not the same as indifference, however. Numbers 14:18 holds both truths in the same verse: the LORD is "slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love," yet He "will by no means clear the guilty." Patience delays judgment; it does not cancel it. It is mercy given time to work, not an endless postponement.

How We Respond

God's patience is an invitation, not a loophole — every day that judgment is delayed is a day of grace meant to be met with repentance, not presumed upon as if consequences will never come. For believers already secure in Christ, this same patience becomes the pattern for how we treat others: slow to anger, generous with the benefit of the doubt, willing to give people the same room to grow and repent that God has given us.

A Communicable Attribute, Handled with Care — Romans 1:18

Wrath

Meaning

God's wrath is His holy, settled opposition to sin — not a flash of temper or an uncontrolled outburst like human anger often is, but the necessary response of a perfectly holy and just God to evil. "The wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men" (Romans 1:18). Because God is holy, He cannot be indifferent to sin; because He is just, sin cannot simply be waved away. Nahum holds wrath and patience together in the same breath: "The LORD is a jealous and avenging God... slow to anger and great in power" (Nahum 1:2-3) — wrath is real, but it is never the whole picture, and it is never quick or careless.

Scripture is careful to show that wrath is not arbitrary. Jesus says plainly, "Whoever believes in the Son has eternal life; whoever does not obey the Son shall not see life, but the wrath of God remains on him" (John 3:36) — wrath is what remains where it has not been removed, not something added on top of a neutral starting point. Paul warns that continued, unrepentant sin is "storing up wrath for yourself on the day of wrath" (Romans 2:5), a sober picture of judgment accumulating rather than arriving out of nowhere.

How We Respond

Wrath is one of the attributes easiest to distort in either direction — into a capricious tyrant who delights in punishment, or quietly edited out altogether into a God who is only ever gentle and never serious about sin. Scripture allows neither caricature. Held alongside God's patience, wrath should produce genuine urgency: patience gives time, but it is not unlimited, and the gospel's good news is precisely that Christ bore the wrath we deserved so that "since we have now been justified by his blood, much more shall we be saved by him from the wrath of God" (Romans 5:9). The right response to this attribute is neither terror nor indifference, but gratitude for the escape God Himself has provided, and a renewed seriousness about sin.

A Communicable Attribute — Psalm 11:7

Righteousness

Meaning

God's righteousness means He always does what is right — His actions and judgments align perfectly with His own holy standard, which is the only standard that truly exists. "The LORD is righteous; he loves righteous deeds" (Psalm 11:7), and this is not merely a legal quality but one that touches every one of His dealings: "The LORD is righteous in all his ways and kind in all his works" (Psalm 145:17). Deuteronomy 32:4 pairs righteousness with faithfulness and justice as inseparable facets of the same unwavering character.

The great tension righteousness creates is how a perfectly righteous God can justify sinful people without compromising His own standard — and Paul says this is exactly what the cross accomplishes: God put Christ forward "so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Romans 3:26). At the cross, God's righteousness is not set aside to save sinners; it is fully satisfied while sinners are fully saved.

How We Respond

God's righteousness means the standard we are measured against is not our own best effort or someone else's worse behavior, but His own perfect character — which should humble any hint of self-righteousness. At the same time, it is the deepest comfort of the gospel: because Christ's righteousness is credited to those who trust Him, we stand before this perfectly righteous God not on the basis of our own record but His, which frees us to pursue righteous living out of gratitude rather than fear.

  • Deuteronomy 32:4 — "A God of faithfulness and without iniquity, just and upright is he." Righteousness, justice, and faithfulness are inseparable in God.
  • Romans 3:26 — God is "just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus." The cross satisfies righteousness while saving sinners.
An Incommunicable Attribute — Psalm 90:2

Eternality (Self-Existence)

Meaning

God's eternality means He has always existed and always will, with no beginning and no end, entirely outside the limits of time that bind everything He has made. "Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever you had formed the earth and the world, from everlasting to everlasting you are God" (Psalm 90:2). Closely tied to this is His self-existence, or aseity — God depends on nothing and no one for His existence. When Moses asks for His name, God answers simply, "I AM WHO I AM" (Exodus 3:14), a name that declares pure, underived, self-sufficient being.

Revelation 1:8 frames this with the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet: "I am the Alpha and the Omega... who is and who was and who is to come, the Almighty" — God stands at both ends of time and everywhere in between. Isaiah adds a striking paradox: the One who "inhabits eternity" also draws near "to revive the spirit of the lowly" (Isaiah 57:15) — the God beyond time chooses to meet us within it.

How We Respond

In a world where everything familiar eventually fades, ages, or ends, God's eternality is a stable place to rest hope — He was not caused, cannot be unmade, and His promises are not subject to the wear of time. This truth also reframes our own mortality: because the eternal God has invited us into relationship with Himself, our own existence is no longer bounded by the grave, and the hope of eternal life is simply an invitation to share, in creaturely measure, in the eternity that has always been His alone.
