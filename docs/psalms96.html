Summary

An enthronement hymn calling all the earth to sing a new song to the LORD and proclaim his salvation and glory among the nations (vv. 1-3). It contrasts the LORD's greatness with the worthlessness of the idols of the peoples, urging all families of the earth to ascribe him glory and strength and worship him in the splendor of his holiness (vv. 4-9). The nations are told that the LORD reigns and the world stands firm, and all creation — heavens, sea, fields, and forest — is summoned to rejoice before him, for he is coming to judge the earth with righteousness and truth (vv. 10-13).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Enthronement / Hymn of Praise
Attributed Author: Anonymous (cf. 1 Chronicles 16:23-33 — Davidic)
Key Themes: A new song, declaring God's glory among the nations, the LORD above all gods, worship in holiness, creation rejoicing, God coming to judge the earth
Historical Context: This psalm appears (with variations) in 1 Chronicles 16:23-33 as part of the song David commissioned when the Ark was brought to Jerusalem. It is one of the "enthronement psalms" proclaiming God's kingship. The call to sing a "new song" indicates a fresh experience of God's saving power requiring fresh worship. The missionary scope is remarkable: "Declare his glory among the nations" (v.3), "all the earth" (v.1), "all peoples" (v.3). The psalm ends with the most comprehensive call to creation worship in Scripture — heavens, earth, sea, fields, and trees all rejoicing because God comes to judge.
O Sing unto the LORD a New Song (vv.1-3): O sing unto the LORD a new song: sing unto the LORD, all the earth. Nearly this whole psalm stands in 1 Chronicles 16:23-33, sung when David brought up the ark, which is the ground of the Davidic attribution even though the psalm itself carries no superscription. The audience is the widest the psalter uses, all the earth, and the commission is to report abroad, declare his glory among the heathen, his wonders among all people.
All the Gods of the Nations Are Idols (vv.4-6): For the LORD is great, and greatly to be praised: he is to be feared above all gods. Then the line that settles what above all gods meant, for all the gods of the nations are idols. The Hebrew turns on a likeness of sound between elohim and elilim, gods and nothings, which no English version reproduces. The proof offered is not argument but authorship, but the LORD made the heavens, and the section closes inside the building where that is confessed, strength and beauty are in his sanctuary.
Worship the LORD in the Beauty of Holiness (vv.7-9): Give unto the LORD, O ye kindreds of the people, give unto the LORD glory and strength. The imperative is repeated three times and it is addressed to families of nations, not to Israel, which means foreigners are told to bring an offering and come into his courts. O worship the LORD in the beauty of holiness. The phrase can be read as worship in holy attire or as worship in the splendor that holiness is, and the Hebrew allows both.
Say among the Heathen That the LORD Reigneth (v.10): Say among the heathen that the LORD reigneth: the world also shall be established that it shall not be moved: he shall judge the people righteously. One verse holding the whole message the singers are to carry, and it is the announcement of Psalm 93 turned into an errand. Stability and judgment arrive together, which is the psalm's reason for treating a coming assize as good news.
For He Cometh to Judge the Earth (vv.11-13): Let the heavens rejoice, and let the earth be glad; let the sea roar, and the fulness thereof. Sky, land, sea, field and forest are each given a part, and the sea that was chaos in Psalm 93 is here simply loud. Then the reason, said twice because the repetition is the joy, for he cometh, for he cometh to judge the earth. Judgment is what creation is glad about, and that is only coherent if the standard is the one named in the last line, he shall judge the world with righteousness, and the people with his truth.

Deep Dive

Setting: Psalm 96 has no title in the Hebrew text, but almost all of it appears again in 1 Chronicles 16:23–33, part of the song David gave Asaph and the Levites when the ark of the covenant was brought up to Jerusalem (about 1000 BC). That is why many, including Henry and Guzik, take David as its author. Scholars debate whether Chronicles quotes an older psalm or whether the psalm was later drawn out of that festival song for temple use. The Greek translation gives it a heading about the house being rebuilt after the exile, which shows it was sung again in that later setting. It belongs to the "The LORD reigns" psalms (93–100) and repeats that cry in v10. Psalm 95 called Israel to worship and warned them to listen. Psalm 96 widens the circle: first God's people, then "all the earth," then the nations, and finally the heavens, sea, fields and trees are called to praise the King who is coming to judge. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the ESV's stanza breaks.

Sing to the LORD a New Song (vv1–6)

  • "Oh sing to the LORD a new song" (v1). "Sing" is said three times in two verses. A "new song" in the Psalms answers a fresh act of God (Ps 33:3; 40:3; 98:1; 144:9). It is not merely new music but praise for new mercies. Revelation uses the phrase for the song of the redeemed from "every tribe and language and people and nation" (Rev 5:9; 14:3), which is exactly the range this psalm has in view.
  • "Sing to the LORD, all the earth!" (v1). The invitation goes beyond Israel. The God of one small nation calls the whole earth to sing. This was unusual in the ancient world, where each people sang to its own gods.
  • "Bless his name; tell of his salvation from day to day" (v2). To "bless" God is to speak well of him, to praise him with thankfulness. "Tell of" (basseru) means to announce good news, the verb used for a messenger bringing news of victory (1 Sam 31:9; Isa 40:9; 52:7). The Greek translation renders it with euangelizomai, the word that gives us "evangelize" and "gospel." "From day to day" means it is news that never goes stale.
  • "Declare his glory among the nations" (v3). "Glory" (kavod) comes from a root meaning "heavy" or "weighty." God's glory is the weight of who he is, his real worth made visible. Praise is not only for the gathered congregation. It is to be spoken out among the nations, a missionary note that the New Testament takes up (Matt 28:19; Rom 15:9–11).
  • "For great is the LORD, and greatly to be praised" (v4). The same words open Psalm 48:1 and 145:3. The praise is fitted to its object: a great God deserves great praise. "He is to be feared above all gods." "Feared" here means held in awe, reverenced.
  • "For all the gods of the peoples are worthless idols" (v5). Here is a pun. The word for "gods" is elohim. The word for "worthless idols" is elilim, which sounds almost the same but means "nothings," "weak things." Job uses it for "worthless physicians" (Job 13:4). Isaiah uses it often for idols (Isa 2:8, 18, 20). The nations' gods sound like God but have nothing inside.
  • "But the LORD made the heavens" (v5). That is the proof. Pagan peoples often worshiped the sun, moon and stars. The LORD made them all. An idol is made by human hands; the LORD made the heavens with his own (Ps 115:4; Isa 44:9–20).
  • "Splendor and majesty are before him; strength and beauty are in his sanctuary" (v6). The qualities of a king are pictured as attendants standing before his throne. Psalm 104:1 uses "splendor and majesty" as God's clothing. In 1 Chronicles 16:27, sung before the temple existed, the second line reads "strength and joy are in his place." Psalm 96 has "sanctuary" instead, the form suited to temple worship. "Beauty" (tiph'eret) means glory or splendor, the honor that makes something admirable.
  • Commentators: Henry calls the new song "a song for new favours, for those compassions which are new every morning," and also "a song that shall be ever new." He reads "let all the earth" as "a prophecy of the calling of the Gentiles," and v3 as the commission later given to the apostles. JFB says "show forth" is literally "declare joyful tidings," and that God "is not a local God, but of universal agency, while idols are nothing." Guzik describes the psalm as a series of widening circles, from God's people to the nations to creation. He explains the elilim wordplay as a parody of the word for God, and suggests the sanctuary is both earthly and heavenly, since the earthly one was a copy of the heavenly (Heb 8:5).

Ascribe to the LORD the Glory Due His Name (vv7–9)

  • "Ascribe to the LORD, O families of the peoples" (v7). "Ascribe" (havu) literally means "give." These verses echo Psalm 29:1–2, but with one striking change. There the call goes to the "heavenly beings." Here it goes to the "families of the peoples," the clans of the earth. The word "families" (mishpachot) is the one God used in his promise to Abraham: "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:3). The psalm pictures that promise coming true.
  • "Ascribe to the LORD glory and strength!" (v7). No one can add to God's glory or strength. To "give" them is to acknowledge them, to say aloud what is already true of him.
  • "Ascribe to the LORD the glory due his name" (v8). "Due" means rightly owed. Worship is not a favor done for God. It is giving him what he is worth.
  • "Bring an offering, and come into his courts!" (v8). "Offering" (minchah) was the grain offering, a gift of tribute brought to a king (Lev 2:1; Judg 3:15). The temple courts were normally closed to Gentiles beyond an outer area, and the Tyndale notes point out how striking it is that the nations are invited in. Isaiah saw the same thing: foreigners bringing offerings, because "my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples" (Isa 56:6–7; compare Mal 1:11).
  • "Worship the LORD in the splendor of holiness" (v9). The phrase (hadrat qodesh) may mean the majestic holiness of God, or the holy garments worn by worshipers (the ESV footnote has "in holy attire"), as priests dressed for their service. Either way, worship matches the holiness of the one worshiped. "Tremble before him, all the earth!" Joy (v1) and trembling (v9) are not opposites here. The nearer God comes, the more glad and the more awed his people are.
  • Commentators: Henry says that in gospel times "the court of the Gentiles shall no longer be an outward court, but shall be laid in common with the court of Israel," and that what we give God "is debt, it is rent, it is tribute," which "if it come from holy love, God is pleased to accept… as a gift." He applies the offering first to ourselves, "the offering up of the Gentiles" (Rom 15:16). JFB reads "give" as "ascribe" due honor "by acts of appointed and solemn worship," and the offering as one "of thanks." Guzik ties "families of the peoples" to the promise to Abraham in Genesis 12:3, and notes that to "give" glory is to recognize glory God already has.

Say Among the Nations, "The LORD Reigns!" (v10)

  • "Say among the nations, 'The LORD reigns!'" (v10). This verse stands by itself in the ESV, and it is the message the whole psalm has been building to. God's people are to announce to the world the same truth Psalm 93 began with: Yahweh malak, the LORD is King.
  • A textual note. Some early Christian writers, starting with Justin Martyr in the second century, quoted this line as "The Lord reigned from the tree," meaning the cross, and Justin accused Jewish scribes of removing the words. The phrase is not in the Hebrew or in the main Greek manuscripts, and scholars agree it was a Christian addition that crept into some Greek and Latin copies. It survives in the old hymn Vexilla Regis ("God reigned from the tree"). It is a devout reading, not the original text.
  • "Yes, the world is established; it shall never be moved" (v10). The line is repeated from 93:1. The King holds creation steady.
  • "He will judge the peoples with equity" (v10). "Equity" (mesharim) means evenness, uprightness, a level standard for everyone. The nations' kings ruled for their own benefit. This King will judge fairly, without favoritism. That is why his reign is good news, not a threat, to those who long for justice.
  • Commentators: Henry calls these verses "instructions given to those who were to preach the gospel to the nations what to preach." He mentions the "from the tree" gloss, which "by degrees crept into the text," and observes that judging here "is put for ruling." JFB says that when the world's government is "ordered in justice" the nations "shall enjoy firm and lasting peace" (Ps 72:3, 7; Isa 9:6–7). Guzik notes that the LORD reigns whether the world acknowledges him or not, and quotes the old commentators on Justin Martyr's reading.

He Comes to Judge the Earth (vv11–13)

  • "Let the heavens be glad, and let the earth rejoice" (v11). Now creation itself is invited to join. Many readers have noticed that the first letters of the four Hebrew words in this line (yismechu hashamayim wetagel ha'arets) spell God's name, YHWH. Whether the poet intended it is impossible to know, but it fits the line well. Heavens and earth (Gen 1:1) together mean all things.
  • "Let the sea roar, and all that fills it" (v11). The roaring sea, which in Psalm 93:3–4 stood for chaos lifting its voice, now roars in praise.
  • "Let the field exult… Then shall all the trees of the forest sing for joy" (v12). Fields and forests are pictured as worshipers. This is poetry, but it is also hope. The ground was cursed because of human sin (Gen 3:17–18), and the prophets promised that when God set things right, "the trees of the field shall clap their hands" (Isa 55:12; compare Isa 35:1–2; 44:23). Paul says creation is "groaning" and waiting to be "set free from its bondage to corruption" (Rom 8:19–22).
  • "Before the LORD, for he comes, for he comes to judge the earth" (v13). The repetition is urgent: "he comes, he comes." The verb can be read "he has come" or "he is coming." The psalm's joy rests on an arrival. The King is not distant. He is on his way.
  • Why would judgment make creation glad? In the Old Testament, to "judge" often means to rule and to set things right, not only to condemn (Ps 72:1–4; Isa 11:3–5). For the oppressed and for a groaning creation, the coming of a just judge is the best possible news.
  • "He will judge the world in righteousness, and the peoples in his faithfulness" (v13). "Righteousness" means doing what is right. "Faithfulness" (emunah) means reliability, keeping his word. He will judge according to what is true and according to his promises. Paul used the first half of this line almost word for word in Athens: God "has fixed a day on which he will judge the world in righteousness by a man whom he has appointed," and the proof is that he raised him from the dead (Acts 17:31).
  • Commentators: JFB says "even inanimate nature (Ro 8:14-22) is poetically represented as capable of joining in the anthem of praise." Henry takes Christ's judging and ruling with righteousness and truth to mean "all the laws and ordinances of his kingdom shall be consonant to the rules and principles of eternal truth and equity," and that he rules "by the commanding power of truth." He notes the coming may include the last judgment, though he thinks it refers more immediately to Christ's first coming and the spread of the gospel. Guzik links the creation's joy to Romans 8:21, and sees the coming as the time when God's reign, real now, is openly established over the whole world.

Pointing to Christ

Psalm 96 is a psalm the church has never stopped singing, because it describes what the gospel does. The "good news" of God's salvation (v2) is now told among the nations (Matt 24:14), the families of the earth promised to Abraham are being blessed in his descendant (Gal 3:8, 16), and Gentiles are brought near and welcomed into God's house (Eph 2:13, 19). The King the psalm says is coming has come once, in humility, and will come again "to judge the living and the dead" (2 Tim 4:1). Paul named him in Athens as "a man whom he has appointed" to judge the world in righteousness (Acts 17:31). And the "new song" of v1 is the one sung in heaven to the Lamb who was slain: "by your blood you ransomed people for God from every tribe and language and people and nation" (Rev 5:9).

Reflection

  • Fresh Songs for a Faithful God (v.1) — The call is for a new song, not a recycled one, suggesting worship should keep pace with fresh experiences of God's goodness. Has your worship gone stale, or does it still reflect what God is currently doing in your life?
  • Declaring God's Glory Beyond Your Own Circle (v.3) — The psalm pushes worship outward, calling people to make God's glory known among the nations, not just privately enjoyed. Who in your life has never heard you talk about what God has done for you?
  • Everything Else Is Nothing by Comparison (v.5) — Every rival god is called worthless next to the Lord, who alone made the heavens. What competes for the worship that belongs to God in your life right now?
  • Worship That Trembles (v.9) — Worship here is pictured with holy reverence, not casual familiarity. Does your approach to God carry a sense of awe, or has it become too comfortable?
  • Creation Rejoices at God's Coming Judgment (v.13) — The psalm ends with the sea, fields, and trees celebrating because God is coming to judge the earth with justice. Do you view God's coming judgment as something to dread or as good news that sets things right?
