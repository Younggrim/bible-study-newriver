1When the LORD your God has cut off the nations whose land He is giving you, and when you have driven them out and settled in their cities and houses,

2then you are to set apart for yourselves three cities within the land that the LORD your God is giving you to possess.

3You are to build roads for yourselves and divide into three regions the land that the LORD your God is giving you as an inheritance, so that any manslayer can flee to these cities.

4Now this is the situation regarding the manslayer who flees to one of these cities to save his life, having killed his neighbor accidentally, without intending to harm him:

5If he goes into the forest with his neighbor to cut timber and swings his axe to chop down a tree, but the blade flies off the handle and strikes and kills his neighbor, he may flee to one of these cities to save his life.

6Otherwise, the avenger of blood might pursue the manslayer in a rage, overtake him if the distance is great, and strike him dead though he did not deserve to die, since he did not intend any harm.

7This is why I am commanding you to set apart for yourselves three cities.

8And if the LORD your God enlarges your territory, as He swore to your fathers, and gives you all the land He promised them,

9and if you carefully keep all these commandments I am giving you today, loving the LORD your God and walking in His ways at all times, then you are to add three more cities to these three.

10Thus innocent blood will not be shed in the land that the LORD your God is giving you as an inheritance, so that you will not be guilty of bloodshed.

11If, however, a man hates his neighbor and lies in wait, attacks him and kills him, and then flees to one of these cities,

12the elders of his city must send for him, bring him back, and hand him over to the avenger of blood to die.

13You must show him no pity. You are to purge from Israel the guilt of shedding innocent blood, that it may go well with you.

14You must not move your neighbor’s boundary marker, which was set up by your ancestors to mark the inheritance you shall receive in the land that the LORD your God is giving you to possess.

15A lone witness is not sufficient to establish any wrongdoing or sin against a man, regardless of what offense he may have committed. A matter must be established by the testimony of two or three witnesses.

16If a false witness testifies against someone, accusing him of a crime,

17both parties to the dispute must stand in the presence of the LORD, before the priests and judges who are in office at that time.

18The judges shall investigate thoroughly, and if the witness is proven to be a liar who has falsely accused his brother,

19you must do to him as he intended to do to his brother. So you must purge the evil from among you.

20Then the rest of the people will hear and be afraid, and they will never again do anything so evil among you.

21You must show no pity: life for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, and foot for foot.

1When the Lord your God destroys the nations whose land he is about to give you and you dispossess them and settle in their cities and houses,

3You shall build a roadway and divide into thirds the whole extent of your land that the Lord your God is providing as your inheritance; anyone who kills another person should flee to the closest of these cities.

Summary

Moses instructs Israel to set apart three cities of refuge once they possess the land, so that someone who kills another accidentally can flee there and be protected from revenge, and commands that three more be added later if the land's borders expand (vv. 1-10). He distinguishes this protection from the case of premeditated murder, where the elders must hand the killer over to be put to death without pity (vv. 11-13). Moses also forbids moving a neighbor's boundary marker and requires at least two or three witnesses to establish any charge, with the penalty a false witness intended for the accused applied instead to the false witness himself, so that others will hear and be afraid to do likewise (vv. 14-21).

Authorship & Background

Author: Moses. Deuteronomy is the fifth and final book of the Pentateuch, written at the end of the 40 years of wilderness wandering (approximately 1406 BC). Hebrew title: "Devarim" — "Words/Things." Greek title: "Deuteronomion" — "Second Law" (a repetition/expansion of the Law for the new generation). The book consists of Moses' farewell speeches to Israel on the plains of Moab, just before they enter the Promised Land. Moses will not enter with them — these are his final words to the nation he has led for 40 years.
Historical Context: Chapter 19 addresses the protection of life and the integrity of justice through three related laws: (1) cities of refuge for accidental manslayers (vv.1-13), (2) the prohibition against moving boundary markers (v.14), and (3) the law of witnesses and false testimony (vv.15-21). The cities of refuge were first established in Numbers 35:9-34 and are expanded here for life in the Promised Land. Three cities are to be set apart in the land west of the Jordan (in addition to the three already designated east of the Jordan — Deuteronomy 4:41-43), with provision for three more if God enlarges the territory (vv.8-9). These cities distinguished between intentional murder and accidental killing — a distinction fundamental to justice. The accidental killer could flee to a city of refuge and be safe from the "avenger of blood" (the victim's kinsman). The intentional murderer received no such protection (vv.11-13). The boundary marker law (v.14) protected property rights — moving a landmark was theft of inheritance. The witness laws (vv.15-21) reinforce the two-witness requirement (from 17:6) and add the crucial principle of lex talionis for false witnesses: a false accuser receives the punishment he sought for his victim (v.19). This is justice in its purest form — the punishment fits the crime exactly.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: roads and equal spacing commanded to the cities of refuge so that distance itself never determines a man's guilt, the same refuge explicitly denied to anyone who lies in wait out of hatred rather than kills by accident, a boundary stone protected by law as though moving it were theft of land itself, a rule that no one may be convicted of any offense on a single witness's word alone, and a false witness sentenced to suffer exactly what he intended for his victim rather than any lesser penalty
Cities of Refuge and the Roads to Them (vv.1-10): Three cities are to be set apart when the land is possessed, and the practical instruction attached is about infrastructure: thou shalt prepare thee a way, and divide the coasts of thy land into three parts. Roads and equal spacing, so that no killer is caught by distance. The case described is deliberately ordinary, two men in the wood and the head slippeth from the helve and killeth his neighbour, and the danger named is the avenger pursuing while his heart is hot and overtaking him because the way is long. The law is protecting a man who is not guilty from a man who is not wrong to be angry. Three more cities are promised if the borders widen, so that innocent blood be not shed in thy land.
No Refuge for Murderers (vv.11-13): "But if any man hate his neighbour, and lie in wait for him, and rise up against him, and smite him mortally" (v.11). Premeditated murder — hatred, ambush, lethal attack. If he flees to a city of refuge: "the elders of his city shall send and fetch him thence, and deliver him into the hand of the avenger of blood, that he may die" (v.12). The city of refuge does not protect murderers. "Thine eye shall not pity him" (v.13). "Thou shalt put away the guilt of innocent blood from Israel, that it may go well with thee" (v.13). Unpunished murder pollutes the land.
Boundary Markers (v.14): "Thou shalt not remove thy neighbour's landmark, which they of old time have set in thine inheritance" (v.14). Boundary stones marked property lines. Moving them was theft — stealing land from your neighbor by stealth. The inheritance was God-given; to move a landmark was to steal what God had assigned.
The Two-Witness Rule (v.15): "One witness shall not rise up against a man for any iniquity, or for any sin" (v.15). No conviction on a single testimony — for any offense. "At the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established" (v.15).
The Law of False Witnesses (vv.16-21): "If a false witness rise up against any man to testify against him that which is wrong" (v.16). Both parties appear "before the LORD, before the priests and the judges" (v.17). "The judges shall make diligent inquisition" (v.18) — thorough investigation. If the witness is false: "Then shall ye do unto him, as he had thought to have done unto his brother" (v.19). The false witness receives the punishment he sought for his victim. "So shalt thou put the evil away from among you" (v.19). Deterrence: "those which remain shall hear, and fear" (v.20). The principle of proportional justice: "life shall go for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot" (v.21).

Deep Dive

Setting: The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life, in the law section of his second sermon (chapters 12–26). Chapters 16–18 described Israel's leaders: judges, priests, king and prophet. Chapter 19 turns to the justice they were to keep, especially in the gravest matter of all, the taking of a human life. Moses had already set apart three cities of refuge east of the Jordan (4:41–43), and Numbers 35 gave the fuller law. Here he commands three more west of the Jordan once the land is taken. The chapter then forbids moving boundary stones and sets rules for witnesses, ending with the famous "eye for eye." Three things run through it all: protect the innocent, punish the guilty, and keep the land free of bloodguilt.

Laws Concerning Cities of Refuge (vv1–13)

  • "When the LORD your God cuts off the nations… and you dispossess them" (v1). The command looks ahead to settled life, when Israel would live in "their cities and in their houses." Laws for a nation on the move are becoming laws for a nation at home.
  • "You shall set apart three cities for yourselves" (v2). Joshua later named them: Kedesh in Galilee in the north, Shechem in the central hills, and Hebron in the hills of Judah in the south (Josh 20:7). Across the Jordan were Bezer, Ramoth-gilead and Golan (Josh 20:8). All six were Levite cities (Num 35:6), so the people sheltering there lived among those who taught God's law.
  • "You shall measure the distances and divide into three parts… so that any manslayer can flee to them" (v3). The Hebrew says "prepare the way" (takhin lekha ha-derekh). Roads to the cities had to be kept open. Later Jewish tradition, recorded in the Talmud, said the roads were kept wide and in good repair, with signs at every crossroads reading "Refuge, refuge." Whether or not that was always kept, the point of the law is plain: refuge had to be within reach of anyone in the land.
  • "If anyone kills his neighbor unintentionally without having hated him in the past" (v4). The test is the heart behind the act. "Unintentionally" is literally "without knowledge." "Hated" is a settled hostility, and "in the past" is literally "yesterday and the day before," an old Hebrew way of saying "previously."
  • "The head slips from the handle and strikes his neighbor so that he dies" (v5). Moses gives a vivid example: two men cutting wood, an iron axe-head flying loose. Iron axes had loose-fitting heads, which is why Elisha once had to make a lost one float (2 Kgs 6:5–6). No one planned it. No one deserved to die for it.
  • "Lest the avenger of blood in hot anger pursue the manslayer" (v6). The "avenger" is the go'el haddam, literally "the redeemer of blood." The go'el was the nearest male relative, the man who bought back family land, freed a relative sold into slavery, and married a brother's widow (Lev 25:25, 48–49; Ruth 3:12–13). Where a family member had been killed, he was the one who saw that blood was repaid (Num 35:19). The law did not abolish this old custom. It put a fence around it, so that grief and rage could not kill an innocent man. "Because the way is long": if the city was too far, the avenger would catch him. That is why the cities had to be spread through the land.
  • "Therefore I command you, You shall set apart three cities" (v7). The command is repeated for emphasis.
  • "If the LORD your God enlarges your territory… then you shall add three other cities" (vv8–9). If Israel obeyed and God gave them the full extent of the land promised to Abraham, as far as the Euphrates (Gen 15:18; Deut 11:24), they would need three more cities for the larger area. The Tyndale notes observe that this never happened, because the condition was never met. "By loving the LORD your God and by walking ever in his ways" (v9) is the condition, and it is the language of the Shema (6:5).
  • "Lest innocent blood be shed in your land… and so the guilt of bloodshed be upon you" (v10). If an innocent man was killed because the nation had failed to provide refuge, the whole nation shared the guilt. Bloodshed "pollutes the land" (Num 35:33–34). Justice in Israel was not only between the killer and the family. The land and the people stood before God.
  • "But if anyone hates his neighbor and lies in wait for him" (v11). A murderer could not use the city to escape. The Tyndale notes say "hates" points to long-standing hostility, the kind of evidence that showed intent. "The elders of his city shall send and take him from there" (v12): his own town's elders, not the avenger acting alone, had to fetch him and hand him over. Numbers 35:24–25 says the congregation judged the case first. The refuge was a place to wait for a fair trial, not a hiding place. Exodus 21:14 says even God's altar would not shelter a deliberate murderer, and Solomon had Joab killed while he clung to it (1 Kgs 2:28–34).
  • "Your eye shall not pity him" (v13). Mercy that lets a murderer go free is cruelty to the victim and danger to the community. Murder is especially serious because people are made in God's image (Gen 9:6). "Purge the guilt of innocent blood from Israel" uses the verb ba'ar, literally "burn out," the same verb as in 13:5 and 17:7. "So that it may be well with you": the land's blessing depended on justice.
  • How long did the manslayer stay? Numbers 35:25–28 adds that he had to remain in the city until the death of the high priest, and then could go home. If he left early, the avenger could kill him. The city was both protection and a kind of confinement, a reminder that a life had been lost even by accident.
  • Commentators: All three see the cities as a picture of Christ. Henry: "In Christ, the Lord our Righteousness, refuge is provided for those who by faith flee unto him," but "there is no refuge in Jesus Christ for presumptuous sinners, who go on still in their trespasses." Guzik lists likenesses: the refuge was within reach, open to all, had to be lived in and not merely visited, and offered full freedom only after the high priest's death. He also names the great difference: the old cities protected only the innocent, but Christ shelters the guilty who flee to him. JFB calls the avenger custom "goelism," which Moses did not abolish but regulated, and records the tradition about the wide roads and signposts.

Property Boundaries (v14)

  • "You shall not move your neighbor's landmark, which the men of old have set" (v14). Fields were marked by a row of stones or a single upright stone, easy to shift a few feet in the night. Moving one was theft of land, and land was a family's living and its share in God's gift. The curse in 27:17 repeats it: "Cursed be anyone who moves his neighbor's landmark." Proverbs adds a warning about the helpless: "Do not move an ancient landmark or enter the fields of the fatherless, for their Redeemer is strong" (Prov 23:10–11; compare 22:28). Hosea compared corrupt leaders to men who move landmarks (Hos 5:10). Boundary stones were sacred in other ancient cultures too, and Babylonian boundary stones often carried curses on anyone who moved them.
  • "In the inheritance that you will hold" (v14). Each family's land was an inheritance from God, assigned by lot (Num 26:52–56). That is why Naboth would not sell his vineyard even to the king: "The LORD forbid that I should give you the inheritance of my fathers" (1 Kgs 21:3).
  • Why is this verse here? It sits between laws about life and laws about testimony, and all three protect a neighbor: his life, his land and his good name. These match the sixth, eighth and ninth commandments.
  • Commentators: Henry calls it "a moral precept, and still binding," and applies it simply: "Let every man be content with his own lot, and be just to his neighbours in all things." JFB describes how fields in Palestine were often marked only by a small trench or a single stone, easily moved by a dishonest neighbor. Guzik also draws a careful secondary application: long-held, faithful ways of doing things should not be pulled up lightly, though tradition is not to be defended just because it is old.

Laws Concerning Witnesses (vv15–21)

  • "A single witness shall not suffice… Only on the evidence of two witnesses or of three witnesses" (v15). Chapter 17:6 set this rule for death sentences. Here it covers "any crime or… any wrong." One person's word against another's could not convict. Jesus applied it to church discipline (Matt 18:16), Paul to his own dealings with the Corinthians (2 Cor 13:1) and to charges against elders (1 Tim 5:19), and Jesus appealed to it for his own claims, with the Father as his second witness (John 8:17–18).
  • "If a malicious witness arises" (v16). Literally "a witness of violence" ('ed chamas). The word chamas is the violence that filled the earth before the flood (Gen 6:11). A lying witness is doing violence with his tongue. Exodus 23:1 uses the same phrase.
  • "Both parties to the dispute shall appear before the LORD, before the priests and the judges" (v17). A suspected false witness was taken to the central court (17:8–9). Standing "before the LORD" reminded everyone that God himself was watching the case.
  • "The judges shall inquire diligently" (v18). The same careful investigation as in 13:14 and 17:4. Accusations had to be tested.
  • "You shall do to him as he had meant to do to his brother" (v19). The false witness received the penalty he tried to bring on the accused. If he lied to get a man executed, he was executed. This made perjury terrifying. The principle shows up in Scripture's stories: Haman was hanged on the gallows he built for Mordecai (Esth 7:10), and Daniel's accusers were thrown to the lions (Dan 6:24). The book of Susanna, an addition to Daniel in the Greek Bible, tells of two elders who were put to death under this very law when Daniel exposed their false testimony.
  • "The rest shall hear and fear" (v20). Public justice taught the whole community.
  • "Life for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth" (v21). This is the lex talionis, the "law of like for like," also in Exodus 21:23–25 and Leviticus 24:19–20. It sounds harsh, but it was a limit, not a license. The punishment had to fit the crime and could not exceed it. It ruled out blood feuds that killed a whole family for one man's injury, and it ruled out lighter penalties for the rich. Similar laws appear in the Code of Hammurabi, though there the penalty could vary by social class. In Israel the rule was applied by judges, never by private revenge. Many Jewish interpreters held that, apart from murder, it was applied as a fixed payment of equal value, and Exodus 21:26–27 already lets a slave go free for an eye or a tooth, so the law itself shows some flexibility.
  • What Jesus did with it. In the Sermon on the Mount Jesus quoted "an eye for an eye" and said, "Do not resist the one who is evil" (Matt 5:38–39). He was not abolishing justice in courts. Paul still says the governing authority "does not bear the sword in vain" (Rom 13:4). Jesus was forbidding his followers from using the courtroom rule to justify personal revenge. Paul says the same: "Never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God" (Rom 12:19).
  • Commentators: Henry: "Sentence should never be passed upon the testimony of one witness alone," and "A false witness should suffer the same punishment which he sought to have inflicted upon the person he accused." JFB says the rules for testimony rest on "natural justice." Guzik notes that physical evidence can count as a second witness, points out that the false witnesses at Jesus' trial (Matt 26:59–61) would themselves have deserved death under this law, and stresses that "eye for eye" limited vengeance. The Tyndale notes agree, and add that vengeance belongs to God, not to individuals.

Pointing to Christ

The writer of Hebrews uses the language of this chapter for every believer: "we who have fled for refuge" to the hope set before us (Heb 6:18). Christ is the city within reach, open to anyone who runs to him. But there is a difference. The old cities sheltered only the man who had not hated his neighbor. Christ shelters the guilty, people who did plan their sins. He can do so because he was condemned on false testimony that was never punished, and he took the penalty the guilty deserved. And the manslayer went free when the high priest died. Our High Priest has died once for all, and those who flee to him are free indeed, with no avenger who can touch them (Rom 8:1, 33–34).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik notes that the cities of refuge are a picture of Christ — the sinner flees to Him for protection from the just penalty of the law. He emphasizes that the roads to the cities had to be maintained and clearly marked — access to refuge must be easy. He highlights the false witness law as one of the most just principles in Scripture: you receive what you intended for another.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • God Distinguishes Intent From Accident (vv.4-6) — The cities of refuge exist because God separates a heart that hated from a genuine accident, even when the outcome looks the same. When you evaluate someone's mistake, do you take the time to consider their intent, or do you judge purely by the result?
  • No Shelter for Premeditated Evil (vv.11-13) — A refuge city offered no protection to someone who hated, ambushed, and killed with intent. Are there areas of your life where you hope for grace to cover something you actually planned rather than merely stumbled into?
  • Stealing by Stealth Is Still Stealing (v.14) — Moving a boundary marker was a quiet, gradual way to steal a neighbor's inheritance without an obvious confrontation. Is there a slow encroachment in your own dealings — with money, credit, or reputation — that you'd never call theft outright but functions the same way?
  • Corroboration Before Conviction (v.15) — No one could be condemned for any offense on the word of a single witness; the matter needed two or three. How quick are you to believe and repeat an accusation about someone based on just one source?
  • The Weight of a False Accusation (v.19) — A false witness was to receive the very punishment he intended for his victim. Before making a serious accusation against someone, are you willing to bear the consequences yourself if you turn out to be wrong?
