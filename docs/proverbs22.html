Summary

This chapter closes the first Solomonic collection (vv. 1-16) and opens the "words of the wise" (vv. 17-29), moving through a range of practical sayings. A good name is valued above great riches (v. 1), and children raised in the right path are promised they will not depart from it later in life (v. 6). The proverbs also observe that the rich rule over the poor and the borrower is servant to the lender (v. 7), while repeatedly warning against exploiting the poor or the fatherless, since the Lord himself will take up their case (vv. 16, 22-23). The chapter ends by promising that a person skilled in his work will stand before kings rather than obscure men (v. 29).

Authorship & Background

Author: Primarily Solomon (1:1, 10:1, 25:1), with sections by Agur (ch.30) and King Lemuel (ch.31). Compiled over several centuries. Proverbs is wisdom literature — practical instruction for godly living. Hebrew title: 'Mishlei' — 'Proverbs/Comparisons.'
Historical Context: Chapter 22 is transitional — vv.1-16 conclude the first Solomonic collection (10:1-22:16), while vv.17-29 begin the "Words of the Wise" section (22:17-24:34). This section has notable parallels to the Egyptian "Instruction of Amenemope," suggesting shared ancient Near Eastern wisdom traditions. Key proverbs include "train up a child" (v.6), "a good name" (v.1), "the rich ruleth over the poor" (v.7), and "the diligent shall stand before kings" (v.29).
Themes in This Chapter: Reputation and Character (v.1); Training Children (v.6); Wealth, Debt, and Servitude (vv.2, 7); Protection of the Poor (vv.16, 22-23); Diligence Brings Promotion (v.29)
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: the final sixteen verses of the first great Solomonic collection weighted heavily toward money and class, the rich and poor meet together, the LORD is the maker of them all, a sentence that has described debt ever since it was written, the borrower is servant to the lender, the single most quoted and most argued verse in the whole book, train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old, he will not depart from it, and a clean break at verse seventeen into an entirely new collection, the words of the wise, whose form and content closely track an ancient Egyptian instruction text centuries older than Solomon himself
The Last of the Solomonic Couplets (vv.1-16): These sixteen verses end the collection that began at chapter 10, and they are weighted toward money and class. The rich and poor meet together, the LORD is the maker of them all, and then the sentence that has described debt ever since, the rich ruleth over the poor, and the borrower is servant to the lender. The block contains the most quoted and most argued verse in the book, train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old, he will not depart from it, whose Hebrew is terse enough that it has been read both as a promise and as a plain observation about habit. It closes on oppression of the poor for private gain.
The Words of the Wise Begin (vv.17-29): Verse 17 starts a new collection with a new voice, bow down thine ear, and hear the words of the wise, and the form changes with it, from single couplets back to short addressed instructions of two and three verses each. Verse 20 says the writer has already written excellent things, a phrase whose Hebrew some read as thirty sayings, and scholars have long noted how closely this section through 24:22 tracks the Egyptian Instruction of Amenemope, which is arranged in thirty chapters and dates several centuries earlier. The block warns against robbing the poor because the LORD will plead their cause, against friendship with an angry man, against standing surety, and against removing the ancient landmark, and it ends on competence as a way into the room, a man diligent in his business shall stand before kings.

Setting: Proverbs 22 is a hinge. Verses 1–16 are the last sayings of the long collection headed "The proverbs of Solomon" (10:1–22:16; see Proverbs 10 on how to read a proverb). The ESV has no headings inside that collection, so the first headings below simply group the sayings. At v17 a new collection begins, "the words of the wise" (22:17–24:22), with its own introduction and a different style: sayings that run two or three verses and give direct commands ("Do not…"). That section mentions "thirty sayings" (v20), and it closely resembles an Egyptian teaching text called the Instruction of Amenemope, also in thirty chapters, which most scholars date before Solomon. This chapter holds two of the best-known verses in the book: "A good name is to be chosen rather than great riches" (v1) and "Train up a child in the way he should go" (v6).

A Good Name, Rich and Poor (vv1–5)

  • "A good name is to be chosen rather than great riches, and favor is better than silver or gold" (v1). "Name" here means reputation, what people know you to be. "Favor" is goodwill, being well thought of. Money can be lost and rebuilt. A ruined reputation is very hard to restore. Ecclesiastes: "A good name is better than precious ointment" (Eccl 7:1). The Tyndale notes: a wise person won't risk reputation for money. Guzik passes on the reminder that the deepest joy is not a good name among people but having one's name "written in heaven" (Luke 10:20). Henry: be more concerned to gain and keep a good name than to grow rich.
  • "The rich and the poor meet together; the LORD is the Maker of them all" (v2). "Meet together" may mean they live side by side in the same town, or that they stand on equal footing before God. Both are true. The one thing they share outweighs everything that separates them: one Maker. The Tyndale notes: remembering this stops exploitation of the poor. Compare 14:31 and 29:13. Job asked, "Did not he who made me in the womb make him?" (Job 31:15). Henry: the poor are as welcome at God's throne as the rich.
  • "The prudent sees danger and hides himself, but the simple go on and suffer for it" (v3). Repeated word for word in 27:12. The wise person sees trouble coming and takes cover. The simple walk straight into it. Wisdom is not always about fighting. Sometimes it is about getting out of the way, as Joseph ran from Potiphar's wife (Gen 39:12). Henry: the prudent "looks to Jesus Christ as the sure refuge from the storm."
  • "The reward for humility and fear of the LORD is riches and honor and life" (v4). The two belong together. JFB: they are "in apposition," one producing the other. Guzik: humility is a right view of oneself, and the fear of the LORD a right view of God. The rewards are what wisdom promised in 3:16 and 8:18. As always in Proverbs, this is the general pattern, not a promise of wealth to every believer. Henry: "Where the fear of God is, there will be humility."
  • "Thorns and snares are in the way of the crooked; whoever guards his soul will keep far from them" (v5). The twisted path is full of thorns that tear and traps that catch. Guarding one's life means keeping off that path altogether. Guzik makes the practical point: the way to face fewer temptations is often to change the road one travels. Henry: "The way of sin is vexatious and dangerous."
  • Commentators: JFB on v1 supplies "good" before "name" from Ecclesiastes 7:1 (the Hebrew has just "a name"). Guzik and the Tyndale notes both stress on v4 that humility and the fear of God are two sides of one thing.

Training, Debt and the Generous Eye (vv6–12)

  • "Train up a child in the way he should go; even when he is old he will not depart from it" (v6). "Train up" is chanak, a verb used elsewhere for dedicating a house (Deut 20:5) or the temple (1 Kgs 8:63). The holiday name Hanukkah, "dedication," comes from it. The idea is starting a child off, setting him apart for a purpose. "In the way he should go" is literally "according to his way," and it has been read in three ways. (1) In the right way, the way of wisdom, which JFB, Henry and the Tyndale notes take. (2) In a way suited to the child, his age and nature, which Guzik also sees, following Kidner. (3) A few scholars read it as a warning: let a child have his own way, and he will never break the habit. The first is most widely held. Like all proverbs, this describes how things usually go, not an ironclad guarantee. Proverbs knows children make their own choices (Ezek 18:20), and Solomon himself wandered (1 Kgs 11:4). Guzik offers it as hope for parents of wayward adult children. JFB: "early training secures habitual walking in it." Henry urges parents to bring children early to know the Savior.
  • "The rich rules over the poor, and the borrower is the slave of the lender" (v7). Observation, not approval. In Israel a man unable to repay could sell himself or his children into service (Exod 21:2–7; 2 Kgs 4:1; Neh 5:1–5). Debt really did mean slavery. The Tyndale notes take it as a warning against borrowing. Paul: "Owe no one anything, except to love each other" (Rom 13:8).
  • "Whoever sows injustice will reap calamity, and the rod of his fury will fail" (v8). Sowing and reaping (Job 4:8; Hos 8:7; Gal 6:7). "The rod of his fury" is the power he used to hurt others, and it will break. JFB: the wrongdoer's power to do evil will be destroyed. Henry: "The power which many abuse, will soon fail them."
  • "Whoever has a bountiful eye will be blessed, for he shares his bread with the poor" (v9). Literally "good of eye," the opposite of the "evil eye" of the stingy (23:6; 28:22). Jesus used the same idiom: "if your eye is healthy" (generous), "your whole body will be full of light" (Matt 6:22–23). JFB: a generous disposition, proven by giving. Guzik passes on the note that "his bread" may mean food he himself goes without.
  • "Drive out a scoffer, and strife will go out, and quarreling and abuse will cease" (v10). Some conflicts have a source, and removing the source ends them. Abraham sent away Ishmael after his mocking (Gen 21:9–10, where "laughing" can mean mocking). Paul told churches to have nothing more to do with a divisive person after two warnings (Titus 3:10). Henry: "Profane scoffers and revilers disturb the peace."
  • "He who loves purity of heart, and whose speech is gracious, will have the king as his friend" (v11). Integrity inside, grace on the lips. Good rulers value such people (16:13). The Tyndale notes: even a bad ruler wants loyal advisers. Daniel served pagan kings this way (Dan 6:3). Henry lifts it higher: God himself is "the Friend of a man in whose spirit there is no guile." Jesus: "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God" (Matt 5:8).
  • "The eyes of the LORD keep watch over knowledge, but he overthrows the words of the traitor" (v12). God guards the truth and those who hold it, and he overturns deceit. Henry: God turns treacherous plans "to their own confusion."
  • Commentators: Guzik gives v6 his longest treatment, noting the "dedicate" sense of chanak from Clarke, and insisting it is a principle, not a guarantee. JFB and Henry read "the way he should go" as the moral path. All agree that early training matters deeply.

The Sluggard's Lion and the Deep Pit (vv13–16)

  • "The sluggard says, 'There is a lion outside! I shall be killed in the streets!'" (v13). Lions did live in Israel then (Judg 14:5; 1 Sam 17:34), but not in town streets. The excuse is absurd, and that's the joke. 26:13 repeats it. The Tyndale notes call it the funniest of the laziness proverbs. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's line that laziness is a great maker of lions. Henry notes the real danger the sluggard ignores: "his own slothfulness, which kills him."
  • "The mouth of forbidden women is a deep pit; he with whom the LORD is angry will fall into it" (v14). "Forbidden women" (zarot, "strange" women) is the adulteress of chapters 2, 5 and 7, whose "lips drip honey" (5:3). Her words are a pit dug to trap animals. The second line is sobering: falling into it can be God's judgment on someone who has already turned from him, as Romans 1:24 says God "gave them up… to impurity." JFB: God turns their own sin into their punishment. The sluggard's lion is imaginary, Guzik notes; this danger is real.
  • "Folly is bound up in the heart of a child, but the rod of discipline drives it far from him" (v15). "Bound up" means tied fast. Children are not born neutral. Folly comes naturally, and wisdom has to be taught. David: "I was brought forth in iniquity" (Ps 51:5). The rod was the standard tool of correction in the ancient world. Proverbs' point is that loving correction must actually happen (13:24; 23:13–14), and the New Testament adds that it must not provoke or discourage the child (Eph 6:4; Col 3:21). Faithful Christians today differ on whether physical discipline is wise, but all agree on the proverb's main claim: children need loving, firm correction. Henry: "We all need to be corrected by our heavenly Father" (Heb 12:5–11).
  • "Whoever oppresses the poor to increase his own wealth, or gives to the rich, will only come to poverty" (v16). Two ways of trying to get ahead: squeezing those below and buttering up those above. Both backfire. JFB: both come from selfishness. Henry: "We are but stewards." The Tyndale notes contrast this with the honest skill of v29. This verse closes Solomon's long collection.
  • Commentators: Guzik and the Tyndale notes agree that v15 is about moral folly, not childish immaturity. Guzik passes on Bridges' line separating foolishness from mere childishness. JFB reads the rod as both deterring and reforming.

Words of the Wise (vv17–21)

  • "Incline your ear, and hear the words of the wise" (v17). A new heading in the text, and a new voice: "my knowledge." The style goes back to the father's appeals of chapters 1–9: ear, heart, lips. Who "the wise" were isn't stated. They may have been royal scribes or teachers at the court who gathered sayings, some of them adapted from Egyptian teaching. 24:23 adds "These also are sayings of the wise." JFB: a new division begins here, with sayings usually two verses long.
  • "It will be pleasant if you keep them within you, if all of them are ready on your lips" (v18). "Within you" is literally "in your belly," stored deep inside. Wisdom is meant to be memorized and ready for use. Psalm 119:11: "I have stored up your word in my heart." JFB: lessons should be "fixed" on the lips.
  • "That your trust may be in the LORD, I have made them known to you today, even to you" (v19). This is the purpose of the whole collection, and it marks the difference from Amenemope. The Egyptian text borrowed from is practical advice. Here practical advice exists to build trust in the LORD. "Today, even to you" makes it personal and urgent. The Tyndale notes: this wisdom aims to inspire trust in the Lord.
  • "Have I not written for you thirty sayings of counsel and knowledge" (v20). The Hebrew word here is spelled oddly (shilshom), and the scribes who preserved the text marked it to be read differently. It can mean "formerly" ("day before yesterday"), "officers" or "excellent things," which the King James has, and JFB considers "of former times." Since Amenemope was published in the 1920s, with its thirty chapters, most scholars read "thirty," and the ESV and most modern versions follow. The Tyndale notes say the early translations also read thirty. Counting the sayings from 22:22 to 24:22 gives roughly thirty, though scholars count them a little differently.
  • "To make you know what is right and true, that you may give a true answer to those who sent you" (v21). The student is pictured as a messenger or official who must report back accurately. Wisdom makes a person reliable. JFB: the teacher wants accuracy so the pupil can pass it on. Peter: "always being prepared to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you" (1 Pet 3:15).
  • Commentators: Guzik and the Tyndale notes both mention Amenemope and the thirty sayings. Guzik passes on Waltke's view that the collection borrows Amenemope's structure but not its theology. JFB, writing before Amenemope was found, reads "excellent things." Henry: the way to be sure of the truth is to take one's duty seriously.

The First Sayings: The Poor, the Angry and the Landmark (vv22–29)

  • "Do not rob the poor, because he is poor, or crush the afflicted at the gate" (v22). Saying one. "Because he is poor" means because he can't fight back. The city gate was the courtroom where elders sat to judge (Ruth 4:1–11). One of Amenemope's opening sayings is strikingly similar: "Beware of robbing a wretch." The Tyndale notes: God protects the weak.
  • "For the LORD will plead their cause and rob of life those who rob them" (v23). The poor have a lawyer after all: God himself. "Plead their cause" is court language, and the punishment matches the crime: robbers robbed. This line has no parallel in the Egyptian text. It is Israel's God who defends the poor (Ps 140:12). Henry: "if men will not appear for them, God will." Compare 23:10–11.
  • "Make no friendship with a man given to anger, nor go with a wrathful man, lest you learn his ways and entangle yourself in a snare" (vv24–25). Saying two. Temper is contagious. Spend time with a hothead and you learn to explode too. Paul: "Bad company ruins good morals" (1 Cor 15:33). Henry: "Our corrupt hearts have so much tinder in them." Guzik passes on Bridges' picture of living with a hot-tempered man as living in a burning house.
  • "Be not one of those who give pledges, who put up security for debts. If you have nothing with which to pay, why should your bed be taken from under you?" (vv26–27). Saying three. The cosigning warning again (6:1–5; 17:18). If you can't pay, the creditor takes even the bed you sleep on. The law protected a poor man's cloak overnight (Exod 22:26–27), but a guarantor could lose everything. Henry: every man ought to be "just to himself, and his family."
  • "Do not move the ancient landmark that your fathers have set" (v28). Saying four. Boundary stones marked family land, which in Israel was a gift from God divided by lot (Josh 18–19). Moving one secretly stole a neighbor's inheritance, and the law cursed anyone who did it (Deut 19:14; 27:17). Naboth refused to sell his vineyard for this reason (1 Kgs 21:3). 23:10 adds "or enter the fields of the fatherless." Guzik passes on the point that moving a stone a little each year could steal a field over time. Many have applied the verse to the faith handed down, not to be shifted lightly (Jude 3), and Guzik is careful that this cuts both ways: neither discard old truth lightly nor defend mere habit as if it were truth.
  • "Do you see a man skillful in his work? He will stand before kings; he will not stand before obscure men" (v29). Saying five. "Skillful" (mahir) means quick and expert, the word used of Ezra, "a scribe skilled in the Law" (Ezra 7:6). Excellent work opens doors to the highest places. Joseph (Gen 41:46), Bezalel (Exod 31:1–5) and Daniel (Dan 1:19–20) all stood before kings. Paul: "Whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord" (Col 3:23). The Tyndale notes: improve your skills and you'll get the best work.
  • Commentators: JFB groups the sayings in pairs and says of v28, "Do not entrench on others." Guzik on v29 points to Jesus' parables where faithfulness in small things leads to greater responsibility (Matt 25:21). Henry and the Tyndale notes both see God as the defender of the poor in vv22–23.

Pointing to Christ

Proverbs 22 says "the rich and the poor meet together; the LORD is the Maker of them all" (v2). They meet most fully at the cross, where the rich and the poor alike stand as sinners and are welcomed on the same terms (Gal 3:28). Jesus is the one who "though he was rich… became poor" (2 Cor 8:9), the LORD who pleads the cause of the poor (v23; Luke 4:18), and the King who befriends those of pure heart and gracious speech (v11), though he went further and became the friend of sinners (Luke 7:34). He is the true Wise One whose words, kept within us and ready on our lips (v18), lead us to trust in the LORD (v19). Debt makes the borrower a slave (v7), and every person owes a debt to God that cannot be paid. Christ took that debt, "canceling the record of debt that stood against us… nailing it to the cross" (Col 2:14). And he earned the name above every name (v1; Phil 2:9) by making himself of no reputation.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes v.6 with nuance: this is a general principle, not an absolute guarantee. Many godly parents have wayward children — the proverb states the norm, not the exception. On v.1, he notes: "A good name takes decades to build and seconds to lose. Guard it above your bank account." On v.29, Guzik states that excellence, not self-promotion, is the path to recognition.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.

Reflection

  • A Good Name Outweighs Wealth (v.1) — Reputation and character are valued above riches. What decisions are you making that protect your integrity even when they cost you financially?
  • Training a Child Shapes a Lifetime (v.6) — Consistent, intentional guidance in a child's early years sets a direction they're likely to keep. What example and habits are you passing on right now?
  • Debt Creates Servitude (v.7) — Borrowing puts a person under the lender's control, trading freedom for convenience. Where is debt costing you more freedom than you realize?
  • God Defends the Poor (vv.22-23) — Taking advantage of the vulnerable invites the LORD Himself to take up their case against you. How are you actively caring for those with less power than you?
  • Diligence Opens Doors (v.29) — Skillful, consistent work earns recognition without needing self-promotion. Is your work quality doing the talking, or are you relying on image instead?
