1Now the LORD said to Samuel, “How long are you going to mourn for Saul, since I have rejected him as king over Israel? Fill your horn with oil and go. I am sending you to Jesse of Bethlehem, for I have selected from his sons a king for Myself.”

2“How can I go?” Samuel asked. “Saul will hear of it and kill me!” The LORD answered, “Take a heifer with you and say, ‘I have come to sacrifice to the LORD.’

3Then invite Jesse to the sacrifice, and I will show you what you are to do. You are to anoint for Me the one I indicate.”

4So Samuel did what the LORD had said and went to Bethlehem. When the elders of the town met him, they trembled and asked, “Do you come in peace?”

5“In peace,” he replied. “I have come to sacrifice to the LORD. Consecrate yourselves and come with me to the sacrifice.” Then he consecrated Jesse and his sons and invited them to the sacrifice.

6When they arrived, Samuel saw Eliab and said, “Surely here before the LORD is His anointed.”

7But the LORD said to Samuel, “Do not consider his appearance or height, for I have rejected him; the LORD does not see as man does. For man sees the outward appearance, but the LORD sees the heart.”

8Then Jesse called Abinadab and presented him to Samuel, who said, “The LORD has not chosen this one either.”

9Next Jesse presented Shammah, but Samuel said, “The LORD has not chosen this one either.”

10Thus Jesse made seven of his sons pass before Samuel, but Samuel told him, “The LORD has not chosen any of these.”

11And Samuel asked him, “Are these all the sons you have?” “There is still the youngest,” Jesse replied, “but he is tending the sheep.” “Send for him,” Samuel replied. “For we will not sit down to eat until he arrives.”

12So Jesse sent for his youngest son and brought him in. He was ruddy, with beautiful eyes and a handsome appearance. And the LORD said, “Rise and anoint him, for he is the one.”

13So Samuel took the horn of oil and anointed him in the presence of his brothers, and the Spirit of the LORD rushed upon David from that day forward. Then Samuel set out and went to Ramah.

14Now the Spirit of the LORD departed from Saul, and a spirit of distress from the LORD began to torment him.

15Saul’s servants said to him, “Surely a spirit of distress from God is tormenting you.

16Let our lord command your servants here to seek out someone who can skillfully play the harp. Whenever the spirit of distress from God is upon you, he is to play it, and you will be well.”

17And Saul commanded his servants, “Find me someone who plays well, and bring him to me.”

18One of the servants answered, “I have seen a son of Jesse of Bethlehem who knows how to play the harp. He is a mighty man of valor, a warrior, eloquent and handsome, and the LORD is with him.”

19So Saul sent messengers to Jesse and said, “Send me your son David, who is with the sheep.”

20And Jesse took a donkey loaded with bread, a skin of wine, and one young goat and sent them to Saul with his son David.

21When David came to Saul and entered his service, Saul loved him very much, and David became his armor-bearer.

22Then Saul sent word to Jesse, saying, “Let David remain in my service, for I am pleased with him.”

23And whenever the spirit from God came upon Saul, David would pick up his harp and play. Then Saul would find relief and feel better, and the spirit of distress would depart from him.

Summary

The LORD tells Samuel to stop mourning for Saul and sends him to Bethlehem to anoint a new king from among the sons of Jesse, using a sacrifice as cover so Saul will not hear of it (vv. 1-5). As Jesse's sons pass before him, Samuel is drawn to Eliab's appearance, but the LORD corrects him: God does not look at outward appearance as man does, but looks at the heart (vv. 6-7). Seven sons are rejected before Jesse sends for the youngest, David, who is out tending sheep; when he arrives, the LORD tells Samuel this is the one, and Samuel anoints him in the presence of his brothers as the Spirit of the LORD comes upon him from that day forward (vv. 8-13). Meanwhile the Spirit departs from Saul and an evil spirit troubles him instead, so his servants bring in David - described as skilled, brave, and favored by the LORD - to soothe Saul by playing the harp, and David becomes his armor-bearer (vv. 14-23).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Samuel (for the early chapters), Nathan, and Gad (1 Chronicles 29:29). Originally one book with 2 Samuel in the Hebrew Bible. Written approximately 1050-950 BC. The book covers the transition from the judges to the monarchy — from the birth of Samuel through the death of Saul (approximately 1105-1010 BC).
Historical Context: Chapter 16 is the great turning point of 1 Samuel — the anointing of David. God sends Samuel to Bethlehem, to the house of Jesse, to anoint a new king. The chapter contains the book's defining theological statement: "man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart" (v.7). This principle explains everything that has come before (Saul's selection based on appearance) and everything that follows (David's selection based on heart). David is the youngest son, a shepherd, overlooked by his own family — not even brought to the sacrifice until Samuel insists. Yet God says "this is he" (v.12). The Spirit of the LORD comes upon David "from that day forward" (v.13) — a permanent empowerment, unlike Saul's intermittent experiences. Simultaneously, "the Spirit of the LORD departed from Saul, and an evil spirit from the LORD troubled him" (v.14). The transfer of divine favor is complete: what Saul loses, David receives. The chapter then brings David into Saul's court through providence — Saul needs a musician to soothe his tormented spirit, and David is recommended as "cunning in playing, and a mighty valiant man, and a man of war, and prudent in matters, and a comely person, and the LORD is with him" (v.18). The irony is profound: Saul unknowingly brings his own replacement into his household. David serves Saul, plays the harp, and drives away the evil spirit. The man after God's own heart now stands in the presence of the man God has rejected — and Saul "loved him greatly" (v.21), not knowing that this shepherd boy will take his throne.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: Grief rebuked as something that must not paralyze obedience any longer, man looking on the outward appearance while the LORD looks on the heart stated as the chapter's own thesis, seven older sons passed over before the youngest is even sent for from the sheep, a father who did not think to bring his own youngest son to the sacrifice, and the Spirit departing from Saul in the same verse an evil spirit begins to trouble him
Samuel Sent to Bethlehem (vv.1-5): God rebukes Samuel's prolonged grief: "How long wilt thou mourn for Saul, seeing I have rejected him?" (v.1). Grief has its place, but it must not paralyze obedience. God's command: "fill thine horn with oil, and go." The horn (not a vial, as with Saul — 10:1) suggests permanence and abundance. Samuel fears Saul's reaction: "If Saul hear it, he will kill me" (v.2). God provides a legitimate cover: bring a heifer for sacrifice. This is not deception — Samuel genuinely sacrifices — but it is discretion. God does not require His servants to be foolishly transparent with dangerous men. The elders of Bethlehem tremble at Samuel's arrival (v.4) — a prophet's unexpected visit could mean judgment. Samuel reassures them and consecrates Jesse's family for the sacrifice.
God's Choice: Not by Appearance (vv.6-10): When Jesse's sons arrive, Samuel sees Eliab — the eldest, presumably tall and impressive — and thinks "Surely the LORD's anointed is before him" (v.6). Even Samuel, the prophet, defaults to judging by appearance. God's correction is immediate and definitive: "Look not on his countenance, or on the height of his stature; because I have refused him: for the LORD seeth not as man seeth; for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart" (v.7). This is the book's thesis statement. Saul was chosen by appearance (9:2); David is chosen by heart. Seven sons pass before Samuel — all rejected. God's choice is not among the obvious candidates.
David Anointed (vv.11-13): Samuel asks: "Are here all thy children?" (v.11). Jesse's answer reveals David's status in the family: "There remaineth yet the youngest, and, behold, he keepeth the sheep." David is so insignificant that his father did not bother to bring him. He is the youngest (ha-qatan), the shepherd — the one left with the menial task while his brothers attend the important event. Samuel insists: "we will not sit down till he come hither" (v.11). David arrives — "ruddy, and withal of a beautiful countenance, and goodly to look to" (v.12). He is attractive, but that is not why God chose him. God says: "Arise, anoint him: for this is he" (v.12). Samuel anoints David "in the midst of his brethren" (v.13) — publicly within the family, though the full significance may not be immediately understood. "The Spirit of the LORD came upon David from that day forward" (v.13) — permanent, ongoing empowerment. Unlike Saul's intermittent Spirit-experiences, David's anointing brings lasting divine presence.
The Spirit Departs from Saul (vv.14-23): The transfer is stark: "the Spirit of the LORD departed from Saul, and an evil spirit from the LORD troubled him" (v.14). The "evil spirit from the LORD" is a spirit of torment permitted (or sent) by God as judgment. It produces anxiety, fear, rage, and instability — the symptoms that will characterize Saul's remaining years. His servants suggest music therapy (v.16), and one recommends "a son of Jesse the Bethlehemite" (v.18). The description of David is remarkable: skilled musician, mighty warrior, man of war, prudent in speech, handsome, and "the LORD is with him" (v.18). This last phrase is the most important — it is what Saul has lost. David enters Saul's service, becomes his armor-bearer, and plays the harp when the evil spirit comes. "Saul was refreshed, and was well, and the evil spirit departed from him" (v.23). The man who carries God's Spirit brings relief to the man from whom God's Spirit has departed. The irony is complete: Saul's comfort comes from the very man who will replace him.

Setting: Around 1025 BC. Saul is still on the throne, but at Gilgal the LORD rejected him as king for sparing Agag and the best of Amalek's flocks (ch 15). Samuel went home to Ramah and grieved, and the two never met as prophet and king again (15:35). Samuel had told Saul that the LORD had given the kingdom to "a neighbor of yours, who is better than you" (15:28), but no one yet knows who that is. Chapter 16 shifts the story to a small town in Judah, Bethlehem, about six miles south of Jerusalem, and introduces the man who will dominate the rest of 1 and 2 Samuel. David is the most important figure in the Old Testament after Moses. His name appears more than a thousand times in Scripture, and the promised Messiah will be called "the Son of David." The chapter tells his story twice in miniature: chosen privately by God and anointed with the Spirit (vv1–13), then brought publicly into Saul's court (vv14–23).

David Anointed King (vv1–13)

  • "How long will you grieve over Saul?" (v1). God's question is gentle but firm. Samuel's grief is right, but it must not keep him from what God is doing next. God's purposes do not die when a man fails.
  • "Fill your horn with oil, and go" (v1). Samuel had anointed Saul from a "flask" (10:1). Now he uses a horn, probably an animal horn used to hold oil. Some see a contrast: the horn is used again for David (v13) and for Solomon (1 Kgs 1:39), the kings whose dynasty lasts.
  • "I have provided for myself a king among his sons" (v1). Literally "I have seen for myself a king." The same verb (ra'ah, "see") appears in Genesis 22, where Abraham says "God will provide (see) for himself the lamb," and names the place "The LORD will provide" (Gen 22:8, 14). Seeing is the theme of this chapter. Saul was the king the people asked for (8:5; 12:13). This king is the one God has picked "for myself."
  • Jesse the Bethlehemite (v1). Jesse was the grandson of Boaz and Ruth the Moabite (Ruth 4:17–22). Bethlehem was a small town in Judah, and the line of Judah had been promised the scepter long before (Gen 49:10). Micah will later name Bethlehem as the town from which Israel's ruler will come (Mic 5:2; Matt 2:5–6).
  • "If Saul hears it, he will kill me" (v2). Samuel's fear shows how far Saul has fallen. The king who once spared his critics (11:13) is now someone even the prophet fears.
  • "Take a heifer with you and say, 'I have come to sacrifice to the LORD'" (v2). Is God telling Samuel to deceive? Most interpreters say no. Samuel really does offer the sacrifice. What he does not do is announce everything he plans to do there. There is a difference between lying and not disclosing all one's purposes, especially to someone who has no right to know and would use the information to kill. Samuel often traveled to sacrifice in different towns (7:16–17; 9:12–13), so the visit was natural.
  • "The elders of the city came to meet him trembling" (v4). The elders of a small town are frightened when the great prophet arrives without warning. News of Agag's execution (15:33) and of Samuel's break with Saul may have reached them. "Do you come peaceably?" is the question one asks when a visit might mean judgment.
  • "Consecrate yourselves" (v5). Before a sacrifice, worshipers prepared by washing and setting themselves apart (Exod 19:10, 14). Samuel himself consecrates Jesse's family. The peace offering was followed by a shared meal (9:12–13, 22–24), so the family would eat together before the LORD.
  • "Surely the LORD's anointed is before him" (v6). Eliab, the eldest, walks in, and Samuel is sure. Eliab must have looked like a king, tall and impressive, like Saul (9:2; 10:23). Even the great prophet makes the people's mistake.
  • "Do not look on his appearance or on the height of his stature, because I have rejected him" (v7). "Height" is the same word used of Saul's stature. "Rejected" is the word used of Saul (15:23, 26). God is telling Samuel not to repeat the choice of Saul. We get a glimpse of Eliab's heart in the next chapter, when he snaps at David in anger and contempt (17:28).
  • "The LORD sees not as man sees: man looks on the outward appearance, but the LORD looks on the heart" (v7). This is one of the key verses of the whole Bible. The Hebrew is literally "man sees to the eyes, but the LORD sees to the heart." People judge by what is visible. God sees what a person truly is (1 Chr 28:9; Ps 139:1–4; Jer 17:10). In Scripture "heart" means more than feelings. It is the center of a person's thinking, will, desires and loyalties. Jesus warned against judging "by appearances" (John 7:24), and Peter calls the beauty God values "the hidden person of the heart" (1 Pet 3:3–4).
  • Abinadab, Shammah, and the rest (vv8–10). One by one, seven sons pass before Samuel, and each time the answer is no. "Seven" suggests the full set. Jesse had shown Samuel what he thought was all his sons. 1 Chronicles 2:13–15 lists seven sons and names David the seventh, perhaps because one brother died young. Here David is the eighth.
  • "There remains yet the youngest, but behold, he is keeping the sheep" (v11). Jesse had not even thought to bring him. "The youngest" (haqqatan) can also mean "the smallest" or "the least." Keeping sheep was the job for the one who mattered least. Yet in the ancient Near East "shepherd" was a common title for a king, and God will later say to David, "You shall be shepherd of my people Israel" (2 Sam 5:2; Ps 78:70–72). The lowly job is training for the throne.
  • "We will not sit down till he comes here" (v11). Literally "we will not turn around," probably meaning they would not sit down to the sacrificial meal. Everyone waits for the overlooked boy.
  • "He was ruddy and had beautiful eyes and was handsome" (v12). "Ruddy" ('admoni) means reddish, perhaps red-haired or with a healthy, ruddy complexion. The same word describes Esau at birth (Gen 25:25). David is attractive too, but the text has just said appearance is not why God chose him. His looks are mentioned, not made the reason.
  • "Arise, anoint him, for this is he" (v12). God's choice is spoken plainly. David's name is not even given until the next verse.
  • "Anointed him in the midst of his brothers" (v13). The anointing happened in front of the family, but what they understood is not said. In chapter 17 Eliab still treats David with scorn. It will be many years before David is crowned, first over Judah at Hebron (2 Sam 2:4) and then over all Israel (2 Sam 5:3). Anointing with oil marked a person as set apart for God's service: priests (Exod 29:7), kings, and sometimes prophets (1 Kgs 19:16). The Hebrew word for "anointed one" is mashiach, which gives us "Messiah"; its Greek equivalent is Christos, "Christ."
  • "The Spirit of the LORD rushed upon David from that day forward" (v13). The verb is tsalach, the same used of the Spirit on Samson (Judg 14:6) and on Saul (10:10; 11:6). For the judges and for Saul, the Spirit came at particular moments for particular tasks. With David the text adds "from that day forward." The Spirit stays. Only here, at the end of v13, is his name finally given: David, probably meaning "beloved." When David later sinned with Bathsheba, he prayed, "take not your Holy Spirit from me" (Ps 51:11), perhaps with Saul's fate in mind.
  • Commentators: On v2, Guzik, following Clarke, says God was not telling Samuel to lie, since Samuel truly sacrificed and was not obliged to tell all his purpose. JFB calls Samuel's fear "another instance of human infirmity," and says the sacrifice was his usual reason for such visits. Henry on v7: "We can tell how men look, but God can tell what they are." He also notes that "God does not favour our children according to our fond partiality, but often most honours and blesses those who have been least regarded." On David's age JFB reports that "Josephus says that David was ten," but most think about fifteen, and Guzik gives a similar range. JFB says the anointing "must have been strictly private." Guzik reflects on David's years with the sheep as the place where his heart for worship was formed, pointing to psalms like 8, 19 and 23. The Tyndale notes list the ways David's anointing mirrors Saul's (both unlikely choices, both good-looking, both receiving the Spirit) and the one difference that matters: the heart.

David in Saul's Service (vv14–23)

  • "Now the Spirit of the LORD departed from Saul" (v14). The very next sentence after the Spirit comes on David. The writer sets the two side by side: as one king receives the Spirit, the other loses him. Under the old covenant, the Spirit's empowering for office could be withdrawn. Saul's loss is not first a mental-health story but a spiritual one.
  • "And a harmful spirit from the LORD tormented him" (v14). The Hebrew is ruach ra'ah, which can mean an "evil spirit" (as KJV) or a "harmful" or "troubling" one, since ra' covers both moral evil and calamity. "Tormented" means terrified or overwhelmed with dread. Saying it came "from the LORD" does not mean God is the author of evil. Scripture shows God ruling even over evil powers, and sometimes using them in judgment when people persist in rejecting him (Judg 9:23; 1 Kgs 22:19–23). In the same way Romans 1 says that God "gave them up" to what they had chosen (Rom 1:24, 28). Christians differ on how directly to describe God's role. Some say God actively sent the spirit as judgment, others that he withdrew his protection and permitted it. All agree that God remained sovereign, Saul remained responsible, and the spirit served God's purposes.
  • "A man who is skillful in playing the lyre" (v16). The kinnor was a small, portable stringed instrument, usually translated "harp" in older versions. The servants recognize Saul's suffering before he does. Music was widely believed to soothe troubled minds. Elisha later asked for a musician before he prophesied (2 Kgs 3:15).
  • "A son of Jesse… skillful in playing, a man of valor, a man of war, prudent in speech, and a man of good presence, and the LORD is with him" (v18). This is a full portrait in a single sentence. "Man of valor" can also mean a man of standing or ability. "Man of war" may surprise, since David is still young, but he has already fought lions and bears to protect the flock (17:34–36). The last phrase is the most important: "the LORD is with him." The phrase becomes a refrain in David's story (18:12, 14, 28; 2 Sam 5:10). The servant does not know what Samuel has done, but he sees its results.
  • "Send me David your son, who is with the sheep" (v19). Saul himself sends for the man God has chosen to replace him. The irony is complete. Saul does not know, and God brings David into the court by Saul's own order. David does not scheme his way to the throne. He goes where he is sent.
  • "A donkey laden with bread and a skin of wine and a young goat" (v20). A gift for the king, a customary sign of respect (10:27). It also shows a modest family. These are farm goods, not treasure.
  • "Saul loved him greatly, and he became his armor-bearer" (v21). At first, Saul loves David. "Armor-bearer" may be an honorary title here, a sign that David was one of the king's trusted attendants. Jonathan's armor-bearer was his closest companion in battle (14:6–7). This love will soon turn to jealousy and hatred (18:8–12).
  • "Let David remain in my service" (v22). Apparently David moved back and forth between Saul's court and his father's sheep for a while (17:15).
  • "David took the lyre and played it… So Saul was refreshed" (v23). "Refreshed" (ravach, "to be roomy, to have relief") sounds like the word for "spirit" (ruach), a wordplay running through the verse: the troubling spirit leaves and Saul has room to breathe. The harmful spirit departs, at least for a time. The future king serves the present king, bringing peace to the man who will later try to kill him. Many of David's psalms were later sung in Israel's worship, and believers have found the same relief in them ever since.
  • Commentators: JFB describes Saul after his rejection as "jealous, irritable, vindictive, and subject to fits of morbid melancholy," and ties the harmful spirit to that inner state. It notes the ancient belief that music could heal troubled minds. Guzik discusses the spirit "from the LORD" at length. He argues that God did not send evil into Saul in an active sense but withdrew his protection from a man who had resisted him, and he stresses that the trouble was spiritual and not only psychological. He uses David's description in v18 to sketch the qualities of anyone who leads worship, with "the LORD is with him" as the most important. Henry adds a pastoral warning: "Music, diversions, company, or business, have for a time often been employed to quiet the wounded conscience," but "nothing can effect a real cure but the blood of Christ, applied in faith." The Tyndale notes observe that even a rejected king could find relief through the Lord's anointed servant.

Pointing to Christ

The LORD went looking for a king "for myself," and he found him in Bethlehem, the least of Jesse's sons, out keeping the sheep. A thousand years later, the true Son of David was born in the same small town, and the first to hear of it were shepherds in its fields (Luke 2:8–11). David was anointed with oil and the Spirit rushed on him "from that day forward." At the Jordan, Jesus was anointed with the Spirit, who came down and remained on him (John 1:32–33), and the Father called him "my beloved Son" (Mark 1:11), echoing the very meaning of David's name. Jesus is the Good Shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep (John 10:11). And as David's music brought relief to a tormented king, Jesus came to drive out evil spirits and bring rest to the weary (Matt 11:28; 12:28). The LORD who looks on the heart chose him, and in him he chooses people the world overlooks (1 Cor 1:26–29).

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes that David was so overlooked that his own father did not bring him to the sacrifice. God's choices often surprise everyone — including the family. He notes that "the LORD looketh on the heart" does not mean David was sinless but that his fundamental orientation was toward God.

Reflection

  • Grief That Delays Obedience (v.1) — God asks Samuel how long he intends to keep mourning over Saul when God has already moved on and has new work for him to do. Is there a loss or disappointment you're still grieving that's keeping you from stepping into what God has next?
  • Seeing Past the Surface (v.7) — Samuel is drawn to Eliab's impressive appearance, but God corrects him, explaining that people judge by outward looks while He judges by the heart. Where do you catch yourself evaluating people — or yourself — by appearance, achievement, or status instead of character?
  • The One Nobody Thought to Call (vv.11-12) — David is left out in the fields with the sheep, so overlooked that his own father doesn't think to bring him in for the sacrifice, yet he's the one God has already chosen. Have you ever felt passed over, only to sense later that God had His eye on you the whole time?
  • What Is Given and What Is Taken Away (vv.13-14) — The Spirit comes upon David to stay, while at the same moment the Spirit leaves Saul and a tormenting spirit takes its place. Does your life show ongoing dependence on God's Spirit, or have you settled for functioning in your own strength?
  • Comfort From an Unlikely Source (v.23) — Saul finds relief from his torment through the music of the very shepherd boy who will one day take his throne, without ever realizing who David is. Have you seen God work through someone or something you initially overlooked or underestimated?
