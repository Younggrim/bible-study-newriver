1Then the people of the land took Jehoahaz son of Josiah and made him king in Jerusalem in place of his father.

2Jehoahaz was twenty-three years old when he became king, and he reigned in Jerusalem three months.

3And the king of Egypt dethroned him in Jerusalem and imposed on Judah a levy of a hundred talents of silver and a talent of gold.

4Then Neco king of Egypt made Eliakim brother of Jehoahaz king over Judah and Jerusalem, and he changed Eliakim’s name to Jehoiakim. But Neco took Eliakim’s brother Jehoahaz and carried him off to Egypt.

5Jehoiakim was twenty-five years old when he became king, and he reigned in Jerusalem eleven years. And he did evil in the sight of the LORD his God.

6Then Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon came up against Jehoiakim and bound him with bronze shackles to take him to Babylon.

7Nebuchadnezzar also took to Babylon some of the articles from the house of the LORD, and he put them in his temple in Babylon.

8As for the rest of the acts of Jehoiakim, the abominations he committed, and all that was found against him, they are indeed written in the Book of the Kings of Israel and Judah. And his son Jehoiachin reigned in his place.

9Jehoiachin was eighteen years old when he became king, and he reigned in Jerusalem three months and ten days. And he did evil in the sight of the LORD.

10In the spring, King Nebuchadnezzar summoned Jehoiachin and brought him to Babylon, along with the articles of value from the house of the LORD. And he made Jehoiachin’s relative Zedekiah king over Judah and Jerusalem.

11Zedekiah was twenty-one years old when he became king, and he reigned in Jerusalem eleven years.

12And he did evil in the sight of the LORD his God and did not humble himself before Jeremiah the prophet, who spoke for the LORD.

13He also rebelled against King Nebuchadnezzar, who had made him swear by God. But Zedekiah stiffened his neck and hardened his heart against turning to the LORD, the God of Israel.

14Furthermore, all the leaders of the priests and the people multiplied their unfaithful deeds, following all the abominations of the nations, and they defiled the house of the LORD, which He had consecrated in Jerusalem.

15Again and again the LORD, the God of their fathers, sent word to His people through His messengers because He had compassion on them and on His dwelling place.

16But they mocked the messengers of God, despising His words and scoffing at His prophets, until the wrath of the LORD against His people was stirred up beyond remedy.

17So He brought up against them the king of the Chaldeans, who put their young men to the sword in the sanctuary, sparing neither young men nor young women, neither elderly nor infirm. God gave them all into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar,

18who carried off everything to Babylon—all the articles of the house of God, both large and small, and the treasures of the house of the LORD and of the king and his officials.

19Then the Chaldeans set fire to the house of God and broke down the wall of Jerusalem. They burned down all the palaces and destroyed every article of value.

20Those who escaped the sword were carried by Nebuchadnezzar into exile in Babylon, and they became servants to him and his sons until the kingdom of Persia came to power.

21So the land enjoyed its Sabbath rest all the days of the desolation, until seventy years were completed, in fulfillment of the word of the LORD spoken through Jeremiah.

22In the first year of Cyrus king of Persia, to fulfill the word of the LORD spoken through Jeremiah, the LORD stirred the spirit of Cyrus king of Persia to send a proclamation throughout his kingdom and to put it in writing as follows:

23“This is what Cyrus king of Persia says: ‘The LORD, the God of heaven, who has given me all the kingdoms of the earth, has appointed me to build a house for Him at Jerusalem in Judah. Whoever among you belongs to His people, may the LORD his God be with him, and may he go up.’”

Summary

Judah's final kings each reign briefly and badly: Jehoahaz is deposed by Egypt after three months, Jehoiakim is bound and taken by Nebuchadnezzar after doing evil for eleven years, and the boy king Jehoiachin is likewise carried to Babylon after only three months on the throne (vv. 1-10). Zedekiah, the last king, does evil, refuses to humble himself before the prophet Jeremiah, and rebels against Nebuchadnezzar despite having sworn loyalty to him in God's name (vv. 11-13). The priests and people compound the guilt by defiling the temple and mocking the messengers God repeatedly sends until there is no remedy left (vv. 14-16). The Lord finally hands them over to the Babylonians, who kill many in the temple, burn the city and its palaces, and carry the survivors and the temple's treasures into exile, fulfilling Jeremiah's prophecy of seventy years (vv. 17-21). The book ends on a note of hope as Cyrus of Persia issues a decree, stirred by the Lord, permitting the exiles to return and rebuild the house of God in Jerusalem (vv. 22-23).

Authorship & Background

Author: Traditionally attributed to Ezra the scribe. Originally one book with 1 Chronicles. Written post-exile (approximately 450-400 BC). 2 Chronicles covers Solomon's reign through the Babylonian exile and Cyrus's decree of return (approximately 970-538 BC). The Chronicler focuses exclusively on Judah (ignoring the northern kingdom) and emphasizes Temple worship, reform movements, and the principle of 2 Chronicles 7:14: "If my people... shall humble themselves, and pray... then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and will heal their land."
Historical Context: Chapter 36 is the Chronicler's rapid-fire conclusion — compressing Judah's final 23 years (609-586 BC) into a single devastating chapter. Four kings reign in succession, each doing "evil in the sight of the LORD," and the nation spirals irreversibly toward destruction. Where the Chronicler lavished chapters on faithful kings like Hezekiah and Josiah, these final rulers receive only brief, damning summaries — their reigns are theologically uninteresting because they produced only rebellion. The four kings represent Judah's last gasps: Jehoahaz (3 months, deposed by Egypt); Jehoiakim (11 years, bound by Nebuchadnezzar); Jehoiachin (3 months and 10 days, exiled to Babylon); and Zedekiah (11 years, the last king, who presided over Jerusalem's destruction). The chapter traces the progressive stripping of Judah's sovereignty: first Egypt controls king-making (vv.1-4), then Babylon takes Temple vessels and installs puppet kings (vv.6-10), and finally Babylon destroys everything — Temple, walls, palaces, and people (vv.17-19).
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: Four kings compressed into twenty-three years, each installed and deposed at the convenience of whichever empire is nearest, an age given differently here than in the parallel account in Kings with neither book explaining the divergence, a compassion named as God's motive for sending messenger after messenger rather than patience simply running out, mockery and misuse of every prophet sent until there was no remedy left at all, and a book that ends fifty years later on a Persian king's own proclamation, crediting his empire to Israel's God and commanding the exiles to go up
Four Kings in Twenty-Three Years (vv.1-14): After Megiddo the throne changes hands at the convenience of whichever empire is nearest. Jehoahaz is made king by the people and deposed by Necho after three months, taken to Egypt, and his brother installed under a new name and a tribute. Jehoiakim lasts eleven years and is bound in fetters by Nebuchadnezzar, who also begins removing the vessels of the house of the LORD. Jehoiachin lasts three months and ten days; his age is given here as eight where 2 Kings 24:8 has eighteen, one of several such divergences between the books. Zedekiah is installed by Babylon and the charge against him is specific about what he would not do, he humbled not himself before Jeremiah the prophet speaking from the mouth of the LORD, and he broke an oath sworn by God. The section closes by widening the blame past the palace, the priests and the people trespassed very much, and polluted the house of the LORD.
The Messengers Mocked, and the Land Keeping Sabbath (vv.15-21): Verses 15 and 16 are the Chronicler's summary of the entire history he has been writing, and the motive he assigns to God is compassion rather than patience running out, the LORD God of their fathers sent to them by his messengers, rising up betimes and sending, because he had compassion on his people, and on his dwelling place. What answers it is a sequence of verbs, they mocked the messengers of God, and despised his words, and misused his prophets, until there was no remedy. The destruction is then reported without mitigation: no compassion shown to young or old, the vessels great and small carried to Babylon, the house of God burnt, the wall broken down, the palaces burnt. And a single verse interprets it, reading Leviticus 26 and Jeremiah together, the land lay desolate to fulfil the word of the LORD, until the land had enjoyed her sabbaths, to fulfil threescore and ten years.
Cyrus, and a Book That Ends on a Command to Go Up (vv.22-23): The last two verses jump fifty years without transition, to the first year of Cyrus king of Persia, when the LORD stirred up his spirit to make a proclamation throughout all his kingdom. Ezra 1 opens with the same decree, and the seam is deliberate: the Chronicler's readers are the people the decree is addressed to. The wording matters at two points. Cyrus credits his empire to Israel's God, all the kingdoms of the earth hath the LORD God of heaven given me. And the decree ends in the second person, who is there among you of all his people, the LORD his God be with him, and let him go up. Because Chronicles stands last in the Hebrew order, that is the final sentence of the Old Testament, and it is not a conclusion but an instruction left open.

Map & Geography

  • Parallels 1-2 Kings but focuses exclusively on Judah (the southern kingdom). Jerusalem and the Temple are the theological center throughout.

Deep Dive

Setting: Jerusalem, 609–586 BC, with a closing glance at 538 BC. Josiah, the last good king of Judah, has just fallen at Megiddo trying to stop Pharaoh Neco (35:20–24), and all Judah mourned him. In the twenty-three years after his death, four kings follow in quick succession, three of them his sons and one his grandson. Egypt and then Babylon push them around like pieces on a board, and the kingdom of David ends in fire. 2 Kings 23:31–25:30 tells the same story at greater length, and Jeremiah, who preached through all of it, fills in the human detail. The Chronicler compresses it into a few verses per king, because his real interest is why it happened and what God does next. In the Hebrew Bible, Chronicles is the very last book, so this chapter is the last page of the Old Testament as Jesus knew it, and it ends not with ashes but with a king saying "Let him go up."

Judah's Decline (vv1–16)

  • "The people of the land took Jehoahaz" (v1). "The people of the land" were the landowning citizens of Judah, the same group that had put the boy Josiah on the throne after his father's murder (33:25). Jehoahaz was not the eldest son. His older brother Eliakim was passed over (1 Chr 3:15 lists the sons; Jeremiah 22:11 calls Jehoahaz by his other name, Shallum). The Tyndale notes say the reason is not given. Many think the people chose the son most likely to carry on Josiah's anti-Egyptian policy.
  • "He reigned three months" (v2). Three months in 609 BC. 2 Kings 23:32 says he "did what was evil," which the Chronicler leaves out, perhaps because his reign was too short to tell. Jeremiah wrote his epitaph: "Weep bitterly for him who goes away, for he shall return no more to see his native land" (Jer 22:10). Pharaoh summoned him to his camp at Riblah in Syria (2 Kgs 23:33), and he died in Egypt.
  • "A hundred talents of silver and a talent of gold" (v3). A talent was about 75 pounds (34 kg), so this was roughly three and a half tons of silver and 75 pounds of gold. Judah is now an Egyptian vassal state, paying for the privilege of existing.
  • "Changed his name to Jehoiakim" (v4). Renaming a vassal showed who was master (compare Daniel and his friends, Dan 1:7). The change is small but telling. Eliakim means "God raises up," and Jehoiakim means "the LORD raises up." Neco keeps the God of Israel in the name while making it plain who actually did the raising.
  • "He did what was evil in the sight of the LORD his God" (v5). Jehoiakim reigned eleven years (609–598 BC). Jeremiah draws his portrait: he built a fine palace with unpaid forced labor while the poor were crushed (Jer 22:13–17), he killed the prophet Uriah (Jer 26:20–23), and he cut up Jeremiah's scroll column by column and burned it in his brazier (Jer 36:23). The man who sat on Josiah's throne undid nearly everything Josiah stood for.
  • "Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon… bound him in chains to take him to Babylon" (v6). In 605 BC Nebuchadnezzar crushed Egypt at Carchemish on the Euphrates and became master of the whole region. Jehoiakim switched sides, then rebelled three years later (2 Kgs 24:1). Chronicles says he was bound "to take him" to Babylon, but it does not say he went. 2 Kings 24:6 simply says he "slept with his fathers," and Jeremiah said he would have "the burial of a donkey," dragged and dumped outside the gates (Jer 22:19; 36:30). How these fit together is not clear. One view is that he was chained, then released as a vassal, and died during the siege of 598 BC.
  • "Part of the vessels of the house of the LORD" (v7). This is the beginning of the plundering of the temple, told in three stages (vv7, 10, 18). Daniel 1:1–2 records the same event and adds that Daniel and other young nobles were taken then too. These same vessels appear again in Belshazzar's drunken feast, the night Babylon fell (Dan 5:2–3), and they come home with the exiles (Ezra 1:7–11). The Chronicler keeps an eye on them because they are a thread of continuity between the first temple and the second.
  • "The abominations that he did, and what was found against him" (v8). A hint of more than the text tells. Jewish tradition later imagined pagan marks found on his body, but that is legend. The phrase most likely means simply the record of his guilt.
  • "Jehoiachin was eighteen years old" (v9). Most Hebrew manuscripts read "eight" here, while 2 Kings 24:8, the Greek Septuagint and the Syriac read "eighteen," and the ESV follows them (its footnote gives the other reading). A copyist's slip with Hebrew number words is the likely explanation. Eighteen also fits better, since 2 Kings 24:15 mentions his wives. He reigned "three months and ten days," the end of 598 to early 597 BC.
  • "In the spring of the year King Nebuchadnezzar sent and brought him to Babylon" (v10). Literally "at the turn of the year," the season "when kings go out to battle" (2 Sam 11:1). The Babylonian Chronicle, a cuneiform tablet now in the British Museum, records that Nebuchadnezzar took "the city of Judah" on the second day of Adar, which works out to March 16, 597 BC. That is one of the most precisely dated events in the Old Testament. Ezekiel was taken in this same deportation (Ezek 1:2), along with ten thousand of Jerusalem's leaders and craftsmen (2 Kgs 24:14).
  • Jehoiachin's strange future. Jeremiah pronounced that none of his offspring would sit on David's throne (Jer 22:30), and yet his name (as Jeconiah) stands in Matthew's genealogy of Jesus (Matt 1:11–12). Joseph descends from him, and Jesus is Joseph's legal heir but not his biological son, which many readers see as God's way through the curse. Clay ration tablets found in Babylon list oil for "Yaukin, king of Judah," and 2 Kings ends with him released from prison and eating at the king's table (2 Kgs 25:27–30).
  • "Made his brother Zedekiah king" (v10). In Hebrew, "brother" can mean any close kinsman. Zedekiah was actually Jehoiachin's uncle, another son of Josiah (2 Kgs 24:17; 1 Chr 3:15). His original name was Mattaniah, "gift of the LORD," and Nebuchadnezzar renamed him Zedekiah, "the LORD is righteous," a name that turns out to be the verdict on his reign.
  • "He did not humble himself before Jeremiah the prophet" (v12). "Humble himself" is the key word of Chronicles' theology of repentance. It goes back to God's promise to Solomon: "if my people… humble themselves, and pray" (7:14). Rehoboam humbled himself and was spared (12:6–7), and even Manasseh did (33:12). Josiah's "heart was tender and you humbled yourself" (34:27). Zedekiah is the one who would not. Jeremiah's book shows him as weak more than wicked: he consulted Jeremiah in secret (Jer 37:17; 38:14–16), admitted he was afraid of his own officials (Jer 38:19), and still did nothing.
  • "Who had made him swear by God" (v13). Vassal treaties of the time were sealed with oaths in the name of the vassal's own god, and the Tyndale notes compare Assyrian treaty texts. So Zedekiah's rebellion was not just bad politics. It was perjury in the LORD's name. Ezekiel, prophesying in Babylon at the same time, made exactly that point: "Shall he break the covenant and yet escape?" (Ezek 17:15–19).
  • "He stiffened his neck and hardened his heart" (v13). A "stiff neck" is the picture of an ox that will not bend to the yoke. It is the old charge against Israel at the golden calf (Exod 32:9). Six centuries later, Stephen used the same words to the council in Jerusalem (Acts 7:51).
  • "All the officers of the priests and the people likewise" (v14). The guilt is shared. Ezekiel saw in a vision what was going on in the temple in these very years: idol images on the walls, women weeping for the pagan god Tammuz, and men worshiping the sun with their backs to the sanctuary (Ezek 8). "They polluted the house of the LORD that he had made holy." The house God had hallowed, they made unclean.
  • "The LORD… sent persistently to them by his messengers" (v15). The Hebrew is a vivid idiom, "rising early and sending," used again and again in Jeremiah (Jer 7:13, 25; 25:3–4). It pictures God getting up at dawn to send yet another prophet. And the reason is given: "because he had compassion on his people and on his dwelling place." The prophets were not God's way of building a case. They were his mercy.
  • "Mocking… despising… scoffing" (v16). Three verbs pile up. Jeremiah was beaten, put in stocks, thrown into a muddy cistern and called a traitor (Jer 20:2; 38:6). Uriah was hunted down and killed (Jer 26:23). Jesus later named this pattern as the history of Jerusalem: "you who kill the prophets and stone those who are sent to you" (Matt 23:37).
  • "Until there was no remedy" (v16). The word is marpē', "healing" or "cure," used of a wound that can be healed (Prov 4:22; Jer 14:19). The prophets were God's medicine. When the patient refuses every dose, the illness runs its course. This is not God running out of mercy. It is people refusing it until judgment is the only thing left.
  • Commentators: Henry sums up the four reigns: "Additions were made to the national guilt, and advances towards the national destruction, in each of those reigns." On v13 he observes that "all nations looked upon an oath as a sacred thing," which makes Zedekiah worse than the pagans. JFB calls the revolt "the crime of perjury" and, on v9, agrees that "eight" should read "eighteen." Guzik notes the text never says Jehoiakim actually reached Babylon and points to Jeremiah's donkey's-burial prophecy. On v15 he draws out the picture of God as an anxious parent rising early to send word, and on v16 Henry writes that the people brought "wrath upon them without remedy, for it was sinning against the remedy." Guzik, following Selman, makes the same point: "no remedy" is literally "no healing."

Jerusalem Captured and Burned (vv17–21)

  • "Therefore he brought up against them the king of the Chaldeans" (v17). "Therefore" ties the disaster straight to vv15–16. The Chaldeans were the people of southern Mesopotamia from whom Nebuchadnezzar's dynasty came, so "Chaldean" and "Babylonian" mean much the same here. Note who is acting: he, the LORD, brought them. Nebuchadnezzar is the tool. Jeremiah even has God call him "my servant" (Jer 25:9).
  • "In the house of their sanctuary" (v17). The young men are killed in the very place they thought would protect them. Jeremiah had warned against trusting the slogan "This is the temple of the LORD" (Jer 7:4). Lamentations 2:20 asks in horror, "Should priest and prophet be killed in the sanctuary of the Lord?"
  • "Had no compassion" (v17). Bitter irony. Verse 15 said the LORD "had compassion" on his people. They spurned his compassion, so he handed them to a king who had none. Young man and virgin, old man and aged: the list covers everyone.
  • "All the vessels… great and small" (v18). The word "all" runs through vv17–19. Guzik passes on Selman's count of the word, and the effect is plain: nothing is held back this time. The first two raids took "part" of the vessels (v7) and the "precious" ones (v10). Now everything goes.
  • "They burned the house of God" (v19). This was in August 586 BC, on the seventh to tenth of the fifth month (2 Kgs 25:8; Jer 52:12). Jews still mark the day as Tisha B'Av, a fast that also remembers the Roman destruction of the second temple in AD 70. Solomon's temple had stood for nearly four hundred years. Chronicles opened its story of the monarchy with David planning it and Solomon building it (1 Chr 22–2 Chr 7), so its burning is the end of everything the book has been about.
  • What Chronicles leaves out. 2 Kings 25 and Jeremiah 39 tell how Zedekiah fled by night, was caught near Jericho, saw his sons killed and was then blinded and taken to Babylon in chains. They also tell of Gedaliah's governorship and murder. The Chronicler skips all of it. He is not writing a full history. He wants the reader to see the cause (vv15–16), the judgment (vv17–20) and the hope (vv21–23).
  • "Servants to him and to his sons until the establishment of the kingdom of Persia" (v20). The exile lasted through Nebuchadnezzar, his son Evil-merodach and the later kings of Babylon, until Cyrus the Persian took Babylon in 539 BC. Jeremiah had told the exiles to settle in, build houses, plant gardens and pray for the city (Jer 29:4–7). Psalm 137 shows the other side: "By the waters of Babylon, there we sat down and wept."
  • "Until the land had enjoyed its Sabbaths" (v21). The law required that every seventh year the fields lie fallow (Lev 25:1–7). It also warned that if Israel broke the covenant, God would scatter them, and "then the land shall enjoy its Sabbaths… as long as it lies desolate" (Lev 26:34–35, 43). Israel had skipped those rest years. Now the land takes them all at once. The exile is not random cruelty but the covenant's own warning coming true, and there is mercy in it too: the land rests and is made ready for the people's return.
  • "To fulfill seventy years" (v21). Jeremiah foretold seventy years of Babylonian rule (Jer 25:11–12; 29:10). Counting from the first deportation in 605 BC to the first return in about 538 BC gives close to seventy. Counting from the temple's destruction in 586 BC to its rebuilding in 516 BC gives almost exactly seventy (Ezra 6:15). Either way, the number is likely meant as a full lifetime, a whole generation. Daniel read this very prophecy and began to pray (Dan 9:2). Some suggest the seventy years equal the Sabbath years missed over about 490 years of monarchy, a neat calculation the text itself does not make.
  • Commentators: Henry gives the fullest moral reading: in the land's Sabbaths "God will be no loser in his glory at last by the disobedience of men." He also says that "the word of God was herein fulfilled," both the threat and the limit on it. JFB reads the Sabbath years as a "season of rest to all classes, even to the land itself," neglected for generations and now repaid. Guzik notes the irony of v17, that the people who refused God's compassion meet a king who has none, and like the Tyndale notes he connects v21 to Leviticus 26. He passes on the 490-year calculation, which is a commentators' reckoning rather than the text's.

The Proclamation of Cyrus (vv22–23)

  • "In the first year of Cyrus king of Persia" (v22). Cyrus had been king of Persia for years, but this is his first year as ruler of Babylon, 538 BC. He took the city in October 539 BC almost without a fight. The Greek historian Herodotus tells of his army diverting the Euphrates and entering along the riverbed, and Daniel 5 tells of the same night from inside the palace.
  • A gap of nearly fifty years. Between v21 and v22 lie the entire exile and the fall of an empire. The Chronicler jumps straight from the burning to the decree. He wants the last word to be grace.
  • "That the word of the LORD by the mouth of Jeremiah might be fulfilled" (v22). This is the second mention of Jeremiah in two verses. The God who kept his word to judge keeps his word to restore. Isaiah had named Cyrus generations earlier: "Cyrus… shall fulfill all my purpose… saying of Jerusalem, 'She shall be built'" (Isa 44:28; 45:1). Readers differ on how Isaiah came to name him. Those who hold that one Isaiah wrote the whole book see a prediction about 150 years ahead. Many critical scholars assign chapters 40–55 to a later prophet in the exile. Either way, the Chronicler sees the decree as God's doing.
  • "The LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus" (v22). The verb 'ur means to rouse or awaken, the way one wakes a sleeper. Chronicles has used it of God stirring up enemies to punish Judah (1 Chr 5:26; 2 Chr 21:16). Now he stirs a foreign king to bless them. A pagan emperor's policy is put down to the LORD's working in his spirit (compare Prov 21:1).
  • "Put it in writing" (v22). The famous Cyrus Cylinder, a clay barrel found at Babylon in 1879 and now in the British Museum, records Cyrus's general policy of returning captured peoples and their gods to their homelands and rebuilding their shrines. It does not mention the Jews, and in it he credits Marduk, the god of Babylon. That fits Persian practice: Cyrus spoke to each people in terms of its own gods. Ezra 6:3–5 preserves a second, more official version of his decree for the Jews, found later in the archives at Ecbatana.
  • "The LORD, the God of heaven, has given me all the kingdoms of the earth" (v23). "God of heaven" was a common Persian-era title for Israel's God (Ezra 1:2; Neh 1:4; Dan 2:18). Whether Cyrus personally believed it, he is made to speak the truth: the kingdoms are the LORD's to give (Dan 4:17).
  • "He has charged me to build him a house at Jerusalem" (v23). The book ends where David's hopes began. In 1 Chronicles 17 God promised David a "house" in two senses, a dynasty and a temple. The dynasty has just been carried off and the temple burned. But the call to build again is going out.
  • "Let him go up" (v23). In Hebrew the book ends on a single word, ya'al, "let him go up." It is the word for going up to Jerusalem, used of pilgrims and returning exiles. It is an open door with the sentence left unfinished. Ezra 1:1–3 repeats these verses word for word and carries the story on. Some think the repetition shows that one author or school wrote both books, others that an editor stitched them together. Either way, the overlap points the reader forward.
  • Commentators: Henry calls these verses "the dawning of the day of their deliverance." Guzik stresses that God stirred Cyrus in his first year to keep Jeremiah's word, and he passes on Payne's view that Chronicles is at heart a book of hope. He also notes, following Selman, that "build him a house" echoes the promise to David, so the end of the book is also a new beginning. JFB here simply refers the reader on to Ezra 1:1–3.

Pointing to Christ

The last page of the Hebrew Bible has a temple in ashes, David's line in exile, and a door opening: "Let him go up." Everything that follows waits for the house and the king. Jesus came as both. He spoke of his body as the temple that would be destroyed and raised in three days (John 2:19–21), and he is the son of David from Jehoiachin's own line who takes the throne no human king could keep (Matt 1:11–12; Luke 1:32–33). He also wept over Jerusalem for doing what this chapter describes, mocking the messengers God sent in compassion (Luke 13:34; Matt 23:34–37). In him the remedy that Judah refused is offered again, and the call to "go up" becomes an invitation to come home to God.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes that the rapid succession of evil kings demonstrates how quickly a nation can collapse after godly leadership is removed. He highlights verses 15-16 as the Chronicler's definitive explanation for the exile — not divine cruelty but exhausted patience after persistent, compassionate warning. The ending on Cyrus's decree shows that God always has the last word, and His last word is grace.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Refusing the Word Even From a Trusted Prophet (v.12) — Zedekiah won't humble himself before Jeremiah, even though Jeremiah was clearly speaking for the Lord. Is there a person in your life whose counsel you already know is right but keep refusing to receive?
  • Defiling the Very Place Meant for Worship (v.14) — The priests and people bring pagan practices into the temple itself, corrupting the one place set apart to meet with God. Is there an area of your life you've labeled "spiritual" that has quietly been compromised by things that don't belong there?
  • Persistent, Compassionate Warning (v.15) — God sends messenger after messenger, rising up early, not out of harshness but genuine compassion for his people. When you've had to correct or warn someone repeatedly, is compassion still driving it, or has it turned to frustration?
  • A Patience That Eventually Runs Out (v.16) — The people mock God's messengers so persistently that the text says wrath finally rises until there is no remedy. Are you treating God's patience with you as infinite, or taking seriously that continued rejection has real consequences?
  • Judgment That Still Serves a Purpose (v.21) — Even the exile fulfills a specific purpose, letting the land finally receive the rest it had been denied for generations. When you're in a season of discipline or consequence, can you trust there's a restorative purpose inside it, even if you can't see it yet?
  • God's Last Word Is Always an Invitation (v.23) — The book closes not with destruction but with Cyrus's call for God's people to rise up and return. No matter how far you feel you've drifted, is going up, actually returning, something you believe is still open to you?
