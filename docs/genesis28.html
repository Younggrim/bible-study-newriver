Summary

Isaac blesses Jacob again and sends him to Paddan-aram to find a wife among Laban's family rather than the local Canaanites, while Esau, sensing his father's disapproval, marries a daughter of Ishmael (vv. 1-9). Stopping for the night on his journey, Jacob dreams of a stairway reaching from earth to heaven with angels ascending and descending on it, and the LORD stands above it, reaffirming to Jacob the covenant promises made to Abraham and Isaac (vv. 10-15). Jacob wakes in awe, calls the place Bethel, sets up his stone pillow as a memorial pillar, and vows that if God brings him back safely, the LORD will be his God and he will give a tenth of all he has (vv. 16-22).

Authorship & Background

Author: Moses (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 28 records Jacob's departure from home (fleeing Esau's wrath) and his encounter with God at Bethel — "Jacob's Ladder." This is Jacob's first personal encounter with God. Until now, God was "the God of Abraham" and "the God of Isaac." At Bethel, God becomes Jacob's God too. The deceiver, the supplanter, the fugitive — meets the God of grace who makes unconditional promises to undeserving people. Jacob leaves home a schemer; he will return (ch.32) transformed.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: a fugitive fleeing his own brother's murderous anger receiving, of all things, his father's deliberate blessing before he even leaves, a stairway reaching from earth to heaven with angels ascending and descending witnessed in a dream on a stone pillow, the Abrahamic promises repeated to a man who has done nothing yet to earn them, a startled confession, surely the LORD is in this place, and I knew it not, and a vow that is still conditional, phrased as if God's faithfulness were somehow still in question
Isaac's Blessing and Commission (vv.1-5): Isaac blesses Jacob intentionally this time (v.1). Commands: don't marry a Canaanite (v.1); go to Paddan-aram, marry from Laban's daughters (v.2). Blesses with "the blessing of Abraham" — fruitfulness, land, inheritance (vv.3-4). Jacob departs (v.5).
Esau's Response (vv.6-9): Esau sees that Canaanite wives displease Isaac (vv.6-8). He marries Ishmael's daughter — trying to please but still missing the point (v.9).
Jacob's Dream at Bethel (vv.10-15): Jacob sleeps with a stone pillow (v.11). He dreams: a ladder (stairway) from earth to heaven, angels ascending and descending (v.12). The LORD stands above it and speaks (v.13). The Abrahamic promises given to Jacob: land, descendants like dust, blessing to all families, God's presence and protection (vv.13-15). "I will not leave thee, until I have done that which I have spoken to thee of" (v.15).
Jacob's Response (vv.16-22): "Surely the LORD is in this place; and I knew it not" (v.16). "This is none other but the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven" (v.17). He sets up the stone as a pillar, pours oil on it (v.18). Names the place Bethel — "house of God" (v.19). Makes a conditional vow: If God does these things, then the LORD shall be my God (vv.20-21). Promises a tithe (v.22).

Map & Geography

  • Beer-sheba (v.10) — Jacob departs from his family home heading north.
  • Bethel / Luz (vv.11-19) — approximately 60 miles north of Beer-sheba in the central hill country. Jacob names it "Bethel" meaning "house of God" after his dream of the ladder/stairway reaching heaven.
  • Haran / Paddan-aram (v.10) — Jacob's destination: the family homeland in northwest Mesopotamia (modern Harran, Turkey, near the Syrian border).
  • The journey from Beer-sheba to Haran traces the entire length of Canaan and beyond — roughly 500 miles.

Deep Dive

Setting: The deception of chapter 27 is done and cannot be undone. Isaac has blessed Jacob, believing he was Esau, and then said, "Yes, and he shall be blessed" (27:33). Esau is waiting for his father to die so he can kill his brother (27:41). Rebekah has persuaded Isaac to send Jacob to her family, using Esau's Hittite wives as the reason (27:46). Jacob, by older reckoning somewhere in his seventies and still unmarried, leaves Beersheba in the Negeb for Haran in upper Mesopotamia, about 450 miles to the northeast. He leaves as a fugitive with nothing but a staff (32:10). Somewhere on the road, alone and outside, he meets the God of his fathers for the first time. The chapter turns on that meeting. The man who grabbed for the blessing is now handed it freely, first by his father and then by God himself.

Jacob Sent to Laban (vv1–5)

  • "Then Isaac called Jacob and blessed him" (v1). This time Isaac knows exactly whom he is blessing, and he does it willingly. The Tyndale notes observe that he holds nothing back now, because he has accepted what God wants. Isaac's trembling in 27:33 seems to have turned into submission.
  • "You must not take a wife from the Canaanite women" (v1). This is the charge Abraham laid on his servant when he found a wife for Isaac (24:3). The concern was not ethnic pride but faith. The Canaanites absorbed other clans into their society and their gods through marriage (34:20–23). Esau's Hittite wives had already shown what that looked like inside the family (26:34–35). The New Testament carries the same concern into marriage between believers (2 Cor 6:14).
  • "Go to Paddan-aram" (v2). The name probably means "the plain (or field) of Aram," the region around Haran in what is now southeastern Turkey and northern Syria. Abraham's brother Nahor's family settled there. Bethuel is Rebekah's father and Laban her brother (24:15, 29). Isaac had stayed in the land while a servant fetched his bride. Jacob must go himself, and it will cost him twenty years.
  • "God Almighty bless you" (v3). "God Almighty" is El Shaddai, the name God used when he made the covenant of circumcision with Abraham (17:1) and will use again when he confirms Jacob's new name (35:11). The lexicon says the origin of Shaddai is uncertain. "Almighty" follows the old Greek and Latin translations, and other proposals link it to "mountain" or to "sufficient." Whatever its roots, in Genesis it is the name God uses when he promises fruitfulness to people who look unlikely to have it.
  • "That you may become a company of peoples" (v3). "Company" is qahal, "assembly" or "congregation," the word later used for Israel gathered before God (Deut 9:10). The Greek Old Testament often translates qahal with ekklesia, the New Testament word for "church."
  • "The blessing of Abraham" (v4). This is the piece missing from 27:28–29. There Isaac spoke of dew, grain and rule over brothers. Here he explicitly hands over Abraham's promise: offspring, and the land "that God gave to Abraham." Isaac calls it "the land of your sojournings." Like his father and grandfather, Jacob will own it by promise while living in it as a resident foreigner.
  • "Rebekah, Jacob's and Esau's mother" (v5). The narrator names her as mother of both sons, quietly recalling that she has lost them both in a sense, one to exile and one to hatred. Jacob never sees her alive again. The next time Genesis mentions her household, it records the death of her nurse, Deborah (35:8).
  • Commentators: Guzik thinks Isaac had come to accept that God had chosen the younger son, and he notes that every party in the family conflict seems unworthy of the blessing, which highlights God's sovereign choice. Henry reads Jacob's departure as a fitting correction for his deceit, and also as a reminder that heirs of the promise should expect hardship. JFB says Isaac "entered fully" into Rebekah's view, and stresses that the blessing was now given cordially and openly. JFB has no notes on vv2–5.

Esau Marries an Ishmaelite (vv6–9)

  • "Esau saw" (vv6, 8). Esau has watched it all: the blessing, the charge, and that "Jacob had obeyed his father and his mother" (v7). That last phrase is pointed. Jacob is the obedient son in this scene, at least outwardly. Esau finally grasps that "the Canaanite women did not please Isaac his father" (v8). That had been true for years (26:35).
  • "Esau went to Ishmael" (v9). Ishmael himself was probably dead by now (25:17), so "went to Ishmael" means his family. Esau marries his cousin Mahalath, Ishmael's daughter. In 36:3 the same woman seems to appear as "Basemath, Ishmael's daughter, the sister of Nebaioth," another case of a person with two names. Nebaioth was Ishmael's firstborn (25:13).
  • "Besides the wives he had" (v9). This is the telling detail. Esau does not leave the marriages that grieved his parents. He adds a third wife. The Tyndale notes observe that he still does not understand what set the covenant family apart. He chooses a wife from the other line that was not chosen, as if being "more related" to Abraham were the point. Two sons set out to win their father's approval in these two chapters. One used deceit, the other a new marriage, and neither seems to understand that the blessing is God's to give.
  • Commentators: Henry says Esau thought that by pleasing his parents in one thing he could make up for other wrongs. JFB says he "became wise when too late," and "made bad worse" by joining a family God had set aside. Guzik reads it as Esau suddenly valuing what he had once despised and trying to win his father's favor.

Jacob's Dream (vv10–22)

  • "Jacob left Beersheba and went toward Haran" (v10). He is retracing in reverse the road Abraham traveled into Canaan (12:4–6), and the Tyndale notes put that some 125 years earlier. Bethel is about 60 miles north of Beersheba, a long walk of several days through the central hill country.
  • "A certain place" (v11). The Hebrew is literally "the place," with the definite article, and the word "place" (maqom) comes six times in vv11–19. In later Hebrew, "the place" can mean a holy site (compare 22:3–4; Deut 12:5). Jacob stops only "because the sun had set." He has no idea where he is. The point is that God meets him somewhere that seems ordinary.
  • "Taking one of the stones of the place, he put it under his head" (v11). The phrase is literally "at his head," so it may mean beside his head rather than under it as a pillow. The Tyndale notes suggest a large stone, more for shelter than comfort. Either way, the son who grew up in his parents' tents now sleeps on the ground with a rock for company. This is the same stone that becomes a pillar in the morning.
  • "A ladder set up on the earth" (v12). "Ladder" is sullam, a word the lexicon lists only here in the whole Bible. The ESV footnote gives "a flight of steps," and many scholars think of the ramp or stairway up the side of a Mesopotamian temple tower, a ziggurat. The Tyndale notes make that link. If so, the contrast with Babel is sharp. There people tried to build a tower "with its top in the heavens" to reach up to God (11:4). Here the stairway's "top reached to heaven," but God set it up, and he comes down to meet a man who was not looking for him.
  • "The angels of God were ascending and descending on it" (v12). Angels going up first has caught readers' attention. A Jewish tradition took it to mean the angels who guarded Jacob in the land went up, and others came down to go with him abroad. Others suggest only that they were already on earth doing God's errands. The Tyndale notes put the plain point simply: God and his messengers were with Jacob on his journey. They will meet him again, at Mahanaim, as he returns (32:1–2).
  • "The LORD stood above it" (v13). The ESV footnote gives "beside him" as an alternative, and the Hebrew allows either. Above the stairway or standing by the sleeping Jacob, the LORD is the one who speaks. There is a wordplay here too. The ladder was "set up" (v12) and the LORD "stood" (v13) use the verb natsab, and the "pillar" Jacob raises in v18 is matstsebah, a noun from the same root, as the lexicon notes. What God stood over, Jacob stands up as a marker.
  • "I am the LORD, the God of Abraham your father and the God of Isaac" (v13). God introduces himself to Jacob through his family. Abraham is called "your father," that is, grandfather. Then God gives, unasked and with no rebuke for chapter 27, everything promised to Abraham: the land "on which you lie," offspring "like the dust of the earth," spreading in all four directions, and "in you and your offspring shall all the families of the earth be blessed" (v14; compare 12:3; 13:14–16; 22:17–18). The Tyndale notes add that Jacob now inherits the whole Abrahamic covenant.
  • "I am with you and will keep you wherever you go" (v15). This goes beyond the earlier promises and fits Jacob's need exactly. He is leaving the land, so God promises to "bring you back to this land." He is alone, so God says, "I will not leave you until I have done what I have promised you." "Keep" means to guard or watch over, the word of the priestly blessing, "The LORD bless you and keep you" (Num 6:24), and of Psalm 121, "The LORD will keep your going out and your coming in."
  • "Surely the LORD is in this place, and I did not know it" (v16). Jacob's first words are an admission. He had thought of God as his father's God (27:20). Now he finds that God was present where he least expected it.
  • "How awesome is this place!" (v17). "Awesome" is from the verb "to fear," and the line begins "he was afraid." It is reverent dread, not panic. "The house of God" and "the gate of heaven" are temple language. The gate was where a city's business and judgment took place, and here it is where heaven opens onto earth. Ironically, "Babel" was popularly understood in Babylon as "gate of god." The true gate of heaven is where God chooses to come down.
  • "Set it up for a pillar and poured oil on the top of it" (v18). Standing stones (matstsebah) marked important places across the ancient Near East: a meeting with a god, a treaty, a grave. Jacob will set up others (31:45; 35:14, 20). Pouring oil set the stone apart as holy, much as oil later consecrated the tabernacle, its priests and Israel's kings (Exod 40:9; 1 Sam 10:1). Later law forbade such pillars because Canaanite worship had tied them to idols (Deut 16:22). Jacob's stone is a memorial, not an object of worship.
  • "He called the name of that place Bethel" (v19). Bethel means "house of God" (ESV footnote). The town had been called Luz, which is spelled like the Hebrew word for "almond tree" (30:37), though the lexicon lists the place name as a separate word. It is usually identified with the village of Beitin, about 12 miles north of Jerusalem. Abraham had built an altar near it (12:8; 13:3). Bethel had a mixed later history. It was a place of worship in the time of the judges (Judg 20:18) and on Samuel's circuit (1 Sam 7:16). But Jeroboam set up a golden calf there (1 Kgs 12:28–29), and the prophets mocked it as Beth-aven, "house of wickedness" (Hos 4:15; compare Amos 5:5).
  • "If God will be with me" (vv20–21). Jacob's vow lists back exactly what God has just promised: presence, protection, bread and clothing, and a safe return. Readers have heard it two ways. Some hear the old Jacob bargaining, setting terms with God. Others point out that ancient vows were normally phrased with "if... then" (compare Num 21:2; 1 Sam 1:11) and that Jacob is simply repeating God's own promise back to him, so the "if" amounts to "since." The text leaves room for both. Either way, God holds to his promise, and Jacob does return and fulfill his vow (35:1–7).
  • "Then the LORD shall be my God" (v21). This is the turn from "the LORD your God" (27:20) to "my God." It is the beginning of Jacob's own faith, though there is still a long road and a wrestling match ahead (32:24–30).
  • "Of all that you give me I will give a full tenth to you" (v22). The Hebrew doubles the verb: literally "tithing I will tithe." Abraham had given a tenth to Melchizedek (14:20). Giving a tenth was a familiar way in the ancient world to acknowledge a king or a god as the true owner. The Tyndale notes observe that the tithe confesses that everything comes from God, and Israel's law later built on it (Num 18:21; Deut 14:22). Notice the words "all that you give me." The man who took things now speaks of receiving them.
  • Commentators: Henry turns the scene into comfort: Jacob was alone, "yet he was not alone, for the Father was with him," and "God's time to visit his people with his comforts is when they are most destitute of other comforts." He sees Christ in the ladder: "Christ is the way; all God's favours come to us, and all our services go to him, by Christ." On v16 he writes, "We sometimes meet with God where we little thought of meeting with him," and on the tithe, "The tenth is a very fit proportion to be devoted to God." Guzik also reads the ladder through John 1:51 and John 14:6. On v16 he adds that Jacob was right that God was there, but wrong if he thought God was confined to one spot (Ps 139:7). Guzik and JFB part ways on the vow. Guzik sees Jacob making a deal instead of simply trusting a promise that had no conditions, while JFB argues the vow implies no doubt and that "if" should be read as "since." JFB notes that "some writers" doubt a literal ladder and picture instead a stepped hillside, and calls the oil "a consecration." JFB has no notes on vv14–15, 17 or 21–22.

Pointing to Christ

Jesus himself draws the line from this chapter to him. When Nathanael, "an Israelite indeed, in whom there is no deceit" (John 1:47), a sharp contrast with Jacob, marvels at him, Jesus answers, "you will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man" (John 1:51). Jesus is the stairway. He is the place where heaven and earth meet, the true Bethel and "gate of heaven." He is not a tower people build to climb up to God, but God come down. And the promise Jacob received on a stone in the dark, "I am with you and will keep you wherever you go... I will not leave you," is the promise Christ makes to all his people: "I am with you always, to the end of the age" (Matt 28:20). Jacob did nothing to earn that night's grace, and fled from his own wrongs straight into it. That is the gospel's shape too.

Reflection

  • Christ, the True Ladder to Heaven (v.12) — Jacob dreams of a stairway reaching from earth to heaven with angels moving between the two, a picture Jesus later applies to Himself as the connection between God and man. Are you actually using the access to God that Christ provides, or living as if heaven is still distant?
  • Grace Given to the Undeserving (vv.13-14) — God repeats the Abrahamic promises to Jacob not because he has earned them but purely as an act of grace toward a schemer fleeing his own family. Do you rest in the fact that your standing with God rests on His promise rather than your merit?
  • A Promise Made to a Man at His Worst (v.15) — God commits to stay with Jacob and finish what He started, speaking this to a deceiver on the run rather than waiting until Jacob cleaned himself up. Do you believe God meets you in your worst moments, or do you feel you need to get yourself right before approaching Him?
  • God Present Where We Don't Expect Him (v.16) — Jacob wakes up realizing the LORD was in that ordinary, unremarkable place all along, even though he hadn't recognized it. Where in your current circumstances might God be present in a way you haven't noticed yet?
  • Conditional Commitment vs. Full Surrender (vv.20-21) — Jacob's vow to God is framed as a bargain: if God provides and protects him, then God will be his God. Is your own commitment to God still conditional on what He does for you, or has it become genuine surrender?
