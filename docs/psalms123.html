Summary

A Song of Ascents in which the psalmist lifts his eyes to the LORD enthroned in heaven, comparing his posture to a servant watching his master's hand for direction (vv. 1-2). He pleads for mercy, describing how thoroughly he and the people have been filled with the contempt and scorn of the proud and those who are at ease (vv. 3-4).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Song of Ascents / Communal Lament
Attributed Author: Anonymous (superscription: "A Song of degrees")
Key Themes: Looking to God for mercy, patient waiting as a servant, contempt from the proud, humble dependence on God
Historical Context: Psalm 123 is the fourth Song of Ascents — a short prayer of four verses from a people experiencing contempt and scorn from those "at ease" (the arrogant wealthy). The central image is stunning: eyes fixed on God's hand like a servant watching a master for the slightest signal. The psalm likely reflects post-exilic Israel, despised by surrounding nations. It teaches that when scorned by the world, the proper response is not retaliation but looking upward — fixing your gaze on God's hand, waiting for His mercy to act.
Unto Thee Lift I Up Mine Eyes (v.1): Unto thee lift I up mine eyes, O thou that dwellest in the heavens. Psalm 121 lifted its eyes to the hills and had to ask what they were good for; this one goes straight past them. Four verses is the whole psalm.
As the Eyes of Servants unto the Hand of Their Masters (v.2): Behold, as the eyes of servants look unto the hand of their masters, and as the eyes of a maiden unto the hand of her mistress; so our eyes wait upon the LORD our God. The comparison is exact and worth slowing down for: a servant watches the hand, not the face, because instructions and provision both come from there. What is described is attention without any power to hurry the outcome, which the last clause admits, until that he have mercy upon us.
Have Mercy upon Us, O LORD (v.3): Have mercy upon us, O LORD, have mercy upon us: for we are exceedingly filled with contempt. The request is doubled because the psalm has nothing else to offer, and the ground given is not innocence but saturation. Filled is a word for being full of food.
The Scorning of Those That Are at Ease (v.4): Our soul is exceedingly filled with the scorning of those that are at ease, and with the contempt of the proud. The psalm's one piece of analysis is in the phrase at ease: the scorn comes from people whose own position is comfortable, and that is offered as the explanation of how they can afford it. The psalm ends here, with no reply and no rescue, which is the servant's posture of verse 2 held to the last line.

Deep Dive

Setting: Psalm 123 is the fourth of the fifteen "Songs of Ascents" (Psalms 120–134), the pilgrim songs sung on the way up to Jerusalem for the great festivals. Psalm 122 ended inside the city gates, praying for Jerusalem's peace. Psalm 123 lifts its eyes higher, past the city and its temple to the throne of God in heaven. It is one of the shortest psalms, only four verses, and it has no author's name. It does not say who was mocking God's people or when. Many scholars think it fits the years after the return from exile, when the returned Jews were small, poor and laughed at by their neighbors. Sanballat and Tobiah sneered at the "feeble Jews" rebuilding Jerusalem's walls (Neh 2:19; 4:1–4), and "great trouble and shame" was the report from the city (Neh 1:3). But the psalm is written so that any of God's people, in any age of contempt, can pray it. Its movement is simple: a single voice looks up ("I"), the community joins in ("our eyes"), and together they ask for mercy. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's two halves.

To You I Lift Up My Eyes (vv1–2)

  • "To you I lift up my eyes" (v1). Psalm 121 began, "I lift up my eyes to the hills." Here the pilgrim looks past the hills altogether, straight to God. The words "to you" come first in the Hebrew for emphasis: not to the mountains, not to the city, not to any human helper, but to you. Lifting the eyes is a picture of prayer and expectation. It is the posture of someone who knows help must come from above.
  • "O you who are enthroned in the heavens!" (v1). The verb is yashab, "to sit" or "to dwell," and when used of God it often means to sit as king (Ps 2:4; 29:10). The ESV's "enthroned" catches that. The point is not that God is far away but that he rules. Those who scorn God's people sit "at ease" on earth (v4). God sits on the throne of heaven, above all of them. Jesus taught his disciples to pray the same way: "Our Father in heaven" (Matt 6:9).
  • "As the eyes of servants look to the hand of their master" (v2). In a large household, servants stood quietly near their master and watched his hand. A gesture could mean "bring this," "clear that," "come here" or "go." A good servant kept his eyes fixed there so he would not miss the signal. The hand also gave: wages, food, protection, and sometimes discipline. The picture is attentive, patient dependence.
  • "As the eyes of a maidservant to the hand of her mistress" (v2). The psalm doubles the image, man and woman, master and mistress, so that every member of the household is pictured. Hagar, Sarah's servant, is the most famous maidservant in Israel's story, and the "God who sees" met her when her mistress's hand was hard against her (Gen 16:6–13). The psalm assumes a better master than Sarai.
  • "So our eyes look to the LORD our God" (v2). The single "I" of v1 becomes "our." The pilgrim is not alone. The covenant name, the LORD (YHWH), and the personal "our God" sit side by side. The one enthroned in heaven is also the God who bound himself to this people.
  • "Till he has mercy upon us" (v2). This is the length of the wait. The servants do not glance once and look away. They keep looking until the master acts. Waiting on God, in the Psalms, is not a shrug. It is steady, expectant attention that refuses to look anywhere else for rescue (Ps 25:15; 130:5–6). The word for "have mercy" (chanan) means to show favor or grace freely, to someone who has no claim on it.
  • Commentators: Henry calls heaven "a place of prospect and a place of power": God sees his people's troubles from there and has the power to help. He expands the servant's watching into a list of what the master's hand does: direct, supply, assist, protect, correct and reward. JFB finds the same range in the figure, saying it expresses "deference, submission, and trust," and takes "dwellest in the heavens" as God "sitting as enthroned." Guzik pictures a butler standing behind his seated master at dinner, alert to the slightest sign, and stresses that the psalmist does not demand an instant answer but keeps looking until mercy comes.

Have Mercy upon Us, O LORD (vv3–4)

  • "Have mercy upon us, O LORD, have mercy upon us" (v3). The last word of v2, "mercy," becomes the first word of v3, and then it is repeated. The prayer gets shorter and more urgent. There is no argument here, no list of the people's merits and no claim that they deserve better. They simply ask for grace. The tax collector in Jesus' parable prayed the same way: "God, be merciful to me, a sinner!" (Luke 18:13), and blind Bartimaeus cried, "Son of David, have mercy on me!" (Mark 10:47).
  • "For we have had more than enough of contempt" (v3). The Hebrew word behind "had more than enough" is saba', "to be satisfied" or "full," the word used of eating until full (Deut 8:10). Here it is turned around: they have been fed contempt until they cannot swallow any more. "Contempt" (buz) means being looked down on, treated as worthless. It is not physical violence but it wounds deeply. Being mocked for faithfulness wears down the soul.
  • "Our soul has had more than enough" (v4). The same verb comes back, made stronger in the Hebrew ("our soul is greatly filled"). The contempt has not stayed on the surface. It has reached the inner life.
  • "The scorn of those who are at ease" (v4). "At ease" translates sha'anan, which means secure and untroubled, but often with the added sense of careless, complacent and arrogant. Amos uses it for the wealthy who lounge on ivory beds: "Woe to those who are at ease in Zion" (Amos 6:1). Zechariah uses it of "the nations that are at ease," who added to Israel's misery (Zech 1:15). People with comfortable lives who do not need God often find it easy to laugh at those who do.
  • "The contempt of the proud" (v4). The proud are those who lift themselves up. The psalm ends with them, which is striking. There is no line of resolution, no "and the LORD answered me." The last word in the Hebrew is "the proud." The psalm leaves them hanging there, and the reader is left where the psalmist is, still looking up, still waiting. That itself is a kind of answer. The scornful are "at ease" on earth, but the one the psalmist looks to is "enthroned in the heavens." Mary sang of the same God: "he has scattered the proud... and exalted those of humble estate" (Luke 1:51–52).
  • Who were the scorners? The psalm does not say. Many link it to the time of Nehemiah, when Israel's neighbors mocked the rebuilding of Jerusalem (Neh 2:19; 4:1–4). Others see it as a song of any time of humiliation, before or after the exile. Because the enemies are unnamed, believers in every era have been able to pray it as their own.
  • Commentators: Henry notes that they ask for mercy without pleading any merit, and that "we find little mercy with men." He is blunt that "scorning and contempt have been, and are, and are likely to be, the lot of God's people in this world." JFB links the contempt to "the heathen, and, perhaps, Samaritans" in Nehemiah's day and describes those at ease as self-complacent people who disregard God's law and despise his people. The lexicon supports that reading of sha'anan: it lists "careless, wanton, arrogant" as one of its senses, with this verse among the examples. Guzik adds that some contempt slides off easily, but other times it fills a person until it lays them low, and then only God's mercy can lift them. He hears in the psalm a quiet confidence that God's mercy will outlast the scorn of the proud.

Pointing to Christ

No one has known "the scorn of those who are at ease" and "the contempt of the proud" like Jesus. "He was despised and rejected by men" (Isa 53:3). At the cross the comfortable and the powerful mocked him: "He trusted in God; let God deliver him now" (Matt 27:43). Through it all he kept his eyes on his Father, the true Servant looking to the hand of his Master. He "learned obedience through what he suffered" (Heb 5:8), and "when he was reviled, he did not revile in return... but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23). The Father answered by raising him and seating him at his right hand. Now the throne in heaven that the psalmist looked toward is the "throne of grace," where believers "receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need" (Heb 4:16), because the one who sits beside it was scorned in their place. Christians are told to run their race "looking to Jesus... who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame" (Heb 12:2).

Reflection

  • Lifting Eyes to the One Enthroned (v.1) — The psalmist looks past his circumstances straight to God, who is seated in the heavens. When you're under pressure, is your first move to look up to God or to fixate on the people causing the pain?
  • Watching Like a Servant for Mercy (v.2) — He compares himself to a servant watching his master's hand, waiting attentively for the signal of mercy. Are you willing to wait actively and expectantly for God to act, rather than growing impatient or numb?
  • Bringing Contempt to God Instead of Retaliating (vv.3-4) — Overwhelmed by the scorn of the proud and complacent, he takes his hurt straight to God in prayer rather than striking back. When you're mocked or looked down on, do you bring that pain to God, or let it fester into bitterness?
