Summary

The Preacher praises wisdom for brightening a person's countenance, then counsels obedience to the king's command since royal authority is absolute and no one can question it (vv. 1-4). He advises that the wise heart knows there is a right time and way to act, even though people cannot know the future or the timing of their own death, and that no one escapes life's ultimate battle (vv. 5-8). He grapples with the troubling fact that the wicked are sometimes honored and forgotten in peace at their burial, and that because judgment on evil is delayed, people's hearts grow bold to do wrong (vv. 9-11). Still, he affirms that ultimately it will go well for those who fear God, and not well for the wicked, even though on earth the righteous sometimes suffer as the wicked prosper and vice versa (vv. 12-14). He closes by commending enjoyment of food, drink, and labor as life's fitting portion, admitting that the full scope of God's work under the sun lies beyond human ability to discover (vv. 15-17).

Authorship & Background

Author: 'The Preacher' (Qoheleth), traditionally identified as Solomon. Written late in life as a reflection on life's meaning. Hebrew title: 'Qoheleth' — 'The Assembler/Preacher.'
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: Authority obeyed without illusions about it, sentences delayed until the heart is set on evil, the fear of God distinguished from outcomes, and God's work declared beyond finding out
Historical Context: Chapter 8 addresses two great difficulties of life "under the sun": the problem of human authority (vv.1-9) and the problem of delayed justice (vv.10-17). The Preacher first counsels wisdom in dealing with those in power — keep the king's command, know when to speak and when to be silent, recognize that earthly authority is real even when unjust. The second section wrestles with one of the Bible's most persistent questions: why do the wicked prosper and the righteous suffer? Solomon observes that "sentence against an evil work is not executed speedily" (v.11) and this delay emboldens sinners. Yet he affirms faith: "it shall be well with them that fear God" (v.12). The chapter concludes with another enjoyment passage (v.15) and the humbling admission that God's ways are ultimately beyond human comprehension (v.17). This is wisdom literature at its most honest — affirming both faith and mystery simultaneously.
Wisdom Under Authority (vv.1-9): Practical counsel for living near power without illusions about it. Keep the king's commandment, do not be hasty to leave his presence, and be aware that where the word of a king is there is power and no one can say to him what doest thou. Verse 8's four clauses widen it — no man has power over the spirit, over the day of death, over war, or to be delivered by wickedness. Authority is real and also bounded.
The Problem of Delayed Justice (vv.10-14): The observation is exact and uncomfortable: because sentence against an evil work is not executed speedily, the heart of men is fully set in them to do evil. Delay is named as a cause of wickedness rather than merely a frustration. He then holds two things together without reconciling them — it shall be well with them that fear God, and there are just men to whom it happens according to the work of the wicked. He calls the second one vanity and does not withdraw the first.
Eat, Drink, and Be Merry (v.15): The fourth enjoyment conclusion, and by now the refrain has weight behind it. Placed directly after the injustice he has just described, commending mirth is not evasion but the counsel of someone who has looked at the alternative and found nothing better under the sun.
God's Work Not Found Out (vv.16-17): He applied his heart to know wisdom and to see the business done on the earth, and records what the effort produced: that a man cannot find out the work of God, though he labour to seek it and go without sleep. Even if a wise man says he knows it, he cannot find it. The chapter ends by naming the limit rather than lamenting it.

Deep Dive

Setting: Chapter 7 ended with the Preacher admitting that wisdom was "far off, and deep, very deep" (7:24), and with his one sure finding: "God made man upright, but they have sought out many schemes" (7:29). Chapter 8 carries on with wisdom in practice, starting in the royal court. How should a wise person live under a king with absolute power (vv1–9)? Then he turns again to the problem that keeps troubling him: the wicked are honored at their funerals, justice is slow, and good and bad people often get what the other deserves (vv10–14). Here he says some of his firmest words of faith, "I know that it will be well with those who fear God" (v12). He ends by commending joy once more (v15) and admitting that no one, however wise, can figure out all that God is doing (vv16–17). For the question of who wrote the book, see the Setting of chapter 1.

Keep the King's Command (vv1–9)

  • "Who is like the wise? And who knows the interpretation of a thing?" (v1). A rhetorical question: there are few truly wise people. "Interpretation" (pesher) is the word for explaining something hidden. Its Aramaic form is used throughout Daniel for interpreting dreams (Dan 2:4–7; 5:12), and Daniel is the Bible's model of a wise man at a royal court.
  • "A man's wisdom makes his face shine, and the hardness of his face is changed" (v1). A "hard face" is a stern or insolent look (Deut 28:50; Prov 7:13). Wisdom softens it. The wise person is gracious and calm, not harsh. The Tyndale notes say that worry, anger and frustration harden a face, and wisdom brightens it. JFB calls it "a sunny countenance, the reflection of a tranquil conscience," and links it to Moses' face shining after meeting God (Exod 34:29). The priestly blessing asks that the LORD "make his face to shine upon you" (Num 6:25).
  • "Keep the king's command, because of God's oath to him" (v2). The Hebrew is literally "because of the oath of God," which can be read two ways. It may be the oath of loyalty that subjects swore to their king before God. Israel's elders made a covenant with David "before the LORD" (2 Sam 5:3), and the leaders pledged allegiance to Solomon (1 Chr 29:24). Clarke, cited by Guzik, gives these. Or it may be God's own oath to the king, his covenant with David's house (2 Sam 7:12–16; Ps 89:3–4, 35), which the ESV's "to him" suggests. Either way, obedience to the king is tied to God. Guzik connects this to Paul's teaching that rulers are "instituted by God" (Rom 13:1–7). JFB takes "the king" here as God himself.
  • "Be not hasty to go from his presence. Do not take your stand in an evil cause" (v3). Do not storm out of the king's presence in anger, or quit his service in a huff. And do not join a plot against him. "Take your stand" can also mean "persist." Do not dig in once you are in the wrong.
  • "For he does whatever he pleases… who may say to him, 'What are you doing?'" (vv3–4). In the ancient world a king's word was law. Opposing him could cost one's life. These words are close to how Scripture speaks of God (Job 9:12; Dan 4:35). The courtier's wisdom is a picture of how much more one should take God's word seriously, as Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, applies it.
  • But obedience has limits. The Bible honors those who refused a king's command when it went against God's: the Hebrew midwives (Exod 1:17), Daniel (Dan 6:10), his three friends (Dan 3:16–18), and the apostles: "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). Guzik and Kidner both make this point. The Preacher is giving everyday advice for a normal court, not saying a king must be obeyed in sin.
  • "Whoever keeps a command will know no evil thing, and the wise heart will know the proper time and the just way" (v5). "Proper time and the just way" is literally "time and judgment" ('et umishpat). The wise know when to speak, when to act and how to do it rightly. The theme of "a time for everything" from 3:1–8 returns here. JFB also hears in "judgment" the future judgment of God (3:17).
  • "For there is a time and a way for everything, although man's trouble lies heavy on him. For he does not know what is to be" (vv6–7). The wise know there is a right time. But no one, not even the wise, knows what that time will bring. That uncertainty is a weight every person carries (6:12; 7:14).
  • "No man has power to retain the spirit, or power over the day of death" (v8). "Spirit" (ruach) can also mean "wind," and some translations take it that way: no one can stop the wind. But in context it means the breath of life (12:7), and JFB reads it so. No one can hold on to his own life when its time comes. Even a king with power over others (v4) has no power over that.
  • "There is no discharge from war, nor will wickedness deliver those who are given to it" (v8). In Israel some men were excused from military service (Deut 20:5–8). But from this war, death, there is no exemption. JFB points to the military allusion. And wickedness, however clever or rich it makes someone, will not save him on that day. "Those who are given to it" is literally "its masters." Even those who have mastered wickedness cannot use it to escape death.
  • "When man had power over man to his hurt" (v9). The section ends with an observation about power. Rulers often harm their subjects. JFB notes they may also harm themselves, as Rehoboam did (1 Kgs 12).
  • Commentators: Henry reads the section as calling us "to keep in due subjection to the government God has set over us," and to "get ready for sudden evils, and especially for sudden death." Guzik notes, with a smile, that advice to obey the king sounds a little self-serving coming from a king. He insists that God's law outranks the king's when they conflict. JFB on v8: in "that war" of death "there is no exemption."

Those Who Fear God Will Do Well (vv10–13)

  • "Then I saw the wicked buried. They used to go in and out of the holy place and were praised in the city where they had done such things" (v10). This verse is hard in Hebrew, and translations vary. The picture seems to be this. Wicked people who came and went at the temple, keeping up a religious front, died and got an honored burial. Then they were praised in the very city where everyone knew what they had done. Most Hebrew manuscripts read "forgotten" (yishtakkechu). A few manuscripts and the Greek translation read "praised" (yishtabbechu), and the two Hebrew words differ by a single letter. The ESV follows "praised" and gives "forgotten" in its footnote. Either way, it is hebel: the wicked were honored, or the righteous were forgotten, and either one is unjust.
  • "Because the sentence against an evil deed is not executed speedily, the heart of the children of man is fully set to do evil" (v11). This is one of the most quoted verses in the book. When punishment is slow, people conclude that it will never come, and they grow bold. "Sentence" (pitgam) is a Persian loanword for an official decree (Esth 1:20). Israel's history shows it: David failed to punish his son Amnon, and Absalom took matters into his own hands (2 Sam 13:21–29). JFB adds Joab, who went on murdering because he was never punished. God's patience is not his indifference. Peter says it plainly: "The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish" (2 Pet 3:9). Paul warns against presuming on "the riches of his kindness and forbearance" (Rom 2:4).
  • "Though a sinner does evil a hundred times and prolongs his life, yet I know that it will be well with those who fear God, because they fear before him" (v12). A remarkable turn. The Preacher has just admitted that the wicked often prosper. He does not deny what he sees. But he states what he knows: those who fear God will be well. "I know" is not what he observes under the sun. It is faith in God's justice. The Tyndale notes say injustice is temporary and does not upset God's plan (3:17; 12:14). Guzik reads it more cautiously, as a hope within the under-the-sun view.
  • "But it will not be well with the wicked, neither will he prolong his days like a shadow, because he does not fear before God" (v13). This seems to contradict v12, where the sinner "prolongs his life." But the Preacher is looking further. Even if the wicked live long by human count, their days are a shadow, gone at sundown (6:12). JFB says the wicked man's long days are only apparent: measured against eternity, they are short. The difference between the two is the fear of God, named three times in vv12–13.
  • Commentators: Henry draws two lessons: oppressors grow bolder when they go unpunished (v11), and in the end it will go well with the righteous and badly with the wicked (vv12–13). Guzik passes on Maclaren's line that human ingratitude for God's patience is as deep a mystery as that patience itself. JFB uses an old saying on v11: people do not fear the fire because they do not see the smoke of the pit.

Man Cannot Know God's Ways (vv14–17)

  • "There are righteous people to whom it happens according to the deeds of the wicked, and there are wicked people to whom it happens according to the deeds of the righteous" (v14). He circles back to the problem (7:15). Good people get what bad people deserve, and bad people get what good people deserve. He does not explain it away. He calls it hebel, a vapor beyond his grasp. This is the question of Job and of Psalm 73. Guzik notes it needs an eternal view to be answered.
  • "And I commend joy, for man has nothing better under the sun but to eat and drink and be joyful" (v15). The fifth enjoyment passage (2:24; 3:12–13, 22; 5:18–20), and the most emphatic. "Commend" is literally "praise," the same verb as in 4:2, where he "thought the dead… more fortunate." There he praised the dead. Here he praises joy. Faced with injustice he cannot fix, he does not tell people to despair. He tells them to receive the gifts of daily life with joy. The Tyndale notes say the Hebrew means finding joy in ordinary daily life, not chasing entertainment. This is not "eat, drink and be merry, for tomorrow we die" in the careless sense Paul condemns (1 Cor 15:32), because these are days "that God has given him."
  • "For this will go with him in his toil through the days of his life" (v15). "Go with him" means "accompany" or "stay with." JFB notes the word is literally "adhere." Joy is the companion God gives for the hard road of work. It will not remove the toil, but it will walk with him through it.
  • "When I applied my heart to know wisdom… how neither day nor night do one's eyes see sleep" (v16). He searched without rest. The "business" ('inyan) is the same word as the "unhappy business" of 1:13. People work so hard at living they lose sleep, and the Preacher worked just as hard at understanding them.
  • "Then I saw all the work of God, that man cannot find out the work that is done under the sun" (v17). Here is the conclusion of all his searching. He saw "the work of God," and he could not figure it out. The phrase "cannot find out" comes three times in this one verse. However hard a person tries, and even if a wise man claims to know, no one can see the whole of what God is doing. This echoes 3:11: God has put eternity in our hearts, "yet so that he cannot find out what God has done from the beginning to the end." The Tyndale notes say some reasons, such as the mystery of God's justice, will remain hidden.
  • Commentators: Henry's lesson on the end of the chapter is to "humbly and silently adore the depth of his unsearchable counsels," and on v15, to "cheerfully use the gifts of God's providence." JFB says that since we cannot discover the reasons for God's dealings, believers should accept them as good because they are his. JFB also notes, a little uneasily, that "abide" may be "too strong language, perhaps, for a religious man to apply to 'eating' and 'mirth.'" Guzik passes on Eaton's advice to be content not to know everything, and observes that the Preacher is beginning to see that what he observes under the sun is not the whole of reality.

Pointing to Christ

"Because the sentence against an evil deed is not executed speedily…" (v11). The Preacher saw justice delayed and the wicked buried with honor. The gospel says the sentence has been carried out, but not on the guilty. At the cross, the judgment against sin fell on Christ, the one righteous man to whom it happened "according to the deeds of the wicked" (v14). "He was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities" (Isa 53:5). And the delay of final judgment is God's mercy, giving time for repentance (2 Pet 3:9). The Preacher said, "I know that it will be well with those who fear God" (v12), and could not see how. In Christ we can. The King whose word is supreme (v4) is the Son, to whom the Father has given all authority (Matt 28:18). No one has power over the day of death (v8), but Christ does: "I died, and behold I am alive forevermore, and I have the keys of Death and Hades" (Rev 1:18).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik highlights v.11 as perhaps the single most important verse for understanding human behavior in the face of God's patience. People sin boldly because consequences are not immediate — they confuse delay with absence. But v.12 represents Solomon's faith breaking through: despite everything he observes, he "knows" (not merely hopes) that it will be well with God-fearers. The chapter models honest faith: acknowledging what you cannot explain while affirming what you know to be true.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Wisdom for Living Under Imperfect Power (vv.2-5) — The Preacher counsels keeping the king's command and knowing when to speak and when to stay quiet under an authority you didn't choose. How do you handle authority you disagree with without either exploding at it or worshiping it?
  • When Consequences Don't Come Quickly (v.11) — Because sentence against wrongdoing isn't carried out right away, people grow bold enough to keep doing it. Where has delayed consequence made you, or someone you know, mistake God's patience for His indifference?
  • Trusting What You Can't Yet See Proven (v.12) — Despite all the confusing evidence around him, the Preacher still affirms that it will go well for those who fear God. What do you know to be true about God's character that you need to hold onto even when circumstances seem to argue otherwise?
  • Joy That Doesn't Wait for Answers (v.15) — He commends simple enjoyment — eating, drinking, finding gladness in labor — as the fitting response to a world he can't fully explain. Are you withholding joy until every mystery in your life gets resolved?
  • Making Peace With What You Can't Figure Out (v.17) — He concludes that no one, however hard they search, can fully grasp the work God is doing under the sun. Can you rest in not knowing instead of demanding an explanation for everything?
