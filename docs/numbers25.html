Summary

While camped at Shittim, Israelite men begin relationships with Moabite women and are drawn into worshiping their gods, provoking the Lord's anger and a plague against the people (vv. 1-3, 9). God commands that the leaders of the offenders be executed, and as Moses and the community weep at the entrance of the tent, an Israelite man brazenly brings a Midianite woman into his tent in full view of everyone (vv. 4-6). Phinehas, grandson of Aaron, follows them and kills both with a spear, an act of zeal that stops the plague, which had already killed 24,000 people (vv. 7-9). Because of his zeal for God's honor, the Lord grants Phinehas and his descendants a covenant of lasting priesthood (vv. 10-13).

Authorship & Background

Author: Moses. Numbers is the fourth book of the Pentateuch, written during the wilderness period (approximately 1445-1405 BC). Hebrew title: "Bemidbar" — "In the wilderness." The book records Israel's 38 years of wilderness wandering between Sinai and the Promised Land — a journey that should have taken 11 days (Deuteronomy 1:2) but took 40 years due to unbelief.
Historical Context: Chapter 25 records one of Israel's darkest hours — the sin at Baal-Peor. Having failed to curse Israel through Balaam's oracles (chapters 22-24), the enemy succeeds through seduction. Revelation 2:14 reveals that Balaam counseled Balak to use Moabite and Midianite women to entice Israel into sexual immorality and idolatry. The strategy works: Israel "yokes" itself to Baal of Peor, provoking God's fierce anger. A plague kills 24,000 before Phinehas, grandson of Aaron, acts decisively — driving a spear through an Israelite man and a Midianite woman in the very act of their sin. God rewards Phinehas with a "covenant of peace" and a "covenant of perpetual priesthood." This chapter demonstrates that what Balaam's curses could not accomplish, Israel's own sin nearly achieves: their destruction. The enemy's most effective weapon against God's people is not external attack but internal corruption.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: seduction succeeding at Baal-Peor where three chapters of failed curses could not, Israelite men yoking themselves to a local god through Moabite women's invitation to sacrificial feasts, a plague spreading through the camp even while the people weep openly at the Tabernacle entrance, a single spear thrust by Phinehas stopping the plague after twenty-four thousand have already died, and a covenant of peace and of an everlasting priesthood granted, paradoxically, to the man who acted with a spear rather than a word
Israel's Sin at Shittim (vv.1-3): Israel dwells at Shittim (Abel-shittim, "meadow of acacias") in the plains of Moab, directly across from Jericho. The people "began to commit whoredom" (KJV) / "began to whore" (ESV) with Moabite women — both literal sexual immorality and spiritual adultery through idol worship. The women invite Israel to their sacrificial feasts (v.2) — eating, bowing down, and "yoking" themselves to Baal of Peor (v.3). "Baal-Peor" means "lord of Peor" — a local deity worshiped at Mount Peor with sexually immoral rites. The progression is deliberate: social contact → sexual immorality → religious participation → covenant with a false god.
God's Judgment and Moses' Response (vv.4-5): God commands Moses to "take all the heads of the people and hang them up before the LORD against the sun" (KJV) — public execution of the ringleaders as a deterrent. Moses instructs the judges to execute those who joined themselves to Baal-Peor (v.5). The judgment is swift and public — sin in the covenant community demands corporate accountability.
Phinehas' Zeal (vv.6-9): In brazen defiance, an Israelite man (later identified as Zimri, a Simeonite prince) brings a Midianite woman (Cozbi, daughter of a Midianite chief) into the camp "in the sight of Moses and in the sight of the whole congregation" (v.6) — while the people are weeping at the tabernacle entrance over the plague already underway. Phinehas, son of Eleazar, son of Aaron, rises from the congregation, takes a spear, follows them into the tent, and pierces both through. The plague stops — but not before 24,000 have died (v.9).
God's Covenant with Phinehas (vv.10-13): God declares that Phinehas "turned back my wrath" because "he was zealous for my sake among them" (KJV) / "he was jealous with my jealousy" (ESV). God gives him: (1) "my covenant of peace" (v.12) — ironic and beautiful: the man of the spear receives peace; and (2) "the covenant of an everlasting priesthood" (v.13) — his line will serve as priests perpetually. Phinehas "made atonement" for Israel — his act of righteous judgment turned away God's consuming wrath.
The Guilty Named (vv.14-15): The Israelite man: Zimri son of Salu, "a prince of a chief house among the Simeonites" (v.14). The Midianite woman: Cozbi daughter of Zur, "head over a people" in Midian (v.15). Both are nobility — this was a deliberate, high-level act of defiance, not a private sin of common people.
Command Against Midian (vv.16-18): God commands Moses to "vex" (KJV) / "harass" (ESV) the Midianites and strike them — "for they vex you with their wiles" (v.18). The Midianites used deception ("wiles") as a weapon — seduction as warfare. This command is fulfilled in chapter 31.

Deep Dive

Setting: Israel is camped on the plains of Moab, across the Jordan from Jericho, at the end of the forty years. Up on the hills Balaam has just blessed Israel four times, unable to curse them however much Balak paid (chs 22–24). Down in the camp, Israel was safe from every curse. But what no curse could do, Israel does to itself. This chapter is the last great rebellion of the wilderness years and one of the darkest. It is the first generation's golden calf all over again, committed by the new generation at the very edge of the promised land. Chapter 31 reveals what lay behind it: Balaam's own advice (31:16).

Baal Worship at Peor (vv1–9)

  • "While Israel lived in Shittim" (v1). Shittim means "acacias," a grove of acacia trees on the plains northeast of the Dead Sea. Joshua will send the spies to Jericho from here (Josh 2:1). The ark itself was made of acacia wood (Exod 25:10). The place where Israel should have been preparing to cross becomes the scene of its fall.
  • "The people began to whore with the daughters of Moab" (v1). The word is literal here, sexual immorality with Moabite women, but in the prophets it is also the standard picture for spiritual unfaithfulness, Israel leaving God as an unfaithful wife leaves her husband (Hos 1–3; Jer 3). Here both meanings happen at once.
  • "These invited the people to the sacrifices of their gods, and the people ate and bowed down" (v2). The steps are clear: an invitation, a feast, a meal of meat sacrificed to idols, then worship. Pagan worship often included sacred meals and, at fertility shrines, ritual sex. Psalm 106:28 says they "ate sacrifices offered to the dead," which may mean lifeless idols, or rites honoring the dead. Paul warns the Corinthians from this very story against eating at idol feasts (1 Cor 10:7–8, 20–21), and Jesus rebukes the church at Pergamum for holding "the teaching of Balaam, who taught Balak to put a stumbling block before the sons of Israel, so that they might eat food sacrificed to idols and practice sexual immorality" (Rev 2:14).
  • "So Israel yoked himself to Baal of Peor" (v3). The verb (tsamad) means to bind or attach, like an animal yoked to a plough. Israel tied itself to a new master. "Baal" means "lord" or "owner," the storm and fertility god worshiped across Canaan in many local forms. Baal of Peor was the form worshiped at Peor, the mountain where Balaam had just stood to bless Israel (23:28). Some think it was another name for Chemosh, Moab's god. The scholars are not agreed. The episode became a byword for apostasy (Deut 4:3; Josh 22:17; Hos 9:10).
  • "The anger of the LORD was kindled against Israel" (v3). The same words that were used of Balaam (22:22). God, who just refused to let a pagan curse Israel, now acts in judgment against them himself. Outside enemies could not harm them, but sin could.
  • "Take all the chiefs of the people and hang them in the sun before the LORD" (v4). The exact method is uncertain. The verb may mean impaling or exposing bodies after execution (2 Sam 21:6, 9). "In the sun" means publicly, in broad daylight. The meaning of "all the chiefs" is debated: either all the leaders, held responsible for the people's sin, or the leaders who took part. Moses' order in v5 suggests the guilty ones.
  • "Each of you kill those of his men who have yoked themselves to Baal of Peor" (v5). The judges appointed back in Exodus 18:21–26 are to carry out the sentence in their own units. Under the covenant, idolatry was a capital crime (Exod 22:20; Deut 13:6–10), because it broke the nation's bond with God at its root. This was Israel as a theocracy, with God as its king. The church does not carry the sword like this; the New Testament applies these stories by church discipline and repentance (1 Cor 5:1–13).
  • "One of the people of Israel came and brought a Midianite woman to his family" (v6). While the people are "weeping in the entrance of the tent of meeting," mourning and pleading with God as the plague spreads, a leader walks his Midianite partner past them in full view of Moses. It is open defiance. The Midianites had joined Moab in hiring Balaam (22:4, 7).
  • "Phinehas the son of Eleazar, son of Aaron the priest" (v7). Aaron's grandson, son of the new high priest (20:28). He will later lead Israel's army against Midian as priest (31:6) and serve as high priest himself (Judg 20:28).
  • "Went after the man of Israel into the chamber and pierced both of them… the woman through her belly" (v8). The word for "chamber" (qubbah) appears only here in the Old Testament and means a large vaulted tent. Some think it was a shrine-tent set up for ritual sex. There is a wordplay: qubbah, "tent," and qobah, "belly." One thrust killed both, which suggests they were caught in the act.
  • "Thus the plague on the people of Israel was stopped" (v8). The text has not mentioned a plague until now. It was already raging as God's judgment.
  • "Those who died by the plague were twenty-four thousand" (v9). Paul says "twenty-three thousand fell in a single day" (1 Cor 10:8). Several honest explanations have been offered: Paul counts those who died "in a single day" while Moses gives the total; or the 24,000 includes the leaders executed in vv4–5; or both are round numbers. None of them requires a contradiction. Either way, this is the last mass judgment of the wilderness years. The census of chapter 26 confirms that the old generation is gone (26:64–65).
  • Commentators: Henry: "We are more endangered by the charms of a smiling world than by the terrors of a frowning world," and "Those are our worst enemies that draw us to sin, for that is the greatest mischief any man can do us." He adds, "Ringleaders in sin ought to be made examples of justice." Guzik links the chapter to Balaam's counsel (31:16) and observes that Israel did more harm to itself by disobedience than any outside enemy could. JFB calls Shittim "a verdant meadow, so called from a grove of acacia trees," and thinks the god was really Chemosh, whose rites were marked by "the grossest obscenity." JFB solves the number by saying 23,000 died of the plague and the 24,000 includes those the judges executed. The Tyndale notes suggest Zimri's tent may have been an unauthorized shrine.

The Zeal of Phinehas (vv10–18)

  • "He was jealous with my jealousy among them" (v11). The word (qana) means zeal or jealousy, and it is a word God uses of himself: "I the LORD your God am a jealous God" (Exod 20:5). God's jealousy is not petty. It is the right passion of a faithful husband who will not share his bride. Phinehas felt what God felt, and acted for God's honor, not his own.
  • "So that I did not consume the people of Israel in my jealousy" (v11). One man's act ended a judgment that could have destroyed the nation. Compare Moses standing in the breach after the golden calf (Exod 32:11–14; Ps 106:23) and Aaron running with the censer between the dead and the living (16:46–48).
  • "I give to him my covenant of peace" (v12). "Peace" (shalom) means wholeness, right relationship and well-being. Malachi recalls it: "My covenant with him was one of life and peace" (Mal 2:5). "A perpetual priesthood" (v13). The high priesthood descended through Phinehas. Zadok, high priest under Solomon, came from his line (1 Chr 6:4–8), and Ezekiel later restricts the temple priesthood to the sons of Zadok (Ezek 44:15).
  • "Because he was jealous for his God and made atonement for the people of Israel" (v13). "Made atonement" (kipper) means to cover or wipe away guilt and turn aside wrath. Usually atonement was made with sacrificial blood. Here it is made by executing judgment on the open rebels, which turned God's anger from the whole people. Psalm 106:30–31 says Phinehas's act "was counted to him as righteousness," the very phrase used of Abraham's faith (Gen 15:6).
  • Was this vigilante justice? No. Moses had just ordered the guilty executed (v5), Phinehas was a priest and leader, and the crime was committed openly at the tabernacle. The story does not license private violence against sinners, and Jewish tradition itself argued about how far "zeal like Phinehas" could go. In Jesus' day, the Zealots took Phinehas as their model for armed revolt against Rome. Jesus' disciples, when they wanted to call down fire, were rebuked (Luke 9:54–55).
  • "Zimri the son of Salu, chief of a father's house belonging to the Simeonites" (v14). He was a leader, which makes his act worse. Simeon's numbers fall sharply between the two censuses, from 59,300 (1:23) to 22,200 (26:14). Many think the plague fell heavily on his tribe.
  • "Cozbi the daughter of Zur, who was the tribal head of a father's house in Midian" (v15). Zur is later named among the five kings of Midian (31:8). This was not a chance affair. A Midianite princess with an Israelite prince looks like a deliberate scheme.
  • "Harass the Midianites and strike them down, for they have harassed you with their wiles" (vv17–18). The same verb twice: they harassed you, so harass them. "Wiles" means cunning deceptions. What Balaam could not do by curses, Midian did by seduction, and Moab and Midian together are named as the tempters (vv1, 6). The war is carried out in chapter 31, where the reason is spelled out: "these, on Balaam's advice, caused the people of Israel to act treacherously against the LORD in the incident of Peor" (31:16). Moab is spared because of God's word to Lot's descendants (Deut 2:9), though they are barred from the assembly (Deut 23:3–4).
  • Commentators: Henry: "Never was vice more daring than it was in Zimri, a prince of a chief house in the tribe of Simeon." He defends Phinehas as a priest and leader acting under Moses' order, and says the story "gives no countenance at all to private persons, under pretence of zeal against sin, to put offenders to death." Henry reads the covenant of peace as "the covenant of priesthood, for that is called the covenant of life and peace." JFB calls God's word "a signal mark of honor" and notes that Zimri's high rank made Phinehas's act brave, since powerful relatives might have sought revenge. JFB adds that the Midianites "seem to have been the most guilty parties." Guzik stresses that one righteous act mattered when sin was widespread, and that atonement is a priestly work, so the reward fit the deed.

Pointing to Christ

Israel was safe from every curse on the hilltops, but not from its own heart. That is still the story of God's people: the real danger is not the enemy's curse but the friendly invitation to compromise. And it took a priest, zealous for God's honor, to turn away wrath and make atonement. Jesus is the greater Priest, of whom it was written, "Zeal for your house will consume me" (John 2:17). But where Phinehas turned God's wrath aside by striking down the guilty, Jesus turned it aside by being struck down himself in the place of the guilty, "a propitiation by his blood" (Rom 3:25). He brings the true "covenant of peace": "we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Rom 5:1), and a priesthood that is perpetual because he "always lives to make intercession" for his people (Heb 7:24–25).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes that Balaam's strategy succeeded where his curses failed. Satan's most effective weapon against God's people is not persecution but seduction. He notes that Phinehas' act was not vigilante justice but righteous execution of God's already-declared sentence (v.4-5).

Reflection

  • Seduction Succeeds Where Curses Failed (vv.1-3) — Israel withstood Balaam's attempted curses but falls to the invitation of Moabite women into idolatry. What invitations from the world might be more dangerous to your faith right now than outright opposition?
  • Brazen Sin in Plain Sight (v.6) — While the rest of the community is weeping in repentance, one man parades his sin openly before everyone, including Moses. Do you still grieve over sin, or have you grown comfortable enough with it to stop noticing?
  • Decisive Action in a Moment of Crisis (v.8) — Phinehas doesn't wait for deliberation; he sees the danger and acts immediately to stop it. Is there a situation right now where faithfulness requires you to act decisively rather than keep deliberating?
  • A Jealousy That Will Not Share (v.11) — God describes His response to Israel's unfaithfulness as a jealousy that mirrors His own zeal for His people's devotion. Do you share God's jealousy for His glory, or are you tolerant of things that clearly offend Him?
  • Peace Purchased Through Atonement (vv.12-13) — God rewards Phinehas' righteous judgment against sin with a covenant of peace, foreshadowing how true peace comes only through dealing decisively with guilt. Where in your life do you need to stop avoiding conflict with sin and instead pursue the peace that comes from confronting it?
