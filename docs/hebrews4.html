Summary

The author warns that although the promise of entering God's rest still stands, believers should fear falling short as the wilderness generation did, since the good news reached them too but failed to benefit them because they did not receive it in faith (vv. 1-2). Those who believe do enter that rest, which God established when he rested on the seventh day after finishing creation, yet the rest remained open after that generation's disobedience, since God later designates a new "today" through David long after Joshua had settled Israel in the land (vv. 3-8). A Sabbath rest therefore still remains for God's people, and believers are urged to strive to enter it, resting from their own works as God did from his, so that no one falls through the same disobedience (vv. 9-11). God's word is described as living and active, sharper than any double-edged sword, able to judge the heart's thoughts and intentions, since every person is fully exposed before the one to whom they must give account (vv. 12-13). Because believers have in Jesus a great high priest who sympathizes with human weakness, having been tempted in every way yet without sin, they are urged to approach God's throne of grace with confidence to receive mercy and find help in time of need (vv. 14-16).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown (see Chapter 1 notes for full discussion).
Historical Context: Chapter 4 continues the theme of God's "rest" from chapter 3. The wilderness generation failed to enter Canaan (God's rest) because of unbelief. But a rest still remains for God's people (v.9). The chapter then transitions to one of the most beloved passages in Hebrews: Christ as our sympathetic High Priest who invites us to the throne of grace (vv.14-16). Two great themes: entering God's rest by faith, and approaching God boldly through Christ.
Classification: Epistle — Sermon or Treatise
Key Themes: A rest still open though a whole generation failed to enter it, the good news that profits only when mixed with faith, God's own rest offered as the pattern rather than Canaan alone, a word described as living and sharper than a sword before any comfort is offered, and a throne of grace approached boldly because the High Priest has already been tempted in every way
God's Rest Still Available (vv.1-11): The promise of rest remains (v.1). The gospel was preached to Israel but didn't profit them because it wasn't mixed with faith (v.2). Believers enter rest (v.3). This rest is not just Canaan (Joshua didn't give final rest, v.8) but a Sabbath-rest for God's people (v.9) — ceasing from self-effort as God ceased from His works (v.10). "Let us labour to enter" — strive to enter by faith, not works (v.11).
The Word of God (vv.12-13): A powerful description of Scripture's penetrating power — living, active, sharper than a sword, dividing soul and spirit, discerning thoughts and intentions (v.12). Nothing is hidden from God (v.13).
Our Great High Priest (vv.14-16): Jesus has passed through the heavens (v.14). He sympathizes with our weaknesses — tempted in every way yet without sin (v.15). Therefore: come boldly to the throne of grace for mercy and help (v.16).

Deep Dive

Setting: This is still the same anonymous sermon, written to weary Jewish Christians, probably in the 60s, who were tempted to drift back to the safer life of synagogue and temple. Chapter 1 set the Son above the prophets and the angels. Chapter 2 warned the hearers not to "drift away" and showed Jesus becoming fully human to become "a merciful and faithful high priest" (2:17). Chapter 3 set him above Moses, then turned to a long warning from Psalm 95: "Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts" (3:7–8). The psalm looks back to Kadesh-barnea (Numbers 14), where the generation rescued from Egypt heard the spies' report, refused to go in, and was sentenced to die in the wilderness. God swore, "They shall not enter my rest." Chapter 3 ended with the verdict: "they were unable to enter because of unbelief" (3:19). Chapter 4 keeps preaching on that one psalm. The ESV section "A Rest for the People of God" began at 3:7 and runs to 4:13. The writer's point is that the rest Israel missed is still on offer, and the warning still applies. Then, in vv14–16, he turns to Jesus the great high priest, the theme that will carry the sermon through chapter 10.

A Rest for the People of God (vv1–13)

  • "While the promise of entering his rest still stands, let us fear" (v1). "Rest" is katapausis, the word the Greek Old Testament uses in Psalm 95:11 for the Hebrew menuchah, a place of settled rest. For Israel it meant the land, where God would give them "rest from all your enemies" (Deut 12:9–10). The writer's argument is that the promise was never used up. It "still stands," so a door is open, and an open door can be missed. The fear he urges is not panic but sober seriousness, the opposite of the casual drift he warned against in 2:1.
  • "Lest any of you should seem to have failed to reach it" (v1). "Failed to reach" is hystereō, to come late or fall behind, then to come short. "Seem" softens the wording, but it may also mean "be judged" to have fallen short. Notice "let us" and "any of you": the writer includes himself, and the church watches out for each one (3:12–13).
  • "Good news came to us just as to them" (v2). Israel at Kadesh also had "good news": a land flowing with milk and honey, and God's promise to give it. The content differed, but the situation is the same: God speaks a promise, and people trust it or not.
  • "Because they were not united by faith with those who listened" (v2). The verb is synkerannymi, to mix or blend together, and as the lexicon notes, used of persons, to be united with. The manuscripts differ by one letter. Most of the oldest have the plural: the unbelievers did not join in faith with "those who listened," probably Joshua and Caleb (Num 14:6–9, 30). Others have the singular, meaning that the message "did not meet with faith in the hearers," which the ESV gives in a footnote and the KJV follows ("not being mixed with faith"). Either way the meaning is the same: hearing was not enough without believing.
  • "For we who have believed enter that rest" (v3). The verb is present tense, "are entering." This raises one of the main questions in the chapter: is the rest present, future, or both? Some read it as the peace with God believers already have (Rom 5:1; Matt 11:28). Others stress that vv1 and 11 still speak of entering as ahead of us, and take it as the final rest of the age to come (Rev 14:13). Many hold both: believers begin to enter now and will enter fully at the end.
  • "Although his works were finished from the foundation of the world" (v3). Here the argument deepens. "My rest" in Psalm 95 is not only Canaan. It is God's own rest, and God has been resting since creation was complete. So the rest has been ready and waiting all along. The problem was never on God's side.
  • "He has somewhere spoken of the seventh day" (v4). "Somewhere" sounds vague to modern ears, as if the writer had forgotten where it was. It was a normal way of citing a well-known text (compare 2:6), and the stress falls on who spoke: God himself. The quotation is Genesis 2:2.
  • "They shall not enter my rest" (v5). Placing Genesis 2:2 next to Psalm 95:11 is a standard Jewish way of reading two texts by a shared word, "rest." Together they show that Israel was shut out of God's own seventh-day rest, not just a patch of land.
  • "It remains for some to enter it" (v6). The logic is simple. God meant someone to enter his rest. The first hearers did not, "because of disobedience." So the promise is still waiting for others. In this sermon unbelief (3:19) and disobedience (4:6) are two sides of the same failure.
  • "Again he appoints a certain day, 'Today,' saying through David so long afterward" (v7). This is the hinge of the argument. Psalm 95 was written centuries after Joshua led Israel into Canaan, yet it still says "Today." If the promise had been fully kept when Israel took the land, the psalm would have no reason to offer the rest again. God speaks "through David": the psalm is God's own voice to each new generation.
  • "For if Joshua had given them rest" (v8). "Joshua" in Greek is Iēsous, exactly the same name as "Jesus." The KJV even reads "if Jesus had given them rest." Joshua 21:44 says "the LORD gave them rest," but it was partial and earthly, and the psalm shows God promised more. The original readers could not miss the wordplay: the first Joshua could not bring them into God's rest, but the second can.
  • "So then, there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God" (v9). Here the writer changes words. "Sabbath rest" is sabbatismos, found only here in the New Testament. The lexicon gives "a keeping sabbath, a sabbath rest." The word is very rare in Greek writing, and the writer may have coined it or taken it from Jewish usage. It ties the promised rest to God's seventh-day rest, and it suggests celebration as well as stopping work, the festive rest of God's family.
  • What about Sabbath observance? Christians read v9 differently here. Seventh-day Adventists and Seventh Day Baptists see in it support for keeping the seventh day, since "there remains" a sabbath-keeping. In the Reformed and Puritan tradition (the Westminster Confession, for example), the weekly day of rest continues as the Christian Sabbath on the Lord's Day, and JFB takes this verse as "indirectly" supporting it. Many other Christians, including most Lutherans, Baptists and others, read the Sabbath as a sign fulfilled in Christ (Col 2:16–17), keeping Sunday as a day of worship but not as a binding Sabbath law. All three read the verse with care. What the passage itself is mainly about is not a calendar but a deeper rest that the weekly Sabbath pointed to.
  • "Whoever has entered God's rest has also rested from his works as God did from his" (v10). God did not rest because he was tired (Isa 40:28). He rested because his work was finished. Most readers take the verse to mean that those who enter God's rest stop trying to earn their standing with God and rest in a finished work, the way Jesus later cries, "It is finished" (John 19:30). This does not mean doing nothing. The ESV footnote offers "labor" for "works," which shows the idea is the end of toil. Some take it of Jesus himself, resting after his finished work (10:12).
  • "Let us therefore strive to enter that rest" (v11). "Strive" is spoudazō, to hurry, to be eager, to make every effort (as in 2 Tim 2:15). There is a deliberate paradox: make every effort to enter rest. The effort is not earning, which v10 has just ruled out. It is the energy of faith that keeps going, keeps listening and refuses to turn back. "So that no one may fall" recalls the bodies that "fell in the wilderness" (3:17, the same verb).
  • "For the word of God is living and active" (v12). This verse is often read on its own, but its first word is "For." It explains why the warning matters: the same God who spoke "Today" in Psalm 95 still speaks, and his word cannot be dodged. "Active" is energēs, at work, effective, the root of English "energy." It does things (Isa 55:11; Jer 23:29).
  • "Sharper than any two-edged sword" (v12). "Sharper" is tomōteros, from a root meaning "to cut." "Two-edged" is distomos, literally "two-mouthed," a common ancient way of describing a blade that cuts both ways, as if each edge could "eat." The same image appears in Revelation, where a sharp two-edged sword comes from the risen Christ's mouth (Rev 1:16; 2:12), and Paul calls the word "the sword of the Spirit" (Eph 6:17).
  • "Piercing to the division of soul and of spirit, of joints and of marrow" (v12). This is not a lesson in anatomy or psychology. The writer is not mainly teaching that humans have three parts. He is saying God's word reaches the innermost places, where even we cannot tell one thing from another. Joints and marrow are the hidden, deepest parts of the body. Soul and spirit are the hidden, deepest parts of the self.
  • "Discerning the thoughts and intentions of the heart" (v12). "Discerning" is kritikos, able to judge or discern, the source of English "critic" and "critical." Here the word judges its reader. It sorts out what the heart is thinking and planning, which is exactly where Israel's problem began: "an evil, unbelieving heart" (3:12).
  • "Naked and exposed to the eyes of him to whom we must give account" (v13). The writer moves from God's word to God himself. "Naked" (gymnos) means stripped of any covering, like Adam and Eve hiding among the trees (Gen 3:8–10). "Exposed" is tetrachēlismena, from trachēlos, the neck. The lexicon says it was used of wrestlers seizing an opponent by the throat, hence to overthrow, and that the exact metaphorical sense here is uncertain. It suggests "laid open," and others have suggested "downcast," with the head forced down. Either way, nothing can be hidden.
  • "To whom we must give account" (v13). In Greek this is literally "to whom is our logos." Logos is the same word as "word" in v12, and it also meant an account or reckoning in business. So the sentence closes with a wordplay: God's logos comes to us, and we must give our logos back to him.
  • Commentators: All three see the rest as layered. Henry traces several rests: the seventh-day rest of creation, Canaan as "a typical rest," "a rest of grace, and comfort, and holiness, in the gospel state," and "the everlasting sabbatism of heaven." On v2 he writes, "it is faith in the hearer that is the life of the word." Guzik and Henry both read v10 as resting from works as the ground of being right with God, and Henry says the believer "hath ceased from his own works of righteousness, and from the burdensome works of the law." Guzik makes the second-Joshua point: Jesus finishes what the first Joshua left undone. On v1 JFB says "seem" softens the wording "though not lessening the reality," and glosses the verb as coming "too late, when the 'today' is gone." On v12 JFB argues, "The personal Word is not here meant: He is not the sword, but has it," meaning the preached word. Guzik compares it to a surgeon's scalpel. Henry warns on v11 that "this is the only way to rest": rest comes after "diligent labour." On v13, JFB reads the word as a sacrifice "thrown on the back, so as to have the neck laid bare," and Guzik gives the wrestler's grip. The lexicon supports Guzik's wrestling sense as the word's basic use. The sacrificial picture is a possible meaning, not the settled one, and the lexicon admits the sense here is uncertain.

Jesus the Great High Priest (vv14–16)

  • A turn in the sermon. These three verses look back and forward. They close the long exhortation that began in 3:1 ("consider Jesus, the apostle and high priest of our confession") and open the central teaching of Hebrews, Jesus as high priest, which runs to 10:25. Many readers notice that 4:14–16 and 10:19–23 work like bookends: both have a great priest, a call to hold fast the confession, and a call to draw near with confidence.
  • "Since then we have a great high priest" (v14). Israel's high priest was the chief mediator between God and the people. Once a year, on the Day of Atonement, he alone went behind the curtain into the Most Holy Place with blood for the people's sins (Lev 16). No high priest in the Old Testament is ever called "great" in this way. "We have" is the point: the readers did not need to go back to the temple for a priest.
  • "Who has passed through the heavens" (v14). The high priest passed through the court and the holy place to reach the inner room. Jesus passed through the heavens to the presence of God himself (9:24). The tabernacle was only a copy of that real throne room (8:5).
  • "Jesus, the Son of God" (v14). "Jesus" is his human name, the same as Joshua's (v8). "Son of God" is the title of chapter 1. The priest is truly human and truly divine, exactly what a perfect go-between needs.
  • "Let us hold fast our confession" (v14). "Confession" (homologia) means the faith openly declared, probably the church's shared statement that Jesus is Lord and Son of God, made at baptism and repeated in worship.
  • "Unable to sympathize with our weaknesses" (v15). "Sympathize" is sympatheō, to have a fellow-feeling with someone. The same verb describes the readers themselves when they stood by fellow believers in prison (10:34). Greek philosophers often taught that the gods were beyond feeling. Hebrews says the opposite: the Son of God knows from the inside what human weakness is like. "Weaknesses" are not sins but human frailties: tiredness, grief, fear, pain (5:2, 7).
  • "In every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin" (v15). The verb peirazō means both "tested" and "tempted." It is the same word used in 3:9, where Israel "put me to the test" in the wilderness. Israel was tested in the wilderness and failed. Jesus was tested in the wilderness for forty days (Matt 4:1–11) and did not. "In every respect" means the full range of human testing, up to Gethsemane (5:7–8), not every exact situation. "Without sin" means he never gave in (2 Cor 5:21; 1 Pet 2:22). Christians debate whether he could have sinned, but all agree the testing was real. Only someone who never gives in feels temptation's full force to the end.
  • "Let us then with confidence draw near to the throne of grace" (v16). "Confidence" is parrēsia, which first meant freedom of speech, the right to speak openly, and then boldness. "Draw near" is the priestly word for approaching God in worship (7:25; 10:22), and in this sermon it is the opposite of drifting away. Once only the high priest came near, once a year. Now every believer may come. The throne in v13 before which everything is laid bare is the same throne in v16, now called "the throne of grace," because a priest sits beside it.
  • "That we may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need" (v16). Mercy is for past failure, and grace is help for present weakness. "In time of need" can mean "help that comes at the right time," as help just when it is needed, not too early or too late. The weakness of v15 is met by grace in v16.
  • Commentators: Henry calls Christ a priest who "once for all has passed into the heavens" and says "A throne of grace speaks great encouragement even to the chief of sinners." Guzik says no other high priest was called great, passed through the heavens, or was God's Son, and that "sympathize" carries the idea of suffering with someone, against the Greek ideal of a god beyond feeling. The lexicon defines sympatheō as having fellow-feeling. "Suffering with" reflects how the word is formed more than how it was usually used, but the point stands. Guzik also notes that Jesus felt temptation from outside at full strength but had no sinful nature pulling from within. JFB glosses "boldly" as "'with confidence,' 'freedom of speech,'" which matches the lexicon, and "without sin" as "separate from sin," adding, "Pleading Jesus' meritorious death, we shall always find God on a throne of grace." The Tyndale notes add that "draw near" can carry the sense of coming again and again, a habit and not a single visit.

Pointing to Christ

Israel had a Joshua who could take them into a land but not into God's rest, and a high priest who could go behind the curtain once a year but not stay. Hebrews 4 shows the better Jesus who does both. He finished his work and entered God's rest, and he has gone through the heavens to the throne and stays there for his people. The same chapter that strips everyone "naked and exposed" before God's searching word then opens the throne of grace, because the one sitting beside it was tested in every way and never fell. The rest the wilderness generation missed is not a reward for effort. It is a person to trust, and the sermon's call is to keep trusting him "today."

Reflection

  • A Rest That Still Remains (v.9) — A Sabbath-rest remains for God's people, one that means ceasing from self-effort just as God ceased from His work. Are you resting in what God has already accomplished, or still striving as though it's up to you?
  • The Living, Piercing Word (v.12) — Scripture is described as living and active, sharper than any sword, cutting deep enough to expose the true thoughts and intentions of the heart. When you read God's word, do you let it examine you, or do you keep it at a safe distance?
  • A High Priest Who Understands Temptation (v.15) — Christ was tempted in every way we are, yet without sin, so He can sympathize with our weaknesses rather than judge them from a distance. Does knowing Christ truly understands your struggles change how freely you bring them to Him?
  • Boldly Approaching Grace in Your Time of Need (v.16) — Believers are invited to approach God's throne with confidence, not because they've earned it, but because it's a throne of grace offering mercy and help exactly when it's needed. What need are you currently carrying that you haven't yet brought boldly to Him?
