Summary

Job answers God with the theological high point of the book: he confesses that God can do all things and that no purpose of His can be thwarted, admitting he spoke of things too wonderful for him, which he did not understand (vv. 1-3). Where before he had only heard of God secondhand, now his own eyes have seen Him, and he repents in dust and ashes (vv. 4-6). God then rebukes Eliphaz and his two companions, declaring that they, unlike Job, have not spoken rightly about Him, and instructs them to offer seven bulls and seven rams while Job — the very man they accused — prays on their behalf (vv. 7-9). When Job prays for his friends, the LORD restores his fortunes, giving him twice what he had before: 14,000 sheep, 6,000 camels, 1,000 oxen, and 1,000 donkeys (vv. 10-12). He is given seven sons and three daughters, remarkably beautiful and granted an inheritance alongside their brothers, and Job lives another 140 years, seeing four generations before he dies old and full of days (vv. 13-17).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown (see Chapter 1 notes for full discussion).
Historical Context: Chapter 42 is the conclusion of the book of Job — and one of the most important chapters in the Old Testament. It contains: (1) Job's final repentance and transformation (vv.1-6), (2) God's rebuke of the three friends (vv.7-9), and (3) Job's restoration (vv.10-17). The chapter resolves the entire book, but not in the way anyone expected. Job is not vindicated by receiving an explanation — he is vindicated by receiving GOD. He repents not of specific sins (the friends were wrong about that) but of speaking beyond his knowledge. God declares Job spoke rightly and the friends wrongly — a shocking reversal. And God restores Job doubly — not because Job earned it through suffering, but because that is who God is.
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: a confession that moves from secondhand hearing to direct sight, I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear, but now mine eye seeth thee, repentance expressed not over specific invented sins but over speaking of things too wonderful to understand, God's own astonishing verdict that Job, who accused him of cruelty, spoke rightly, while the friends, who defended him with orthodox theology, spoke wrongly, restoration arriving specifically when Job prays for the very friends who wronged him, and a doubled inheritance, fourteen thousand sheep, six thousand camels, exactly twice what Job had before, closing a book that never once explains why any of it happened
Job's Repentance (vv.1-6): This is the theological summit of the book. Job declares: "I know that thou canst do every thing, and that no thought can be withholden from thee" (v.2). He confesses: "I have uttered that I understood not; things too wonderful for me, which I knew not" (v.3). He distinguishes past from present: "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee" (v.5). And he concludes: "Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes" (v.6). Job moved from hearing about God (secondhand theology) to seeing God (direct encounter). His repentance is not "I was guilty of the sins my friends accused" but "I spoke beyond my knowledge about things too wonderful for me."
God's Verdict on the Friends (vv.7-9): God tells Eliphaz: "My wrath is kindled against thee, and against thy two friends: for ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath" (v.7). This is astonishing. Job — who accused God of cruelty, demanded explanations, and insisted on his own righteousness — spoke rightly about God. The friends — who defended God's justice, called for repentance, and upheld orthodox theology — spoke wrongly. How? Because the friends reduced God to a formula (sin = suffering), while Job engaged God as a Person. Honest wrestling is more right than dishonest defense. God prefers raw truthfulness to polished falsehood.
The friends must now offer seven bulls and seven rams (v.8) and have Job pray for them. The man they accused of sin becomes their intercessor — the ultimate vindication.
Job's Restoration (vv.10-17): "The LORD turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends" (v.10). Note the timing: restoration comes when Job prays for those who wronged him. God gives Job twice as much as he had before: 14,000 sheep, 6,000 camels, 1,000 yoke of oxen, 1,000 donkeys (v.12) — exactly double his original possessions (1:3). He has seven sons and three daughters again (v.13). His daughters are the most beautiful in the land and receive inheritance alongside their brothers (v.15) — remarkable in the ancient world. Job lives 140 more years and sees four generations (v.16). He dies "old and full of days" (v.17).

Setting: God has finished speaking from the whirlwind. Two long speeches (38:1–40:2; 40:6–41:34) have taken Job through creation, from the foundations of the earth to Leviathan, "king over all the sons of pride" (41:34). After the first speech Job put his hand over his mouth and fell silent (40:3–5). Now he answers a second time, and this is the last thing he says in the book. Then the book returns to the prose of chapters 1–2 and comes full circle: God speaks his verdict on Job's three friends, Job prays for the men who wounded him, and his fortunes are restored. The accuser of chapters 1–2 is never mentioned again, Elihu is never mentioned again, and Job is never told about the heavenly scene that started it all.

Job's Confession and Repentance (vv1–6)

  • "Then Job answered the LORD" (v1). Job had demanded an answer for chapters on end. Now he is the one answering, which is exactly what God told him to do (38:3; 40:7).
  • "I know that you can do all things" (v2). Job's first words are not about himself but about God. Two things have sunk in. God can do anything, and "no purpose of yours can be thwarted." The word for purpose is mezimmah, a plan or design. Elsewhere it often means a scheme of the wicked (21:27; Ps 10:2), but the lexicon lists Job 42:2 under God's purposes. Job had once complained that whatever God desires, he does, as if that were a threat (23:13). Now the same truth is a comfort. If God's purpose cannot be stopped, then even Job's suffering was not outside it.
  • "Who is this that hides counsel without knowledge?" (v3). Job quotes God's opening question back to him (38:2). He takes the charge to himself and confesses: "I have uttered what I did not understand, things too wonderful for me." "Too wonderful" (nifla'ot) is the word for God's marvels, things beyond human reach (Ps 131:1; 139:6). Job does not say his suffering was deserved. He says he spoke about God's ways as if he understood them, and he did not.
  • "Hear, and I will speak; I will question you" (v4). Again Job quotes God (38:3; 40:7), and this line may also echo his own earlier demand, "Call, and I will answer; or let me speak, and you reply to me" (13:22). He had wanted a courtroom where he asked the questions. God turned the questioning around, and Job repeats the words as a man who has been answered.
  • "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you" (v5). This is the heart of the chapter, and arguably of the whole book. Job was a man of real faith before his trial (1:1, 8), but his knowledge of God was secondhand. Now he has met God himself. He saw no face, since God spoke from the storm (38:1), and no one sees God's face and lives (Exod 33:20). But he has been in God's presence. He had longed for this: "in my flesh I shall see God... my eyes shall behold, and not another" (19:26–27). What changed Job was not an explanation. God never gave one. It was God's presence.
  • "Therefore I despise myself" (v6). The verb is ma'as, "reject, refuse, despise." There is no "myself" in the Hebrew. The verb stands without an object, and translators supply one. Some render it "I retract" or "I reject what I said," which fits vv3–4, where the issue is Job's words. Either way it is not despair. It is a man taking back what he said.
  • "And repent in dust and ashes" (v6). "Repent" is nacham, a word that covers being sorry, changing one's mind, and being comforted. The lexicon lists this verse under "be sorry, repent," with ma'as as its pair. The ESV footnote offers another reading, "and am comforted upon dust and ashes," using the "comfort" sense. That fits the scene too: Job has been sitting in ashes since 2:8, and now he finds comfort right there. "Dust and ashes" is what Abraham called himself when he spoke with God (Gen 18:27), and what Job said he had become (30:19). Faithful readers take v6 differently. Many read it as real repentance for proud and rash speech. Others stress that Job repents not of hidden sin, as his friends claimed, but of speaking beyond his knowledge. Some think Job is giving up his mourning and lament. These readings overlap: Job lays down his case before the God he has now seen.
  • Commentators: Henry calls v6 the mark of a true penitent: "Self-loathing is evermore the companion of true repentance." On v5 he says that when the Spirit enlightens the mind, knowledge of God exceeds what came before as far as seeing exceeds hearsay. JFB, on v3, notes that Job leaves out God's words "by words" from 38:2 and so "goes even further than God's accusation": not just his words but his "whole thoughts and ways" were without knowledge. It takes v6 as "I abhor, and retract the rash speeches." JFB also says the seeing of v5 was "not God's face" but his presence in the cloud. Guzik's main point is that God's presence, not his speech, changed Job. He passes on scholars who note "myself" is not in the Hebrew and read the verb as retracting what Job said, which the lexicon supports. He also cites G. Campbell Morgan, who says the word means at root "to disappear." The lexicon gives the verb as "reject" or "despise" and does not support "disappear." Guzik stresses that Job did not concede his friends had been right.

The LORD Rebukes Job's Friends (vv7–9)

  • "After the LORD had spoken these words to Job" (v7). The prose epilogue begins. God turns from Job to Eliphaz, the oldest and first of the three to speak (ch 4), whose arguments the other two echoed.
  • "My anger burns against you and against your two friends" (v7). This is a stunning reversal. For thirty chapters the friends defended God, or thought they did. They insisted that suffering always means sin and that Job must be hiding something (4:7–8; 8:4–6; 11:6; 22:5–9). Now God is angry with them. Job had warned them of this: "Will you speak falsely for God?... He will surely rebuke you" (13:7–10).
  • "You have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (v7). "Right" is nekonah, from the verb kun, "to be firm, established." What is "right" is what is sound and true, something that holds up. The friends' tidy system did not. Job said many wild things, and God has just rebuked him for them (38:2; 40:2, 8). Yet God says Job spoke what is right. How can both be true? Job spoke honestly to God, refused to fake a confession of sins he had not committed, and held on to God even while crying out against him. The friends spoke smoothly about God and turned him into a vending machine of reward and punishment. Some note that "of me" can also be read "to me," which would sharpen the contrast: Job talked to God, while the friends only talked about him.
  • "My servant Job" (vv7–8). God says it four times in two verses, the same title he used in the prologue (1:8; 2:3). The accuser bet that Job would curse God (1:11; 2:5). He did not. God's last word on Job is the same as his first.
  • "Seven bulls and seven rams" (v8). A burnt offering was wholly given to God. Seven bulls and seven rams is a very large sacrifice, the same that Balaam ordered (Num 23:1) and that was offered when the ark came to Jerusalem (1 Chr 15:26). The size matches the seriousness of their wrong. Job lives before the law of Moses and the Levitical priesthood, when the head of a household offered sacrifice himself, as Job had done for his children (1:5).
  • "Go to my servant Job... and my servant Job shall pray for you" (v8). The friends cannot go straight to God. They have to go to the man they wronged, bring their sacrifice to him, and ask for his prayers. Job longed for a mediator to stand between him and God (9:33; 16:19–21). Now God makes Job the mediator for his friends. There is irony here too. Eliphaz once promised Job that if he repented, God would deliver even the guilty "through the cleanness of your hands" (22:30). That is what happens, but Eliphaz is the guilty one.
  • "Not to deal with you according to your folly" (v8). "Folly" is nebalah, a strong word, often used for disgraceful acts (Gen 34:7; 2 Sam 13:12). The lexicon notes that in this verse it can mean "disgrace": God could treat them as the fools they had been. Their wisdom speeches turn out to be foolishness. They had called Job a fool (5:2–3).
  • "The LORD accepted Job's prayer" (v9). Literally "the LORD lifted up Job's face," the idiom for showing favor. Job who was despised is now honored, and the friends obey without argument.
  • Where is Elihu? He is not mentioned. Some think he needed no correction, others that God passes over him. The text does not say.
  • Commentators: Henry notes God calls Job "my servant" four times, as if to say, "Though he is poor and despised, he is my servant notwithstanding." On Elihu he says Elihu "acted, not as a party, but as a moderator." JFB reads "right" as "well-grounded," and explains that Job was right against the friends' theory that calamity proves secret guilt, though wrong when he "fell into the opposite extreme of almost denying all guilt." It sees Job as a praying mediator "foreshadowing the true Mediator" (1 Tim 2:5), and on v9 says Job's forgiving spirit foreshadows Christ's love for enemies (Luke 23:34). Guzik notes that God's rebuke of the friends is also Job's vindication, and passes on Spurgeon's phrase that praying for them was Job's "noble revenge."

The LORD Restores Job's Fortunes (vv10–17)

  • "The LORD restored the fortunes of Job, when he had prayed for his friends" (v10). The phrase is shuv shevut, literally "turned the turning" or, in older versions, "turned the captivity." Elsewhere it is used for God bringing Israel back from exile (Deut 30:3; Ps 126:1; Jer 29:14). The lexicon lists this verse as its one use for an individual. Job's suffering was a kind of exile, and God brings him home. Note the timing: the turn comes when Job prays for the men who hurt him. Forgiveness comes before restoration.
  • "Twice as much as he had before" (v10). Under the law, a thief caught with stolen livestock repaid double (Exod 22:4). Job was not robbed by God, but the doubling has the feel of full restitution. Double is also the promise to restored Zion (Isa 40:2; 61:7). Bildad, of all people, had predicted it: "your latter days will be very great" (8:7). The friends were wrong about why Job suffered, but some of their promises still came true in God's way.
  • "All his brothers and sisters and all who had known him before" (v11). In his misery Job said his brothers were far from him and his acquaintances were "wholly estranged" (19:13–14). Now they come back, eat with him and comfort him. Eating bread together was a sign of restored friendship. Comfort is still needed: ten children are still buried.
  • "All the evil that the LORD had brought upon him" (v11). The narrator does not say Satan brought it. The ESV footnote offers "disaster" for "evil." The book has shown both: the accuser struck Job (1:12; 2:7), but only with God's permission and within God's limits. The relatives say what Job himself said, "the LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away" (1:21). The book never softens that.
  • "A piece of money and a ring of gold" (v11). "Piece of money" is qesitah, a unit of money of unknown value. It appears only here and in Genesis 33:19 and Joshua 24:32, where Jacob buys land with it. That rare term, instead of the usual shekel, is one reason many think the story is set in the age of the patriarchs. The ring (nezem) could be worn in the nose or ear (Gen 24:47; 35:4). The gifts helped rebuild Job's wealth and also honored him.
  • "14,000 sheep, 6,000 camels, 1,000 yoke of oxen, and 1,000 female donkeys" (v12). Compare 1:3: 7,000 sheep, 3,000 camels, 500 yoke of oxen and 500 donkeys. Every figure is exactly doubled. "The LORD blessed the latter days of Job more than his beginning."
  • "Seven sons and three daughters" (v13). The children are not doubled. Job has the same number as before (1:2). Many have taken that as a quiet hint that the first ten were not lost for good, so that Job still has twenty. The Hebrew for "seven" here is an unusual form found nowhere else, and some ancient Jewish interpreters, including the Targum, read it as "twice seven," fourteen sons. Most translations keep "seven." The book never says whether Job's wife is the same wife (2:9; 19:17).
  • "Jemimah... Keziah... Keren-happuch" (v14). Strikingly, the daughters are named and the sons are not. Jemimah is often taken as "dove," though the lexicon offers that only tentatively, comparing an Arabic word. Some older interpreters took it as "daylight." Keziah is "cassia," a fragrant spice used in perfume (Ps 45:8). Keren-happuch is "horn of antimony," a container of dark eye paint, the ancient world's makeup. All three names speak of beauty, sweetness and peace. A man whose skin was covered in sores (2:7) and whose breath was offensive (19:17) now names his girls after perfume and cosmetics.
  • "No women so beautiful as Job's daughters... their father gave them an inheritance among their brothers" (v15). Normally daughters inherited only if there were no sons (Num 27:1–8). Job gives them a share alongside their brothers. It shows his wealth, but also a generous and honoring heart. He once said he never despised the rights of his servants, male or female (31:13). His daughters are treated the same way.
  • "After this Job lived 140 years" (v16). That is twice the usual span of seventy (Ps 90:10). If he was about seventy at the start, he reached about 210, like the patriarchs (Gen 25:7; 35:28), though the text does not say so. The Greek Septuagint gives different figures: 170 years after the trial, 240 in all. "Saw his sons, and his sons' sons, four generations": the blessing of seeing one's descendants (Gen 50:23; Ps 128:6).
  • "Job died, an old man, and full of days" (v17). The same words are used of Abraham (Gen 25:8) and Isaac (Gen 35:29). Job's life ends as Eliphaz once said a righteous man's would, coming to the grave "in ripe old age" (5:26). The Septuagint adds a long ending: that Job "will rise again with those whom the Lord raises up," and that he is to be identified with Jobab, a king of Edom (Gen 36:33). That addition is not in the Hebrew text and most scholars treat it as later tradition, but it shows how early readers longed for Job's hope in 19:25–27 to be fulfilled.
  • Is the happy ending a cheat? Some feel it undercuts the book, as if the friends' theory wins after all. But the order matters. Job is restored only after he has let go of his case and been satisfied with God himself (v5). The restoration is a gift, not wages, which is the point of 41:11, "Who has first given to me, that I should repay him?" James draws the lesson: "You have heard of the steadfastness of Job, and you have seen the purpose of the Lord, how the Lord is compassionate and merciful" (Jas 5:11).
  • Commentators: Henry says mercy did not return while Job argued, "though he had right on his side," but while he prayed, "for God is better served and pleased with our warm devotions than with our warm disputations." On the relatives he writes, "Swallow-friends, that are gone in winter, will return in the spring, though their friendship is of little value." He thinks the children were not doubled because the dead ones "were not lost, but gone before to a better world." JFB reads "turned the captivity" as a pledge of the future vindication of body and soul at the resurrection (19:25–27). It ties "twice" to restored Jerusalem (Isa 40:2), and notes that for both Job and Christ "the glorious recompense follows the 'intercession' for enemies" (Isa 53:12). JFB derives qesitah from an Arabic word meaning "weighed out" and guesses it was worth four shekels. The lexicon gives the value as unknown and the root as uncertain, so that guess should not be pressed. JFB also notes the Septuagint's added line on resurrection, and suggests it may have been borrowed from Matthew 27:52–53. Guzik stresses that relationships were restored before possessions, and that the new children were a real comfort but no replacement for those who died.

Pointing to Christ

Job never learned why he suffered. What he received was God himself: "now my eye sees you" (v5). Then the man who had been mocked and condemned became the one through whom his accusers were forgiven. He prayed for them, God accepted his prayer, and only then was he lifted up and given a double portion. That pattern is fulfilled in Jesus. He suffered though innocent, was counted guilty by those around him, and prayed for those who wronged him: "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). Isaiah says the Servant would receive "a portion with the many... because he poured out his soul to death... and makes intercession for the transgressors" (Isa 53:12). God called Job "my servant." He calls Jesus "my servant" too (Isa 42:1; 52:13), the true mediator Job longed for (1 Tim 2:5), who "always lives to make intercession" for his people (Heb 7:25). Job's restoration is a small, earthly preview. The fuller one is the resurrection Job glimpsed (19:25–27), when those who have seen the Lord only by hearing of the ear will see him face to face (1 Cor 13:12; Rev 22:4).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes that v.5 is the resolution of the entire book: Job moved from theology about God to encounter with God. The answer to suffering is not information but relationship. Guzik notes the stunning verdict of v.7: God validates Job's raw honesty over the friends' systematic theology. This does not mean Job never erred (God rebuked him in 38:2), but that his overall posture — honest engagement with God as a Person — was more "right" than the friends' reduction of God to a formula. Guzik also observes that Job's restoration (double possessions) is not the point of the book — it is a grace appendix, not a vindication of retribution theology.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Speaking Beyond What You Actually Know (v.3) — Job admits he spoke about things too wonderful for him, matters he didn't truly understand. Where have you spoken with more certainty about God's ways than your actual understanding could support?
  • From Hearing About God to Seeing Him (v.5) — Job distinguishes between the secondhand knowledge he had before and the direct encounter he has now. Is your faith still mostly inherited information about God, or has it become your own genuine encounter with Him?
  • True Sight of God Produces Honest Humility (v.6) — Coming face to face with God leads Job to repent, not because he was guilty of what his friends accused him of, but because God's presence reveals the truth about every human heart. What would an honest, unfiltered encounter with God reveal in you right now?
  • Honest Wrestling Over Polished Defense (v.7) — God tells the friends they spoke wrongly about Him while Job, who voiced raw complaints and hard questions, spoke rightly. Are you more comfortable offering God polished, safe words, or honest wrestling with what you actually feel?
  • Restoration Came Through Praying for Those Who Wronged Him (v.10) — Job's fortunes turned not simply when he repented, but specifically when he prayed for the friends who had misjudged and wounded him. Is there someone who wronged you that God may be calling you to intercede for before you can move fully forward?
  • God's Final Word Is Abundance, Not Just Loss (v.12) — God restores Job with double what he had before, without erasing or trivializing what he lost. Where do you need to trust that God's ending for your story is not defined by what's already been taken from you?
