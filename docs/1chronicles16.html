12Remember the wonders He has done, His marvels, and the judgments He has pronounced,

24Declare His glory among the nations, His wonders among all peoples.

27Splendor and majesty are before Him; strength and joy fill His dwelling.

Summary

With the ark set in its tent, David offers burnt and peace offerings, blesses the people, and distributes bread, meat, and wine to every Israelite (vv. 1-3). He appoints Asaph and fellow Levites to minister before the ark with music and thanksgiving, giving them a psalm that calls Israel to remember God's wondrous works and His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (vv. 4-22). The psalm calls all the earth to declare God's glory, worship Him in holiness, and rejoice that the Lord reigns, closing with praise for His enduring mercy (vv. 23-36). David then leaves Asaph's family ministering before the ark in Jerusalem while Zadok and the priests continue sacrifices at the tabernacle in Gibeon, and all the people return home (vv. 37-43).

Authorship & Background

Author: Traditionally attributed to Ezra the scribe. Originally one book with 2 Chronicles. Written post-exile (approximately 450-400 BC) for the returned remnant. The Chronicler retells Israel's history from a priestly/worship perspective, emphasizing the Davidic covenant, Temple worship, and God's faithfulness.
Historical Context: Chapter 16 records the ark's arrival in Jerusalem and the establishment of ongoing worship ministry before it. The centerpiece is David's great psalm of thanksgiving (vv. 8-36), which draws from Psalm 105:1-15, Psalm 96:1-13, and Psalm 106:1, 47-48. This composite hymn celebrates God's covenant faithfulness, His sovereignty over creation, and His saving acts on behalf of Israel. The chapter then details the permanent worship arrangements David established—Levitical ministers before the ark in Jerusalem, and the sacrificial system continuing at the tabernacle in Gibeon.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: Bread, meat and wine distributed to every man and woman in Israel before a single note of the psalm is sung, a psalm delivered into Asaph's hand that readers of the Psalter will recognize almost word for word as Psalm 105, touch not mine anointed and do my prophets no harm read by exiles as a promise still standing over them, creation itself called to respond, let the heavens be glad, let the sea roar, let the trees of the wood sing, and two sanctuaries left standing at once, the ark in Jerusalem and the altar still at Gibeon, exactly the problem the temple will solve
The Ark Set in Place (vv.1-6): Offerings, a blessing in the name of the LORD, and a distribution to every one of Israel, both man and woman, of a loaf of bread, a good piece of flesh and a flagon of wine. Then the appointments before the ark, and the job description is unusually specific, to record, and to thank and praise the LORD God of Israel. Asaph is given the cymbals and two priests the trumpets continually.
O Give Thanks Unto the LORD (vv.7-22): The psalm David delivers into Asaph's hand, and readers of the Psalter will recognize it, because this section is Psalm 105 almost word for word. The opening is a run of imperatives, give thanks, call upon his name, sing unto him, talk ye of all his wondrous works. Then the argument turns to the covenant with Abraham, the word which he commanded to a thousand generations, quoted as still in force, saying, Unto thee will I give the land of Canaan. And the protection clause the exiles would have read closely, touch not mine anointed, and do my prophets no harm.
Sing Unto the LORD, All the Earth (vv.23-36): The second half of the psalm is Psalm 96 with the end of Psalm 106 attached, and its reach is wider than the first: declare his glory among the heathen, for all the gods of the people are idols, but the LORD made the heavens. Creation is called in to respond, let the heavens be glad, let the sea roar, let the fields rejoice, then shall the trees of the wood sing out. The closing petition is the one an exile prays, save us, O God of our salvation, and gather us together, and deliver us from the heathen, and the people answer Amen.
Ministers at Both Places (vv.37-43): A practical arrangement that shows the state of worship at this point: the ark is in Jerusalem with Asaph and his brethren before it continually, and the tabernacle with the altar of burnt offering is still at Gibeon, with Zadok and his priests there to offer morning and evening. Two sanctuaries, one with the ark and no altar, one with the altar and no ark, which is exactly the problem the temple is built to solve. Then everyone goes home, and David returns to bless his house.

Map & Geography

  • Largely parallels 1-2 Samuel geographically. Jerusalem (Zion/City of David) is the theological and political center.
  • Key sites: Hebron (David's first capital, 7 years), the threshing floor of Ornan/Araunah (future Temple site, ch.21).
  • Chapters 1-9 (genealogies) contain tribal allotments but minimal narrative geography.

Deep Dive

Setting: Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC, for a small community back in the land with a rebuilt temple, no king, and many questions about whether God's promises still stood. In chapter 15 David finally brought the ark up to Jerusalem the right way, carried by Levites, with singing and sacrifice. Chapter 16 tells what happened once it arrived. 2 Samuel 6:17–20 covers the same day in four verses: the ark set in the tent, offerings, a blessing, food for everyone, and David going home. The Chronicler keeps that frame (vv1–3, 43) and fills the middle with two things that mattered deeply to his readers: a long song of thanksgiving and the permanent arrangements for worship that David set up. The song is stitched together from three psalms the returned exiles would have known well, and its last line is a prayer that God would gather his scattered people. For readers living after the exile, that prayer was their own.

The Ark Placed in a Tent (vv1–7)

  • "Set it inside the tent that David had pitched for it" (v1). This is David's new tent on Zion (15:1), not the old tabernacle of Moses, which stood at Gibeon (v39). The ark had sat for about twenty years at Kiriath-jearim (1 Sam 7:2) and then three months at Obed-edom's house (13:14). Now it is in the king's own city, at the center of the nation's life.
  • "Burnt offerings and peace offerings" (v1). A burnt offering was burned whole and given entirely to God, a picture of full surrender and of atonement (Lev 1). A peace offering was shared: part burned, part given to the priests, and the rest eaten by the worshipers in a meal before God (Lev 3; 7:11–15). One offering says "we belong to you," the other "we eat at your table in peace."
  • "He blessed the people in the name of the LORD" (v2). Blessing the people was usually a priestly act (Num 6:22–27), but kings also blessed the assembly (Solomon in 1 Kings 8:14, 55). David is not taking over priestly duties. He is acting as the covenant king who speaks God's favor over God's people.
  • "To each a loaf of bread, a portion of meat, and a cake of raisins" (v3). "Both men and women" stresses that no one was left out. The middle item, eshpar, is a rare word whose meaning is uncertain. "Portion of meat" fits the peace offerings, but some suggest a cake of dates. Either way, ordinary Israelites rarely ate meat, so this was a feast, and the whole nation went home fed. The same three gifts appear in 2 Samuel 6:19.
  • "To invoke, to thank, and to praise the LORD" (v4). These three verbs sum up the Levites' new work. "Invoke" is lehazkir, a form of zakar, "remember": to bring something to remembrance, to call on God by recalling what he has done. The same form appears in the titles of Psalms 38 and 70 ("for the memorial offering"). "Thank" is lehodot, to give thanks or confess. "Praise" is lehallel, the root of "hallelujah." Remembering, thanking and praising is the shape of the song that follows.
  • Asaph the chief (v5). In 15:17–19 Heman led the singers during the procession. Now Asaph is chief before the ark in Jerusalem, while Heman and Jeduthun serve at Gibeon (vv41–42). Twelve psalms carry Asaph's name (Ps 50; 73–83), and "the sons of Asaph" were still singing when the exiles laid the foundation of the second temple (Ezra 3:10). Many of the Chronicler's first readers would have known Asaph's descendants personally.
  • "Benaiah and Jahaziel the priests were to blow trumpets regularly" (v6). The silver trumpets were a priestly instrument from Moses' day (Num 10:1–10), blown over offerings and festivals "as a reminder" before God. The word "regularly" (tamid, "continually") marks the change: this was no longer a one-day event but a standing ministry.
  • "Then on that day David first appointed that thanksgiving be sung" (v7). This is the start of organized, sung worship in Israel, as the Chronicler sees it. Later generations traced the temple choirs back to this day (Neh 12:45–46). David is remembered as "the sweet psalmist of Israel" (2 Sam 23:1), and here he gives the singers a song.
  • Commentators: Henry draws a lesson from v3: "Those whose hearts are enlarged with holy joy, should show it by being open-handed." He also notes how quickly the Levites took up their duties once the ark rested. Guzik treats the offerings as consecration and fellowship, and points out that the peace-offering meal made the people guests at God's table. He fastens on "invoke" (remember) as the neglected discipline of recalling what God has done. JFB says the leftover thank offerings were shared out "that the ceremonial might terminate with appropriate festivities," and notes that the Septuagint reads the raisin item as a cake.

David's Song of Thanks (vv8–36)

  • Three psalms in one (vv8–36). The song is made of Psalm 105:1–15 (vv8–22), Psalm 96 (vv23–33), and Psalm 106:1, 47–48 (vv34–36). Scholars disagree about which came first. Some think David's original song was later divided and expanded into the Psalter's psalms. Others think the Chronicler, or the singers of his day, drew on psalms already in use to show what that day's worship sounded like. Either way, the text presents it as the praise Asaph's choir began to sing at David's appointment (v7).
  • "Oh give thanks to the LORD; call upon his name; make known his deeds among the peoples!" (v8). The song opens with a string of commands: give thanks, call, make known, sing, tell, glory, seek, remember (vv8–12). Some are aimed at God, some at the nations, and some at the worshiper's own heart. Praise in Israel was never private. It told others what God had done.
  • "Seek the LORD and his strength; seek his presence continually!" (v11). "Seeking" is one of the Chronicler's favorite words. Saul died because he "did not seek guidance from the LORD" (10:14), and David's first act as king was to bring back the ark that "we did not seek" in Saul's days (13:3). Here, with the ark before them, Israel is called to seek God's "presence" (literally his "face") continually, the same word as "regularly" in v6.
  • "O offspring of Israel his servant" (v13). Psalm 105:6 reads "offspring of Abraham." The change keeps the focus on the nation descended from Jacob, the people actually standing there, and by extension the returned exiles who read this.
  • "Remember his covenant forever… for a thousand generations" (v15). Israel is told to remember because God remembers (Ps 105:8 has "He remembers his covenant forever"). The covenant was made with Abraham, sworn to Isaac and confirmed to Jacob (vv16–17; Gen 15; 26:3; 28:13), and its heart was the land: "To you I will give the land of Canaan" (v18). For a community back in that land after exile, this was not abstract history. God had kept the promise again.
  • "When you were few in number, of little account, and sojourners in it" (v19). Psalm 105:12 says "when they were few." Chronicles has "when you were few," which brings the listener into the story. The patriarchs were a single family of wandering herdsmen. The post-exilic community was also small and weak, a province of the Persian Empire. The God who protected the few then could protect the few now.
  • "Touch not my anointed ones, do my prophets no harm!" (v22). In context this is about the patriarchs. God "rebuked kings on their account" (v21): Pharaoh (Gen 12:17) and Abimelech of Gerar (Gen 20:3–7; 26:11), and Abraham is called a "prophet" in Genesis 20:7. "Anointed" here does not mean a ritual anointing with oil. It means people set apart by God and under his protection. The verse is sometimes used to shield church leaders from all criticism, but that is not its point. It speaks of God guarding his covenant people as a whole.
  • "Sing to the LORD, all the earth!" (v23). The song now turns to Psalm 96, and the audience widens from Israel to every nation. Israel's God is not a tribal god. "All the gods of the peoples are worthless idols" (v26). The Hebrew is a pun: the gods (elohim) are elilim, "nothings," "worthless things." The proof is simple: "the LORD made the heavens."
  • "Strength and joy are in his place" (v27). Psalm 96:6 reads "strength and beauty are in his sanctuary." No temple stood yet, only a tent, and the song speaks of God's "place." In v29 Psalm 96:8's "come into his courts" likewise becomes "come before him." The Tyndale notes suggest these changes fit the occasion: the ark was in a tent, not a temple.
  • "Worship the LORD in the splendor of holiness" (v29). "Splendor" is hadrah, "adornment" or "glory." The phrase appears only in connection with public worship (also 2 Chr 20:21; Ps 29:2; 96:9). It can mean God's own holy splendor, or the holy attire and readiness worshipers bring before him (the old "beauty of holiness"). Both ideas fit: God is beautiful in his holiness, and those who come before him come prepared.
  • "Let them say among the nations, 'The LORD reigns!'" (v31). In Psalm 96 this line comes a little earlier (96:10). Here it sits in the middle of creation's praise, as if heaven, earth, sea, fields and trees all join the announcement (vv31–33).
  • "For he comes to judge the earth" (v33). Psalm 96:13 continues, "He will judge the world in righteousness." Chronicles stops at the first half and also leaves out Psalm 96:10's "he will judge the peoples with equity." The note of coming judgment is still there, but more briefly. In the Bible God's coming to "judge" is good news for the world. He comes to set things right, which is why the trees sing.
  • "Oh give thanks to the LORD, for he is good; for his steadfast love endures forever!" (v34). This refrain opens Psalm 106 and several others (Ps 107; 118; 136). "Steadfast love" is hesed, God's loyal, covenant-keeping love. The Chronicler loves this line. It returns in v41, when the temple is dedicated (2 Chr 5:13; 7:3) and when Jehoshaphat's choir marches out before the army (2 Chr 20:21).
  • "Save us, O God of our salvation, and gather and deliver us from among the nations" (v35). These words come from Psalm 106:47, a psalm that ends Book 4 of the Psalter and likely dates from the exile. On David's lips they look forward. To the Chronicler's readers they were urgent and present: many Jews were still scattered across the Persian Empire. The goal of being gathered is worship: "that we may give thanks to your holy name."
  • "Then all the people said, 'Amen!' and praised the LORD" (v36). Psalm 106:48 ends with a command: "And let all the people say, 'Amen!'" Chronicles reports that they did. The whole assembly made the song its own.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Let God be glorified in our praises," and in the middle of praise "we must not forget to pray for the servants of God in distress," as v35 does. JFB says the song "seems to have been compiled from other psalms of David previously known to the Israelites," and points out how Psalm 106's "let them praise" has become "and they praised." Guzik counts about eight distinct ways of praising in vv8–12, some directed to God, some to others and some to oneself, and argues that speaking more often about God's works leaves less room for complaint. He notes the claim, from J. Barton Payne, that v33 may be one of Scripture's earliest statements of the Lord's coming to judge, while allowing that Job 19:25 may be older. That is a debatable reading and not one the text settles.

Worship Before the Ark (vv37–43)

  • Two places of worship (vv37–40). David now splits the worship of Israel between two sites. In Jerusalem, Asaph and his brothers minister before the ark "as each day required" (v37). At Gibeon, about six miles northwest, Zadok and the priests serve before "the tabernacle of the LORD in the high place," offering the burnt offerings "morning and evening" (v40) on the bronze altar Moses had made (21:29; 2 Chr 1:3–5). The ark and the altar were apart until Solomon's temple united them.
  • Why was the ark not taken to Gibeon? The text does not say. Jerusalem was David's capital, newly won, and he wanted God's throne in his city. He was also already thinking of a permanent house for God (17:1). The divided arrangement was temporary, and the Chronicler simply records it without criticism.
  • "To do all that is written in the Law of the LORD" (v40). The morning and evening burnt offering was the "regular" (tamid) offering of Exodus 29:38–42 and Numbers 28:3–8. The Chronicler is careful to show that David's new arrangements in Jerusalem never replaced what Moses commanded. They were added alongside it.
  • Obed-edom and the gatekeepers (v38). Obed-edom, whose house God had blessed while the ark stayed there (13:14), is now a gatekeeper with sixty-eight relatives. "Obed-edom the son of Jeduthun" may be the same man or a different Obed-edom. The text gives no way to be sure. Gatekeepers guarded the holy place, controlled access and kept order (9:17–27).
  • Heman and Jeduthun (vv41–42). These two lead music at Gibeon. Jeduthun appears to be the same man called Ethan in 15:17, 19, though the reason for the two names is unknown. Psalms 39, 62 and 77 mention him in their titles. So all three musical families of the later temple, Asaph, Heman and Jeduthun, trace back to this day (25:1).
  • "For his steadfast love endures forever" (v41). These singers were "expressly named" (literally "designated by name") for this refrain. The words become the motto of the Levitical choirs throughout Chronicles.
  • "David went home to bless his household" (v43). This matches 2 Samuel 6:20, but Samuel goes on to tell how Michal met him with scorn. The Chronicler mentioned her contempt in a single verse (15:29) and stops here. His focus is on what the day began, not on the quarrel in the palace.
  • Commentators: Henry: "The worship of God ought to be the work of every day," and he sees in the singing at the ark beside the sacrifices at Gibeon an early sign that "spiritual worship" would take the place of "ceremonial." Guzik, following Martin Selman, notes the split between ark and altar and how uncertain it is how long the Gibeon service continued. JFB explains that Zadok "was stationed at Gibeon" to serve the old tabernacle "as the law enjoined," and calls Obed-edom and Hosah "porters," that is, doorkeepers.

Pointing to Christ

The song begins with remembering the covenant made with Abraham and ends with a prayer to be gathered from the nations, and the middle calls every nation to sing because the LORD reigns and comes to judge the earth. The New Testament hears that coming as the coming of Christ. In him the promise to Abraham reaches "all the families of the earth" (Gal 3:8, 16), the nations are invited to the praise once kept for Israel (Rom 15:9–11), and the scattered children of God are gathered into one (John 11:52). The ark in a tent and the altar at Gibeon stood apart for a generation. In Christ the presence of God and the sacrifice for sin meet in one person, who "tabernacled" among us (John 1:14) and offered himself once for all (Heb 10:10–14). And the refrain the Levites were appointed to sing is still the church's song: "his steadfast love endures forever."

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik notes the significance of David establishing both a new worship center (the tent in Jerusalem) while maintaining the old one (the tabernacle at Gibeon). He sees this as a transitional period that honored tradition while moving toward the fuller worship God intended in the Temple.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Worship That Overflows Into Witness (v.8) — David calls Israel not only to praise God but to make his deeds known among the peoples. Does your worship stay private, or does it spill over into telling others what God has done for you?
  • Seeking His Presence, Not Just His Provision (v.11) — The psalm calls Israel to seek the LORD's strength and his face continually, not only in a crisis. Do you pursue God consistently, or mostly when you need something from Him?
  • A Promise That Outlasts Generations (v.15) — David ties Israel's present worship to a covenant word commanded to a thousand generations, reaching back to Abraham. Does knowing God's faithfulness to those who came before you strengthen your trust in His faithfulness to you?
  • Worship as Offering, Not Performance (v.29) — Israel is called to bring an offering and worship the LORD in the beauty of holiness, not empty ritual. What would it look like for your worship this week to cost you something rather than just be words?
  • Thanksgiving as a Daily Rhythm (v.37) — David stationed Levites before the ark to minister continually, as every day's work required, making praise a scheduled part of ordinary life. Is gratitude a habit you practice, or only a reaction when you happen to feel it?
