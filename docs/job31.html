Summary

In his final defense, Job declares he made a covenant with his own eyes never to look on a woman with lust, confident that God has counted every one of his steps and would find his integrity if weighed on honest scales (vv. 1-6). He then works through a long series of self-curses, inviting disaster on himself if he is guilty of deceit, adultery, mistreating his servants, denying justice to the poor, the widow, or the orphan, trusting in wealth, worshiping the sun or moon, gloating over an enemy's ruin, neglecting travelers, or hiding his sin (vv. 7-34). He closes by wishing his accuser would put the charges in writing so he could answer them like a prince, and even that his own land would testify against him if he had wronged it (vv. 35-40).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown (see Chapter 1 notes for full discussion).
Historical Context: Chapter 31 is Job's magnificent oath of innocence — his final, definitive defense. In ancient Near Eastern legal tradition, a self-imprecatory oath was the most solemn form of testimony. By invoking curses upon himself if he is lying ("if I have done X, let Y happen to me"), Job stakes his life on his integrity. The chapter is structured as a series of conditional statements: "If I have sinned in this way... then let this punishment come upon me." Job systematically denies every major category of sin — lust, deceit, injustice, greed, idolatry, hatred, secrecy, and exploitation. He then challenges God directly to answer (v.35). This is not pride but desperate confidence in his innocence. The final line — "The words of Job are ended" (v.40) — is both a literary marker and a legal one: Job rests his case.
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: sexual purity governed at the level of the eyes themselves, I made a covenant with mine eyes, before any outward act is even addressed, servants treated with justice on the explicit ground that master and servant were made in the same womb, generosity toward the poor, the fatherless and the naked itemized point by point as a direct answer to accusations never actually leveled at him until now, worship of neither gold nor sun nor moon offered as proof against idolatry, and a closing legal challenge, oh that one would hear me, behold, my desire is that the Almighty would answer me, that Job says he would carry as a crown rather than fear
Purity of Heart and Body (vv.1-4): Job begins with sexual purity: "I made a covenant with mine eyes; why then should I think upon a maid?" (v.1). He governed not merely his actions but his gaze and his thoughts. This anticipates Christ's teaching on lust in the heart (Matthew 5:28).
Honesty and Integrity (vv.5-8): If Job walked with falsehood or hastened to deceit, let God weigh him in a just balance. If his hands have any blot, let his harvest go to another.
Sexual Fidelity (vv.9-12): If his heart was enticed by another woman, let judgment come. Adultery is "a fire that consumeth to destruction" (v.12).
Justice Toward Servants (vv.13-15): Remarkably, Job treated his servants with justice, recognizing that God made both master and servant in the same womb (v.15). This is extraordinary ethical reasoning for the ancient world.
Generosity to the Poor (vv.16-23): If Job withheld from the poor, failed to feed the fatherless, left the naked unclothed, or raised his hand against the helpless — let his arm fall from his shoulder. This section directly refutes Eliphaz's accusations in 22:6-9.
Freedom from Idolatry (vv.24-28): Job trusted neither gold (v.24) nor the heavenly bodies (v.26). He did not worship the sun or moon — which "were an iniquity to be punished by the judge" (v.28). His worship belonged to God alone.
Love for Enemies (vv.29-30): Job did not rejoice at his enemy's destruction or wish curses on him. This anticipates Proverbs 24:17 and Christ's command to love enemies.
Hospitality and Transparency (vv.31-34): Job opened his doors to strangers and did not hide his sins "as Adam" (v.33). He lived openly, without concealment.
The Challenge to God (vv.35-37): "Oh that one would hear me! behold, my desire is, that the Almighty would answer me" (v.35). Job demands God's response. He would carry any indictment God writes "as a crown" (v.36) — so confident is he of his innocence.
Stewardship of Land (vv.38-40): Even the land has not cried out against Job — he has not exploited it or cheated its workers.

Setting: After Bildad's short speech and Job's hymn to God's power (chs 25–26), Zophar never takes his turn. Job keeps talking. In chapter 27 he swears, "As God lives, who has taken away my right" (27:2), that he will not let go of his integrity, and chapter 28 is a poem on where wisdom can be found. Then Job sums up his case (chs 29–31). In chapter 29 he looks back on "the months of old," when "the friendship of God was upon my tent" (29:4), when elders rose for him at the city gate and he was "eyes to the blind and feet to the lame" (29:15). Chapter 30 turns on the words "But now" (30:1). Young men of no account mock him and spit at him, his body burns, and God is silent: "I cry to you for help and you do not answer me" (30:20). Chapter 31 is the last step. Job takes a formal oath of innocence, naming one sin after another and calling a curse down on himself if he has done it. Then he signs his name and dares God to answer. A line from the narrator closes it: "The words of Job are ended." The friends have no reply. Elihu speaks next (chs 32–37), then God (chs 38–41).

Job's Final Appeal (vv1–40)

  • An oath of clearance (vv1–40). The chapter is built on "If I have... then let..." Job names a wrong, then the curse he deserves if he did it. In the ancient world this was how a person cleared himself when there were no witnesses: he swore under a curse and left the matter in God's hands. Eliphaz had finally accused Job of specific crimes: taking pledges for nothing, stripping the naked, refusing bread to the hungry, sending widows away empty (22:6–9). Job answers with specifics of his own. Guzik passes on Elmer Smick's comparison with the Egyptian Book of the Dead, where the dead man lists before Osiris the wrongs he has not done. The form is similar, but Job is no dead man reciting a formula. He is asking the living God to look at his life. The Tyndale notes observe that most of the sins Job denies match the second half of the Ten Commandments, the duties toward a neighbor, though Job lived before Moses. Some go deeper than any court could reach, down to a glance or a private thought.
  • "I have made a covenant with my eyes; how then could I gaze at a virgin?" (v1). Job begins with a sin no one else could have seen. "Covenant" is berit, the ordinary word for a binding agreement, used of God's covenants with Noah and Abraham. The lexicon lists this verse under a pledge made with oneself. Job had decided ahead of time where his eyes would not go. "Virgin" is betulah, a young unmarried woman in her father's house. A man of Job's wealth could easily have taken such a girl as a concubine, and few would have blamed him. Job did not let even his thoughts go there. Jesus says the same about the eye and the heart: "everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (Matt 5:28).
  • "Does not he see my ways and number all my steps?" (vv2–4). Job gives his reasons. Impurity earns no good "portion" or "heritage" from God (v2), the words Zophar used for what God gives the wicked (20:29). "Calamity" is for the unrighteous (v3), the friends' own teaching, which Job does not deny in general. He denies that it explains him. And God sees everything (v4). Earlier Job complained that God watched "all my paths" (13:27). Here the same fact is a reason for holiness.
  • "If I have walked with falsehood" (vv5–8). Next, honesty in business. "Falsehood" is literally "emptiness." Francis Andersen, through Guzik, notes that the curse of crop failure in v8 points to crooked trading. "My heart has gone after my eyes" (v7) ties this back to v1: coveting also begins with looking. Henry recalls Achan: "I saw... then I coveted them and took them" (Josh 7:21).
  • "Let me be weighed in a just balance, and let God know my integrity!" (v6). In a bracketed aside Job asks to be put on God's own scales. A cheat used false weights (Prov 11:1). Henry thinks of Belshazzar, "weighed in the balances and found wanting" (Dan 5:27), a verdict Job did not fear. "Integrity" is the word God used of Job: "He still holds fast his integrity" (2:3).
  • "Then let me sow, and another eat" (v8). The first curse fits the crime: if Job got rich by cheating, let someone else eat his harvest. The ESV footnote gives "let my descendants be rooted out" as another reading. JFB prefers "what I plant," which suits the farm language.
  • "If my heart has been enticed toward a woman" (vv9–10). Now adultery itself. "Lain in wait at my neighbor's door" means watching for the husband to leave, the man Job described in 24:15: "The eye of the adulterer also waits for the twilight." The curse in v10 is hard, and it falls on his wife, which troubles modern readers. To "grind" (tachan) at the handmill was a slave woman's work (Exod 11:5; Isa 47:2), and the lexicon reads it here as serving another man as his slave. "Let others bow down on her" is a modest way of speaking of sexual use, and Henry and the Tyndale notes both allow that "grind" may carry that sense too. Job means that a man who took another's wife would deserve to lose his own. JFB compares God's word to David after Bathsheba (2 Sam 12:11).
  • "A heinous crime... a fire that consumes as far as Abaddon" (vv11–12). "Heinous crime" is zimmah. The lexicon gives its root sense as a scheme, and in a bad sense wickedness, especially sexual wickedness: incest (Lev 18:17), the outrage at Gibeah (Judg 20:6), and, here, adultery. "Punished by the judges" makes it a crime the community court would try, not only a private sin. JFB notes it was capital long before Moses (Gen 38:24). "Abaddon," "Destruction," is a name for the realm of the dead (26:6). The Tyndale notes observe that the Old Testament rarely links fire with that realm (compare Deut 32:22). Henry: "Burning lusts bring burning judgments."
  • "If I have rejected the cause of my manservant or my maidservant" (vv13–14). Job turns to the people with the least power in his house. "Cause" is rib, a legal complaint. When a servant had a grievance, Job heard it, though in most of the ancient world a slave had no standing against a master. Henry says Job was gentle with them "even when they contended with him." Andersen, through Guzik, judges this an ethic unequaled in the ancient world. Job's reason is that he had a Master too. God "rises up" like a judge standing to give sentence. Paul tells Christian masters the same: "he who is both their Master and yours is in heaven" (Eph 6:9).
  • "Did not he who made me in the womb make him?" (v15). This is the theological heart of the chapter. Master and slave were shaped by the same God in the same way. The Tyndale notes point to Proverbs: "Whoever oppresses a poor man insults his Maker" (Prov 14:31). Malachi asks, "Have we not all one Father?" (Mal 2:10). JFB, writing in the 1870s, answers slaveholders who claimed the slave was inferior by nature: Scripture makes common origin the ground of brotherly love.
  • "If I have withheld anything that the poor desired" (vv16–18). Here Job answers Eliphaz point by point. Eliphaz said he "withheld bread from the hungry" and "sent widows away empty" (22:7, 9). Job never let "the eyes of the widow fail" as she looked for help, and never ate "my morsel alone." Henry pictures orphans at Job's own table, not eating "of the crumbs that fell from his table, but with him." In another aside (v18), the ESV footnotes say the Hebrew has "he" for the orphan and "her" for the widow: Job has particular people in mind. "From my mother's womb" is a deliberate overstatement for "as long as I can remember." This is the "father to the needy" of 29:16.
  • "If his body has not blessed me" (vv19–20). The footnote gives the Hebrew as "his loins." The man with nothing to wear was clothed "with the fleece of my sheep," and his warmed body, so to speak, thanked Job. Eliphaz said Job "stripped the naked of their clothing" (22:6). Job did the reverse.
  • "Because I saw my help in the gate" (vv21–22). The "gate" was the law court, where elders sat (29:7). Job had friends there and could have bullied an orphan knowing the court would back him. JFB: he could "calculate on the 'help' of a powerful party in the court of justice." He had the power and did not use it. The curse fits again. Eliphaz said "the arms of the fatherless were crushed" (22:9). If Job raised his arm against an orphan, let his own be torn from its socket. The Tyndale notes compare Jesus' words about the hand that causes sin (Matt 5:30).
  • "For I was in terror of calamity from God" (v23). Job's kindness was not mere good manners. It came from the fear of God, which chapter 28 called wisdom (28:28). Henry calls God "the patron of oppressed poverty."
  • "If I have made gold my trust" (vv24–25). Job turns from neighbor to God. Eliphaz had told him to "lay gold in the dust" and make the Almighty his gold (22:24–25), implying that wealth was Job's real god. Job had plenty of gold, but it was never his "confidence." The Tyndale notes point out that Job no longer bothers to state the curse; the pattern is set. JFB calls greed "covert idolatry" (compare Col 3:5), leading into open idolatry in vv26–27. JFB has no separate note on v25.
  • "If I have looked at the sun when it shone... and my mouth has kissed my hand" (vv26–27). Sun and moon worship was among the oldest false religions of the ancient Near East. The ESV footnote says the Hebrew for "sun" is "the light." Moses warns against being "drawn away" to bow to them (Deut 4:19). A worshiper who could not reach the object would kiss his hand and throw the kiss toward it. JFB notes that the word "adoration" literally means this (from the Latin ad os, "to the mouth"). Compare the mouths that had not "kissed" Baal (1 Kgs 19:18). Job's heart was never "secretly enticed," the same verb used of a woman in v9. False worship and adultery begin the same way, with the eye leading the heart.
  • "I would have been false to God above" (v28). "To be punished by the judges" here uses pelili, an adjective meaning a matter for judges, which the lexicon finds only in this verse. To worship the sun is to deny the God who made it. JFB notes that Moses' law later made idolatry capital (Deut 17:2–7), and argues that this verse does not prove Job lived after Moses: the godly felt this way before the law was written.
  • "If I have rejoiced at the ruin of him who hated me" (vv29–30). No court could try this one. Job never even gloated when his enemy fell, as Proverbs warns: "Do not rejoice when your enemy falls" (Prov 24:17). He never asked for his enemy's life "with a curse." JFB says Job here "realizes the Gospel spirit," long before Jesus said, "Love your enemies" (Matt 5:44). There is an irony: Job calls curses down on himself all through the chapter, but he never cursed his enemy.
  • "Who is there that has not been filled with his meat?" (vv31–32). Translations differ. The ESV, with JFB's preferred reading, takes it as hospitality: Job's household could say no one left his table hungry. The King James reads "Oh that we had of his flesh!", and Henry takes it as Job's servants so angry at his enemy that they wanted to tear him apart, while Job restrained them. The ESV fits v32 better. In a land without inns, a stranger with no host slept in the town square, as the Levite nearly did at Gibeah (Judg 19:15–20). Job's door was open. The New Testament makes this a command (Rom 12:13; Heb 13:2).
  • "If I have concealed my transgressions as others do" (v33). The friends' whole case was that Job was hiding something. "As others do" is ke'adam, and the ESV footnote gives "as Adam did." Both are possible, since adam means "man" and is also the first man's name. Henry and JFB both see the fig leaves and the hiding in the garden (Gen 3:7–10). JFB adds that the very same Hebrew word for "hiding" is used of Adam in Genesis 3. The lexicon does not support that. Genesis uses chaba' for Adam hiding himself, while Job uses taman, to bury or hide something away, the verb used of Achan hiding the stolen goods in his tent (Josh 7:21). The point stands either way: "Whoever conceals his transgressions will not prosper" (Prov 28:13).
  • "Because I stood in great fear of the multitude" (v34). A man hiding his sin fears exposure, so he stays indoors and keeps quiet. Job was the opposite, taking his seat in the square while princes fell silent (29:7–10). The ESV takes the verse as part of the "if." JFB reads it as a curse instead: if Job hid his sin, let him be shamed before everyone.
  • "Oh, that I had one to hear me! (Here is my signature!...)" (v35). Job breaks off with one item still to come (vv38–40). He cannot hold back the cry heard all through the book: "Then call, and I will answer" (13:22). "Signature" is taw, the last letter of the Hebrew alphabet, which the lexicon defines as a mark, here a written mark signing a statement. In the old Hebrew script taw was written as an X or a cross. Ezekiel uses the word for the "mark" on the foreheads of the faithful (Ezek 9:4). Guzik passes on Mike Mason's reflection that Job was saying, in effect, "Here is my cross." That is a devotional thought, not what Job meant, though the letter's shape is real, and JFB notes it too. Job once wished his words "inscribed in a book" (19:23). Now he has signed them.
  • "Oh, that I had the indictment written by my adversary!" (v35). "Adversary" is literally "the man of my dispute," rib again. "Indictment" is sepher, which the lexicon defines here as a written charge. Job wants the charges in writing so he can answer them. JFB takes the adversary to be God, left vague on purpose. Henry thinks of the friends. Guzik notes that the reader of chapters 1–2 knows the real accuser was Satan, "the adversary." Job is right that someone has brought charges against him in heaven. He is wrong about who.
  • "I would bind it on me as a crown... like a prince I would approach him" (vv36–37). Job is so sure the charges are false that he would wear them proudly, and walk up to God with "an account of all my steps" (compare v4). It is the boldest line in the book. When God comes, Job will say instead, "I lay my hand on my mouth" (40:4). JFB says such confidence before God comes "not in Job's way," but "only through Jesus Christ" (Heb 10:22).
  • "If my land has cried out against me" (vv38–40). After the climax Job adds one more item. The land is a witness, and its furrows would weep if he had taken it by fraud, like Abel's blood crying from the ground (Gen 4:10). James borrows the picture: the withheld wages of "the laborers who mowed your fields... are crying out against you" (Jas 5:4). "Made its owners breathe their last" (v39) is the sin of Ahab against Naboth (1 Kgs 21), whom Henry names. JFB takes it as wearing owners down until they gave up the land, rather than murder. The last curse is "thorns... instead of wheat, and foul weeds instead of barley" (v40). "Foul weeds" is bo'shah, found only here, which the lexicon defines as stinking or noxious weeds. The Tyndale notes hear Eden: "thorns and thistles it shall bring forth for you" (Gen 3:18).
  • Why vv38–40 come last. Some scholars move these verses earlier in the chapter, since vv35–37 read like the natural end. No manuscript does so. JFB rejects Eichhorn's move of them after v25 as "quite unwarranted," and says an agitated man naturally comes back to his innocence after the usual stopping point, which shows Job's speech is not polished rhetoric.
  • "The words of Job are ended" (v40). This is the narrator's note. Job will speak again, but only briefly, to God, and in humility (40:3–5; 42:1–6). Henry: he "has now said all he would say in answer to his friends." Guzik passes on G. Campbell Morgan's thought that God often waits until people have said everything, then speaks into the silence. The friends will not answer, and Elihu has to start fresh (32:1–5).
  • Commentators: Henry gives the clearest outline, eleven sins Job clears himself of, from lust and fraud to cruelty to servants, trust in wealth, idolatry, revenge and seizing land, with the appeal to God (vv35–37) as the intended close. He calls the chapter "a noble pattern of piety and virtue." JFB frames chapter 29 as Job's public life and chapter 31 as his private life. It is the most careful on words (the "shoulder" and "forearm" of v22, "adoration" in v27, the cross-shaped taw), but its claim that v33 shares a verb with Genesis 3 does not hold, and it has no separate notes on vv16, 19 or 25. Guzik is the most practical. He passes on Mason's description of the chapter as Job's Sermon on the Mount and applies the covenant with the eyes directly to today's flood of pornography. He is also the most critical of vv35–37: Job was right about his own life but far too sure about what he could not see, and this is the demand he later repents of (42:5–6). Henry instead hears the rightful confidence of a clean conscience. Together they hold both truths: the life was real, and the confidence went too far. Guzik also relays Bullinger's view that v1 means a covenant with God against the eyes. The Hebrew allows it, but the lexicon simply lists the verse as a pledge made with oneself.

Pointing to Christ

Job's oath reaches where no court could: a glance, a gloating thought, a servant's complaint, a meal eaten alone. God himself called Job "blameless and upright" (1:8), and the chapter shows what that looked like. Yet when God comes, Job does not approach "like a prince." He puts his hand over his mouth. Only one man could sign this chapter without qualification: the one "tempted as we are, yet without sin" (Heb 4:15), who "committed no sin, neither was any deceit found in his mouth" (1 Pet 2:22). He fed the hungry, welcomed the outsider and defended the widow, and on the cross he prayed for his enemies rather than cursing them. Job wanted the charges against him written down so he could wear them as a crown. Christ took the written charges against his people, "the record of debt that stood against us," and wore a crown of thorns while that record was nailed "to the cross" (Col 2:14). Job asked to be weighed "in a just balance." In Christ, God laid our sin on his Son, "so that in him we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Cor 5:21). So believers can do what Job only longed for, "draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith" (Heb 10:22), resting not on their own oath but on his.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik calls this chapter "one of the highest ethical statements in the Old Testament." Job's standard of conduct exceeds the Mosaic Law in several areas — particularly his treatment of servants (v.15), his governance of inner thoughts (v.1), and his love for enemies (vv.29-30). Guzik notes that the self-imprecatory oath form means Job literally stakes his life on each claim. This is not boasting — it is sworn testimony with life-or-death consequences.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • A Covenant Made Before Temptation Arrives (v.1) — Job set a boundary with his own eyes long before any test came, deciding his standard of purity in advance rather than in the moment. What boundaries do you need to set now, before the moment of temptation is already upon you?
  • Every Person Bears the Same Maker's Image (v.15) — Job's fair treatment of his servants was rooted in the theological truth that the same God formed both master and servant. How does remembering that everyone shares equal standing before God change how you treat people with less power than you?
  • Generosity as a Non-Negotiable Practice (vv.16-23) — Job invites judgment on himself if he ever withheld help from the poor, the fatherless, or the naked. Is your generosity toward the vulnerable a settled practice or something you only get to when it's convenient?
  • Guarding the Heart From Subtler Idols (vv.24-28) — Job denies trusting in his wealth or bowing to the sun and moon, guarding his worship from anything that could quietly replace God. What modern equivalents of gold or the heavens compete for the trust that belongs to God alone?
  • Confidently Inviting God's Scrutiny (v.35) — Job doesn't fear an audit of his life; he demands one, so sure is he of his own integrity. Could you invite God to search your life the way Job does, or does that thought make you uneasy?
