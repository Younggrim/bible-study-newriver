Summary

A Song of Ascents comparing those who trust in the LORD to Mount Zion, immovable and enduring forever, just as the mountains surround Jerusalem (vv. 1-2). The psalmist affirms that the LORD surrounds his people the same way, and that the rule of the wicked will not remain over the land of the righteous, lest the righteous themselves be tempted toward evil (v. 3). He asks God to do good to the upright, while those who turn to crooked ways will be led away with evildoers, and closes with a blessing of peace upon Israel (vv. 4-5).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Song of Ascents / Psalm of Trust
Attributed Author: Anonymous (superscription: "A Song of degrees")
Key Themes: The immovability of those who trust God, God surrounding His people like mountains, the scepter of wickedness will not remain, peace upon Israel
Historical Context: Psalm 125 is the sixth Song of Ascents — a psalm of quiet confidence. Its central image is geographical: Jerusalem is surrounded by mountains, and God surrounds His people just as those mountains surround the city. For a pilgrim who has walked through the hills to reach Jerusalem, this imagery is viscerally real — they can see the mountains encircling the city and understand the metaphor with their eyes. The psalm also addresses a danger: if wickedness rules too long, even the righteous might be tempted to do wrong (v.3). But God will not allow it.
As Mount Zion, Which Cannot Be Removed (v.1): They that trust in the LORD shall be as mount Zion, which cannot be removed, but abideth for ever. The comparison is to a hill rather than to a fortress, so what is claimed is permanence and not strength. Trust is the only condition named.
As the Mountains Are Round About Jerusalem (v.2): As the mountains are round about Jerusalem, so the LORD is round about his people from henceforth even for ever. The psalm looks at the actual geography of the city, which sits in a bowl with higher ground on most sides, and reads the ring of hills as a picture of protection. Verse 1 made the people a mountain; this verse puts the mountains around them, and the two figures are not consistent, which is normal in Hebrew poetry and not a fault.
The Rod of the Wicked Shall Not Rest (v.3): For the rod of the wicked shall not rest upon the lot of the righteous. The promise is about duration, not absence: the rod is real and what is denied is its permanence. And the reason given is the most psychologically honest line in the Songs of Ascents, lest the righteous put forth their hands unto iniquity, which says plainly that oppression prolonged past a certain point corrupts the people it falls on. God is said to shorten it for their sake rather than to spare their feelings.
Peace Shall Be upon Israel (vv.4-5): Do good, O LORD, unto those that be good, and to them that are upright in their hearts. The prayer is frankly discriminating and the psalm makes no apology for it. As for such as turn aside unto their crooked ways, the LORD shall lead them forth with the workers of iniquity, so those who leave the road are counted with the people they resemble rather than with the people they came from. Then the last three words, which belong to the pilgrimage rather than to the argument, but peace shall be upon Israel.

Deep Dive

Setting: Psalm 125 is the sixth of the fifteen "Songs of Ascents" (Psalms 120–134), the pilgrim songs sung on the way up to Jerusalem for the festivals. Psalm 124 looked back on a narrow escape from enemies. Psalm 125 looks around at the hills of Jerusalem and finds in them a picture of how safe God's people are. The psalm has no author's name and no clear date. Its mention of "the scepter of wickedness" resting on the land (v3) suggests a time when Israel lived under some kind of foreign or ungodly rule. Many scholars place it after the exile, when Judah was a small province under Persian governors and pressure from neighbors was constant. Others place it in the time of the kings, when wicked rulers sometimes governed God's people. Pilgrims arriving at Jerusalem would have seen exactly what the psalm describes: the city on its ridge, ringed on every side by higher hills. The psalm has two parts: a confession of trust (vv1–3) and a prayer that separates the upright from those who turn aside (vv4–5). The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

Like Mount Zion (vv1–3)

  • "Those who trust in the LORD are like Mount Zion" (v1). Zion was the hill in Jerusalem where David's city stood, and later the name was used for the temple mount and for Jerusalem as a whole. The comparison is not with the people of Zion but with the mountain itself. The ones who are steady are not those who live in a certain place or belong to a certain family, but "those who trust in the LORD." The verb batach means to rely on, to lean one's whole weight on. Trust is the condition of the promise.
  • "Which cannot be moved, but abides forever" (v1). "Moved" (mot) is the word for tottering or slipping, used often in the Psalms: "he will never permit the righteous to be moved" (Ps 55:22; also 16:8; 62:2, 6). A mountain does not wobble. The pilgrims had already sung that God "will not let your foot be moved" (Ps 121:3). Those who trust God are not made immune to trouble, but they are kept from being toppled by it.
  • "As the mountains surround Jerusalem" (v2). Jerusalem sits on a ridge, with deep valleys on three sides, the Kidron on the east and the Hinnom on the south and west. Beyond the valleys rise higher hills: the Mount of Olives to the east, and the heights to the north and south. Zion is not the tallest peak in the area. It is the one surrounded. A pilgrim approaching the city would see it nestled in an amphitheater of hills.
  • "So the LORD surrounds his people" (v2). The hills are the picture, but the LORD himself is the protection. Zechariah later heard God promise that he would be "a wall of fire all around" Jerusalem (Zech 2:5). Elisha's servant had his eyes opened to see "the mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire all around Elisha" (2 Kings 6:17). Trust (v1) and protection (v2) belong together: those who lean on the LORD are the ones he encircles.
  • "From this time forth and forevermore" (v2). The same phrase closes Psalm 121:8 and 131:3. The protection is not for one trip or one season.
  • "The scepter of wickedness shall not rest on the land allotted to the righteous" (v3). A scepter (shebet, a rod or staff) was a symbol of rule. "The scepter of wickedness" means wicked rule, possibly a foreign power, possibly corrupt leaders within Israel. "Shall not rest" means it will not settle down and stay. "The land allotted" is literally "the lot of the righteous" (goral), recalling how the land was divided among the tribes by lot under Joshua (Josh 14:2). The land was God's gift to his people, and ungodly rule over it would not be permanent.
  • "Lest the righteous stretch out their hands to do wrong" (v3). This is the reason God will not let wicked rule last forever. Long oppression wears down the faithful. If the wicked seem to win for too long, even good people may be tempted to give up, compromise, or join in. God limits the length of the trial for the sake of his people's faithfulness. Paul makes the same promise: "God is faithful, and he will not let you be tempted beyond your ability" (1 Cor 10:13).
  • Commentators: Henry reads v1 broadly ("the church in general is called Mount Zion") and says believers "cannot be removed from their integrity nor from their confidence in God." On v2 he adds that "mountains may moulder and come to nought," but the LORD's protection will not. On v3 he says "God will cut the work short in righteousness" so that the trial does not overwhelm. JFB agrees that God may let the wicked oppress his people for a time as discipline, but not long enough to drive them into sin or despair, and says "sin's dominion shall not permanently come between the believer and his inheritance." Guzik notes that Zion is not the highest of the surrounding hills, and passes on Trapp's picture of a musician who keeps the strings of his instrument tight enough to play but not so tight they snap. He also warns, with Boice, against trusting in Zion itself, the place, rather than in the LORD.

Do Good, O LORD, to Those Who Are Good (vv4–5)

  • "Do good, O LORD, to those who are good" (v4). The confession turns into prayer. "Good" (tob) is the same word used for the goodness of creation in Genesis 1. The "good" here are not people without sin. They are the ones the psalm has already described, those who trust the LORD (v1) and refuse to reach for evil (v3).
  • "And to those who are upright in their hearts!" (v4). "Upright" (yashar) means straight or level. It describes a life that runs straight toward God, without hidden bends. "In their hearts" means it goes deeper than outward behavior. God sees the heart (1 Sam 16:7).
  • "But those who turn aside to their crooked ways" (v5). The contrast is with the straight. "Crooked ways" translates a rare Hebrew word ('aqalqallot), "twisted paths." It is used elsewhere only in the Song of Deborah, where travelers kept to "byways" because the main roads were too dangerous (Judg 5:6). These people are not open enemies of Israel. They are people within the community who "turn aside," who once walked the road and then drifted onto side paths. Proverbs describes them as those "who forsake the paths of uprightness to walk in the ways of darkness... whose paths are crooked" (Prov 2:13–15).
  • "The LORD will lead away with evildoers!" (v5). Those who choose crooked paths will end up with the people they chose to walk beside. The picture may be of prisoners led away, or simply of sharing the same end. It is a sober warning: belonging to Israel outwardly was no guarantee for someone who turned away in heart.
  • "Peace be upon Israel!" (v5). The psalm ends with a blessing, the same words that close Psalm 128:6. "Peace" (shalom) means wholeness, security and well-being. After separating the straight from the crooked, the psalm blesses the true Israel, the people who trust the LORD. Paul seems to echo it at the end of Galatians: "peace and mercy be upon them, and upon the Israel of God" (Gal 6:16).
  • Commentators: Henry puts v4 memorably: "Those that are as they should be shall be as they would be." On v5 he pictures the crooked led forth "as prisoners are led forth to execution," and he reads "peace be upon Israel" as both a prayer and a promise. JFB says the word "their" in v5 is emphatic: the crooked ways are their own choice, not forced on them. He adds that "the true Israel is here distinguished from the false." Guzik is careful to set the prayer of v4 in the light of the gospel: God's goodness reaches the ungodly too, since "while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Rom 5:8). He also passes on Boice's observation that the psalmist prays for the good to be blessed but does not need to pray for the crooked to be judged, because that is already certain.

Pointing to Christ

Psalm 125 promises that those who trust in the LORD will be like a mountain that cannot be moved, surrounded by God on every side. Jesus gives that promise its fullest meaning. He is the rock on which the wise man builds a house that does not fall (Matt 7:24–25), and those who trust in him are kept: "no one will snatch them out of my hand" (John 10:28). The Christian's security is not in a place, a city or a family line, but in a person. Hebrews tells believers they have come "to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God... and to Jesus" (Heb 12:22–24), and they are receiving "a kingdom that cannot be shaken" (Heb 12:28). The upright in heart the psalm prays for are, in the end, those whom Christ makes upright. He alone walked the straight path without ever turning aside, and he gives his righteousness to people who had wandered onto crooked ways. The scepter of wickedness will not rest forever on God's people, because the true scepter belongs to him: "the scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your kingdom" (Heb 1:8; Ps 45:6).

Reflection

  • Trust That Cannot Be Shaken (v.1) — Those who trust in the Lord are compared to Mount Zion, which cannot be moved and endures forever. Is your sense of stability rooted in your circumstances, or in a trust in God that would hold steady even if everything around you changed?
  • Surrounded on Every Side (v.2) — Just as mountains encircle Jerusalem without a gap, the Lord surrounds His people completely, now and forever. Where in your life do you need to believe there's no opening for the enemy to get through, because God has you surrounded?
  • A Limit on Wickedness's Reach (v.3) — God promises that the power of the wicked will not be allowed to rest permanently over the righteous, so they aren't tempted to compromise to survive. When you're under pressure from an unjust situation, are you tempted to compromise, or do you trust God to limit how long it lasts?
  • A Prayer for the Upright, a Warning for the Crooked (vv.4-5) — The psalm closes praying good for those who are honest in heart while warning that those who turn to crooked ways will be swept away with evildoers. Which direction is your own heart bending toward right now — integrity or compromise?
