1In the spring, at the time when kings march out to war, Joab led out the army and ravaged the land of the Ammonites. He came to Rabbah and besieged it, but David remained in Jerusalem. And Joab attacked Rabbah and demolished it.

2Then David took the crown from the head of their king. It was found to weigh a talent of gold and was set with precious stones, and it was placed on David’s head. And David took a great amount of plunder from the city.

4Some time later, war broke out with the Philistines at Gezer. At that time Sibbecai the Hushathite killed Sippai, a descendant of the Rephaim, and the Philistines were subdued.

5Once again there was a battle with the Philistines, and Elhanan son of Jair killed Lahmi the brother of Goliath the Gittite, the shaft of whose spear was like a weaver’s beam.

6And there was also a battle at Gath, where there was a man of great stature with six fingers on each hand and six toes on each foot—twenty-four in all. He too was descended from Rapha,

7and when he taunted Israel, Jonathan the son of David’s brother Shimei killed him.

8So these descendants of Rapha in Gath fell at the hands of David and his servants.

Summary

In spring, the season when kings typically went to war, Joab leads Israel's army to besiege and destroy Rabbah, the Ammonite capital, while David stays behind in Jerusalem (v. 1). David takes the enormous crown from Ammon's king, claims a large amount of plunder, and subjects the city's people to forced labor, his policy toward all the Ammonite cities (vv. 2-3). The rest of the chapter recounts three later battles with the Philistines: Sibbekai kills the giant Sippai at Gezer (v. 4), Elhanan kills Goliath's brother Lahmi (v. 5), and David's nephew Jonathan kills a taunting six-fingered, six-toed giant at Gath (vv. 6-7). All these giants descend from the Rephaim of Gath and fall by the hand of David and his men (v. 8), echoing David's own earlier triumph over Goliath.

Authorship & Background

Author: Traditionally attributed to Ezra the scribe. Originally one book with 2 Chronicles. Written post-exile (approximately 450-400 BC) for the returned remnant. The Chronicler retells Israel's history from a priestly/worship perspective, emphasizing the Davidic covenant, Temple worship, and God's faithfulness. Key themes: genealogical continuity (God preserved His people), proper worship (the Temple and its services), the Davidic line (pointing to Messiah), and hope for restoration.
Historical Context: Chapter 20 is one of the most theologically significant chapters in Chronicles for what it omits rather than what it includes. This brief chapter of only eight verses covers the capture of Rabbah (the Ammonite capital) and three encounters with Philistine giants. It parallels 2 Samuel 11:1, 12:26-31, and 21:18- 22 — but the Chronicler deliberately removes the entire Bathsheba- Uriah narrative that fills 2 Samuel 11-12. The chapter moves directly from "David tarried at Jerusalem" (the very words that in Samuel introduce the adultery) to the capture of Rabbah, skipping over two full chapters of sin, murder, prophetic confrontation, and repentance. This is not ignorance or carelessness — the Chronicler clearly knew the full story — but a deliberate editorial choice.
The Taking of Rabbah (vv.1-3): Three verses where Samuel has three chapters, and the omission is the most conspicuous in the book. Joab besieges Rabbah, and the Chronicler notes only that David tarried at Jerusalem, without saying what he did there. Bathsheba, Uriah, Nathan's parable and the death of the child are all absent. What is kept is the crown taken from the king's head, a talent of gold with precious stones in it, and the forced labour imposed on the people of the city.
The Sons of the Giant at Gath (vv.4-8): Three short encounters with unusually large men, and each is credited to a named soldier rather than to David: Sibbechai kills Sippai, Elhanan kills Lahmi the brother of Goliath, and Jonathan the son of Shimea kills a man with six fingers on every hand and six toes on every foot. The Chronicler's summary makes the point about who did it, they fell by the hand of David, and by the hand of his servants, which is his way of assigning credit to a reign rather than to a man.

Map & Geography

  • Largely parallels 1-2 Samuel geographically. Jerusalem (Zion/City of David) is the theological and political center.
  • Key sites: Hebron (David's first capital, 7 years), the threshing floor of Ornan/Araunah (future Temple site, ch.21).
  • Chapters 1-9 (genealogies) contain tribal allotments but minimal narrative geography.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik highlights the Chronicler's deliberate omission of the Bathsheba-Uriah narrative as a key interpretive feature. He notes that this is not dishonesty but a different purpose: Chronicles presents David as the covenant king whose reign establishes the pattern for Temple worship. Guzik also observes that the giant- killing passages show David raising up a generation of warriors who could do what only he had done before.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Faithful Work Continues Under Imperfect Leaders (v.1) — While David stays behind in Jerusalem, Joab leads the army and completes the siege of Rabbah regardless. Does your service to God depend on the people leading you being flawless, or can you stay faithful even when leadership disappoints?
  • Recurring Giants Don't Mean You're Losing (v.4) — A second giant from the same family line shows up after the first has already fallen. When a familiar temptation or struggle resurfaces after you thought you had beaten it, does that shake your faith, or remind you God has beaten it before?
  • No Giant Is Beyond God's Reach (v.6) — Even the intimidating six-fingered, six-toed giant falls to an ordinary warrior. What oversized problem in your life have you assumed is simply too big for God to handle?
  • Multiplying Victory Through Others (v.8) — The chapter credits the giants' defeat to David and his servants together, not David alone. Are you investing in others so they can fight battles you will not always be there to fight for them?
