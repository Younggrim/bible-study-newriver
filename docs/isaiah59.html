1Surely the arm of the LORD is not too short to save, nor His ear too dull to hear.

2But your iniquities have built barriers between you and your God, and your sins have hidden His face from you, so that He does not hear.

3For your hands are stained with blood, and your fingers with iniquity; your lips have spoken lies, and your tongue mutters injustice.

4No one calls for justice; no one pleads his case honestly. They rely on empty pleas; they tell lies; they conceive mischief and give birth to iniquity.

5They hatch the eggs of vipers and weave a spider’s web. Whoever eats their eggs will die; crack one open, and a viper is hatched.

6Their cobwebs cannot be made into clothing, and they cannot cover themselves with their works. Their deeds are sinful deeds, and acts of violence are in their hands.

7Their feet run to evil; they are swift to shed innocent blood. Their thoughts are sinful thoughts; ruin and destruction lie in their wake.

8The way of peace they have not known, and there is no justice in their tracks. They have turned them into crooked paths; no one who treads on them will know peace.

9Therefore justice is far from us, and righteousness does not reach us. We hope for light, but there is darkness; for brightness, but we walk in gloom.

10Like the blind, we feel our way along the wall, groping like those without eyes. We stumble at midday as in the twilight; among the vigorous we are like the dead.

11We all growl like bears and moan like doves. We hope for justice, but find none, for salvation, but it is far from us.

12For our transgressions are multiplied before You, and our sins testify against us. Our transgressions are indeed with us, and we know our iniquities:

13rebelling and denying the LORD, turning away from our God, speaking oppression and revolt, conceiving and uttering lies from the heart.

Summary

Isaiah opens by insisting that the exile is not due to God's inability to save but to the people's own sin, which has separated them from him (vv. 1-2). A sweeping catalog exposes their violence, lies, and injustice (vv. 3-8), prompting communal confession (vv. 9-15). Seeing no one willing to intervene, God himself takes up the cause, arming himself with righteousness as a breastplate and salvation as a helmet (vv. 16-17) before coming as Redeemer to Zion for those who turn from sin (v. 20). The chapter closes with God's covenant promise that his Spirit and his words will never depart from his people or their descendants (v. 21).

Authorship & Background

Author: Isaiah son of Amoz (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — Confession and Divine Warrior
Key Themes: Sin as separation from God, the catalog of human wickedness, no human intercessor found, God arms Himself as warrior, the Redeemer comes to Zion, the Spirit-and-Word covenant
Historical Context: Israel wonders why God hasn't delivered them. God's answer (v.2): it's not My inability — it's your iniquity. The chapter provides a comprehensive indictment of social and moral corruption (vv.3-8), followed by communal confession (vv.9-15a). When no human champion can be found to intervene (v.16), God arms himself with the armor of righteousness and salvation. Paul draws on this imagery for the Christian's armor (Ephesians 6:14-17). The chapter ends with a New Covenant promise: God's Spirit and words will remain with His people forever (v.21).
Your Iniquities Have Separated (vv.1-2): Behold, the LORD's hand is not shortened, that it cannot save, neither his ear heavy, that it cannot hear. Both clauses answer complaints made earlier in the book, at 50:2 and 58:3, and they answer them by denying the premise rather than by explaining the delay. Then the cause is located, but your iniquities have separated between you and your God, and your sins have hid his face from you, that he will not hear. The hiddenness of 45:15 and 57:17 is here given an address.
Their Webs Shall Not Become Garments (vv.3-8): For your hands are defiled with blood, and your fingers with iniquity. The catalogue that follows is the longest in the second half of the book and it is organized by body part, hands, fingers, lips, tongue, feet. The legal system is described as unusable, none calleth for justice, nor any pleadeth for truth. Then two images from natural history, they hatch cockatrice eggs, and weave the spider's web, with the point of each spelled out: the eggs produce something that bites, and their webs shall not become garments, that is, all that effort and nothing you could wear. Paul quotes verses 7 and 8 in Romans 3.
We Grope for the Wall (vv.9-15a): Therefore is judgment far from us, neither doth justice overtake us, we wait for light, but behold obscurity. This is a confession in the first person plural and it does not plead extenuation. The images are of impaired movement, we grope for the wall like the blind, and we grope as if we had no eyes, we stumble at noonday as in the night. Then the sounds, we roar all like bears, and mourn sore like doves. And the summary is legal, judgment is turned away backward, and justice standeth afar off, for truth is fallen in the street, and equity cannot enter.
There Was No Intercessor (vv.15b-16a): And the LORD saw it, and it displeased him that there was no judgment. Then the sentence that answers Ezekiel 22:30 and Jeremiah 5:1 from the same direction, and he saw that there was no man, and wondered that there was no intercessor. Three prophets looking for one person to stand in the gap and reporting the same result. The half-verse division here is the text's own: the sentence turns at the middle of verse 16 from what was not found to what was done instead.
He Put On Righteousness as a Breastplate (vv.16b-19): Therefore his arm brought salvation unto him, and his righteousness, it sustained him. Since no human intercessor was available, the intervention is direct, and it is described as a man dressing for battle: for he put on righteousness as a breastplate, and an helmet of salvation upon his head, and he put on the garments of vengeance for clothing. Paul redistributes the same armour to the church in Ephesians 6. And the outcome is geographical, so shall they fear the name of the LORD from the west, and his glory from the rising of the sun.
The Redeemer Shall Come to Zion (v.20): One verse, and the Redeemer shall come to Zion, and unto them that turn from transgression in Jacob, saith the LORD. Paul quotes it at the climax of his argument in Romans 11:26, where the direction is slightly different in the Greek. The word Redeemer is the family term used throughout these chapters, the relative obliged to buy his kinsman out.
My Words in Thy Mouth (v.21): As for me, this is my covenant with them, saith the LORD, My spirit that is upon thee, and my words which I have put in thy mouth, shall not depart out of thy mouth, nor out of the mouth of thy seed, nor out of the mouth of thy seed's seed, from henceforth and for ever. Three generations named in one clause, and what is promised is not protection or land but the continuance of a message. It is the same pairing of spirit and words that opens the next chapter but one at 61:1.

Setting: In chapter 58 God's people asked, "Why have we fasted, and you see it not?" (58:3). Chapter 59 answers the bigger form of that question: why has salvation not come? Is God too weak, or not listening? The chapter moves in three steps. First the prophet diagnoses the problem: the people's sin, described from hands to feet (vv1–8). Then the people speak, in a confession that starts as a lament and ends owning their guilt (vv9–15). Finally God himself steps in. Seeing no one to help, he puts on armor and comes as warrior and Redeemer (vv15–21). Paul draws on this chapter twice in Romans: once to show that all are under sin (Rom 3:15–17), and once to show that a Deliverer will come from Zion (Rom 11:26–27). The ESV breaks the chapter into two sections.

Evil and Oppression (vv1–13)

  • "Behold, the LORD's hand is not shortened, that it cannot save" (v1). A "short hand" means power that cannot reach. God asked the same thing in 50:2 ("Is my hand shortened, that it cannot redeem?") and of Moses in the wilderness ("Is the LORD's hand shortened?" Num 11:23). "Or his ear dull, that it cannot hear." The problem is not on God's side.
  • "But your iniquities have made a separation between you and your God" (v2). "Separation" means a dividing wall, the verb used for the curtain that divided the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place (Exod 26:33). Sin puts a barrier between people and God. "Your sins have hidden his face from you so that he does not hear." JFB notes that the Hebrew says the sins have "caused Him to hide" his face. The priestly blessing asked God to make his face shine on his people (Num 6:25). Sin does the opposite.
  • Separated, but not abandoned. Guzik observes that sin does not remove God's presence (Ps 139:7) or his love (Rom 5:8). It breaks fellowship and blocks blessing, like the prodigal son who was still a son in the far country (Luke 15). On the cross Jesus cried, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matt 27:46). He took on himself the separation that sin causes.
  • "Your hands are defiled with blood and your fingers with iniquity" (v3). The chapter goes through the body. Hands and fingers, lips and tongue (v3), feet (v7), thoughts (v7). Sin is not one compartment of life. Isaiah opened the book the same way: "From the sole of the foot even to the head, there is no soundness in it" (1:6) and "your hands are full of blood" (1:15).
  • "No one enters suit justly; no one goes to law honestly" (v4). The courts are corrupt. "Enters suit" means bringing a lawsuit, calling someone to account. Lawsuits were brought to win, not to see justice done. "They conceive mischief and give birth to iniquity." Sin is pictured as a pregnancy that ends in a deadly birth. Psalm 7:14 and James 1:15 use the same picture: "desire when it has conceived gives birth to sin, and sin when it is fully grown brings forth death."
  • "They hatch adders' eggs; they weave the spider's web" (v5). Two pictures of schemes. The adder's eggs hatch something deadly: eat them and you die, crush one and a viper comes out. Their plots harm anyone who gets involved, whatever they do. JFB: they foster evil instead of "crushing evil in the egg." The spider's web is a trap for the unwary. John the Baptist and Jesus called the religious leaders a "brood of vipers" (Matt 3:7; 12:34).
  • "Their webs will not serve as clothing" (v6). A spider's web looks like woven cloth but cannot cover anyone. Their works are flimsy and useless. They cannot clothe themselves with what they make. Compare Adam and Eve's fig leaves (Gen 3:7), and the "garments of salvation" God gives instead (61:10).
  • "Their feet run to evil, and they are swift to shed innocent blood" (v7). Proverbs uses nearly the same words of violent men (Prov 1:16). "Desolation and destruction are in their highways." Wherever they go, they leave ruin behind.
  • "The way of peace they do not know" (v8). Roads and paths run all through vv7–8. They made their roads crooked, and "no one who treads on them knows peace." Compare 57:21, "There is no peace… for the wicked."
  • Paul and the whole world (vv7–8). In Romans 3:10–18 Paul strings together Old Testament verses to show that "none is righteous, no, not one." Verses 15–17 come from here: "Their feet are swift to shed blood; in their paths are ruin and misery, and the way of peace they have not known." Isaiah described Israel. Paul uses it to show that Jews and Gentiles alike are "under sin" (Rom 3:9), so that "every mouth may be stopped" (Rom 3:19). Isaiah is not exaggerating. He is diagnosing.
  • "Therefore justice is far from us, and righteousness does not overtake us" (v9). Now the voice shifts to "us." The people speak. "Justice" and "righteousness" here mean God's saving intervention, the help they have been waiting for. It has not caught up with them. "We hope for light, and behold, darkness." In 58:8, 10, light was promised to those who turned from oppression. They have not turned, so the light has not come.
  • "We grope for the wall like the blind" (v10). A blind person feels along a wall to find the way. "We stumble at noon as in the twilight." This fulfils the covenant curse of Deuteronomy 28:29: "you shall grope at noonday, as the blind grope in darkness." JFB notes the link. "Among those in full vigor we are like dead men." The Hebrew is uncertain here. It may mean "among the healthy" or "in desolate places," and translations differ. The point is clear: they feel lifeless.
  • "We all growl like bears; we moan and moan like doves" (v11). Two animal sounds of distress, the bear's restless growl and the dove's mournful coo (38:14). One is angry frustration, the other helpless sorrow. "We hope for justice, but there is none; for salvation, but it is far from us." The lament repeats v9.
  • "For our transgressions are multiplied before you, and our sins testify against us" (v12). The turning point. They stop blaming God and start confessing. Sins are pictured as witnesses in court. "We know our iniquities." David prayed, "I know my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me" (Ps 51:3).
  • "Transgressing, and denying the LORD, and turning back from following our God" (v13). The confession names sin as it truly is: rebellion against God, not just wrongs against people. "Speaking oppression and revolt, conceiving and uttering from the heart lying words." The picture of conception from v4 comes back. Now they admit it is their heart.
  • Commentators: Henry's introduction sums up the chapter as showing "sin appearing exceedingly sinful, and grace appearing exceedingly gracious." He says their sins had "stopped the current of God's favours to them" and that "they had themselves procured the judgments of God upon them." Guzik quotes Motyer that Isaiah "is not describing but diagnosing." He notes that the description covers sin from head to toe and that Paul uses it for all humanity. JFB links the groping of v10 to the curse of Deuteronomy 28:29, and notes that every part of the body is busy in evil, thoughts as well as deeds.

Judgment and Redemption (vv14–21)

  • "Justice is turned back, and righteousness stands far away" (v14). The confession reaches its lowest point. Justice, righteousness, truth and uprightness are pictured as people trying to get into the city and failing. "Truth has stumbled in the public squares." The public square, near the gate, was where courts met and business was done. Truth tripped and fell there, and no one picked it up.
  • "He who departs from evil makes himself a prey" (v15). Anyone who refuses to go along with corruption becomes a target. Honesty is dangerous. Paul: "all who desire to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted" (2 Tim 3:12).
  • "The LORD saw it, and it displeased him that there was no justice" (v15). God has been watching. "Displeased" is literally "it was evil in his eyes."
  • "He saw that there was no man, and wondered that there was no one to intercede" (v16). "Wondered" means appalled, the same word used of the crowds horrified at the Servant (52:14). God looked for someone to step in, a leader or intercessor, and there was no one. Ezekiel 22:30 says the same: "I sought for a man… who should… stand in the breach before me… but I found none." The word "intercede" is pagaʿ, the same verb used of the Servant who "makes intercession for the transgressors" (53:12). No one in Israel could do it. The Servant would.
  • "Then his own arm brought him salvation, and his righteousness upheld him" (v16). God's "arm" is his own power to save, the arm that was bared in 52:10 and revealed in the Servant in 53:1. When no human could rescue, God did it himself. JFB: there was "no representative man able to retrieve the cause of fallen men."
  • "He put on righteousness as a breastplate, and a helmet of salvation on his head" (v17). God dresses for battle. Paul borrows this armor for believers: "the breastplate of righteousness… the helmet of salvation" (Eph 6:14, 17), and "the breastplate of faith and love, and for a helmet the hope of salvation" (1 Thess 5:8). What God wears to save, he shares with those he saves. JFB notes that believers share the breastplate and helmet, but not the "garments of vengeance" or the "cloak" of zeal, which belong to God alone. Vengeance is his (Rom 12:19).
  • "According to their deeds, so will he repay" (v18). God's coming means judgment on his enemies as well as rescue for his people. "Coastlands" means the distant lands and islands across the sea, the far reaches of the world (41:1). The same day brings both. Isaiah 61:2 will call it "the year of the LORD's favor, and the day of vengeance of our God."
  • "So they shall fear the name of the LORD from the west, and his glory from the rising of the sun" (v19). West to east, the whole world. "Fear" here means reverent awe. Malachi says God's name "will be great among the nations, from the rising of the sun to its setting" (Mal 1:11).
  • "He will come like a rushing stream, which the wind of the LORD drives" (v19). Older readers know this verse from the KJV: "When the enemy shall come in like a flood, the Spirit of the LORD shall lift up a standard against him." The Hebrew can be divided and read either way. The word tsar can mean "enemy" or "narrow," as in a narrow, rushing river. The word for "wind" (ruach) can also mean "Spirit." The verb can mean "lift a banner" or "drive along." The ESV and most modern versions read it as God coming like a flood in a narrow channel, pushed by his wind. JFB also preferred a different sense from the KJV. Both readings say God's power cannot be stopped. The ESV reading keeps the focus on God, as the whole passage does.
  • "And a Redeemer will come to Zion, to those in Jacob who turn from transgression" (v20). The Redeemer is the goʾel, the kinsman-redeemer. Guzik lists his roles in the Law: buying back a relative sold into slavery (Lev 25:48), buying back lost family land (Lev 25:25), avenging a murdered relative (Num 35:19), and marrying a widow to continue the family line (Deut 25:5–10; Ruth 3–4). God himself will do all of this for his people. He comes for those who turn, shuv, the word for repentance. Salvation and repentance go together.
  • Paul and the Redeemer (v20). In Romans 11:26–27 Paul quotes this verse from the Greek version: "The Deliverer will come from Zion, he will banish ungodliness from Jacob." The Greek reads "from" Zion and makes the Deliverer the one who turns them from sin. Paul uses it to say that "all Israel will be saved." Christians differ on what Paul means: a large turning of the Jewish people to Christ near the end, or the full number of God's people from Jews and Gentiles together. Both read Isaiah as fulfilled in Christ.
  • "As for me, this is my covenant with them" (v21). The verse changes from poetry to prose and speaks to a "you" singular. God's covenant is that "my Spirit that is upon you, and my words that I have put in your mouth, shall not depart." God's Spirit rested on the Servant (42:1; 61:1), and God put his words in his mouth (49:2; 50:4; 51:16). Here the gift passes on to "your offspring" and "your children's offspring," forever. JFB reads it as a covenant first with the Messiah, then with those united to him.
  • The new covenant (v21). This is very close to Jeremiah's new covenant ("I will put my law within them," Jer 31:33) and Ezekiel's ("I will put my Spirit within you," Ezek 36:27). At Pentecost Peter promised the Spirit "for you and for your children and for all who are far off" (Acts 2:39), echoing this promise to generation after generation. The Tyndale notes connect it to Joel 2:28 and Acts 2:39.
  • Commentators: Henry reads vv16–19 as God acting "purely for his own name's sake," and says in vv20–21 he will "reserve mercy in store for them and entail it upon them," that is, pass it down like an inheritance. Guzik and JFB both connect the armor of v17 to Ephesians 6. Guzik draws the lesson that God would rather work through willing people, though his purposes stand whether they join him or not. JFB adds that believers share the armor only "in derived form." On v19 JFB prefers a rendering against the KJV, as modern versions do. Guzik explains goʾel in v20 as kinsman-redeemer with its legal duties, which fits the Hebrew word. JFB notes that Paul quotes v20 as "out of Zion."

Pointing to Christ

The chapter shows humanity at its worst and God at his best. No one could stand in the breach, so God's own arm brought salvation. The Servant of 53:12 is the intercessor God could not find among men. Paul quotes the first half of this chapter to prove that everyone is guilty, and the second half to show that a Deliverer comes from Zion to take away sin. Jesus is that kinsman-Redeemer, who became our relative by taking on flesh (Heb 2:14–17), bought us back, and gave his Spirit and his words to his people and their children, "from this time forth and forevermore."

Reflection

  • Your Sins Have Separated You (v.2) — Isaiah insists God's hand isn't too short to save and His ear isn't too dull to hear — it's the people's iniquities that have created the separation. When God feels distant, do you consider whether something on your side needs to be confessed?
  • No One Calls for Justice (v.4) — No one in the community pleads a case honestly; instead they trust in empty arguments and speak lies. Do you value being technically right over actually being honest and just?
  • No Intercessor Found (v.16) — God looks and is appalled that there is no one to intervene, so His own arm brings about salvation instead. Have you ever tried to be your own solution to a problem only God was ever going to be big enough to solve?
  • God Puts On His Own Armor (v.17) — God arms Himself with righteousness as a breastplate and salvation as a helmet before acting on His people's behalf. When you put on the armor of God, do you remember it's His armor lent to you, not something you assembled yourself?
  • The Redeemer Comes to Zion (v.20) — The Redeemer comes to Zion specifically for those who turn from transgression. Is turning from sin something you actively pursue, or something you assume happens passively over time?
  • A Covenant That Never Departs (v.21) — God promises His Spirit and His words will never leave His people or their descendants, from now on and forever. Do you expect God's presence in your life to be permanent, or something that comes and goes with your performance?
