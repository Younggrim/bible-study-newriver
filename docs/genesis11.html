Summary

The whole earth shares one language, and as people migrate they settle in the plain of Shinar and decide to build a city and a tower reaching toward heaven to make a name for themselves and avoid being scattered (vv. 1-4). God observes their unified rebellion and confuses their language so they cannot understand one another, scattering them across the earth and leaving the city named Babel (vv. 5-9). The chapter then traces the genealogy from Shem down through ten generations to Terah, the father of Abram, Nahor, and Haran (vv. 10-26). It closes by introducing Abram's family, noting Sarai's barrenness, and recording their move from Ur toward Canaan, where they settle instead in Haran (vv. 27-32).

Authorship & Background

Author: Moses (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 11 has two parts: the Tower of Babel (vv.1-9) and the genealogy from Shem to Abram (vv.10-32). Babel represents humanity's united rebellion against God — building a tower to "make us a name" rather than filling the earth as commanded. God responds by confusing languages and scattering the people. The genealogy then narrows from all nations to one family — Terah's — setting the stage for Abram's call in chapter 12.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: one language and one people building not to fill the earth as commanded but to make a name and avoid being scattered, a tower so small God must, ironically, come down merely to see it, a confusion of language and a scattering that turns humanity's own act of defiance into the mechanism of its judgment, ten generations from Shem to Abram in which recorded lifespans fall dramatically from six hundred years to two hundred five, and a family that stops short of Canaan at Haran, leaving the story poised exactly where God's call in chapter twelve will pick it up
The Tower of Babel (vv.1-9): One language, one people (v.1). They settle in Shinar (Babylon) and decide to build a city and tower "whose top may reach unto heaven" (v.4). Their motive: "let us make us a name, lest we be scattered" (v.4) — directly opposing God's command to fill the earth (9:1). God "came down" to see their work (v.5 — ironic: their tower reaching heaven is so small God must come down). God confuses their language (v.7) and scatters them (v.8). The city is named "Babel" — confusion (v.9).
The Genealogy of Shem (vv.10-26): Ten generations from Shem to Abram. Lifespans decrease dramatically (Shem 600 → Terah 205). The line narrows toward the one man through whom God will bless all nations.
Terah's Family (vv.27-32): Terah has three sons: Abram, Nahor, Haran. Haran dies young in Ur (v.28). Abram marries Sarai — "she was barren" (v.30). Terah takes the family from Ur toward Canaan but stops at Haran (v.31). Terah dies in Haran (v.32). The stage is set for God's call.

Map & Geography

  • Shinar (v.2) — the plain of Babylonia (modern southern Iraq). The people migrate eastward (away from God's presence, the recurring Genesis pattern).
  • Babel (v.9) — Babylon, one of the most significant cities in ancient history. Its name means "gate of god" but is linked to "confusion" (balal).
  • Ur of the Chaldeans (v.28) — Abram's origin city in southern Mesopotamia (modern Tell el-Muqayyar, Iraq). A major Sumerian urban center.
  • Haran (v.31) — a city in northwest Mesopotamia (modern Harran, Turkey), a caravan crossroads on the route between Ur and Canaan.

Deep Dive

Setting: Chapter 10 laid out the seventy nations spread across the world "each with his own language" (10:5), and it noted in passing that in Peleg's days "the earth was divided" (10:25). Chapter 11 steps back to tell how that happened. The story of Babel is the last of the great judgments in Genesis 1–11, after the garden, Cain and the flood, and it follows the same pattern: human beings reach for what belongs to God, and God responds with judgment mixed with mercy. Then the camera narrows. A genealogy runs ten generations from Shem to Terah, and the chapter ends with one family, one barren woman and a journey that stops halfway. Babel sets the problem: a scattered, divided humanity. The family of Terah sets up God's answer, which begins in chapter 12 with Abram.

The Tower of Babel (vv1–9)

  • "The whole earth had one language and the same words" (v1). Literally "one lip and the same words." The story begins where chapter 10 ended, with the whole earth, but it goes back in time to before the nations separated. The account is carefully built as a mirror: the people speak and build (vv1–4), the LORD comes down (v5), the LORD speaks and acts (vv6–9). Many of the words in the first half come back reversed in the second: "one language," "let us," "build," "name," "dispersed."
  • "As people migrated from the east, they found a plain in the land of Shinar" (v2). Shinar is southern Mesopotamia, the heart of Babylonia, where Nimrod's kingdom began (10:10). Movement "east" in Genesis often means movement away from God: Adam and Eve were driven east of Eden (3:24), Cain settled east of Eden (4:16), and Lot will choose the east (13:11).
  • "Come, let us make bricks… brick for stone, and bitumen for mortar" (v3). Mesopotamia had little stone, so its builders invented fired brick set in bitumen, a natural tar. The Hebrew has a sound-play: nilbenah lebenim, "let us brick bricks." The note "brick for stone" is written for readers in Canaan, a land of stone, and it carries a quiet irony: Babel's great works were made of baked mud. Later, Israel in Egypt is forced to make bricks for Pharaoh's cities (Exod 1:14).
  • "A city and a tower with its top in the heavens" (v4). The tower was most likely a ziggurat, a stepped temple-mountain of brick. Babylon had a famous one called Etemenanki, "house of the foundation of heaven and earth," and Mesopotamian cities treated these towers as stairways where the gods came down to meet people. "Top in the heavens" is a common ancient way of saying "very tall" (Deut 1:28 speaks of cities "fortified up to heaven"). The point is not that they hoped to climb into God's home with bricks, but that they wanted to bring heaven within reach on their own terms.
  • "Let us make a name for ourselves" (v4). Here is the heart of the matter. "Name" is shem, the same word as the name of Noah's son Shem. The builders want fame, security and identity they have made for themselves. In the very next chapter God tells Abram, "I will make your name great" (12:2). A name grasped at becomes Babel. A name received from God becomes blessing. The same pattern appears with David (2 Sam 7:9).
  • "Lest we be dispersed over the face of the whole earth" (v4). God had told Noah's family to "fill the earth" (9:1). The builders resist. They want to stay together, in one place, under one tower. The word for "dispersed" (puts) comes back in vv8–9 when the LORD does exactly what they feared. What they built to prevent came about because of what they built.
  • "The LORD came down to see the city and the tower" (v5). This is gentle irony. The tower that was meant to reach the heavens is so small that God has to "come down" to see it. The phrase doesn't mean God lacked information. It is the language of a judge who investigates before he acts, as with Sodom later (18:21). "The children of man," literally "sons of Adam," reminds the reader that the builders are creatures of dust.
  • "This is only the beginning of what they will do" (v6). God sees where unchecked, united human pride leads. "Nothing that they propose to do will now be impossible for them" is not praise for human potential but a warning about what sinful people united in rebellion can do. Scattering them is a restraint, much as barring the way to the tree of life was a mercy after the fall (3:22–24).
  • "Come, let us go down and there confuse their language" (v7). God answers their "Come, let us" with his own "Come, let us." The plural has been read in several ways, as in 1:26 and 3:22. The Tyndale notes take it as God speaking to his heavenly court of angels. Many Christian readers, including Guzik, hear a hint of the Trinity. Others take it as a plural of majesty or of self-encouragement. The text doesn't settle it. "Confuse" is balal, to mix or mingle, a word used elsewhere for flour mixed with oil (Lev 2:5).
  • "So the LORD dispersed them… and they left off building the city" (v8). The judgment is quiet. No fire, no flood, no one killed. God simply takes away their ability to understand one another, and the great project stops. Notice it is the city they stop building. A unity based on pride cannot survive when people cannot understand each other.
  • "Therefore its name was called Babel, because there the LORD confused" (v9). In Babylon's own language the name Bab-ili meant "gate of god," a proud claim. Genesis answers with a pun: it sounds like balal, "confused." The city the Babylonians called the gate of heaven is, in God's eyes, the place of confusion. It is a mocking wordplay, not a claim about the name's origin. Babylon remains the Bible's picture of proud human power set against God, all the way to "Babylon the great" in Revelation 17–18.
  • Babel and Pentecost. At Pentecost, the Spirit enabled the apostles to speak so that people "from every nation under heaven" heard "each one in his own language" (Acts 2:5–6). Babel's confusion was answered, not by a return to one language but by one gospel heard in many. The prophets looked toward it: "I will change the speech of the peoples to a pure speech, that all of them may call upon the name of the LORD" (Zeph 3:9).
  • Commentators: Henry sums up the builders' attitude: "They would do something to be talked of now," and about God's command to disperse, "we will not, we will live and die together." He says God came down "not merely as a spectator, but as a judge," and that "God has various means, and effectual ones, to baffle and defeat the projects of proud men." He also reflects on the scattering as a mercy, since in this life God's judgments on sinners "are little more than restraints." Guzik also stresses the mercy in the scattering, which limits how far sinful human unity can go. He suggests the tower was built for watching the heavens and ties astrology back to Babel, a link the text doesn't make. He also points out that the tar-sealed brick might have been meant as protection from another flood, which would show distrust of God's promise in 9:11. JFB reads "unto heaven" as "a common figurative expression for great height," and sees the plan "to prevent emigration" as "foolish, wicked, and justly offensive to God." JFB has no comment on vv5, 8 and 9.

Shem's Descendants (vv10–26)

  • "These are the generations of Shem" (v10). This is a new section heading (toledot). Chapter 10 followed Shem's line out through Joktan into Arabia. This list follows the other branch, through Peleg, straight to Abram. Like Genesis 5, it gives ten generations, from Shem to Terah, using the same pattern: age at the son's birth, years lived afterward, "other sons and daughters." It leaves out the refrain "and he died" that marked chapter 5. Here the focus is less on death and more on the line moving forward.
  • "Two years after the flood" (v10). The list is tied to the flood as its starting point. A new world, but the same steady line of promise.
  • Shrinking lifespans. Shem lives 600 years, Arpachshad 438, Eber 464, then the numbers drop sharply after Peleg: 239, 239, 230, 148. The drop after Peleg, the man "in whose days the earth was divided" (10:25), is striking. By Abraham's time 175 years is a full life (25:7), and by Moses' time it's 120 (Deut 34:7). Genesis seems to show that life is winding down after the flood, as human nature drifts further from Eden.
  • Is every generation listed? Hebrew genealogies sometimes skip generations, and "fathered" can mean "became the ancestor of." The Tyndale notes allow for this. Two facts point that way here. The list is shaped into a neat ten, like Genesis 5. And the ancient Greek translation adds a name, Cainan, between Arpachshad and Shelah, a name Luke also includes in Jesus' genealogy (Luke 3:36). The Greek translation also has different numbers in places. Some Christians read the list as a complete chronology, while others see a selected one. Both agree on its purpose: to connect the promise given to Shem (9:26) with Abram.
  • "When Terah had lived 70 years, he fathered Abram, Nahor, and Haran" (v26). Like Noah's three sons (5:32), Terah's three sons are named together, and the one through whom God works comes first, not necessarily because he was born first. This matters for the dates (see v32).
  • Commentators: Guzik and JFB both pass over the list with little comment. Guzik treats it mainly as the bridge from Shem to Abram, the man through whom God would begin his remedy for Babel. JFB has no comment on any verse from v10 to v27.

Terah's Descendants (vv27–32)

  • "These are the generations of Terah" (v27). This heading begins the whole story of Abraham, which runs to 25:11. It is named after Terah, the father, though Abram is its real subject.
  • "Haran died in the presence of his father Terah… in Ur of the Chaldeans" (v28). A son dies before his father, a grief made worse in an ancient family. His son Lot is left fatherless and later travels with his uncle Abram. Ur was a great city of southern Mesopotamia near the Persian Gulf, famous for its wealth and its temple to the moon god. "Of the Chaldeans" uses the name of a people who controlled the region much later, updating the place name for later readers.
  • Terah's family and idolatry. Joshua tells Israel, "Long ago, your fathers lived beyond the Euphrates, Terah, the father of Abraham and of Nahor; and they served other gods" (Josh 24:2). Several names in the family may be connected to moon worship. Terah's name may be related to the Hebrew word for moon, and Milcah's name was a title of a moon goddess's daughter. Both Ur and Haran were centers of moon worship. These links are suggestive, not certain. What is certain is that God didn't call Abram because his family was already devoted to him. The call was pure grace.
  • "The name of Abram's wife was Sarai… Milcah, the daughter of Haran" (v29). Nahor marries his niece Milcah, and their family will matter later: their granddaughter is Rebekah, Isaac's wife (24:15). Sarai's parents aren't named here. Later Abram says she is "the daughter of my father though not the daughter of my mother" (20:12), so she was his half-sister. Such marriages were common then, though Moses' law later forbade them (Lev 18:9). The name Sarai is an older form of Sarah, both meaning "princess." Jewish tradition and JFB identify Sarai with Iscah (v29), but 20:12 makes her Terah's daughter, not his granddaughter, so most interpreters set that idea aside.
  • "Now Sarai was barren; she had no child" (v30). The Hebrew says it twice, once with a word and once with a phrase, so the reader feels the weight of it. This one short line is the most important sentence in the paragraph. The next chapter promises Abram a great nation (12:2), and here, before the promise is even spoken, the reader knows it is humanly impossible. The pattern continues with Rebekah (25:21), Rachel (29:31), Hannah (1 Sam 1:2) and Elizabeth (Luke 1:7): God builds his people through births only he can give.
  • "They went forth together from Ur… to go into the land of Canaan, but when they came to Haran, they settled there" (v31). Haran was about 550 miles northwest of Ur, in what is now southern Turkey, a caravan town on the great trade routes. The route from Ur to Canaan curves north along the rivers, so Haran was on the way. Stephen says God appeared to Abram while he was still in Ur (Acts 7:2–4), and Genesis 15:7 agrees ("I am the LORD who brought you out from Ur"). So the journey began with God's call, but Terah leads it and it stalls halfway. Many readers see a lesson in partial obedience. Others note simply that in that culture the father led the family, and Abram moves on only after Terah's death.
  • "The days of Terah were 205 years, and Terah died in Haran" (v32). Here is a well-known puzzle. If Terah was 70 at Abram's birth (v26) and Abram left Haran at 75 (12:4), Terah would still have had 60 years to live. Yet Stephen says Abram left "after his father died" (Acts 7:4). Two answers are common. Abram may not have been Terah's firstborn, but named first because of his importance, born when Terah was 130. Or the Samaritan version of the Pentateuch, which gives Terah's age at death as 145, may preserve the number Stephen knew. Either way, Genesis tells Terah's death before Abram's departure, so Terah's story closes before Abram's story opens.
  • Commentators: Guzik reads the stop at Haran as Abram's half-obedience, bringing his father along instead of leaving his family as God told him. He also finds meaning in the names, suggesting Sarai means "contentious." The lexicon treats Sarai as an older form of Sarah, "princess," and the Tyndale notes agree, so that reading should be set aside. JFB identifies Ur with Orfa (modern Urfa, in southern Turkey) and says Terah's family "were equally infected" with the idolatry of the place. Most scholars today place Ur in the far south of Iraq, though a minority still argue for a northern Ur near Haran. JFB skips vv29–30 and 32. Guzik and JFB agree with the Tyndale notes that the family worshiped other gods, so Abram's call was grace from start to finish.

Pointing to Christ

Babel is humanity's best attempt to reach heaven, make a name and hold itself together, and it ends in confusion. God's answer begins with a barren woman and a family that has stopped halfway on the road. The name the builders tried to make for themselves, God gives to Abram, and through Abram's line, to the one who is given "the name that is above every name" (Phil 2:9). Babel tried to build a stairway to heaven. Jesus says he is the true ladder between heaven and earth (John 1:51). At Babel the LORD came down to scatter; in Christ he came down to gather, and at Pentecost the scattered languages heard one message about him. One day people "from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages" will stand together before the Lamb (Rev 7:9), united not by a tower of their own but by the name of the Lamb.

Reflection

  • Building for Self Instead of God (v.4) — The builders' stated motive is to make a name for themselves and avoid being scattered, directly defying God's command to fill the earth. Where are you building your own reputation, achievement, or sense of control rather than pursuing what God has actually called you to?
  • A Tower Too Small to Notice (v.5) — God has to "come down" to see the tower, an ironic detail showing how small humanity's greatest achievement looks from His perspective. Does recognizing how small your accomplishments look to God humble you and free you from the pressure to impress others?
  • Scattered in Judgment (v.8) — God confuses the builders' language and scatters them across the earth, undoing their united rebellion. Where has God interrupted a plan you were pursuing apart from Him, and how did you respond?
  • Barrenness Before the Promise (v.30) — The genealogy pauses to note that Sarai was barren, setting up the seeming impossibility God will work through in the next chapter. Where in your life does the situation look impossible, and could that be exactly where God intends to work?
  • Stopping Short of the Destination (v.31) — Terah sets out for Canaan but settles partway, in Haran, and never completes the journey. Are you settling for a halfway obedience instead of following God's call all the way through?
