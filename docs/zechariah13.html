Summary

A fountain will be opened for the house of David and the people of Jerusalem to cleanse them from sin and impurity, and God will remove the names of idols from the land along with the false prophets and the unclean spirit (vv. 1-2). Any who still try to prophesy falsely will be exposed and rejected even by their own parents, and prophets will abandon the prophet's garb and claim instead to be farmers rather than admit what they are (vv. 3-6). God then calls for a sword to strike his own shepherd, scattering the sheep, while he turns his hand against the little ones (v. 7). Two-thirds of the people in the land will perish, but God will bring the remaining third through fire, refining them like silver and gold until they call on his name and are recognized again as his own people (vv. 8-9).

Authorship & Background

Author: Zechariah son of Berechiah. Prophesied 520-480 BC. Contemporary with Haggai. Key theme: Messianic hope and the coming kingdom. The most Messianic of the Minor Prophets. Famous passages: the Branch (3:8, 6:12), "Not by might... but by my spirit" (4:6), the triumphal entry (9:9), "they shall look upon me whom they have pierced" (12:10), the shepherd struck (13:7), and the LORD's return to the Mount of Olives (14:4).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — The Fountain for Sin, the Smitten Shepherd, and the Refined Remnant
Key Themes: A fountain opened for cleansing from sin, the end of idolatry and false prophecy, the shepherd struck by God's own sword, the sheep scattered, the remnant refined through fire, covenant restoration
Historical Context: Chapter 13 flows directly from chapter 12's vision of mourning over the pierced one. The mourning of 12:10-14 leads to the cleansing of 13:1. Repentance produces purification. "In that day" a fountain is opened — not for ritual washing but for sin and uncleanness. The blood of Christ is this fountain, available for all who mourn over what their sins have done to Him.
The Fountain Opened for Sin and Uncleanness (v.1): In that day there shall be a fountain opened to the house of David and to the inhabitants of Jerusalem for sin and for uncleanness. The mourning of chapter 12 runs straight into this without a break, and what is opened is a spring rather than a basin. The law's provision for uncleanness was water carried, measured and used up, and a fountain is the one form of water that cannot be exhausted by the number of people using it.
The Purging of Idols and False Prophets (vv.2-6): I will cut off the names of the idols out of the land, and they shall no more be remembered. Not just the objects but the names, so the vocabulary goes as well. Then the prophets, and the treatment is severe: a man whose own father and mother put him to death for speaking lies in the LORD's name. The result is a trade in disguises, they shall be ashamed every one of his vision, and shall not wear a rough garment to deceive. Each denies the office, I am no prophet, I am an husbandman. And the wounds in his hands get an explanation, those with which I was wounded in the house of my friends, a line read for centuries as pointing past the false prophet to the true one.
The Sword Against the Shepherd and the Scattered Sheep (v.7): Awake, O sword, against my shepherd, and against the man that is my fellow, saith the LORD of hosts, smite the shepherd, and the sheep shall be scattered. The sword is commanded, and it is commanded by God against a man he calls his fellow, his own associate. Jesus quotes the verse on the way to Gethsemane and applies the scattering to the disciples that night. The verse ends with a hand turned not in anger, and I will turn mine hand upon the little ones, the smallest of the flock kept while the rest run.
Two Parts Cut Off and the Third Refined (vv.8-9): Two parts therein shall be cut off and die, but the third shall be left therein. The arithmetic is brutal and it is stated without mitigation. Then what happens to the remainder, and I will bring the third part through the fire, and will refine them as silver is refined, and will try them as gold is tried. Survival is not the end of the process, it is entry into it. And the outcome is the covenant formula restored from both sides, they shall call on my name, and I will hear them, I will say, It is my people, and they shall say, The LORD is my God.

Deep Dive

Setting: This chapter continues the second "oracle" of Zechariah 9–14 (12:1), about the far horizon the prophet calls "that day." Chapter 12 ended with the people of Jerusalem, given "a spirit of grace and pleas for mercy," looking on the one they had pierced and mourning as for an only child. Chapter 13 follows straight on. Mourning leads to cleansing: a fountain is opened to wash away sin (v1). Cleansing leads to purity: idols and false prophets are removed from the land so thoroughly that a false prophet's own parents will not protect him (vv2–6). Then, abruptly, a short poem returns to the shepherd theme of chapter 11. God himself calls for a sword against "my shepherd," the man who stands beside him, and the flock is scattered, refined by fire and brought back into covenant (vv7–9). Jesus quoted v7 on the night of his arrest (Matt 26:31). Like the rest of chapters 9–14, the chapter is undated, written to the small post-exile community in Judah, probably in the early 400s BC. The ESV places v1 at the end of the section begun in 12:10.

Him Whom They Have Pierced (v1)

  • "On that day there shall be a fountain opened" (v1). The mourning of 12:10–14 is not the end. God answers repentance with cleansing. A "fountain" (maqor) is a spring of flowing water, not a cistern that must be filled. Jeremiah called God "the fountain of living waters" whom Israel forsook for broken cisterns (Jer 2:13). Under the Law, cleansing from impurity needed "living," running water (Lev 14:5–6; Num 19:17). This fountain flows continually. Chapter 14 develops the picture with living waters flowing out from Jerusalem (14:8; Ezek 47:1–12).
  • "For the house of David and the inhabitants of Jerusalem" (v1). The same people who looked on the pierced one and mourned (12:10) are the ones cleansed. The leaders and the city, who carried the guilt, receive the grace.
  • "To cleanse them from sin and uncleanness" (v1). "Sin" (chatta't) comes from a root meaning to miss the mark. "Uncleanness" (niddah) is a strong word, used of a woman's monthly impurity (Lev 15:19) and of the "water for impurity" prepared from the ashes of the red heifer to cleanse those defiled by death (Num 19:9, 13). Ezekiel used it of Israel's idolatry (Ezek 36:17). The promise is Ezekiel's new covenant: "I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean from all your uncleannesses, and from all your idols I will cleanse you" (Ezek 36:25).
  • Commentators: Henry says the fountain "is the pierced side of Jesus Christ," linking it directly to 12:10, and stresses that it is open to all who come. JFB puts it memorably: the fountain has always existed, and "It is not the fountain, but their eyes that need to be opened." He also notes that the Hebrew for sin is "literally a missing the mark or way." Guzik, following Spurgeon, stresses that a fountain keeps flowing, so its cleansing is never used up, and that only God's fountain cleanses, not human efforts at reform.

Idolatry Cut Off (vv2–6)

  • "I will cut off the names of the idols from the land, so that they shall be remembered no more" (v2). In the ancient world a name stood for a being's existence and power. To erase the names was to end the idols' hold completely. Hosea promised, "I will remove the names of the Baals from her mouth, and they shall be remembered by name no more" (Hos 2:17). After the exile the Jews as a people largely left behind the open idolatry that had ruined their ancestors, but the prophecy looks to a total purging.
  • "I will remove from the land the prophets and the spirit of uncleanness" (v2). "Prophets" here means false prophets, as the following verses make clear. They are paired with idols and with "the spirit of uncleanness," the opposite of the "spirit of grace" in 12:10. The whole system of false religion, its gods, its spokesmen and the spirit behind it, will be removed.
  • "His father and mother who bore him will say to him, 'You shall not live, for you speak lies in the name of the LORD'" (v3). The Law said a false prophet must die, and that even a close family member who led others to other gods was not to be spared (Deut 13:1–11; 18:20). In Israel's history, the true prophets were the ones persecuted and false prophets were welcomed (Jer 26:7–11; Matt 23:31). In this purified people, loyalty to God will outweigh even a parent's love. "Father and mother who bore him" is repeated to stress how close the bond is.
  • "Shall pierce him through when he prophesies" (v3). The verb is daqar, the same word used for the one "whom they have pierced" in 12:10. The contrast is stark. Once the people pierced the true one sent by God. Now they will pierce only the false.
  • "Every prophet will be ashamed of his vision" (v4). False prophets will be embarrassed to admit what they once claimed. "He will not put on a hairy cloak in order to deceive." The rough cloak of goat or camel hair was the mark of a prophet. Elijah wore one (2 Kgs 1:8), and John the Baptist wore camel's hair (Matt 3:4). Jesus warned of false prophets who come "in sheep's clothing" (Matt 7:15). The cloak was a costume, and the deceivers will be glad to hang it up.
  • "I am no prophet, I am a worker of the soil, for a man sold me in my youth" (v5). The man denies any prophetic calling and says he has been a farmhand since boyhood. The words seem to echo Amos, a true prophet, who said, "I was no prophet… but I was a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore figs" (Amos 7:14). Amos said it to defend his calling. This man says it to escape his. "A man sold me in my youth" is uncertain Hebrew. The ESV takes it as being bought as a laborer. Others read "the land has been my possession since my youth."
  • "What are these wounds on your back?" (v6). The Hebrew is literally "wounds between your hands," meaning on the chest or the back (ESV footnote). False prophets in the ancient world sometimes cut themselves in frenzied worship, as the prophets of Baal did on Carmel, "after their custom, with swords and lances, until the blood gushed out" (1 Kgs 18:28). Such cutting was forbidden in Israel (Lev 19:28; Deut 14:1). The scars would give the man away.
  • "The wounds I received in the house of my friends" (v6). His excuse: "I got them at a friend's place." The word for "friends" (me'ahavim, "lovers") is the word the prophets used for the false gods and foreign allies Israel chased (Hos 2:5–13; Jer 22:20). The irony is that his answer may be truer than he means. He was wounded in the house of his "lovers," the idols.
  • Does v6 speak of Christ? Many Christians have read "What are these wounds in your hands? Those with which I was wounded in the house of my friends" (KJV) as words of Jesus about the nail prints. It is a moving thought, and it appears in hymns and sermons. But in context the speaker is a false prophet trying to hide his past, and most commentators across traditions think the verse does not refer to Christ. The true shepherd's wounds come in the next verse.
  • Commentators: Henry sees the idol names cut off fulfilled partly in the Jews' aversion to idols after the exile, and in Gentile converts who "turned from idols" to Christ. On v6 he takes the primary sense as a repentant false prophet speaking of wounds received as discipline, then reports that "some good interpreters" apply it to Christ, and develops that reading too. JFB also reads v6 literally of the false prophet and only typically of the Messiah. Guzik points out that the Hebrew says "between your hands," on the body, not in the hands as the KJV has it, and agrees with those who think the context rules out applying the verse to Jesus. The Tyndale notes connect the self-inflicted wounds with 1 Kings 18:28 and the farmer's words with Amos 7:14.

The Shepherd Struck (vv7–9)

  • "Awake, O sword, against my shepherd" (v7). The poem bursts in suddenly. God speaks to a sword as if to a soldier and calls it to rise. The sword is often God's instrument of judgment (Isa 34:5–6; Jer 47:6; Ezek 21:9–17). What is shocking is the target: not the worthless shepherd of 11:17, who deserved the sword on his arm and eye, but "my shepherd," the one God appointed.
  • "Against the man who stands next to me" (v7). The phrase is literally "a man, my associate." The word 'amit appears only here and in Leviticus, where it always means one's fellow or neighbor, a person on the same level (Lev 6:2; 19:11, 15; 25:14). Used by God, it describes a man who is God's equal and companion. "Man" (gever) stresses that he is truly human. Here the shepherd is both a man and God's peer. Many interpreters, Jewish and Christian, have found this phrase hard to explain of any ordinary king. Christians see in it the God-man.
  • "Strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered" (v7). God himself gives the order. On the night of his arrest, on the way to the Mount of Olives, Jesus said to the disciples, "You will all fall away because of me this night. For it is written, 'I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered'" (Matt 26:31; Mark 14:27). Hours later, "all the disciples left him and fled" (Matt 26:56). Isaiah said the same: "it was the will of the LORD to crush him" (Isa 53:10). The cross was not an accident or only the work of evil men. It was God's own purpose.
  • "I will turn my hand against the little ones" (v7). "Little ones" are the weak and young of the flock, the humble. To "turn the hand against" someone usually means judgment (Isa 1:25). Some take it so: the flock shares in the suffering that follows the shepherd's death. Others read it in a protective sense: God turns his hand toward the little ones to gather them. The next verses suggest both. The flock will be refined through suffering, and a remnant will be kept.
  • "Two thirds shall be cut off and perish, and one third shall be left alive" (v8). The numbers recall Ezekiel 5:1–12, where the prophet's hair was divided into thirds for the fate of Jerusalem. Judgment is severe, and only a remnant survives. Many see this fulfilled in the Roman war of AD 66–70, which killed vast numbers in Judea. Others look to a final time of trouble before Christ's return (Jer 30:7; Dan 12:1). Both may be in view.
  • "I will put this third into the fire, and refine them as one refines silver" (v9). Even the survivors must go through fire, not to destroy them but to purify them. Refining heats metal until the impurities rise and can be skimmed away (Isa 1:25; 48:10; Mal 3:2–3). Peter applies the image to believers' trials: "so that the tested genuineness of your faith—more precious than gold that perishes though it is tested by fire—may be found to result in praise and glory and honor at the revelation of Jesus Christ" (1 Pet 1:7).
  • "They will call upon my name, and I will answer them. I will say, 'They are my people'; and they will say, 'The LORD is my God'" (v9). The broken covenant is restored. In 11:10–14 the shepherd broke his staffs Favor and Union. Hosea's children had been named Lo-ammi, "Not my people" (Hos 1:9), with the promise that one day God would say, "You are my people," and they would say, "You are my God" (Hos 2:23). After the sword and the fire, that day comes.
  • Commentators: Henry reads v7 as God calling his justice to strike the Son who offered himself for sin: "Awake, O sword! against my Shepherd." He sees the scattering in the disciples' flight, the two thirds in the Roman destruction of Jerusalem, and the refined third in the trials of the early church. He allows that "I will turn my hand upon the little ones" can mean either suffering or gathering. JFB renders "my fellow" as "the man of my union," God's equal and nearest kinsman, and reads the "little ones" as the humble Jewish believers whom God preserved. He first links the two thirds to the Roman wars, then argues that the passage looks further ahead. Guzik stresses that it is the Father who calls for the sword, so the cross was God's victory, not Satan's. He shows that the Hebrew for "fellow" is used in Leviticus of a close neighbor, which the lexicon confirms, and reads vv8–9 as the Jewish remnant of the final tribulation.

Pointing to Christ

In two verses the whole gospel stands side by side. First the shepherd is struck, and God himself calls for the sword. Jesus, "the man who stands next to me," the Son who is God's equal and yet truly a man, took the blow. He is the pierced one of 12:10, and his side, opened by the spear, released "blood and water" (John 19:34). From that wound the fountain for sin and uncleanness was opened: "the blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin" (1 John 1:7). The sheep scattered that night, but the risen shepherd gathered them again in Galilee (Matt 26:32; 28:7, 16), and he has been refining his people through fire ever since, so that they call on his name and he answers. The promise of v9 is the last word of the Bible's story: "They will be his people, and God himself will be with them as their God" (Rev 21:3).

Reflection

  • A Fountain Freely Opened (v.1) — A fountain for sin and uncleanness is opened for the people, not something they had to earn access to. Do you approach God as though you must clean yourself up first, or do you come freely to the cleansing He's already provided?
  • The Shepherd Struck as God's Equal (v.7) — God commands the sword against the shepherd described as His own associate and equal, then the sheep scatter. Does it change how you view the cross to see it as something the Father commanded rather than merely allowed?
  • Refined Through the Fire (v.9) — The surviving third are brought through fire and refined like silver and gold, not as punishment but as purification. If you're in a difficult season, can you view it as God refining you rather than abandoning you?
  • A Personal, Not Abstract, Faith (v.9) — The refined remnant doesn't just acknowledge God exists but personally declares, "The Lord is my God." Has your own faith become genuinely personal, or does it still feel more like general belief about God than a relationship with Him?
