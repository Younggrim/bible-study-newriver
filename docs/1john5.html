Summary

John explains that everyone who believes Jesus is the Christ is born of God, and that loving God means keeping his commandments, which are not burdensome because faith itself is the victory that overcomes the world (vv. 1-5). He points to three witnesses to Christ — the Spirit, the water, and the blood — testifying together that God has given eternal life in his Son, a testimony greater than any human witness (vv. 6-11). John states his purpose directly: he writes so that believers may know they have eternal life (v. 13), and he assures them that God hears prayers asked according to his will, including prayer for a brother caught in sin that does not lead to death (vv. 14-17). He closes with three certainties — that those born of God do not live in habitual sin, that believers belong to God while the world lies under the power of the evil one, and that the Son of God has come and given true understanding of God — ending with a final warning to keep away from idols (vv. 18-21).

Authorship & Background

Author: The Apostle John (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Historical Context: Chapter 5 concludes 1 John with the themes of faith that overcomes (vv.1-5), the witness to Christ (vv.6-12), assurance of eternal life (v.13), confidence in prayer (vv.14-17), and three certainties (vv.18- 21). Verse 13 states the letter's purpose: "that ye may know that ye have eternal life." John wants believers to have assurance, not doubt.
Classification: Epistle — General
Key Themes: Faith named as the victory that overcomes the world rather than only the means to it, water and blood joined as one witness against a Christ reduced to water alone, three witnesses agreeing where human testimony would need only two, assurance stated as the letter's declared purpose rather than an incidental comfort, and a final warning to keep from idols placed at the very end without transition
Faith Overcomes the World (vv.1-5): Believing Jesus is the Christ = born of God (v.1). Loving God + keeping commandments = loving God's children (v.2). His commandments are not burdensome (v.3). "Whatsoever is born of God overcometh the world" — the victory is our faith (v.4).
The Witness to Christ (vv.6-12): Christ came by water (baptism) and blood (cross) — not water only (v.6). Three witnesses: Spirit, water, blood (v.8). God's testimony about His Son is greater than human testimony (v.9). "He that hath the Son hath life" (v.12).
Assurance and Prayer (vv.13-17): "That ye may know that ye have eternal life" (v.13). Confidence in prayer: ask according to His will and He hears (vv.14- 15). Praying for a brother who sins (vv.16-17).
Three Certainties (vv.18-21): (1) The born-again don't practice sin; the evil one doesn't touch them (v.18). (2) We are of God; the world lies in the evil one (v.19). (3) The Son of God has come and given us understanding (v.20). Final word: "Keep yourselves from idols" (v.21).

Deep Dive

Setting: The apostle John, late in the first century, closing his letter to churches in Asia that had been unsettled by teachers who left them (2:19). Those teachers denied that the man Jesus is the Christ and Son of God come in the flesh, and they treated obedience and love as optional for the enlightened. Chapter 4 ended by joining love for God to love for one's brother. Chapter 5 ties together the letter's three threads, faith in Jesus, love for God's children and obedience, and shows that faith is the victory over the world. It sets out God's testimony to his Son and then states why John wrote: "that you may know that you have eternal life" (v13). The letter ends with prayer, three confident "we know" statements and a final warning about idols.

Overcoming the World (vv1–5)

  • "Everyone who believes that Jesus is the Christ has been born of God" (v1). "Has been born" is perfect tense. The new birth comes first, and faith is its first sign. This is the content of saving faith that the false teachers denied (2:22): Jesus, this man, is the Messiah.
  • "Everyone who loves the Father loves whoever has been born of him" (v1). It is a family principle. To love a father is to love his children. Love for God and love for fellow believers cannot be separated, as 4:20–21 has just said.
  • "When we love God and obey his commandments" (v2). John turns the test around. In 4:20 love for the brother proved love for God. Here love for God and obedience prove that our love for his children is real, not just sentiment. Each test checks the other.
  • "His commandments are not burdensome" (v3). "Burdensome" is barys, "heavy." Jesus accused the Pharisees of tying on "heavy burdens" (Matt 23:4), and he said his own yoke is easy and his burden light (Matt 11:30). God's commands are not light because they ask little. They are light because the new birth gives a new desire to keep them.
  • "This is the victory that has overcome the world—our faith" (v4). "Victory" is nikē, which appears only here in the New Testament. It was the name of the Greek goddess of victory, whose image was common across Asia. "Overcome" is nikaō, a favorite word of John's (2:13–14; 4:4; Rev 2–3). The verb "has overcome" is aorist, pointing back to a decisive victory, Christ's own: "Take heart; I have overcome the world" (John 16:33). Faith shares in a victory already won.
  • "Who is it that overcomes the world except the one who believes that Jesus is the Son of God?" (v5). The world is not overcome by willpower or special knowledge but by trusting in Jesus as the Son of God.
  • Commentators: Henry calls faith "the cause of victory, the means, the instrument, the spiritual armour and artillery by which we overcome." Guzik stresses that overcoming faith is an ongoing reliance, not a one-time decision. He gives three reasons the commands are not burdensome: they are wise, they are written on a new heart (Jer 31:33), and they are far lighter than the Pharisees' rules. He also presses v1 hard: love for God's children has to cross the lines Christians draw between churches, races and politics.

Testimony Concerning the Son of God (vv6–12)

  • "This is he who came by water and blood" (v6). One of the hardest phrases in the letter. There are three main views. (1) Most interpreters take "water" as Jesus' baptism and "blood" as his death. At the Jordan the Father declared him Son and the Spirit came on him (Mark 1:10–11). At the cross he shed his blood. (2) Some see the water and blood that flowed from his side at the cross, which John himself witnessed (John 19:34–35). (3) Others, from the early church onward, have seen a reference to baptism and the Lord's Supper. The first view fits best, because John is answering teachers who said a heavenly "Christ" came upon Jesus at his baptism and left him before his death.
  • "Not by the water only but by the water and the blood" (v6). That explains John's emphasis. Cerinthus, a teacher placed in Ephesus in John's day by early writers such as Irenaeus, said the divine Christ joined Jesus at the baptism but departed before the crucifixion, so only a man died. John insists that the same Jesus Christ came through both. The Son of God himself died.
  • "The Spirit is the one who testifies, because the Spirit is the truth" (v6). Jesus promised the Spirit would bear witness about him (John 15:26). The Spirit was present at the baptism and is the inner witness in believers now.
  • "There are three that testify: the Spirit and the water and the blood" (vv7–8). The Law required two or three witnesses to establish a matter (Deut 19:15). John offers three, and "these three agree." Literally, "the three are into the one," they all point to one conclusion.
  • The Comma Johanneum. The King James Version has extra words here: "in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one. And there are three that bear witness in earth." This is called the "Johannine Comma" ("comma" meaning a short clause). It is not in any early Greek manuscript, and no Greek church father cites it, even during the great fourth-century debates about the Trinity, when it would have been the perfect proof text. It first appears in Latin writers around the fourth and fifth centuries, probably as an explanatory note that later slipped into the text, and then in later copies of the Latin Vulgate. Erasmus left it out of the first two editions of his Greek New Testament (1516, 1519), then added it in his third (1522) after a Greek manuscript containing it was produced, a copy probably made in the early sixteenth century. From there it entered the Textus Receptus and the KJV. Virtually all modern translations leave it out. Nothing about the doctrine of the Trinity depends on it. That teaching rests on the whole New Testament (Matt 28:19; 2 Cor 13:14; John 1:1; 14–16).
  • "The testimony of God is greater" (v9). People accept human testimony every day, in courts and in ordinary life. How much more should they accept God's word about his Son?
  • "Whoever believes in the Son of God has the testimony in himself" (v10). Outward witness becomes inward conviction, the work of the Spirit (Rom 8:16). The person who refuses "has made him a liar," because unbelief calls God's testimony false. Compare 1:10.
  • "God gave us eternal life, and this life is in his Son" (v11). This is the testimony in one sentence. Eternal life is not a separate gift that could be had apart from Jesus. It is in him.
  • "Whoever has the Son has life" (v12). The point is as plain as possible. There is no third category. "Has" means a personal possession, a relationship, not just knowing about him.
  • Commentators: The three part ways most sharply over the Johannine Comma. JFB rejects it: "All the old versions omit the words," "the oldest manuscripts of the Vulgate omit them," and he treats it as a marginal comment that crept into the text. Guzik agrees and warns against defending human additions to Scripture, since the Trinity stands firmly on other texts. Henry, writing in 1710 before the manuscript evidence was well known, admits "the contest there is about the genuineness of v. 7" but argues for keeping it. That was a common view in his day and is almost universally abandoned now. On "water and blood," Guzik follows the early writer Tertullian (baptism and cross) and sets aside both the sacramental reading and the reading of the water and blood from Jesus' side. JFB likewise speaks of Jesus undergoing "the double baptism of water and blood." Henry reads the water more spiritually: "By the water our souls are washed and purified for heaven."

That You May Know (vv13–21)

  • "That you may know that you have eternal life" (v13). This is the letter's purpose statement. John's Gospel was written "so that you may believe" (John 20:31). This letter is written so that believers may know. After the false teachers' attacks, John wants those who stayed to have settled assurance. The tests of the letter (obedience, love, right belief about Jesus) are not meant to make sincere believers anxious. They are meant to steady them.
  • "If we ask anything according to his will he hears us" (v14). "Confidence" is parrēsia one last time (2:28; 3:21; 4:17), now in prayer. "According to his will" is not a loophole. It is how children pray, as Jesus did in Gethsemane: "not my will, but yours, be done" (Luke 22:42).
  • "We know that we have the requests" (v15). To be heard by God is, in John's mind, to have the answer already granted, even if it has not yet appeared.
  • Praying for a sinning brother (v16). Prayer is not only for ourselves. A believer who sees a brother sinning should pray, "and God will give him life." The ESV footnote notes that the Greek says "he will give," which could mean God or the one praying, who becomes the means of life (compare James 5:20).
  • "There is sin that leads to death" (v16). This is much debated. The main views: (1) In the context of this letter, it means the deliberate and final rejection of Jesus by those who had left the church: apostasy, denying the Son (2:19, 22–23). This is the view of the Tyndale notes and many modern commentators. (2) Some link it to the blasphemy against the Spirit (Matt 12:31–32) or the falling away of Hebrews 6:4–6. (3) Others think of sin that God judges with physical death, as with Ananias and Sapphira (Acts 5) or at Corinth (1 Cor 11:30). (4) Roman Catholic teaching has used this verse in distinguishing mortal and venial sins. John does not forbid prayer for such a person. He simply says, "I do not say that one should pray for that." He leaves it with God.
  • "All wrongdoing is sin" (v17). No sin is small. But not every sin is final.
  • "Everyone who has been born of God does not keep on sinning" (v18). This repeats 3:6 and 3:9: not sinless perfection, but no settled practice of sin.
  • "He who was born of God protects him" (v18). The first "born of God" is perfect tense (the believer). The second is aorist, and most take it to mean Jesus, the Son born of God, who keeps his people (John 17:12). A few manuscripts read "keeps himself," which would mean the believer guards himself. The ESV and most modern translations follow the reading that points to Christ. "The evil one does not touch him" means he cannot grasp or seize him to harm him (compare John 10:28).
  • "The whole world lies in the power of the evil one" (v19). Literally, "lies in the evil one," like a body lying in the grip of something. Believers are "from God." Everything else lies in the hand of the enemy (compare John 12:31; 2 Cor 4:4).
  • "The Son of God has come and has given us understanding" (v20). Knowledge of God is not a secret discovered by an elite. It is given by the Son who came. "Him who is true" means the true God, as opposed to every false one.
  • "He is the true God and eternal life" (v20). Who is "he"? Grammatically it could be the Father, "him who is true." But the nearest name is "his Son Jesus Christ," and John has just called Jesus "the eternal life" (1:2). Many interpreters, from the early church onward, read this as one of the clearest statements in the New Testament that Jesus is God (compare John 1:1; 20:28). Others take it as referring to the Father. Both readings fit John's theology, since John knows the Son and the Father are one (John 10:30).
  • "Little children, keep yourselves from idols" (v21). This is an abrupt ending, with no greeting or blessing. "Idols" (eidōla) can mean images of false gods, and Ephesus was full of them, with the great temple of Artemis (Acts 19). But in a letter that has just named Jesus "the true God," an idol is any false god, including a false Jesus, a Christ without flesh or a God without his Son. "Keep" means to guard. Having named the true God, John's final word warns against anything that takes his place.
  • Commentators: On the sin leading to death, Guzik takes the view that it means a believer's physical death under God's discipline (1 Cor 11:30), which differs from the Tyndale notes' apostasy reading. Henry adds a sobering reminder: "All sin, as to the merit and legal sentence of it, is unto death." On v18, Guzik prefers the reading in which Christ keeps the believer, and JFB cites the Vulgate's "the having been begotten of God keepeth HIM." Guzik reads v20 as a strong statement that Jesus is God, citing Stott's use of it against Arius. On v21 JFB notes that "Christians were then everywhere surrounded by idolaters," and Henry writes, "Your God is jealous." Guzik widens idols to quieter rivals such as career, appetite and money.

Pointing to Christ

The whole letter comes to rest on one sentence: "Whoever has the Son has life" (v12). Jesus is the one who came by water and blood, the Son of God who truly died. He is the victor whose victory faith shares. He is the one born of God who keeps his people so that the evil one cannot touch them. He is, John says, "the true God and eternal life." The letter opened with hands that touched the Word of life. It closes with the assurance that the same Jesus has given understanding, so that his people may know him and know that they have eternal life in him.

Reflection

  • Faith as the Victory Over the World (v.4) — John says the victory that overcomes the world is nothing other than our faith. Is your faith actually overcoming the world's pressures and values right now, or is the world winning?
  • Having the Son Means Having Life (v.12) — John states plainly that whoever has the Son has life, and whoever doesn't, doesn't. Are you living in the present reality of that life, or treating it as something still out of reach?
  • Writing So You May Know (v.13) — John says he wrote this letter so believers could know, not merely hope, that they have eternal life. What actually gives you that certainty?
  • Confidence in Prayer According to His Will (v.14) — John promises that God hears prayers offered according to his will. How do you work at discerning God's will so you can pray with real confidence instead of guessing?
  • Guarding Against Idols (v.21) — John's closing word is a warning to keep yourselves from idols. What competes for the place God alone should hold in your life — money, comfort, approval, control?
