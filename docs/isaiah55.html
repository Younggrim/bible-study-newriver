Summary

Isaiah issues an open invitation to everyone who is thirsty to come freely to the water, wine, and milk God offers, urging them to stop spending their resources on what doesn't satisfy and instead listen so their souls can truly live (vv. 1-3). He calls the people to seek the LORD while he can be found and to turn from their sinful ways, promising abundant forgiveness (vv. 6-7). God reminds them that his thoughts and ways are far higher than theirs, and that just as rain waters the earth and makes it fruitful, his word will not return to him empty but will accomplish exactly what he sends it to do (vv. 8-11). The chapter closes with a picture of creation itself celebrating Israel's release, with mountains and hills bursting into song and thorn bushes replaced by flourishing trees, becoming a lasting sign of God's covenant (vv. 12-13).

Authorship & Background

Author: Isaiah son of Amoz (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Salvation Oracle — The Great Invitation
Key Themes: Free salvation for the thirsty, the everlasting Davidic covenant, God's word always accomplishes its purpose, God's thoughts higher than ours, the joy of exodus/creation
Historical Context: Chapter 55 concludes the Servant section (chs.49-55) with a universal invitation. After the Servant's death and resurrection (ch.53) and the resulting blessing (ch.54), now the invitation goes out to everyone. The tone is marketplace urgency: "Ho!" (the vendor's cry). Everything is free — no money required. This is the gospel in OT form: come, receive, live. The Davidic covenant (v.3) connects the Servant's work to David's eternal dynasty promise (2 Samuel 7). Paul quotes v.3 in Acts 13:34 as fulfilled in Christ's resurrection.
Come, Buy Without Money (vv.1-2): Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters, and he that hath no money, come ye, buy, and eat, yea, come, buy wine and milk without money and without price. The invitation is a market cry and the terms contradict the setting, which is the point. Then the question that gives it its edge, and it is about waste rather than about sin, wherefore do ye spend money for that which is not bread, and your labour for that which satisfieth not. And the promise, hearken diligently unto me, and let your soul delight itself in fatness.
The Sure Mercies of David (vv.3-5): Incline your ear, and come unto me, hear, and your soul shall live, and I will make an everlasting covenant with you, the sure mercies of David. What is remarkable is who the covenant is offered to: the promise made to one king is being extended to a whole discouraged community. Paul quotes the phrase in Acts 13:34. Then the role is transferred with it, behold, thou shalt call a nation that thou knowest not, and nations that knew not thee shall run unto thee, so what David was to the tribes, this people will be to the nations.
Seek Ye the LORD While He May Be Found (vv.6-7): Seek ye the LORD while he may be found, call ye upon him while he is near. The clause while he may be found implies a window, which is the one note of urgency in an otherwise open-handed chapter. Then what turning consists of, stated as two departures and two arrivals, let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts, and let him return unto the LORD. And the ground of it is capacity rather than leniency, for he will abundantly pardon.
My Thoughts Are Not Your Thoughts (vv.8-9): For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the LORD. The verses are usually quoted to explain a disappointment, and in context they are doing the opposite: they follow immediately on he will abundantly pardon, so what is being said to be beyond human reckoning is the scale of the forgiveness. And the measure given is vertical, for as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways.
As the Rain Cometh Down (vv.10-11): For as the rain cometh down, and the snow from heaven, and returneth not thither, but watereth the earth, and maketh it bring forth and bud. The comparison is with weather that does not go back up unused. So shall my word be that goeth forth out of my mouth, it shall not return unto me void, but it shall accomplish that which I please. The claim is about reliability of effect rather than of prediction, and it is the answer to forty chapters of preaching that appeared to achieve nothing.
Ye Shall Go Out with Joy (vv.12-13): For ye shall go out with joy, and be led forth with peace, the mountains and the hills shall break forth before you into singing, and all the trees of the field shall clap their hands. Then the change described botanically, instead of the thorn shall come up the fir tree, and instead of the brier shall come up the myrtle tree. The thorns and briers are the standing image of ruin from 5:6 and 7:23, and what replaces them here is not merely useful but ornamental. And the last clause is about permanence, and it shall be to the LORD for a name, for an everlasting sign that shall not be cut off.

Setting: Isaiah 40–55 speaks to Judeans who would be exiles in Babylon in the 500s BC. These chapters began with "Comfort, comfort my people" (40:1) and a promise that "the word of our God will stand forever" (40:8). Chapter 55 closes them, and it circles back to both. The Servant has died for the sins of many (ch 53). Zion has been promised children, a husband and a jeweled city (ch 54). Now comes the open invitation: come and eat, for free. Some exiles had settled comfortably in Babylon and were spending their lives there. God calls them to something better, then promises that his word will not fail and that they will go out with the hills singing. The Tyndale notes observe that the chapter gathers up the main themes of chapters 40–55: covenant, witness, the nations, forgiveness, God's word and joy. The whole chapter is one ESV section.

The Compassion of the LORD (vv1–13)

  • "Come, everyone who thirsts, come to the waters" (v1). The verse opens with "Ho!" in Hebrew, the shout of a street seller or a herald calling for attention. The word "come" appears three times in one verse. Wisdom calls the same way in Proverbs: "Come, eat of my bread and drink of the wine I have mixed" (Prov 9:5). The only qualification is thirst. Henry: "Where God gives grace he first gives a thirsting after it."
  • "He who has no money, come, buy and eat" (v1). This is a deliberate paradox. You cannot buy without money. "Buy" (shabar) is the word for buying grain, the word used of Joseph's brothers going down to Egypt for food in the famine (Gen 42:2–3). The goods are real and must be taken, but the price has already been paid by someone else. Henry says Christ bought them "not with money, but with his own blood."
  • "Wine and milk without money and without price" (v1). The offer climbs: water to keep you alive, then wine for joy (Ps 104:15) and milk for nourishment, the products of the promised land "flowing with milk" (Exod 3:8). God offers not bare survival but plenty.
  • Jesus and the thirsty. At the Feast of Booths, when water was poured out at the temple each day, Jesus stood up and cried, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37). He offered the Samaritan woman water that would become "a spring of water welling up to eternal life" (John 4:14). The Bible's last invitation echoes this verse: "let the one who desires take the water of life without price" (Rev 22:17).
  • "Why do you spend your money for that which is not bread?" (v2). In Babylon, a thriving market city, some exiles were doing well. Many chose to stay when the way home opened. The question cuts deep in any age: money and effort poured into things that do not feed the soul. Henry: "They flatter, but they do not fill." Jesus said, "Do not labor for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures to eternal life" (John 6:27).
  • "Listen diligently to me, and eat what is good" (v2). "Listen diligently" doubles the verb in Hebrew, "listen, listening." Eating and listening are the same act here. God's food is his word. Moses said, "man does not live by bread alone, but… by every word that comes from the mouth of the LORD" (Deut 8:3). "Delight yourselves in rich food" is literally "in fatness," the richest part, which in Israel belonged to God at the altar (Lev 3:16). Now he gives it to his guests.
  • "Incline your ear, and come to me; hear, that your soul may live" (v3). Three calls: incline, come, hear. "Soul" (nephesh) means the whole person, the living self. Life comes by listening to God.
  • "I will make with you an everlasting covenant, my steadfast, sure love for David" (v3). God promised David that his house and kingdom would last forever (2 Sam 7:12–16; Ps 89:28–37). "Steadfast love" is chesed, plural here: "David's acts of loyal love," made "sure" or "faithful" (neʾemanim). By the exile, David's line seemed finished. The last king was in a Babylonian prison (2 Kings 25:27–30). God says the promise stands, and now it is offered to "you," plural, the whole people. What was given to one king is extended to everyone who comes.
  • Paul and the sure mercies (v3). Preaching in the synagogue at Pisidian Antioch, Paul quotes this verse in its Greek form, "I will give you the holy and sure blessings of David," as proof that God raised Jesus from the dead, "no more to return to corruption" (Acts 13:34). A promise of a forever king needs a king who lives forever. JFB reads the covenant as made with "the antitypical David, Messiah."
  • "Behold, I made him a witness to the peoples, a leader and commander for the peoples" (v4). David conquered and ruled the nations around Israel (2 Sam 8; Ps 18:43, "you made me the head of the nations"). But "witness" is a surprising word for a warrior king. David's psalms bore witness to God among the nations (Ps 18:49; 57:9). The greater David is called "the faithful witness, the firstborn of the dead, and the ruler of kings on earth" (Rev 1:5). Jesus told Pilate he came "to bear witness to the truth" (John 18:37).
  • "You shall call a nation that you do not know" (v5). The "you" is singular. It may address the coming David, or Israel as a whole. Either way the movement is outward: nations that never knew Israel will "run" to her, "because of the LORD your God." The Servant was to be "a light for the nations" (49:6). Here the nations come running. Chapter 60 will develop the picture.
  • "Seek the LORD while he may be found; call upon him while he is near" (v6). The invitation has a time limit. God is near now, offering himself. There will be a time when the door is shut (Matt 25:10–12). Paul: "now is the favorable time; behold, now is the day of salvation" (2 Cor 6:2). Jeremiah wrote to the exiles: "You will seek me and find me, when you seek me with all your heart" (Jer 29:13).
  • "Let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts" (v7). Repentance is a turn of the whole person, outside and inside. JFB notes the order: the outward "way" first, then the inward "thoughts," where sin starts. Guzik stresses the same, linking it to taking every thought captive to Christ (2 Cor 10:5) and being transformed by the renewing of the mind (Rom 12:2).
  • "Let him return to the LORD… for he will abundantly pardon" (v7). "Return" (shuv) is the great prophetic word for repentance, a U-turn. "Abundantly pardon" is literally "he will multiply to forgive." The verb for forgive (salach) is used in the Old Testament only with God as the subject. People pardon one another with other words. This word belongs to God alone.
  • "My thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways" (v8). This verse is often quoted to mean that God is beyond understanding, which is true. But in context the first point is about forgiveness. "For" ties it to v7. Humans forgive grudgingly, in measured doses. God's mercy is not like ours. JFB: God's pardon is not measured by human standards, so "you need not doubt His willingness."
  • "As the heavens are higher than the earth" (v9). The same picture is used of God's love in Psalm 103:11: "as high as the heavens are above the earth, so great is his steadfast love toward those who fear him." The gap between God's thinking and ours is the gap between sky and ground. Guzik notes that being made in God's image means we can grasp something of his thoughts, but we can never master them.
  • "As the rain and the snow come down from heaven and do not return there but water the earth" (v10). In a dry land like Israel, rain was life itself. Rain does not fall and then go straight back up. It soaks in and does its work, and the result is "seed to the sower and bread to the eater," both next year's crop and today's meal.
  • "So shall my word be that goes out from my mouth; it shall not return to me empty" (v11). "Empty" (reqam) means empty-handed, as a messenger might come back without results. God's word is sent like a messenger on a mission, and it always completes the errand. These chapters opened with "the word of our God will stand forever" (40:8). They close with the word that never fails. Paul relies on this when he says the gospel "is the power of God for salvation" (Rom 1:16). Jesus' parable of the sower uses the same picture of seed and soil (Mark 4:1–20).
  • Not every hearer, but every purpose. The verse does not promise that every hearer will believe. Isaiah himself was told his preaching would harden many (6:9–10). It promises that the word "shall accomplish that which I purpose." JFB adds that even where the word seems to fail, it leaves hearers "without excuse." Guzik warns that weak or diluted preaching can blunt what God's word was sent to do, though the word itself does not fail.
  • "For you shall go out in joy and be led forth in peace" (v12). The new exodus again. They left Egypt in a hurry with Pharaoh's army behind them. They will leave Babylon in joy and peace (52:12). "The mountains and the hills… shall break forth into singing, and all the trees of the field shall clap their hands." Creation joins the celebration (44:23; Ps 98:8). Paul says creation itself "waits with eager longing for the revealing of the sons of God," to be set free from its bondage (Rom 8:19–21).
  • "Instead of the thorn shall come up the cypress; instead of the brier shall come up the myrtle" (v13). Thorns and thistles were the curse on the ground after Adam's sin (Gen 3:17–18). Isaiah used briers and thorns as pictures of judgment (5:6; 7:23–25). Now they are replaced by evergreens. The cypress was a tall timber tree. The myrtle (hadas) is a fragrant evergreen shrub. Esther's Hebrew name, Hadassah, comes from it (Esth 2:7), and its branches were used to build booths at the Feast of Booths (Neh 8:15). The curse is reversed. Jesus wore a crown of thorns (John 19:2), taking the curse on himself so that it could be lifted.
  • "It shall make a name for the LORD, an everlasting sign that shall not be cut off" (v13). The renewed land will be a monument to God's reputation. The Servant was "cut off out of the land of the living" (53:8). This sign will never be cut off. Chapter 54 ended with a promise "from me." Chapter 55 ends with a sign that makes "a name for the LORD."
  • Commentators: Guzik and Henry both read v1 as the gospel offer, and Guzik quotes Spurgeon's wonder that God would become like a beggar calling to his own creatures. Guzik adds that growth in grace is as much a free gift as salvation itself, though it still takes listening and discernment. Henry says of what the world offers, "They satisfy not; they yield not any solid comfort and content to the soul." JFB and Henry both read the covenant of v3 and the witness of v4 as fulfilled in Christ, with Acts 13:34 as the key. Guzik sees a further fulfilment in a future reign. On v7 JFB notes the Hebrew sense of multiplied pardon, which the wording supports ("he will multiply to pardon"). JFB reads the myrtle of v13 as a "type of the Christian Church" and the thorn as the wicked. Guzik takes it more simply as the curse reversed. On vv8–9, JFB keeps the link to forgiveness that the "for" demands, which is easy to miss when the verses are quoted alone.

Pointing to Christ

The free banquet of Isaiah 55 is paid for by the Servant of Isaiah 53. That is why it can be "without money and without price." Jesus stood in the temple and repeated its call to the thirsty. Paul preached its "sure mercies of David" as the promise of a risen King who will never die again. The word that does not return empty is the gospel, and Jesus is himself the Word made flesh, sent from heaven to do his Father's will and not to return without finishing it (John 1:14; 6:38; 17:4). The thorn crown he wore is the curse that verse 13 promises will be replaced by cypress and myrtle.

Reflection

  • Come, Thirsty, Without Price (vv.1-2) — Everyone who thirsts is invited to come and receive water, wine, and milk without money, while God asks why they spend their wages on what doesn't satisfy. What are you currently investing your time and energy in that leaves you just as empty afterward?
  • An Everlasting Covenant (v.3) — God offers an everlasting covenant tied to His steadfast love for David, promising that those who listen and come to Him will truly live. Do you come to God's word expecting it to bring life, or treat it as background noise?
  • Seek Him While He May Be Found (v.6) — The invitation carries urgency: seek the Lord while He may be found and call on Him while He is near, turning from wicked thoughts and ways. Is there something you're delaying dealing with, assuming the opportunity to turn back will always be there?
  • Thoughts and Ways Higher Than Ours (v.8) — God says His thoughts and ways are as far above human thoughts and ways as the heavens are above the earth. When His plan doesn't make sense to you, do you trust the gap is His wisdom, or assume something's wrong?
  • A Word That Accomplishes Its Purpose (v.11) — Just as rain and snow water the earth before returning to the sky, God's word will not return to Him empty but will accomplish exactly what He sends it to do. Do you believe the word you've read, prayed, or spoken is still working even when you can't see the results yet?
  • Creation Rejoices, an Everlasting Sign (v.13) — Thorns will be replaced by cypress and briers by myrtle, standing as a lasting sign of what God has done. Does the transformation God has worked in your own life serve as a visible sign to the people watching you?
