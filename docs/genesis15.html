Summary

The Lord comes to Abram in a vision, telling him not to fear because He is his shield and reward, and Abram voices his concern that he remains childless with a servant as his heir (vv. 1-3). The Lord promises that Abram's own son will be his heir and that his offspring will be as numerous as the stars, and Abram believes the Lord, who credits it to him as righteousness (vv. 4-6). God reaffirms the promise of the land and, at Abram's request for confirmation, instructs him to prepare a covenant ceremony involving several divided animals (vv. 7-11). As a deep sleep and terror fall on Abram, God foretells four hundred years of slavery and affliction for his descendants in a foreign land, followed by judgment on that nation and a return to Canaan (vv. 12-16). A smoking pot and flaming torch pass between the pieces, symbolizing God's self-binding oath, and the Lord formally grants Abram's descendants the land from the river of Egypt to the Euphrates (vv. 17-21).

Authorship & Background

Author: Moses (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 15 is one of the most theologically significant chapters in the OT. It contains: (1) God's self-revelation as Abram's shield and reward (v.1), (2) the promise of a biological heir (v.4), (3) Abram's faith counted as righteousness (v.6) — the foundational text for justification by faith, and (4) the covenant ceremony where God alone passes between the pieces (vv.9-21) — making the covenant unconditional. This chapter is quoted extensively by Paul in Romans 4 and Galatians 3.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: God identifying himself to a still-childless man as both shield and exceeding great reward, a promise of countless offspring illustrated by stars rather than by any nearer, more countable thing, a single verse, he believed in the LORD, and he counted it to him for righteousness, that becomes the foundational text for justification by faith throughout the New Testament, a covenant ceremony in which a smoking furnace and a burning lamp alone pass between the divided pieces while Abram sleeps, and four hundred years of coming slavery and deliverance revealed to him well before Israel exists to experience either
God's Promise and Abram's Faith (vv.1-6): "Fear not, Abram: I am thy shield, and thy exceeding great reward" (v.1). Abram's concern: he's still childless (vv.2-3). God promises a biological heir (v.4). "Look now toward heaven, and tell the stars... so shall thy seed be" (v.5). "And he believed in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness" (v.6) — The verse on justification by faith.
The Covenant Ceremony (vv.7-21): Abram asks: "Whereby shall I know?" (v.8). God commands a sacrifice: animals cut in half, pieces arranged (vv.9-10). A deep sleep and "horror of great darkness" falls on Abram (v.12). God reveals the future: 400 years of slavery, then deliverance (vv.13-14). A smoking furnace and burning lamp pass between the pieces (v.17) — GOD alone walks through, making this a unilateral, unconditional covenant. The land promise defined: from the river of Egypt to the Euphrates (v.18).

Map & Geography

  • "From the river of Egypt to the great river, the river Euphrates" (v.18) — the boundaries of the Promised Land defined. The "river of Egypt" is likely the Wadi el-Arish (not the Nile), marking the southwestern border.
  • The Euphrates marks the northeastern boundary — an enormous territory that Israel only briefly controlled under Solomon (1 Kings 4:21).
  • Ten nations listed (vv.19-21): Kenites, Kenizzites, Kadmonites, Hittites, Perizzites, Rephaim, Amorites, Canaanites, Girgashites, Jebusites — all occupying the land between these boundaries.

Setting: Some years have passed since Abram left Haran at seventy-five (12:4); by the next chapter he is eighty-five (16:3). He has walked the land God promised, gone down to Egypt and come back, parted from Lot, and in chapter 14 led 318 trained men on a night raid that defeated four eastern kings and rescued Lot. Then he met Melchizedek, gave him a tenth, and refused to take so much as "a thread or a sandal strap" from the king of Sodom (14:23). Chapter 15 opens "after these things." Abram has made enemies of powerful kings, turned down a fortune, and still has no son. Into that moment God speaks, first with a promise of offspring, then with a solemn covenant ceremony that guarantees the land. The chapter holds one of the most important sentences in the whole Bible: "he believed the LORD, and he counted it to him as righteousness" (v6).

God's Covenant with Abram (vv1–21)

  • The shape of the chapter. The ESV gives the whole chapter one heading, but it falls into two matching halves. In vv1–6 God makes a promise, Abram raises a question, and God answers with a sign (the stars) about offspring. In vv7–21 God makes a promise, Abram raises a question, and God answers with a sign (the covenant ceremony) about land. Both open with "I am" (vv1, 7), and in both Abram answers "O Lord GOD" (vv2, 8).
  • "The word of the LORD came to Abram in a vision" (v1). This phrase is the regular way the prophets describe God speaking to them (Jer 1:2; Hos 1:1), and it appears here for the first time.
  • "Fear not, Abram" (v1). This is the first "fear not" in the Bible, words later said to Isaac (26:24), Jacob (46:3) and the shepherds at Jesus' birth (Luke 2:10). Abram had reason to be afraid. Kings who had been beaten in a night raid could come back with a larger army, and he had no son to carry on his name.
  • "I am your shield" (v1). "Shield" is magen. In the previous chapter Melchizedek blessed "God Most High, who has delivered (miggen) your enemies into your hand" (14:20). The lexicon confirms that the two words are related: the verb is formed from the noun for shield. God who handed Abram's enemies over to him will keep standing between Abram and harm. The Psalms take up the picture again and again: "the LORD God is a sun and shield" (Ps 84:11).
  • "Your reward shall be very great" (v1). "Reward" is sakar, the ordinary word for wages or pay. Abram has just refused the king of Sodom's wages. God says, in effect, that he will not be the poorer for it. The Hebrew can be read two ways. The ESV takes it as a promise that his reward will be great. Older translations such as the KJV read "I am thy shield, and thy exceeding great reward," that is, God himself is the reward. Both readings are possible from the Hebrew, and both are true: God gives great gifts, and God is the greatest gift.
  • "O Lord GOD, what will you give me, for I continue childless?" (v2). Abram's first recorded words to God are a complaint. "O Lord GOD" is Adonai YHWH, "Master LORD," the first time this pairing appears in the Bible. It joins respect with honest frustration: what good is a great reward with no son to receive it?
  • "The heir of my house is Eliezer of Damascus" (v2). The Hebrew behind "heir" is unusual: ben-mesheq, literally "son of mesheq," which sounds like Dammeseq, Damascus. The Tyndale notes take mesheq as "possession," a pun on the city's name. The lexicon is more cautious. Mesheq appears only here, and its meaning ("acquisition, possession") is guessed from the context and the wordplay, so it calls the meaning "very dubious." The sense is clear enough: the man in line to inherit is a household servant from Damascus.
  • "A member of my household will be my heir" (v3). In the ancient Near East a childless man could adopt someone, even a trusted servant, to care for him in old age and inherit. Some scholars point to family documents from Nuzi in Mesopotamia as parallels, though how closely they match is debated. Abram is describing a sensible legal fallback, and God sets it aside.
  • "Your very own son shall be your heir" (v4). Literally "one who shall come out from your own body." This is the first time God says plainly that the promised offspring will be Abram's own child, not an adopted heir. Notice what God does not yet say: that Sarai will be the mother. That gap is what chapter 16 tries, wrongly, to fill. The promise naming Sarai comes in 17:16–19.
  • "Look toward heaven, and number the stars" (v5). God takes Abram outside, under a sky with no city lights. Earlier the picture was dust (13:16); now it is stars, and later both together (22:17). Paul quotes "So shall your offspring be" in Romans 4:18 as the word Abram held to "in hope… against hope."
  • "And he believed the LORD" (v6). "Believed" is a form of aman, the root of "amen." The basic idea of the root is firmness: something solid and reliable that can be leaned on. The lexicon lists the related form meaning "made firm, sure," and points to Isaiah's wordplay, "If you are not firm in faith, you will not be firm at all" (Isa 7:9). To believe God is to lean one's whole weight on his word: not merely that God exists, but that he will do what he said. This is the first time the verb "believe" appears in the Bible.
  • "He counted it to him as righteousness" (v6). "Counted" is chashab, to think, reckon, account or credit, the language of keeping books. The lexicon lists "impute" among its senses and cites this verse. "Righteousness" is tsedaqah, being right with God and in the right before him; this is its first appearance in the Bible. The verse does not say Abram earned righteousness by believing, as if faith were a good work. It says God credited righteousness to his account on the basis of faith. The same phrase is used of Phinehas (Ps 106:31). The Tyndale notes add that v6 reads as the narrator's comment on Abram's settled trust, not the moment he first came to faith; he had left Ur "by faith" years before (Heb 11:8).
  • How the New Testament uses v6. Paul quotes it (Rom 4:3; Gal 3:6) to show that people are made right with God by faith, not works, and stresses the timing: Abram was counted righteous years before circumcision (Rom 4:9–11) and centuries before Sinai. James quotes the same verse (Jas 2:23) after saying that Abraham's faith was "completed" when he offered Isaac (Jas 2:21–22; Genesis 22). The two are not in conflict. Paul is asking how a person is made right with God; James is asking what real faith looks like over time. Protestant traditions stress that righteousness is credited to the believer; Catholic and Orthodox traditions tend to stress that God's declaration also makes the believer truly righteous. All put God's grace and human faith at the center.
  • "I am the LORD who brought you out from Ur of the Chaldeans" (v7). The second half opens with God's self-introduction. It sounds like the opening of the Ten Commandments: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt" (Exod 20:2). The God who brought Abram out of Ur will bring his children out of Egypt.
  • "How am I to know that I shall possess it?" (v8). Moments after believing, Abram asks for confirmation. He is not refusing to believe, as Zechariah did (Luke 1:18–20). He believes the promise and asks for something to hold on to. God does not rebuke him. He answers with a covenant.
  • "Bring me a heifer three years old, a female goat… a ram… a turtledove, and a young pigeon" (v9). Every animal listed is one Israel would later be allowed to sacrifice (Lev 1; 5:7). Abram needed no instructions on what to do with them, which suggests the procedure was familiar.
  • "Cut them in half, and laid each half over against the other" (v10). Here is the background to the Hebrew phrase in v18. To make a covenant is karat berit, literally to "cut a covenant." In the ancient Near East a treaty was sometimes sealed by cutting animals in two; the parties walked between the halves as a self-curse, saying in effect, "May this happen to me if I break my word." An Aramaic treaty from Sefire (8th century BC) spells it out: "Just as this calf is cut up, so may Mati'el be cut up." Letters from Mari, centuries earlier, speak of "killing a donkey" to seal agreements between tribes. Jeremiah describes the same ceremony in Israel's own day and calls down its curse on those who broke their covenant: "the calf that they cut in two and passed between its parts" (Jer 34:18). The birds were left whole, as in later law (Lev 1:17).
  • "When birds of prey came down on the carcasses, Abram drove them away" (v11). "Birds of prey" is ayit, a word used elsewhere for the scavenging birds that pick over the dead (Isa 18:6; Ezek 39:4) and, as a figure, for foreign enemies (Jer 12:9). The Tyndale notes see the birds as a picture of the enemies who would attack Abram's children, as Egypt does in v13. Other readers see a picture of whatever tries to devour faith while it waits for God to act. The text does not explain the detail.
  • "A deep sleep fell on Abram… dreadful and great darkness" (v12). "Deep sleep" is tardemah, the same word used when God put Adam to sleep to make the woman (2:21). The lexicon notes that it usually refers to a sleep sent by God. Abram is put beyond doing anything while God acts.
  • "Your offspring will be sojourners in a land that is not theirs… afflicted for four hundred years" (v13). God gives Abram the long view, including the hard parts. The word "afflicted" is the one used for Egypt's treatment of Israel (Exod 1:11–12). Exodus 12:40 gives 430 years for Israel's stay in Egypt, and Stephen rounds to 400 (Acts 7:6). Paul's 430 years in Galatians 3:17 is counted from the promise to the law, and the Greek translation of Exodus 12:40 includes the patriarchs' years in Canaan in the total. The Tyndale notes take 400 as a round number. Either way the point is clear: suffering was coming, and it had a limit.
  • "I will bring judgment on the nation that they serve… they shall come out with great possessions" (v14). This is the Exodus in miniature: the plagues, and Israel leaving with silver, gold and clothing (Exod 12:35–36).
  • "You shall go to your fathers in peace; you shall be buried in a good old age" (v15). Abram himself will not see the slavery or the Exodus. He will die in peace, which happens in 25:8, where he dies "in a good old age, an old man and full of years." "Go to your fathers" hints at more than a grave, since his fathers were buried far away in Mesopotamia.
  • "They shall come back here in the fourth generation" (v16). "Generation" is dor. The lexicon gives its basic meaning as "period" or "age," a lifetime. With lifespans at this point of well over a hundred years, four such periods roughly equal the four hundred years of v13. It also fits Moses' family line in Exodus 6:16–20: Levi, Kohath, Amram, then Moses and Aaron, who led Israel out.
  • "For the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (v16). One of the most striking lines in the chapter. "Amorites" here stands for the peoples of Canaan as a whole (compare Josh 24:15). "Complete" is shalem, full or whole, the word used for a full and honest weight (Deut 25:15). God delays the gift of the land partly out of patience with its people. He will not drive them out until their sin has reached its full measure. Later passages describe that sin, including child sacrifice (Lev 18:21–28; Deut 9:4–5). The conquest was judgment after centuries of waiting, not favoritism, and even then those who turned to the LORD, like Rahab, were spared (Josh 6:25).
  • "A smoking fire pot and a flaming torch passed between these pieces" (v17). The sun has set and Abram is asleep. "Fire pot" is tannur, a clay oven or fire pot of the kind used for baking; "torch" is lappid. The lexicon notes that in this vision the fire pot is a symbol of the LORD's presence. Smoke and fire are the signs of God's presence throughout Israel's story: the pillar of cloud and fire (Exod 13:21), and Mount Sinai wrapped in smoke "because the LORD had descended on it in fire" (Exod 19:18), where the same word lappid appears for the "flashes of lightning" (Exod 20:18).
  • Only God walked between the pieces. This is the heart of the ceremony. In a normal covenant both parties walked through, each calling the curse down on himself. Here Abram is asleep. God alone passes between the halves, so God alone takes on the obligation and the curse. In effect God says, "May I be torn apart like these animals before I fail to keep this promise." Hebrews 6:13–18 draws on this: "since he had no one greater by whom to swear, he swore by himself." Scholars often compare it to an ancient royal land grant, a gift secured by the king's own oath, rather than a treaty with conditions like Sinai's (Deut 28).
  • "On that day the LORD made a covenant with Abram" (v18). "Made" is karat, "cut." The lexicon notes that Jeremiah 34:18 refers back to the ceremony of this chapter. "I give this land" is in the Hebrew perfect tense, which can be read "I have given": the gift is spoken of as already done.
  • "From the river of Egypt to the great river, the river Euphrates" (v18). The southern border is probably the Wadi el-Arish, a seasonal stream in northern Sinai often called "the Brook of Egypt" (Num 34:5), though some take it to mean the Nile. The northern border is the Euphrates in what is now Syria and Iraq. Israel came closest to holding these borders under Solomon, whose rule reached "from the Euphrates to the land of the Philistines and to the border of Egypt" (1 Kgs 4:21; 8:65). Christians differ on whether a fuller fulfillment is still to come for the nation of Israel or whether the promise is widened in Christ, who makes Abraham "heir of the world" (Rom 4:13).
  • The ten peoples (vv19–21). This is the longest list of Canaan's peoples in the Bible; most lists have six or seven (Exod 3:8; Deut 7:1). The Kenites were a clan of smiths and nomads; Moses' father-in-law came from them (Judg 1:16). The Kenizzites were later linked with Caleb (Josh 14:6). The Kadmonites, "easterners," appear only here. The Rephaim were remembered as people of great size (14:5; Deut 2:11). Ten may be a round, complete number: every people in the land is included.
  • Commentators: All three tie Abram's fear to the threat of revenge after the battle with the kings. Henry: "Where there is great faith, yet there may be many fears." On v1 he reads God as "not only thy rewarder, but thy reward," which follows the KJV reading. Henry is gentle with Abram's complaint ("While promised mercies are delayed our unbelief and impatience are apt to conclude them denied"), and JFB is sterner, finding in it "a latent spirit of fretfulness." Guzik calls it doubt that wants the promise rather than denies it, and commends Abram for bringing his frustration to God. On v6, Guzik calls the verse the gospel in the Old Testament. He distinguishes righteousness achieved from righteousness credited. Henry: "All believers are justified as Abram was." JFB has no comment on vv5–8, so it passes over v6 entirely. JFB's note on v4 actually discusses v8, so it seems to be misplaced. JFB covers vv9–21 in one note. It says Abram did not pass between the pieces because "in this transaction he was bound to nothing," and it says God "bound Himself." Guzik makes the same point: God signed for both parties. On v11, Henry finds a lesson in driving off the birds: "When vain thoughts, like these fowls, come down upon our sacrifices, we must drive them away." On the 400 years: "a long time, but a limited time." On v16: "The measure of sin fills gradually." Henry and the others differ on the fire pot and torch. Henry reads the smoking furnace as "the affliction of his seed in Egypt" and the burning lamp as their "comfort in this affliction." The Tyndale notes and Guzik read both as signs of God's own presence, like the pillar of cloud and fire. The lexicon's note on tannur supports the second reading.

Pointing to Christ

Genesis 15 holds two of the clearest pictures of the gospel in the Old Testament. The first is a man with nothing to show who believed God's promise and was counted righteous. Paul says those words were written "not for his sake alone, but for ours also… who believe in him who raised from the dead Jesus our Lord" (Rom 4:23–24). The second is a covenant in which God alone walked the path of blood and took the curse on himself while the human partner lay helpless. At the cross that picture became history. Abram's children broke covenant again and again, and God, in the person of his Son, bore the curse himself (Gal 3:13–14), so that "the blessing of Abraham" might come to the nations. The offspring as many as the stars come through the one offspring, Christ (Gal 3:16, 29), and the darkness that fell on Abram was not the end of the story, just as the darkness at noon on Good Friday was not.

Reflection

  • God Himself as the Reward (v.1) — God tells Abram not to fear because He is Abram's shield and his very great reward, not merely the giver of blessings but the blessing itself. Is God enough for you, or do you only want Him for what He provides?
  • Faith Credited as Righteousness (v.6) — Abram simply believes God's promise, and God counts that faith as righteousness — the foundational picture of salvation by faith rather than works. Are you resting in what God has credited to you, or still trying to earn it?
  • Told the Hard Season in Advance (v.13) — God warns Abram that his descendants will endure centuries of slavery before the promise is fulfilled, showing that suffering and the promise aren't contradictions. Are you able to trust God's timeline even when it includes a season of waiting or hardship?
  • Patience With Even the Wicked (v.16) — God delays judgment on the Amorites because their sin has not yet reached its full measure, revealing a patience that gives space for repentance. How does God's patience with others' sin shape your own patience with people you're tempted to write off?
  • A Covenant God Carries Alone (v.17) — In the ceremony, only God passes between the divided pieces while Abram sleeps, meaning the covenant's fulfillment depends entirely on God's faithfulness, not Abram's. Does it change how you hold your own promises from God, knowing He alone bears responsibility to keep them?
