Summary

Abram returns from Egypt to the Negev and then Bethel, now very wealthy in livestock, silver, and gold (vv. 1-4). Strife breaks out between the herdsmen of Abram and his nephew Lot because the land cannot support both their growing flocks (vv. 5-7). Abram graciously offers Lot the first choice of land to avoid conflict between kinsmen, and Lot chooses the well-watered plain of the Jordan near Sodom, whose people are described as wicked sinners against the Lord (vv. 8-13). After Lot departs, the Lord reaffirms His promise to Abram, telling him to look in every direction because all the land will belong to his offspring, who will be as numerous as the dust of the earth, and Abram moves his tents to Hebron and builds an altar there (vv. 14-18).

Authorship & Background

Author: Moses (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 13 records Abram's return from Egypt, his separation from Lot, and God's reaffirmation of the land promise. After the failure of chapter 12 (Egypt), Abram returns to the altar at Bethel — back to the place of worship and faith. The separation from Lot demonstrates Abram's generous, faith-filled character: he gives Lot first choice, trusting God to provide. Lot chooses by sight; Abram walks by faith.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: a return from Egyptian failure that leads Abram straight back to the altar at Bethel rather than anywhere else, herdsmen's strife settled by Abram offering Lot first choice of the land rather than claiming it himself, Lot lifting up his own eyes to choose the well-watered plain by sight, God then telling Abram to lift up his eyes and receive land by promise instead, and a single ominous clause noting the men of Sodom were wicked and sinners before the LORD exceedingly, planted right where Lot pitches his tent toward the city
Return to Bethel (vv.1-4): Abram returns from Egypt — wealthy (v.2) — to Bethel, "unto the place of the altar" (v.4). He "called on the name of the LORD" (v.4). After failure, he returns to worship. Restoration.
The Separation (vv.5-13): Both Abram and Lot are too wealthy to share the land (vv.5-6). Strife between their herdsmen (v.7). Abram offers Lot first choice: "Is not the whole land before thee?" (v.9). Lot "lifted up his eyes" and chose the well-watered Jordan plain — "even as the garden of the LORD" (v.10). He "pitched his tent toward Sodom" (v.12). Ominous note: "the men of Sodom were wicked and sinners before the LORD exceedingly" (v.13).
God's Promise Reaffirmed (vv.14-18): After Lot leaves, God tells Abram: "Lift up now thine eyes" (v.14) — contrast with Lot lifting his eyes (v.10). Lot looked and chose for himself; God tells Abram to look and receive from Him. "All the land which thou seest, to thee will I give it, and to thy seed for ever" (v.15). "Thy seed as the dust of the earth" (v.16). "Arise, walk through the land" (v.17). Abram settles at Mamre/Hebron and builds another altar (v.18).

Map & Geography

  • Bethel (v.4) — Abram returns to where he built an altar, re-establishing worship after the Egypt detour. Located in the central hill country.
  • The Jordan plain (v.10) — described as "well watered every where... even as the garden of the LORD" — an Eden-like paradise. Lot chooses it for its appearance, not its righteousness.
  • Sodom and Gomorrah (v.10) — located in the Jordan plain, likely near the southern end of the Dead Sea. Choosing beauty over godliness.
  • Hebron/Mamre (v.18) — Abram settles here after Lot departs. Located in the southern hill country, about 19 miles south of Jerusalem.

Deep Dive

Setting: Abram's story is in its early years, probably sometime in the early second millennium BC. He was 75 when he left Haran (12:4), and Genesis 16:3 shows him still waiting for a child ten years later, so this chapter falls somewhere inside that decade. At the end of chapter 12 a famine drove Abram down to Egypt, where he passed Sarai off as his sister to save his own skin. Pharaoh took her into his house and loaded Abram with sheep, cattle, donkeys, camels and servants, until the LORD struck Pharaoh's house with plagues and Abram was sent away (12:10–20). Chapter 13 picks up on the road back. Abram is richer than ever, but the promise of land is still only a promise, and he has no son. His nephew Lot, the son of his dead brother Haran (11:27–28), is still with him, and the most natural heir in sight. By the end of the chapter Lot has gone, and God has spoken the promise again, larger than before.

Abram and Lot Separate (vv1–18)

  • "So Abram went up from Egypt" (v1). "Went up" is the ordinary Hebrew for travelling from Egypt into the hill country of Canaan, which climbs steadily from the south. It is also the word later used for Israel's exodus and for every pilgrim going up to Jerusalem. The Negeb is the dry southland below Hebron, where Abram had been heading before the famine (12:9). The list "he and his wife and all that he had, and Lot with him" quietly answers the danger of chapter 12: Sarai comes home safe, by God's doing, not Abram's.
  • "Abram was very rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold" (v2). The word for "rich" is kabed, whose basic meaning is "heavy." The lexicon gives "very rich" for exactly this verse, so it is a real sense of the word, not a stretch. The same word described the famine as "severe" (literally "heavy") in 12:10. The land was heavy with famine, so Abram went down. Now he comes back heavy with wealth. Much of it came from Pharaoh (12:16), which makes it uncomfortable wealth, gained around a lie.
  • A small exodus. Readers in Moses' day would have heard the pattern. Famine sends God's people into Egypt; Pharaoh takes what is not his; God sends plagues; Pharaoh sends them away; they go up out of Egypt rich in livestock, silver and gold (Exod 3:22; 12:35–38, where "very much livestock" uses the same word kabed). What happened to Israel had already happened in miniature to Abraham.
  • "As far as Bethel… to the place where he had made an altar at the first" (vv3–4). Bethel lay about 12 miles north of Jerusalem, with Ai just to the east, on the ridge that runs down the spine of the land. Abram does not go somewhere new. He goes back to where his tent and altar had been before Egypt (12:8). Genesis stresses it twice: "at the beginning," "at the first." It reads like a man retracing his steps to where things went wrong and starting again.
  • "And there Abram called upon the name of the LORD" (v4). This is the same phrase as 12:8 and goes back to the days of Seth (4:26). It means worship spoken aloud: prayer and praise using God's covenant name. In Egypt there was no altar and no prayer recorded. Back in the land, the first thing he does is worship. Jacob will make the same kind of return to Bethel after his own wandering years (35:1–7).
  • "Lot, who went with Abram, also had flocks and herds and tents" (v5). Lot is rich because he went with Abram, under the blessing God gave him (12:2–3). The mention of "tents" matters: tents are the thread of Lot's story, and by v12 they are moving toward Sodom.
  • "The land could not support both of them" (v6). Shepherds in the hill country depended on scattered springs and seasonal grazing. Two large herding households in the same district would soon exhaust the water and pasture. The verse says "could not dwell together" twice, so the problem is plain. It is not poverty that divides them, but plenty.
  • "There was strife between the herdsmen" (v7). The quarrel starts with the hired men, as such quarrels often do, arguing over which flock gets the well first. Lot does nothing to stop it.
  • "At that time the Canaanites and the Perizzites were dwelling in the land" (v7). This note does two things. It reminds the reader that the "promised" land was full of other people, and Abram owned none of it. It also shows the danger: two related clans fighting in front of watchful neighbors was both risky and a poor witness. The Perizzites are an obscure people listed among the nations of Canaan (15:20; Exod 3:8). Some link the name to a root meaning open, unwalled country, but the lexicon calls that connection possible, not certain.
  • "Let there be no strife between you and me" (v8). The word for "strife" here is meribah, the same word that later became the name of the place where Israel quarrelled with Moses and tested the LORD for water (Exod 17:7; Num 20:13). The Tyndale notes draw out the link: Abram faces the same pressure, a land too dry for everyone, and answers it with faith rather than a quarrel. Abram, the elder and the one to whom the land was promised, makes the first move.
  • "For we are kinsmen" (v8). The Hebrew reads "we are men, brothers," as the ESV footnote says. Lot was really his nephew, but Hebrew uses "brother" for close relatives. Abram appeals to family before he appeals to rights. Psalm 133:1 sings the same note: "how good and pleasant it is when brothers dwell in unity!"
  • "Is not the whole land before you?… If you take the left hand, then I will go to the right" (v9). In Hebrew idiom, facing east, "left" is north and "right" is south. From the hills near Bethel, Abram offers Lot first pick of the country spread out below them. This is remarkable. God had promised the land to Abram, not Lot. Yet the man who in Egypt grabbed for his own safety now holds the promise with an open hand. He can afford to give Lot the choice because God has already made his.
  • "Lot lifted up his eyes and saw" (v10). The wording echoes Eve, who "saw that the tree was good… a delight to the eyes" and took (3:6). Lot's choice is made entirely by sight. From the ridge east of Bethel the Jordan Valley can be seen far below, green along the river.
  • "The Jordan Valley was well watered everywhere" (v10). "Jordan Valley" translates kikkar ha-yarden, literally "the circle of the Jordan." The lexicon gives kikkar as "a round," used for a round district, a round loaf and a round weight (the talent). It describes the broad, rounded plain around the lower Jordan and the Dead Sea. Where exactly Sodom and its neighbor cities stood is still debated. The Tyndale notes favor the southern end of the Dead Sea, while some recent excavators argue for sites just northeast of it. Today that whole region is among the most barren on earth.
  • "Like the garden of the LORD, like the land of Egypt" (v10). Lot measures the valley by two standards: Eden and Egypt. Both are telling. Eden was paradise lost, and Egypt was the very place Abram had just been rescued from, a land watered by the Nile instead of the rain God sends (Deut 11:10–12). Lot has carried Egypt home in his eyes. Zoar was one of the small cities of the plain (14:2). Genesis 19:20–22 connects its name with "a little one," the town Lot will later beg to flee to.
  • "(This was before the LORD destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah)" (v10). The narrator steps in with a word of warning. The valley looks like paradise, but the reader is told up front that it is under sentence. What Lot sees is real, but it is not the whole truth.
  • "So Lot chose for himself… and Lot journeyed east" (v11). "For himself" sums up the choice: no thanks, no deference to his uncle, no question about God. Going "east" has been a bad sign in Genesis: Adam and Eve were driven east of Eden (3:24), Cain settled east of Eden (4:16), and the builders of Babel came from the east (11:2). "Thus they separated from each other." Abram's family has finally been narrowed to the household God called out of Haran.
  • "Lot settled among the cities of the valley and moved his tent as far as Sodom" (v12). Abram stays "in the land of Canaan," the land of promise. Lot sets up among the cities, just outside it. The ESV's "as far as Sodom" (older versions have "toward Sodom") shows a step-by-step drift. In 14:12 Lot is "dwelling in Sodom," and in 19:1 he is "sitting in the gate," among the town's leading men. No one plans to end up in Sodom. It happens a tent-move at a time.
  • "Now the men of Sodom were wicked, great sinners against the LORD" (v13). This one line hangs over everything Lot has chosen. "Against the LORD" can also be read "before the LORD": their evil is open and done in God's sight. The prophets later name Sodom's sins as pride, excess food and ease, and neglect of the poor (Ezek 16:49), along with the sexual violence of Genesis 19 (Jude 7). Lot chose the best grazing and ignored the neighbors. The New Testament still calls him "righteous Lot," but a man "greatly distressed by the sensual conduct of the wicked" (2 Pet 2:7–8). He was saved, but he lost almost everything he had chosen.
  • "The LORD said to Abram, after Lot had separated from him" (v14). God has been silent through the whole quarrel. He speaks only once Abram has let go. Lot may well have been Abram's hoped-for heir, and now he has gone east. At exactly that moment God speaks.
  • "Lift up your eyes and look… northward and southward and eastward and westward" (v14). God uses Lot's own words from v10, "lift up your eyes." Lot looked one way and took. Abram is told to look every way and receive. From the high ground near Bethel the view really does stretch across much of the land.
  • "All the land that you see I will give to you and to your offspring forever" (v15). In 12:7 God promised the land to Abram's offspring. Here he adds "to you" and "forever." Yet Abram never owned more than a burial cave in it (ch 23), and Stephen says God "gave him no inheritance in it, not even a foot's length" (Acts 7:5). Hebrews explains that the patriarchs died in faith, "not having received the things promised," because they were looking for "a better country, that is, a heavenly one" (Heb 11:13–16). Paul goes further still: the promise was that Abraham would be "heir of the world" (Rom 4:13). Christians differ on how the land promise is fulfilled. Some stress a future for the land and the Jewish people; others see the land widened into the whole renewed earth in Christ. All agree God keeps it.
  • "I will make your offspring as the dust of the earth" (v16). A man in his late seventies or early eighties with a barren wife (11:30) is promised descendants beyond counting. Later the picture becomes the stars of heaven (15:5) and the sand of the seashore (22:17). By Solomon's day Judah and Israel were "as many as the sand by the sea" (1 Kgs 4:20). The promise reaches further than one nation: those who belong to Christ are counted "Abraham's offspring" (Gal 3:29).
  • "Arise, walk through the length and the breadth of the land" (v17). Walking a property was a way of taking it in, and some scholars point to ancient customs of walking boundaries as a sign of ownership. Whether or not that background is in view, the command is a call to act on the promise before seeing it fulfilled. God repeats "I will give it to you" a second time.
  • "Abram moved his tent and came and settled by the oaks of Mamre, which are at Hebron" (v18). Both men "moved" their tents (vv12, 18), in opposite directions. The "oaks" are elon, which the lexicon defines as "terebinth" or another large tree, as the ESV footnote also says. Great trees often marked holy or notable places (12:6). Mamre was the name of an Amorite who became Abram's ally (14:13), and the grove was named after him. Hebron, about 20 miles south of Jerusalem in the high hill country, was an old city once called Kiriath-arba (23:2). It became the family home: Sarah, Abraham, Isaac, Rebekah, Jacob and Leah were all buried there (49:29–32), and David later reigned there for seven years (2 Sam 5:5).
  • "And there he built an altar to the LORD" (v18). The chapter opens and closes at an altar: Bethel in v4, Hebron in v18. In between, Lot's eyes take him toward the richest valley he can see, and Abram's faith takes him to an altar. Abram's altars across the land (Shechem, Bethel, Hebron) are like markers of a claim made in worship rather than by force.
  • Commentators: All three treat the chapter as a contrast between faith and sight. Matthew Henry notes that "very rich" is literally "very heavy," and turns it into a reflection that "riches are a burden" in getting, keeping and losing them, though "Abram was very rich and yet very religious." The lexicon supports the "heavy" root. Henry says the altar itself was gone and Abram prayed without it: "When we want an altar, let us not be wanting in prayer." Guzik counts the Egypt detour as wasted time and reads the return to Bethel as a believer coming back to his first works (Rev 2:4–5). JFB thinks Abram went back to Bethel partly "to express humility and penitence for his misconduct in Egypt," though the text itself records no confession. On the quarrel, Henry observes that "poverty and travail, wants and wanderings, could not separate between Abram and Lot; but riches did," and that "the quarrels of professors are the reproach of profession." JFB says Abram, "waiving his right to dictate, … gave the freedom of choice to Lot," and Guzik, like the Tyndale notes, stresses that this was trust in God, not weakness, as Abram's rescue of Lot in the next chapter shows. Guzik argues Lot should never have come at all, since God had told Abram to leave his relatives (12:1). On Lot's choice, Henry finds no "deference or respect to his uncle in the whole management" and says "sensual choices are sinful choices, and seldom speed well." JFB calls it "excellent from a worldly point of view, but most inexpedient for his best interests," and still calls Lot "a good man," in line with 2 Peter. On the promise, Henry puts it neatly: "though Lot perhaps had the better land, yet Abram had the better title," and "God will abundantly make up in spiritual peace what we lose for the preservation of neighbourly peace." Henry and JFB both point to Solomon's day (1 Kgs 4:20) as a first fulfilment of v16. Guzik says Mamre means "vision" and Hebron "communion." The lexicon gives no meaning for Mamre, which was a man's name (14:13), so "vision" should be set aside. It derives Hebron from a root meaning "association, league," so "communion" is a loose but not baseless reading. JFB has no comment on vv5–6, 8–9, 12–13 and 16–17.

Pointing to Christ

Lot looked at the best land and took it for himself. Abram had every right to the land and gave the choice away, trusting God to give what was his. That open hand is a faint outline of Christ, who "though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself" (Phil 2:6–7), and who was then given "the name that is above every name." Jesus said, "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth" (Matt 5:5), almost an echo of this chapter, and he is the true offspring of Abraham in whom the promise of land and descendants comes to rest (Gal 3:16). Abram walked the land as a stranger and built altars; in Christ his family, countless as the dust, is promised far more than Canaan: a new earth, and God himself dwelling with them.

Reflection

  • Returning to the Altar (v.4) — After the failure in Egypt, Abram goes back to the place where he had first built an altar and calls on the LORD's name again. When you fail, do you return to God, or stay away out of shame?
  • Choosing Peace Over Rights (v.8) — Abram moves to end the strife with Lot's herdsmen even though he could rightfully claim first choice of the land. Are you willing to release your rights for the sake of peace, trusting God to provide either way?
  • Choosing by Appearance (v.10) — Lot looks at the well-watered Jordan plain and chooses it purely on how good it appears. Where are you making decisions based on what looks good rather than what is actually good?
  • A Tent Facing Sodom (v.12) — Lot pitches his tent toward Sodom, a small, gradual step that sets his whole trajectory. What direction is your life quietly facing, and where is it leading over time?
  • A Promise That Follows Generosity (vv.14-17) — Right after Abram gives Lot the better land, God expands His promise to Abram even further. When you release something with open hands, are you trusting God to be the one who fills them?
