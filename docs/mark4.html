1Once again Jesus began to teach beside the sea, and such a large crowd gathered around Him that He got into a boat and sat in it, while all the people crowded along the shore.

2And He taught them many things in parables, and in His teaching He said,

3“Listen! A farmer went out to sow his seed.

4And as he was sowing, some seed fell along the path, and the birds came and devoured it.

5Some fell on rocky ground, where it did not have much soil. It sprang up quickly because the soil was shallow.

6But when the sun rose, the seedlings were scorched, and they withered because they had no root.

7Other seed fell among thorns, which grew up and choked the seedlings, and they yielded no crop.

8Still other seed fell on good soil, where it sprouted, grew up, and produced a crop—one bearing thirtyfold, another sixtyfold, and another a hundredfold.”

9Then Jesus said, “He who has ears to hear, let him hear.”

10As soon as Jesus was alone with the Twelve and those around Him, they asked Him about the parable.

11He replied, “The mystery of the kingdom of God has been given to you, but to those on the outside everything is expressed in parables,

12so that, ‘they may be ever seeing but never perceiving, and ever hearing but never understanding; otherwise they might turn and be forgiven.’”

13Then Jesus said to them, “Do you not understand this parable? Then how will you understand any of the parables?

14The farmer sows the word.

15Some are like the seeds along the path, where the word is sown. As soon as they hear it, Satan comes and takes away the word that was sown in them.

16Some are like the seeds sown on rocky ground. They hear the word and at once receive it with joy.

17But they themselves have no root, and they remain for only a season. When trouble or persecution comes because of the word, they quickly fall away.

18Others are like the seeds sown among the thorns. They hear the word,

19but the worries of this life, the deceitfulness of wealth, and the desire for other things come in and choke the word, and it becomes unfruitful.

20Still others are like the seeds sown on good soil. They hear the word, receive it, and produce a crop—thirtyfold, sixtyfold, or a hundredfold.”

21Jesus also said to them, “Does anyone bring in a lamp to put it under a basket or under a bed? Doesn’t he set it on a stand?

22For there is nothing hidden that will not be disclosed, and nothing concealed that will not be brought to light.

23If anyone has ears to hear, let him hear.”

24He went on to say, “Pay attention to what you hear. With the measure you use, it will be measured to you, and even more will be added to you.

25For whoever has will be given more. But whoever does not have, even what he has will be taken away from him.”

26Jesus also said, “The kingdom of God is like a man who scatters seed on the ground.

27Night and day he sleeps and wakes, and the seed sprouts and grows, though he knows not how.

28All by itself the earth produces a crop—first the stalk, then the head, then grain that ripens within.

29And as soon as the grain is ripe, he swings the sickle, because the harvest has come.”

30Then He asked, “To what can we compare the kingdom of God? With what parable shall we present it?

31It is like a mustard seed, which is the smallest of all seeds sown upon the earth.

32But after it is planted, it grows to be the largest of all garden plants and puts forth great branches, so that the birds of the air nest in its shade.”

33With many such parables Jesus spoke the word to them, to the extent that they could understand.

34He did not tell them anything without using a parable. But privately He explained everything to His own disciples.

35When that evening came, He said to His disciples, “Let us cross to the other side.”

36After they had dismissed the crowd, they took Jesus with them, since He was already in the boat. And there were other boats with Him.

37Soon a violent windstorm came up, and the waves were breaking over the boat, so that it was being swamped.

38But Jesus was in the stern, sleeping on the cushion. So they woke Him and said, “Teacher, don’t You care that we are perishing?”

39Then Jesus got up and rebuked the wind and the sea. “Silence!” He commanded. “Be still!” And the wind died down, and it was perfectly calm.

40“Why are you so afraid?” He asked. “Do you still have no faith?”

41Overwhelmed with fear, they asked one another, “Who is this, that even the wind and the sea obey Him?”

Summary

Teaching from a boat beside the Sea of Galilee, Jesus tells the parable of the sower and explains privately to His disciples that the seed represents the word of God received in different kinds of soil (vv. 1-20). He follows with the parables of the lamp, the seed growing secretly, and the mustard seed, each showing how the kingdom of God begins small and hidden yet inevitably produces a harvest or great growth (vv. 21-32). That evening, crossing the lake, a violent storm arises while Jesus sleeps; He rebukes the wind and waves with a word, and they instantly grow calm, leaving His awestruck disciples wondering who this is that even the wind and sea obey (vv. 35-41).

Authorship & Background

Author: John Mark (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 4 contains Jesus' parable teaching (the Sower, the Growing Seed, the Mustard Seed) and the calming of the storm. Mark presents these parables as Jesus' primary teaching method to the crowds, while explaining them privately to His disciples.
Classification: Gospel — Narrative
Key Themes: A sower parable called foundational to understanding every other parable, four soils sorting hearers by what happens after the seed lands, a seed that grows while the farmer sleeps and does not know how, a mustard seed's disproportion between its start and its finish, and a storm rebuked with two words while the disciples ask what manner of man this is
The Parable of the Sower (vv.1-20): The foundational parable — Jesus says if you don't understand this one, you won't understand any of them (v.13). The seed is the Word of God. Four soils represent four responses: hard path (Satan snatches it), rocky ground (no root, falls away under pressure), thorns (choked by worldly concerns), and good soil (bears fruit — 30, 60, 100 fold).
Nothing Hid That Shall Not Be Manifested (vv.21-25): A candle is not lit to be put under a bed, and the principle drawn from it is about eventual exposure rather than about behavior, for there is nothing hid, which shall not be manifested. Then a warning about attention, take heed what ye hear, and a measure that cuts both ways, with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you, and unto you that hear shall more be given. The chapter's parables are about hearing, and this is the passage that says so.
The Growing Seed (vv.26-29): Unique to Mark. A man plants seed and it grows "he knoweth not how" (v.27). The kingdom grows by God's power, not human effort. The farmer's job is to plant and harvest — the growth is God's work.
The Grain of Mustard Seed (vv.30-34): The kingdom is likened to the smallest seed a farmer sows, and the point is the disproportion between the start and the finish, it groweth up, and becometh greater than all herbs, so that the fowls of the air may lodge under the shadow of it. Then Mark's own note on method: with many such parables spake he the word unto them, and without a parable spake he not unto them, and when they were alone, he expounded all things to his disciples.
The Storm (vv.35-41): Jesus sleeps through a violent storm while the disciples panic. He rebukes the wind and sea with two words: "Peace, be still." Then He asks "Why are ye so fearful? how is it that ye have no faith?" The disciples' question: "What manner of man is this?"

Deep Dive

Setting: The shore of the Sea of Galilee, around AD 28. Jesus has drawn huge crowds from every region (3:7–8), chosen the twelve (3:13–19), and faced two kinds of rejection: his family thought he was out of his mind and the scribes from Jerusalem said he was in league with Satan (3:20–35). Chapter 4 is Mark's longest block of Jesus' teaching. It is a collection of parables about the kingdom of God, most of them about seeds, and it explains why so many people see Jesus and still do not believe. Then, in the evening, the teaching gives way to a storm on the lake that leaves the disciples asking the question this whole section of Mark is driving at: "Who then is this?"

The Parable of the Sower (vv1–9)

  • "He got into a boat and sat in it on the sea" (v1). A teacher sat to teach (Matt 5:1). The boat kept the crowd from crushing him (3:9), and a sloping shore with water in front can carry a voice a long way. A cove near Capernaum, sometimes called the Bay of Parables, forms a natural amphitheater; tests there have shown that a speaker in a boat can be heard clearly by thousands on the bank.
  • "Many things in parables" (v2). A parable (parabolē, from the Hebrew mashal) sets something familiar beside a spiritual truth so that one throws light on the other. It can be a short saying, a riddle, a comparison or a story. Parables draw listeners in, then make them think, and often turn on a surprise.
  • "Listen! Behold, a sower went out to sow" (v3). The parable begins and ends with a call to hear (v9). "Listen" (Akouete) echoes the great Jewish confession, "Hear, O Israel" (Deut 6:4). Farmers in Galilee often scattered seed by hand and plowed it in afterward, so seed could land anywhere in a field crossed by footpaths, with limestone bedrock close under the soil in places and thorn roots hidden in the ground.
  • The four soils (vv4–8). Seed on the hard path is eaten by birds. Seed on thin soil over rock springs up fast, because the warmth near the rock speeds it, but it scorches and dies for lack of roots. Seed among thorns grows but is choked. Seed in good soil produces grain.
  • "Thirtyfold and sixtyfold and a hundredfold" (v8). Ordinary yields in that region were perhaps sevenfold to tenfold. A hundredfold was extraordinary, the kind of harvest God gave Isaac as a sign of blessing (Gen 26:12). The point is that despite all the wasted seed, the harvest in the end is astonishing.
  • "He who has ears to hear, let him hear" (v9). Everyone has ears. Not everyone listens.
  • Commentators: Guzik notes that the seed is the same in every case and only the soil differs, so he suggests "the parable of the soils" as a better name. Matthew Henry says the same: when the word bears no fruit, "the fault is in themselves, not in the word." JFB calls this parable "fundamental, comprehensive, and introductory," the key to the others.

The Purpose of the Parables (vv10–20)

  • "Those around him with the twelve" (v10). A wider circle of followers, not only the twelve. They ask about "the parables," plural.
  • "To you has been given the secret of the kingdom of God" (v11). "Secret" is mystērion, "mystery." In the Bible a mystery is not a puzzle but something hidden that God reveals (Dan 2:28; Rom 16:25–26). The secret is that the kingdom has arrived in the person of Jesus, in a hidden, humble form that most people miss. "Has been given" is a gift; the disciples did not figure it out.
  • "For those outside everything is in parables" (v11). "Outside" recalls Jesus' family "standing outside" in 3:31–32. Insiders are those who gather around Jesus to learn. Outsiders hear the stories but do not come closer.
  • "So that they may indeed see but not perceive… lest they should turn and be forgiven" (v12). This is one of the hardest sayings in the Gospels. It quotes Isaiah 6:9–10, where God sends Isaiah to preach to a people who have already hardened their hearts, and his preaching will harden them further. It is judgment on long refusal. Jesus' parables work the same way. To those who come close they open up the truth; to those who keep their distance they remain riddles, and that itself is a judgment on unbelief. Some scholars read "so that" here as "in order that," others as "with the result that," or as "so that the Scripture might be fulfilled." The Tyndale notes take it as an intentional judgment. Either way, Jesus still speaks to the crowds, still invites them to listen (vv3, 9, 23), and explains everything to anyone who stays to ask (v34). The door is shut only to those who will not knock.
  • "Do you not understand this parable? How then will you understand all the parables?" (v13). The insiders do not understand either. The difference is that they ask. This is the first of many times Mark shows the disciples slow to understand (6:52; 8:17–21).
  • "The sower sows the word" (v14). The word is the message of the kingdom. The sower is first Jesus, then anyone who passes on the message.
  • The path: "Satan immediately comes and takes away the word" (v15). Hard hearts never let the word in. Satan, like the birds, takes it before it can root.
  • The rocky ground: "they have no root in themselves… when tribulation or persecution arises… immediately they fall away" (vv16–17). Quick joy with no depth. "Fall away" (skandalizomai) means to stumble, to be tripped up. Mark's first readers in Rome, facing Nero's persecution, knew exactly what this looked like. So would the disciples, who all fell away the night of the arrest (14:27), though they were restored.
  • The thorns: "the cares of the world and the deceitfulness of riches and the desires for other things" (v19). Not open opposition but slow crowding out. Worry, money and wanting more. Riches are "deceitful" because they promise security and satisfaction they cannot deliver (10:22–23; 1 Tim 6:9–10).
  • The good soil: those who "hear the word and accept it and bear fruit" (v20). Three verbs: hear, accept, bear fruit. The difference between the soils is not whether people hear but what happens after.
  • Christians read the soils differently on one point: whether the rocky and thorny soils describe people who were truly saved and then lost it, or people who made a show of faith that was never real. Both sides agree on what the parable plainly says: only the good soil bears fruit, and Jesus wants hearers to be that soil.
  • Commentators: Guzik, citing Morgan and Geldenhuys, argues that parables were meant to draw people toward the truth, a doorway the interested step through, and that the Isaiah quotation explains why some never do. Henry stresses God's justice in leaving those who refuse to see in the dark, and notes that "Those only who are converted, have their sins forgiven them." JFB says the effect of preaching "depends upon the state of the hearer's heart" and describes the path as "hearts all unbroken and hard." It reads Jesus' question in v13 as preparing the disciples rather than scolding them.

A Lamp Under a Basket (vv21–25)

  • "Is a lamp brought in to be put under a basket, or under a bed?" (v21). A small clay oil lamp lit a one-room house. The "basket" was a grain measure, about two gallons. Putting it over the lamp would put the light out. In Greek the sentence says "Does the lamp come…?" Some see a hint that the lamp is Jesus himself, who "came" to give light (John 8:12). The kingdom is hidden now, but not so that it will stay hidden.
  • "Nothing is hidden except to be made manifest" (v22). The hidden kingdom of vv11–12 will be revealed. What is now a secret given to a few will one day be shown openly to all (13:26).
  • "Pay attention to what you hear" (v24). Matthew and Luke put it "how you hear" and "take care then how you hear" (Luke 8:18). Mark stresses the content. "With the measure you use, it will be measured to you, and still more will be added." In Matthew 7:2 this saying is about judging others. Here it is about listening: the attention one gives to the word is repaid with understanding, and more.
  • "To the one who has, more will be given, and from the one who has not, even what he has will be taken away" (v25). This sounds unfair until it is read as a law of spiritual growth. Those who take in the truth they have are given more. Those who ignore it lose even what they had. Hearing is never neutral.
  • Commentators: Henry applies the lamp to gifts that must be shared, not kept "like a taper in an urn, they burn to themselves." On listening: "What we hear, doth us no good, unless we consider it," and, "As we deal with God, God will deal with us." Guzik, citing Spurgeon, says spiritual growth builds momentum either way. JFB notes that these sayings repeat ones found elsewhere in Matthew, and reads "unto you that hear" as hearing "thankfully, teachably, profitably."

The Parable of the Seed Growing (vv26–29)

  • Only Mark records this parable, the one parable in his Gospel that Matthew and Luke leave out.
  • "He sleeps and rises night and day, and the seed sprouts and grows; he knows not how" (v27). The farmer does his part: he sows. Then he goes about his life, and the seed grows without him. He cannot make it grow faster, and he does not fully understand how it grows. Paul says the same: "I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth" (1 Cor 3:6).
  • "The earth produces by itself" (v28). "By itself" is automatē, the root of "automatic." The growth comes from the life in the seed and the power God put in the soil, not from the farmer's anxiety. "First the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear." Growth comes in stages, slowly and surely.
  • "When the grain is ripe, at once he puts in the sickle, because the harvest has come" (v29). This echoes Joel 3:13, "Put in the sickle, for the harvest is ripe," a picture of the final day of God. The kingdom grows quietly now, but the harvest will come. For people who share the word, the parable is both a relief and a call to patience: the results are God's work.
  • Commentators: Henry: "God carries on his work insensibly and without noise, but insuperably and without fail." JFB calls this parable "peculiar to Mark" and says it teaches "the Imperceptible Growth of the word sown in the heart." Guzik compares it to God's hidden work in believers and ties it to Isaiah 55:11, that God's word will not return empty, and to Zechariah 4:10, not despising "the day of small things." The Tyndale notes stress that the coming of God's kingdom in the end does not depend on human effort.

The Parable of the Mustard Seed (vv30–34)

  • "The smallest of all the seeds on earth" (v31). The black mustard seed is about a millimeter across, and "small as a mustard seed" was a common Jewish saying (Matt 17:20). Botanists know of smaller seeds, such as orchid seeds, but Jesus is speaking as people in Galilee spoke, of the smallest seed a farmer there would sow. It is not a botanical claim.
  • "Larger than all the garden plants and puts out large branches" (v32). Mustard grows quickly into a bush eight to ten feet tall, sometimes more, by the Sea of Galilee. From almost nothing comes something large enough for birds to perch in.
  • "The birds of the air can make nests in its shade" (v32). The image echoes Old Testament pictures of a great kingdom as a tree that shelters birds (Ezek 17:23; 31:6; Dan 4:12). In Ezekiel 17 God plants a small sprig that becomes a noble cedar, and "under it will dwell every kind of bird." Most interpreters see the birds as the nations, Gentiles coming to find shelter in God's kingdom. The kingdom begins with a Galilean teacher and twelve unlikely men. It will spread to the whole world.
  • "As they were able to hear it" (v33). Jesus suited his teaching to his hearers. The parables were a mercy as well as a test.
  • "Privately to his own disciples he explained everything" (v34). The word "explained" (epilyō) means "untied," "unlocked." The secret was given to those who stayed close.
  • Commentators: Henry applies the parable both to the church, whose "beginnings… would be very small," and to the growth of grace in a single soul. Guzik takes a different line on the birds. Because birds in the sower parable stand for Satan (v15), he reads them here as evil finding a home in a church that grows large, and warns that size is not the same as health. That reading has defenders, but most commentators, the Tyndale notes among them, follow the Old Testament tree images and see the birds positively, as the nations finding shelter. JFB refers the parable to its notes on Matthew 13 and says the "many such parables" of v33 match the fuller collection in Matthew.

Jesus Calms a Storm (vv35–41)

  • "Let us go across to the other side" (v35). The eastern shore was Gentile territory, the region of the Decapolis (5:1, 20). The word Jesus gave, "let us go across," was itself a promise that they would arrive.
  • "They took him with them in the boat, just as he was" (v36). Without going ashore first, still in the boat he had been teaching from. Tired from a long day. "Other boats were with him", a detail only Mark keeps, the kind of thing an eyewitness remembers.
  • "A great windstorm arose" (v37). The Sea of Galilee lies about 680 feet below sea level, ringed by hills, with the high Golan plateau to the east. Cool air pouring down through the ravines meets the warm air over the lake and can whip up sudden, violent storms, especially in the evening. Even experienced fishermen like Peter, Andrew, James and John were terrified. In 1986, during a drought, the remains of a fishing boat from about this period were found in the mud on the northwest shore. It is about 26 feet long and 7 feet wide, large enough for a dozen or so men.
  • "He was in the stern, asleep on the cushion" (v38). The stern was a raised deck at the back where the helmsman stood. "The cushion" may be a seat cushion or a sandbag kept for ballast. Only Mark mentions it. This storm is the only time the Gospels show Jesus asleep. He is truly human and truly tired, and he sleeps in a storm with the trust of Psalm 4:8: "In peace I will both lie down and sleep."
  • "Teacher, do you not care that we are perishing?" (v38). Matthew softens it to "Save us, Lord; we are perishing" (Matt 8:25). Mark keeps the raw edge of the question. It is a question believers still ask in storms. Mark's answer is the next verse.
  • "He rebuked the wind and said to the sea, 'Peace! Be still!'" (v39). "Rebuked" is the word Jesus used against the demon in 1:25, and "Be still" is literally "Be muzzled," the same verb as "Be silent" there. He speaks to the storm as to a hostile power. In the Old Testament the raging sea is a picture of chaos that only God can master (Ps 65:7; 89:9; 107:23–30). Psalm 107 describes sailors in a storm who "cried to the LORD in their trouble… He made the storm be still, and the waves of the sea were hushed." Here the disciples cry out, and Jesus does what the psalm says the LORD does.
  • "The wind ceased, and there was a great calm" (v39). After a storm the waves usually keep rolling for hours. Here they stop at once. A "great storm" (v37) is met by a "great calm," and that leads to a "great fear" (v41).
  • "Why are you so afraid? Have you still no faith?" (v40). "Still" points back to everything they have already seen him do. Faith here means trusting who Jesus is, even when he seems to be asleep.
  • "They were filled with great fear" (v41). Literally "they feared a great fear." The story echoes Jonah. Jonah also slept in a boat during a great storm, the sailors woke him in terror, and when the sea was calmed "the men feared the LORD exceedingly" (Jonah 1:16), using the same phrase in the Greek Old Testament. But Jonah was running from God and had to be thrown overboard to still the storm. Jesus stills it with a word. "Something greater than Jonah is here" (Matt 12:41).
  • "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (v41). The disciples are more afraid after the storm than during it. They had feared the wind. Now they fear the one in the boat with them, because only God commands wind and sea. The question hangs over the next chapters until Peter answers it (8:29).
  • Commentators: Henry on the sleeping Lord: "he slept, but his heart was awake," and on the calm, "Jesus Christ can lay it with a word's speaking." He says the rebuke in v40 does not mean the disciples had no faith, only that fear had overwhelmed it for the moment, and JFB agrees: "next to none, or none in present exercise." JFB calls "Peace, be still" "two sublime words of command" and ties the disciples' question to Psalms 89:9 and 93:4. Guzik notes that "rebuked" is the language used for demons, and suggests the storm may have had a satanic side. He also says the disciples' unbelief was unreasonable because Jesus had promised they would cross over, and he quotes Morgan's line that the boat cannot sink with Jesus on board.

Pointing to Christ

The chapter moves from seeds to a storm, and both point to who Jesus is. He is the sower of the word, and his kingdom, small and hidden as a mustard seed, will grow until the nations find shelter in it. The secret of the kingdom is given to those who come close and ask. Then in the boat, the one who taught about seeds sleeps like any tired man, and wakes to speak to the wind as only God can. He is truly human and truly God. The disciples' question, "Who then is this?", is answered in the end not by a calmed sea but by a cross and an empty tomb, where he goes into the deepest storm of all for those who called out to him.

Reflection

  • Four Soils, One Heart Examined (vv.3-8) — Jesus described identical seed landing on a hard path, rocky ground, thorny ground, and good soil, with only the last producing a harvest. Which soil most honestly describes the condition of your heart toward God's word right now?
  • Growth Choked by Competing Cares (v.19) — Jesus named worry, the pursuit of wealth, and desire for other things as thorns that choke the word before it can bear fruit. Which of those three is quietly crowding out your spiritual growth?
  • Generosity That Comes Back to You (vv.24-25) — Jesus taught that the measure you use toward others is the measure you'll receive, and that those who use well what they've been given will be given more. How generously are you giving of your time, attention, and resources to others?
  • Growth You Can't Explain or Force (vv.26-27) — A farmer scatters seed and it grows while he sleeps, in a process he cannot explain or control. Where are you trying to force spiritual growth, in yourself or someone else, that only God can produce?
  • Asleep in the Storm (vv.38-40) — While the disciples panicked in a violent storm, Jesus slept, then calmed the wind and sea with a word before asking why they had no faith. In your current storm, are you panicking or resting in who's in the boat with you?
  • The Question Every Reader Must Answer (v.41) — After witnessing Jesus command the wind and waves, the disciples asked what kind of man this really was. Who do you say Jesus is, based not on what you've heard but on what He's shown you?
