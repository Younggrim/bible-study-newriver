Summary

Isaiah records God's answer to the prayer of chapters 63-64: he was found by a nation that was not even seeking him, a foretaste of Gentile inclusion later cited in Romans 10:20, even while Israel provoked him with idolatry and rebellion (vv. 1-7). Still, God promises to preserve a faithful remnant, like good wine found in a cluster of grapes not to be destroyed along with the rest (vv. 8-12), and he draws a sharp contrast between his obedient servants, who will eat, drink, and rejoice, and the rebels, who will hunger, thirst, and be put to shame (vv. 13-16). The chapter climaxes with the promise of new heavens and a new earth, where weeping and crying are no more, people enjoy long life and the work of their hands, and even the wolf and the lamb feed together in peace (vv. 17-25).

Authorship & Background

Author: Isaiah son of Amoz (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — Divine Response + New Creation
Key Themes: Gentiles finding God, Israel's rebellion, the faithful remnant preserved, judgment on the unfaithful, new heavens and new earth, joy replacing sorrow, long life, peace in nature
Historical Context: This chapter answers the anguished prayer of 63:15-64:12. But God's answer is surprising: He was already being found — by Gentiles who weren't even looking (v.1, quoted in Romans 10:20). Meanwhile, Israel was provoking Him with idolatry (vv.3-5). Yet God will not destroy the whole cluster — a remnant is preserved (v.8). The chapter climaxes (vv.17-25) with the creation of new heavens and A new earth — one of the OT's grandest eschatological visions, expanded in Revelation 21.
I Am Found of Them That Sought Me Not (v.1): I am sought of them that asked not for me, I am found of them that sought me not, I said, Behold me, behold me, unto a nation that was not called by my name. This is the answer to two chapters of prayer and it begins by pointing somewhere else entirely: the people who were not looking are the ones who found him. Paul quotes it in Romans 10:20 of the Gentiles, and reads the following verse of Israel. The doubled Behold me, behold me is the note of availability the prayer had said was missing.
I Have Spread Out My Hands All the Day (vv.2-7): I have spread out my hands all the day unto a rebellious people. The hiddenness complained of at 64:7 is answered with a posture: hands out, all day. Then the practices, and they are specifically the cults of the dead and of the underworld, which remain among the graves, and lodge in the monuments, which eat swine's flesh, and broth of abominable things. And the quoted attitude is the worst of it, which say, Stand by thyself, come not near to me, for I am holier than thou. People engaged in that list telling others to keep their distance for reasons of purity.
As the New Wine in the Cluster (vv.8-12): Thus saith the LORD, As the new wine is found in the cluster, and one saith, Destroy it not, for a blessing is in it, so will I do for my servants' sake, that I may not destroy them all. The remnant is described as a reason not to clear a whole vineyard: one good bunch stays the harvester's hand. Then the division within the people is made by name and it is a division of function, my servants shall eat, but ye shall be hungry, my servants shall drink, but ye shall be thirsty. And the charge in the last verse is about answering, because when I called, ye did not answer, when I spake, ye did not hear.
Ye Shall Leave Your Name for a Curse (vv.13-16): Behold, my servants shall sing for joy of heart, but ye shall cry for sorrow of heart, and shall howl for vexation of spirit. The contrast is drawn six times in four verses, which is the same device as the woes of chapter 5. Then the sentence on the name, and ye shall leave your name for a curse unto my chosen, and the LORD God shall slay thee, and call his servants by another name. And the title given in the last verse is unusual and precise, the God of truth, twice, which in Hebrew is the God of Amen.
New Heavens and a New Earth (vv.17-25): For, behold, I create new heavens and a new earth, and the former shall not be remembered, nor come into mind. Revelation 21:1 takes up the phrase, and what is striking here is how ordinary the content is. What follows is a description of a working town: no more infant deaths, people living out their lives, houses built by the people who will live in them, vineyards planted by the people who will drink from them. And they shall not build, and another inhabit, they shall not plant, and another eat, which reverses the specific misery of Deuteronomy 28:30 and of an occupied country. Prayer answered before it is finished, and it shall come to pass, that before they call, I will answer. And the chapter closes with 11:6 repeated in shorter form, the wolf and the lamb shall feed together, and the lion shall eat straw like the bullock, with one clause added, and dust shall be the serpent's meat, which leaves Genesis 3 in force in the middle of a new creation.

Setting: For a chapter and a half (63:15–64:12) the prophet has prayed for God's people in the ruins after the exile: "Oh that you would rend the heavens and come down," ending with the question, "Will you keep silent?" (64:12). Chapters 65–66 are God's answer, and the Tyndale notes read them that way. The answer is not what the prayer expected. God says he has never been silent or hard to find: he has been calling all along, and many did not answer. So he will divide his people. Those who keep chasing idols will be judged, and his faithful "servants" will be blessed. Then the chapter opens out into one of the Bible's great visions: "new heavens and a new earth" (v17). Isaiah ministered in Jerusalem about 740–700 BC; these closing chapters address God's people after the return from Babylon, when many fell back into old pagan habits even in the land.

Judgment and Salvation (vv1–16)

  • "I was ready to be sought by those who did not ask for me" (v1). The Hebrew verbs are passive in form ("I let myself be sought… I let myself be found"). God has made himself available. "Here I am, here I am" is what a servant says when called (Gen 22:1; 1 Sam 3:4), but here it is God saying it to people who never called. In 58:9 he promised that if his people called, he would say, "Here I am." Now he says it unasked.
  • "To a nation that was not called by my name" (v1). This picks up 63:19, where Israel complained, "We have become like those… who are not called by your name." In context the verse may be describing Israel herself, acting like a pagan nation. Paul reads it of the Gentiles, quoting v1 and v2 in Romans 10:20–21: God "has been found by those who did not seek me," while to Israel he says, "All day long I have held out my hands to a disobedient and contrary people." Paul calls Isaiah "so bold" to say it, and JFB echoes him. Both readings land the same way: God's grace reaches people who weren't looking, and his own people can refuse it.
  • "I spread out my hands all the day to a rebellious people" (v2). Spread hands are a gesture of appeal, the way a parent holds out arms to a child or a person pleads. God has done this "all the day," without stopping. Jesus wept over Jerusalem in the same spirit: "How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!" (Matt 23:37). "Following their own devices." Their own ideas, not God's word (compare Prov 14:12).
  • "Sacrificing in gardens and making offerings on bricks" (v3). The Tyndale notes say sacred gardens and groves belonged to the fertility religion of Baal (1:29; 66:17). God's law required altars of earth or uncut stone (Exod 20:24–25). Brick altars, and perhaps incense burned on rooftop bricks, imitated Babylonian worship. JFB notes the contrast with unhewn stone.
  • "Who sit in tombs, and spend the night in secret places" (v4). This is most likely necromancy, trying to contact the dead, which the law forbade outright (Deut 18:10–11; Isa 8:19). Tombs also made a person unclean (Num 19:16). People sat among graves overnight hoping for dreams or messages from the spirits of the dead.
  • "Who eat pig's flesh, and broth of tainted meat is in their vessels" (v4). Pork was banned in the food laws (Lev 11:7; Deut 14:8). Pigs were also sacrificed in some pagan rites, so this may be part of idol worship as well as plain disobedience (66:3, 17). "Tainted meat" is piggul, the word for sacrificial meat left too long and so made unclean (Lev 7:18; 19:7).
  • "Keep to yourself, do not come near me, for I am too holy for you" (v5). The worst part is not the paganism but the pride that goes with it. These people treat their secret rites as making them holy, and warn others off as if their holiness might be catching. The KJV's "I am holier than thou" gave English a lasting phrase. The Tyndale notes point out that they borrowed the vocabulary of Moses' purity laws for rites shaped by paganism. Jesus' parable of the Pharisee who thanked God he was "not like other men" (Luke 18:11) shows the same spirit. JFB applies it to the "hypocritical self-justifiers" of Jesus' day.
  • "These are a smoke in my nostrils, a fire that burns all the day" (v5). Sacrifices were meant to be "a pleasing aroma to the LORD" (Lev 1:9). These people are smoke that stings God's eyes and nose, a fire that never goes out. The irony is sharp: they warn others not to come near, and they are the ones God cannot stand near.
  • "Behold, it is written before me: 'I will not keep silent, but I will repay'" (v6). This answers the prayer's last question directly: "Will you keep silent?" (64:12). God will not. But his speaking includes judgment as well as rescue. "Written before me" is the language of a court record, like the books opened at the judgment (Dan 7:10; Rev 20:12).
  • "I will indeed repay into their lap" (v6). Grain was measured into the fold of a robe gathered at the waist. Jesus uses the image of generous giving: "good measure, pressed down, shaken together, running over, will be put into your lap" (Luke 6:38). Here the measure is the full weight of their sin. "Your iniquities and your fathers' iniquities together" (v7). This is not punishment for someone else's sin. They have kept up the very worship their fathers practiced "on the mountains… on the hills" (Hos 4:13; 1 Kings 14:23), and so they share in it (compare Matt 23:31–32).
  • "As the new wine is found in the cluster… 'Do not destroy it, for there is a blessing in it'" (v8). This sounds like a vineyard saying. A bunch of grapes may look poor, with some shriveled or spoiled fruit, but a worker says, "Don't throw it out, there's good juice in there." Some think "Do not destroy" was even the name of a vintage song (several psalm titles carry the phrase, Ps 57–59, 75). God will not throw out the whole nation, because his faithful servants are in it. This is the remnant theme that runs through Isaiah (1:9; 6:13; 10:20–22). JFB reads it the same way.
  • "I will bring forth offspring from Jacob… my chosen shall possess it" (v9). The land promise of Abraham (Gen 15:18) holds, but the heirs are "my chosen… my servants." Through these last chapters the word "servants," plural, marks out the faithful (54:17; 56:6; 63:17). They are the offspring of the one Servant of chapters 42–53, who "shall see his offspring" (53:10).
  • "Sharon shall become a pasture… the Valley of Achor a place for herds" (v10). Sharon was the fertile coastal plain on the west; the Valley of Achor was near Jericho on the east, so the two together mean the whole land, as the Tyndale notes observe. Achor means "trouble." It was where Achan was stoned after he stole from Jericho and brought disaster on Israel (Josh 7:24–26). Hosea promised God would make "the Valley of Achor a door of hope" (Hos 2:15). The place of Israel's first sin in the land becomes a peaceful pasture.
  • "Who set a table for Fortune and fill cups of mixed wine for Destiny" (v11). In Hebrew these are Gad and Meni, gods of luck and fate. The Tyndale notes identify them as pagan deities believed to bring fortune and fix a person's destiny, and JFB calls Gad the Babylonian god of fortune. Worshipers laid out banquets for them, like offerings to gods of chance. These people "forget my holy mountain," that is, they ignore the temple of the living God while setting tables for luck.
  • "I will destine you to the sword" (v12). A wordplay. "Destine" is maniti, from the same root as Meni, "Destiny." The Tyndale notes point it out. They trusted Destiny, and the real God sets their destiny. "When I called, you did not answer; when I spoke, you did not listen." These words repeat almost exactly in 66:4. This is the reason for the judgment: not ignorance but refusal (Prov 1:24–31).
  • "My servants shall eat, but you shall be hungry" (vv13–14). Five sharp contrasts in a row: eat and hunger, drink and thirst, rejoice and shame, singing and wailing. The covenant blessings and curses of Deuteronomy 28 are now divided within one people, not between Israel and the nations. Jesus' beatitudes and woes have the same shape: "Blessed are you who are hungry now… Woe to you who are full now" (Luke 6:21, 25).
  • "You shall leave your name to my chosen for a curse" (v15). Their name will become a byword in curses: "May God make you like them" (compare Jer 29:22, where the names of two false prophets became a curse formula). "But his servants he will call by another name." Zion received new names in 62:2–4, 12. The Tyndale notes say a new name marks a new identity and a deeper relationship with God. JFB and Henry both read this as fulfilled when God's people from Jews and Gentiles alike came to be called "Christians" (Acts 11:26); that is their application, not something the text says outright.
  • "Shall bless himself by the God of truth" (v16). "Truth" here is literally "Amen": "the God of Amen." The lexicon confirms it. Amen means "firm, reliable, so be it." He is the God whose word always holds. Jesus calls himself "the Amen, the faithful and true witness" (Rev 3:14), and Paul says all God's promises "find their Yes in him… That is why it is through him that we utter our Amen" (2 Cor 1:20). Guzik draws on the same reading.
  • "Because the former troubles are forgotten and are hidden from my eyes" (v16). This opens the door to the next section. The sorrows of exile will be over so completely that they fade from memory.
  • Commentators: Henry reads the whole chapter as "the rejection of the Jews, and the calling in of the Gentiles," with "the saving of a remnant of them by bringing them into the gospel church." Guzik follows Paul in applying v1 to the Gentiles and stresses that refusing God's call when he plainly speaks is worse than sinning out of weakness. He cites the reading of v8 as a cluster with good juice that the vinedresser values, as JFB does. JFB alone notes the brick altars broke the law's command for unhewn stone. Guzik and the Tyndale notes both identify Fortune and Destiny as gods of luck, and the Tyndale notes add the wordplay of "destine."

New Heavens and a New Earth (vv17–25)

  • "For behold, I create new heavens and a new earth" (v17). "Create" is bara, the verb of Genesis 1:1, used in the Bible only with God as subject. God who made the first world will make a new one. This verse and 66:22 are the source of the phrase "new heavens and a new earth" in 2 Peter 3:13 and Revelation 21:1. Paul already applies it to believers now: "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (2 Cor 5:17; Gal 6:15), as the Tyndale notes point out.
  • "The former things shall not be remembered or come into mind" (v17). Not that God's people will have holes in their memory, but that the old griefs will no longer weigh on them or hurt. Revelation 21:4: "He will wipe away every tear from their eyes… for the former things have passed away." Paul seems to echo "come into mind" in 1 Corinthians 2:9.
  • "I create Jerusalem to be a joy, and her people to be a gladness" (v18). Bara again, three times in two verses. Jerusalem itself is part of the new creation. The Tyndale notes take this Jerusalem as a picture of God's eternal kingdom, the new Jerusalem of Revelation 21:2.
  • "I will rejoice in Jerusalem and be glad in my people" (v19). God's own joy is the center (62:5; Zeph 3:17). "No more shall be heard in it the sound of weeping and the cry of distress." Revelation 21:4 quotes this almost directly: "neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore."
  • "No more… an infant who lives but a few days" (v20). In the ancient world many children died in their first years. The promise is that this grief ends.
  • "The young man shall die a hundred years old, and the sinner a hundred years old shall be accursed" (v20). This is the puzzle of the passage. If this is the new creation, why is there still death, and still sinners? Faithful Christians read it three main ways. Premillennial readers, including Guzik and in part JFB, see vv20–25 as Christ's thousand-year reign on earth (Rev 20:1–6), when life is vastly longer and better but death and sin have not yet been fully removed. JFB says "sin and death shall have place in it," though rarely. Guzik holds that Isaiah moves without warning from the eternal state (v17) to the millennium (v20). Amillennial and many other readers take the whole passage as a picture of the final new creation, described in the language Isaiah's hearers could grasp: dying at a hundred would be thought of as dying young, because life will be so long it is endless. Henry reads it spiritually of the gospel age, where quality of life counts more than length: "the child shall be reckoned to die a hundred years old." What all agree on is that the passage promises a world where the curse is rolled back on a scale never seen before.
  • "They shall build houses and inhabit them; they shall plant vineyards and eat their fruit" (vv21–22). This reverses the covenant curse of Deuteronomy 28:30: "You shall build a house, but you shall not dwell in it. You shall plant a vineyard, but you shall not enjoy its fruit." The new creation is not ghostly. It has houses, vineyards and meaningful work.
  • "Like the days of a tree shall the days of my people be" (v22). Some trees in the land, such as olives and terebinths, live for many centuries. "My chosen shall long enjoy the work of their hands." Work was cursed after the fall, with thorns and sweat (Gen 3:17–19). Here work is a joy again.
  • "They shall not labor in vain or bear children for calamity" (v23). Paul may echo the first phrase: "your labor is not in vain in the Lord" (1 Cor 15:58). Parents will not raise children only to lose them to war, famine or disease.
  • "Before they call I will answer; while they are yet speaking I will hear" (v24). This is the final reply to the prayer of 63:15–64:12. Then God seemed silent and hidden; in the new creation he answers before the words are finished. Jesus told his disciples, "your Father knows what you need before you ask him" (Matt 6:8).
  • "The wolf and the lamb shall graze together; the lion shall eat straw like the ox" (v25). This repeats, in short form, the picture of 11:6–9, where a child of David's line brings peace to all creation. Predators and prey live at peace. Even nature's violence is undone (Rom 8:19–21).
  • "And dust shall be the serpent's food" (v25). This is the only new line in the repeated picture. It recalls Genesis 3:14: "dust you shall eat all the days of your life." The Tyndale notes take the serpent as representing evil. Everything else is restored, but the serpent stays under the curse. JFB reads it as Satan's lasting humiliation (Rev 20:2, 10). "They shall not hurt or destroy in all my holy mountain." Exactly as 11:9 says.
  • Commentators: Henry reads vv17–25 as "the blessings reserved for the Christian church," fulfilled spiritually in the gospel, where reconciliation with God "gives us a new heaven," and the wolf and lamb picture enemies whose hearts God will "alter… by his grace." Guzik is firmly premillennial, placing v17 in the eternal state and vv20–25 in Christ's thousand-year reign, and notes that Revelation 20:7–9 shows not everyone in that age will be saved. JFB ties v17 to Revelation 21:1 and also reads the following verses as an earthly age where sin and death still occur, though rarely. The Tyndale notes take the passage as God's eternal kingdom and believers as already part of the new creation.

Pointing to Christ

The God who says "Here I am" to people who never called came in person in Jesus, who held out his hands all day to a resisting people and was rejected (John 1:11), and whose stretched-out hands on the cross reached the Gentiles too. He is "the Amen, the faithful and true witness" (Rev 3:14), so the God of Amen keeps every promise in him. And the new heavens and new earth begin in him: his resurrection is the first piece of the new creation, and everyone in Christ already belongs to it (2 Cor 5:17). On the day he makes all things new (Rev 21:5), the sound of weeping will not be heard again.

Reflection

  • Found by Those Who Didn't Seek Him (v.1) — God reveals Himself to a nation that wasn't even calling on His name, while His own people were provoking Him. Does grace still surprise you, or have you started assuming you already know where and how God will show up?
  • A Blessing Preserved in the Cluster (v.8) — God preserves a remnant for the sake of the good still found among them, refusing to destroy the whole for the sins of many. Could your own faithfulness be part of why God hasn't given up on a family, friendship, or community you're part of?
  • New Heavens and a New Earth (v.17) — God announces He is creating new heavens and a new earth, so complete that the former things won't even be remembered. Do you tend to hope for God to patch your current circumstances, or to trust He's capable of making something entirely new?
  • Answered Before the Call (v.24) — In this promised future, God will answer before His people even finish calling out to Him. Does that kind of intimacy shape how you wait on God now, even when His answers feel delayed?
  • The Wolf and the Lamb Together (v.25) — The vision closes with predator and prey living together in peace, with no more hurting or destruction anywhere. Do you hold onto this promise as a real future, or has waiting made it feel more like wishful thinking?
