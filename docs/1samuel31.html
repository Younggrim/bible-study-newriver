1Now the Philistines fought against Israel, and the men of Israel fled before them, and many fell slain on Mount Gilboa.

2The Philistines hotly pursued Saul and his sons, and they killed Saul’s sons Jonathan, Abinadab, and Malchishua.

9They cut off Saul’s head, stripped off his armor, and sent messengers throughout the land of the Philistines to proclaim the news in the temples of their idols and among their people.

10They put his armor in the temple of the Ashtoreths and hung his body on the wall of Beth-shan.

11When the people of Jabesh-gilead heard what the Philistines had done to Saul,

12all their men of valor set out, journeyed all night, and retrieved the bodies of Saul and his sons from the wall of Beth-shan. When they arrived at Jabesh, they burned the bodies there.

13Then they took their bones and buried them under the tamarisk tree in Jabesh, and they fasted seven days.

Summary

The Philistines overwhelm Israel's army on Mount Gilboa, and as the men of Israel flee, Saul's sons, including Jonathan, are killed in the fighting (vv. 1-2). Badly wounded by archers, Saul asks his armor-bearer to kill him rather than let him fall into enemy hands, but the armor-bearer refuses out of fear, so Saul falls on his own sword; his armor-bearer then does the same (vv. 3-6). Nearby towns are abandoned as the Philistines advance, and the next day they find Saul's body, cut off his head, display his armor in the temple of Ashtaroth, and fasten his body to the wall of Beth-shan (vv. 7-10). Hearing of this disgrace, the valiant men of Jabesh-gilead travel all night to retrieve the bodies, burn them, and bury the bones with a seven-day fast, giving their fallen king an honorable end (vv. 11-13).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Samuel (for the early chapters), Nathan, and Gad (1 Chronicles 29:29). Originally one book with 2 Samuel in the Hebrew Bible. Written approximately 1050-950 BC. The book covers the transition from the judges to the monarchy — from the birth of Samuel through the death of Saul (approximately 1105-1010 BC).
Historical Context: Chapter 31 is the tragic conclusion of 1 Samuel — the death of Saul and his sons at the Battle of Gilboa. Everything Samuel prophesied (15:28; 28:19) is fulfilled. The Philistines defeat Israel decisively; Saul's sons — including Jonathan — are killed; Saul, badly wounded by archers, falls on his own sword rather than be captured and abused by the uncircumcised (v.4). The Philistines desecrate his body: they cut off his head, strip his armor (placing it in the temple of Ashtaroth), and fasten his body to the wall of Beth-shan (vv.9-10). The chapter ends with a single act of loyalty: the men of Jabesh-gilead — whom Saul rescued in his first act as king (ch.11) — march all night, recover the bodies, and give them honorable burial (vv.11-13). This is the only kindness shown to Saul in death, and it comes from those who remembered his first and best act. The chapter is deliberately sparse and somber — no theological commentary, no divine speech, no prophetic interpretation. The narrator simply records the facts and lets them speak. Saul's story ends not with a bang but with a body on a wall — the man who was chosen for his height and appearance (9:2) now stripped, beheaded, and displayed as a trophy. The contrast with David (who at this same moment is recovering all at Ziklag and distributing gifts to Judah's elders) could not be sharper. 1 Samuel began with Hannah's prayer: "The LORD killeth, and maketh alive: he bringeth down to the grave, and bringeth up" (2:6). Chapter 31 is the fulfillment of the first half; 2 Samuel will fulfill the second.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: A battle whose outcome Samuel had already named years before it is fought, Jonathan dying alongside a father whose rebellion was never his own, a king asking his armor-bearer to finish what the archers could not before falling on his own sword, a head cut off and armor displayed in a temple exactly as Goliath's death was once displayed in Israel, and one act of loyalty from Jabesh-gilead redeeming the chapter, a debt repaid decades after the king who incurred it saved them first
The Battle of Gilboa (vv.1-6): The Philistines attack Israel at Mount Gilboa (v.1). Israel's army breaks and flees; men fall slain on the mountain. The Philistines specifically pursue Saul and his sons (v.2) — targeting the royal family. Jonathan, Abinadab, and Malchi-shua are killed (v.2). Jonathan — David's beloved friend, the faithful prince who accepted God's will — dies alongside his father. His death is one of the great tragedies of the book: a righteous man caught in the consequences of his father's rebellion. The battle presses hard against Saul; archers wound him severely (v.3). Saul asks his armor-bearer to kill him — "lest these uncircumcised come and thrust me through, and abuse me" (v.4). He fears the humiliation of capture more than death itself. The armor-bearer refuses (v.4) — too afraid to kill the king. Saul falls on his own sword (v.4). His armor-bearer follows (v.5). "So Saul died, and his three sons, and his armourbearer, and all his men, that same day together" (v.6). David's prophecy is fulfilled: "he shall descend into battle, and perish" (26:10). Samuel's prophecy is fulfilled: "tomorrow shalt thou and thy sons be with me" (28:19).
The Aftermath (vv.7-10): Israel's collapse is total: cities on both sides of the valley and beyond the Jordan are abandoned; the Philistines occupy them (v.7). The next day, the Philistines strip the dead and find Saul and his sons (v.8). They cut off Saul's head (v.9) — as David cut off Goliath's head (17:51). They strip his armor and send news throughout Philistia "to publish it in the house of their idols" (v.9) — crediting their gods for the victory. Saul's armor is placed in the temple of Ashtaroth (v.10) — a fertility goddess. His body (and his sons' bodies) are fastened to the wall of Beth-shan (v.10) — a city at the junction of the Jezreel and Jordan valleys, publicly displayed as trophies of Philistine triumph. The man who stood head and shoulders above all Israel now hangs headless on a pagan city wall.
The Men of Jabesh-gilead (vv.11-13): One final act of loyalty redeems the chapter's horror. The men of Jabesh-gilead — the city Saul rescued from Nahash the Ammonite in his first act as king (ch.11) — hear of the desecration. "All the valiant men arose, and went all night" (v.12) — a dangerous march through enemy-controlled territory. They take the bodies from Beth-shan's wall, bring them to Jabesh, burn them (unusual in Israel — perhaps to prevent further desecration), and bury the bones under a tree (v.13). They fast seven days — the full mourning period. Gratitude remembered across decades: Saul saved them at the beginning of his reign; they honor him at the end. This is the only dignity Saul receives in death — and it comes from those who remembered his best moment.

Setting: About 1010 BC. The Philistines have gathered at Aphek and marched north into the Valley of Jezreel, the broad, flat plain that cuts across northern Israel (29:1, 11). Saul's army is camped on Mount Gilboa, the ridge at the valley's southeastern edge (28:4). The night before, Saul went in disguise to a medium at En-dor and heard Samuel say, "tomorrow you and your sons shall be with me" (28:19). Meanwhile, far to the south, David has been sent home by the Philistine commanders and has rescued his family from the Amalekites (chs 29–30). The two stories run side by side on purpose: David wins in the south while Saul dies in the north. This short chapter ends the book. It is the end of Israel's first king, and the end of the reign that began with such promise in chapters 9–11.

The Death of Saul and His Sons (vv1–7)

  • "The men of Israel fled before the Philistines and fell slain on Mount Gilboa" (v1). The book does not describe the battle, only the collapse. The Philistines had chariots (2 Sam 1:6), which were deadly on the level valley floor. Israel fell back up the slopes of Gilboa and was cut down there. Samuel's word from the night before comes true exactly: this is "tomorrow."
  • "Jonathan and Abinadab and Malchi-shua" (v2). Three of Saul's sons die together. Jonathan, the man who loved David "as his own soul" (18:1) and who knew David would be king (23:17), dies fighting beside the father who had tried to kill him (20:33). He stayed loyal to both of them to the end. One son, Ish-bosheth (called Eshbaal in 1 Chr 8:33), was not in the battle, and he will become a rival king in 2 Samuel 2.
  • "The archers found him, and he was badly wounded" (v3). "Badly wounded" may also be read "he was terrified" or "he writhed in pain," since the Hebrew verb can mean both to tremble and to be in anguish. Either way, Saul is cornered, hurt and alone.
  • "Lest these uncircumcised come and thrust me through, and mistreat me" (v4). "Uncircumcised" was the standard Israelite insult for the Philistines (14:6; 17:26). "Mistreat" is the Hebrew hitʿallel, to deal ruthlessly with someone or make sport of them. It is used of the Levite's concubine abused at Gibeah, Saul's own hometown (Judg 19:25), and of God's dealing with Egypt (Exod 10:2). There is a bitter twist here. The Philistine priests themselves once used this very word to warn their people about Israel's God, who "had dealt severely" with Egypt (6:6). Now the Philistines will make sport of Israel's king. Saul remembered what they did to Samson (Judg 16:21–25), and he feared the same.
  • "His armor-bearer would not, for he feared greatly" (v4). An armor-bearer was a king's closest attendant, the one who guarded him in battle. David once held that post for Saul (16:21). The man would not raise his hand against the king, which is the same reverence for "the LORD's anointed" that David showed twice (24:6; 26:9).
  • "Saul took his own sword and fell upon it" (v4). A wounded leader asking to be killed so as not to die in shame echoes Abimelech, the would-be king of Judges 9:54. Saul began well, as a humble man hiding among the baggage (10:22). He ends as a man who chooses his own death rather than trust God with it.
  • Was this suicide? The text simply reports it and makes no comment. Readers have answered differently. Some see a plain suicide, the last act of a man who had stopped seeking God. Others note that Saul was already mortally wounded and was choosing between two kinds of death, so they treat it as something less than suicide in the usual sense. Scripture does not tell us Saul's final state before God, and it is wise not to say more than it does. The Chronicler gives God's verdict on his life instead: Saul died "for his breach of faith," because he did not keep the LORD's word and "consulted a medium… He did not seek guidance from the LORD" (1 Chr 10:13–14).
  • A different account in 2 Samuel 1. In the next chapter an Amalekite tells David that he found Saul leaning on his spear and killed him at Saul's request (2 Sam 1:6–10). The narrator here gives the true account. Most readers take the Amalekite's story as a lie told to win David's favor, and he brings Saul's crown and armlet as proof. A minority think Saul survived his fall on the sword and the Amalekite finished him. The narrator's version is the one this book wants us to trust.
  • "The armor-bearer… also fell upon his sword and died with him" (v5). Loyal to the last, he will not outlive his master.
  • "Thus Saul died, and his three sons, and his armor-bearer, and all his men, on the same day together" (v6). The repeated "died" falls like a drumbeat. "All his men" probably means his personal guard and household troops, not the whole army, since Abner, his commander, survives (2 Sam 2:8). Chronicles puts it even more starkly: "all his house died together" (1 Chr 10:6). The dynasty Saul fought so hard to protect (20:31) is finished in one afternoon.
  • "They abandoned their cities and fled. And the Philistines came and lived in them" (v7). "The other side of the valley" means the Jezreel Valley north of Gilboa, and "beyond the Jordan" the towns east of the river. Israel's heartland in the north is now Philistine territory. The very thing the people wanted a king for, to "go out before us and fight our battles" (8:20), has collapsed. The nation is back where it was in the days of the judges.
  • Commentators: Henry sums up the scene in one line: "Thus fell the man that was rejected of God." He warns that no one's eternal state should be judged by the manner of death, and notes that Scripture tells us what happened to the bodies, not to the souls. Guzik, drawing on Adam Clarke, argues that Saul's death was not suicide in the usual sense because he was already dying. He also stresses that Saul, though warned the night before, showed no sign of turning to God, and he urges readers not to put off repentance. JFB passes on a Jewish tradition that the armor-bearer was Doeg the Edomite, who killed the priests at Nob (22:18), so that Saul and his servant "died by one and the same sword." That is a rabbinic guess the text does not support. Guzik and JFB both see God's hand in the death of Saul's three oldest sons, which cleared David's road to the throne. Guzik adds that it spared Jonathan from ever having to stand against his friend.

The Philistines Shame Saul's Body (vv8–10)

  • "When the Philistines came to strip the slain" (v8). After a battle the victors went over the field taking weapons, clothing and jewelry. Only then did they realize whom they had found. The book does not say who took Saul's crown, but in the next chapter the Amalekite shows up with it (2 Sam 1:10).
  • "They cut off his head" (v9). This is the reverse of David and Goliath. David cut off the Philistine champion's head and carried it to Jerusalem (17:51, 54). Now the Philistines cut off the head of Israel's king. Chronicles adds that they "fastened his head in the temple of Dagon" (1 Chr 10:10), the god who had fallen on his face before the ark of the LORD (5:1–5).
  • "To carry the good news to the house of their idols and to the people" (v9). "Carry the good news" is the Hebrew bissar, the same verb used for bringing glad tidings of victory (2 Sam 18:19) and later for the good news of God's salvation (Isa 40:9; 52:7). In the ancient Near East a war between nations was seen as a war between their gods. To the Philistines, Saul's death proved that Dagon and Ashtaroth had beaten the LORD. Readers know better: Saul fell because he had rejected the LORD, not because the LORD was weak.
  • "His armor in the temple of Ashtaroth" (v10). Ashtaroth is the plural of Ashtoreth (Astarte), the Canaanite goddess of fertility and war. Israel had put away these idols under Samuel (7:3–4). Placing a defeated king's weapons in a temple was a way of crediting the gods with the win. David had done something similar with Goliath's sword, which was kept at the LORD's sanctuary at Nob (21:9).
  • "They fastened his body to the wall of Beth-shan" (v10). Beth-shan was a strong walled city where the Jezreel Valley meets the Jordan Valley, a few miles east of Gilboa. It had been an Egyptian garrison town for centuries, and archaeologists have uncovered several temples there. 2 Samuel 21:12 says the bodies hung in "the public square of Beth-shan," likely the open area by the city gate. Hanging a body in public denied it burial and warned anyone who might resist (Josh 10:26). The law of Moses said a hanged body must not remain overnight, "for a hanged man is cursed by God" (Deut 21:22–23). Saul's body hangs in disgrace in a pagan city.
  • Commentators: JFB describes the armor being consecrated to Ashtaroth and the heads hung in Dagon's temple, joining this account with Chronicles. Guzik sees the display as more than cruelty to Saul. It was a public mockery of the God of Israel, and that is part of what made Saul's failure so costly. The Tyndale notes add that the Philistines did not know their triumph was short-lived. They were carrying out God's purpose, which would soon raise up David to defeat them.

Jabesh-gilead Honors Saul (vv11–13)

  • "When the inhabitants of Jabesh-gilead heard" (v11). This town east of the Jordan had a long history with Saul. Saul's first act as king was to rescue Jabesh from Nahash the Ammonite, who had threatened to gouge out the right eye of every man in it (ch 11). His kingship began with Jabesh, and it ends with Jabesh. They have not forgotten. There may also have been family ties, since after the slaughter of Benjamin in Judges 21 wives were taken from Jabesh for the surviving Benjaminites (Judg 21:12–14), and Saul was a Benjaminite.
  • "All the valiant men arose and went all night" (v12). Jabesh was roughly twelve miles from Beth-shan across the Jordan. The men crossed the river, went into an enemy-held city, took down five bodies in the dark and carried them home before dawn. It was a dangerous, costly act of loyalty.
  • "And burned them there" (v12). Israelites buried their dead, and cremation was not their custom. Some think the bodies were burned because they had begun to decay or had been mutilated. Others think it was done so the Philistines could never dig them up and shame them again. Some scholars suggest a ceremonial burning of spices, like the "very great fire" made at later kings' funerals (2 Chr 16:14; Jer 34:5), but the text says the bodies were burned. Either way, they kept the bones.
  • "Buried them under the tamarisk tree in Jabesh" (v13). Saul once held court "under the tamarisk tree on the height" at Gibeah (22:6). A tamarisk is a hardy shade tree of the dry country. The king who sat under a tamarisk is laid to rest under one. This was not his final grave. David later moved the bones of Saul and Jonathan to the family tomb of Kish in Benjamin (2 Sam 21:12–14).
  • "And fasted seven days" (v13). Seven days was a full period of mourning (Gen 50:10). The people of Jabesh grieved their king properly when no one else could.
  • The book ends in the dark. 1 Samuel opened with a barren woman's prayer and the birth of a prophet (ch 1). It closes with a king's bones under a tree and the Philistines holding the land. Israel asked for a king "like all the nations" (8:5, 20), and it got one, with the result we see. But the story is not over. In the south, God's chosen king is already on his way.
  • Commentators: Guzik praises the men of Jabesh for their gratitude and points ahead to David thanking them for this kindness (2 Sam 2:4–7). Henry likewise sees it as a grateful return for the help Saul once gave them, and JFB says they acted "mindful of the important and timely services Saul had rendered them." On the burning, JFB notes "this was not a Hebrew custom," and suggests it was done to remove any risk of the bodies being dug up for further insult.

Pointing to Christ

Saul's story ends with a king who sought his own honor, refused to wait on God and died in shame, his body hung on a wall and counted cursed (Deut 21:23). The Bible's true King took that curse on purpose. Jesus was also hung up in public and mocked by his enemies, but he was "made a curse for us" so that his people could be set free (Gal 3:13). The Philistines thought Saul's death was good news for their gods. Jesus' death, which looked like defeat, is the real good news, because God raised him and "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame" (Col 2:15). The men of Jabesh risked their lives to honor a fallen king who had once saved them. Believers honor a living King who saved them at the cost of his own life.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik notes the tragic irony that Jonathan — who accepted God's will and loved David — dies with his rebellious father. He emphasizes that the men of Jabesh-gilead demonstrate that even one act of kindness (Saul's rescue in ch.11) can produce lasting loyalty. He also observes that Saul's death fulfills every prophecy spoken against him.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • A Righteous Son Caught in His Father's Ruin (v.2) — Jonathan dies alongside Saul, a faithful, godly man whose life is cut short by consequences that were never his own doing. Where do you see your choices today potentially affecting people who don't deserve to carry the weight of them?
  • Fearing Humiliation More Than Death Itself (v.4) — Badly wounded, Saul chooses to fall on his own sword rather than risk being captured and mocked by his enemies. What does it reveal about a life when the fear of shame outweighs every other fear, even at the very end?
  • Enemies Crediting Their Gods for What They Didn't Earn (v.9) — The Philistines celebrate Saul's death as proof of their idols' power, completely missing that it was Saul's own disobedience that brought this about. Do you ever let the wrong explanation for a hardship take root, missing the deeper spiritual reality behind it?
  • A Body Displayed as a Trophy of Shame (v.10) — The man once chosen for his impressive height and appearance ends up headless and hung on a pagan city wall for all to see. How does this stark image of a wasted calling shape the way you think about finishing your own life well, not just starting it well?
  • Loyalty That Outlasts Decades (vv.11-13) — The men of Jabesh-gilead risk their lives marching through enemy territory to recover Saul's body, still grateful for the one great thing he did for them years before. What act of kindness have you done — or received — that quietly kept bearing fruit long after the moment itself was forgotten?
