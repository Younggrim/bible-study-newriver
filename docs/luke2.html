1Now in those days a decree went out from Caesar Augustus that a census should be taken of the whole empire.

2This was the first census to take place while Quirinius was governor of Syria.

3And everyone went to his own town to register.

4So Joseph also went up from Nazareth in Galilee to Judea, to the city of David called Bethlehem, since he was from the house and line of David.

5He went there to register with Mary, who was pledged to him in marriage and was expecting a child.

6While they were there, the time came for her Child to be born.

7And she gave birth to her firstborn, a Son. She wrapped Him in swaddling cloths and laid Him in a manger, because there was no room for them in the inn.

8And there were shepherds residing in the fields nearby, keeping watch over their flocks by night.

9Just then an angel of the Lord stood before them, and the glory of the Lord shone around them, and they were terrified.

10But the angel said to them, “Do not be afraid! For behold, I bring you good news of great joy that will be for all the people:

11Today in the city of David a Savior has been born to you. He is Christ the Lord!

12And this will be a sign to you: You will find a baby wrapped in swaddling cloths and lying in a manger.”

13And suddenly there appeared with the angel a great multitude of the heavenly host, praising God and saying:

14“Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men on whom His favor rests!”

15When the angels had left them and gone into heaven, the shepherds said to one another, “Let us go to Bethlehem and see this thing that has happened, which the Lord has made known to us.”

16So they hurried off and found Mary and Joseph and the Baby, who was lying in the manger.

17After they had seen the Child, they spread the message they had received about Him.

18And all who heard it were amazed at what the shepherds said to them.

19But Mary treasured up all these things and pondered them in her heart.

20The shepherds returned, glorifying and praising God for all they had heard and seen, which was just as the angel had told them.

21When the eight days before His circumcision had passed, He was named Jesus, the name the angel had given Him before He was conceived.

22And when the time of purification according to the Law of Moses was complete, His parents brought Him to Jerusalem to present Him to the Lord

23(as it is written in the Law of the Lord: “Every firstborn male shall be consecrated to the Lord”),

24and to offer the sacrifice specified in the Law of the Lord: “A pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons.”

25Now there was a man in Jerusalem named Simeon, who was righteous and devout. He was waiting for the consolation of Israel, and the Holy Spirit was upon him.

26The Holy Spirit had revealed to him that he would not see death before he had seen the Lord’s Christ.

27Led by the Spirit, he went into the temple courts. And when the parents brought in the child Jesus to do for Him what was customary under the Law,

28Simeon took Him in his arms and blessed God, saying:

29“Sovereign Lord, as You have promised, You now dismiss Your servant in peace.

30For my eyes have seen Your salvation,

31which You have prepared in the sight of all people,

32a light for revelation to the Gentiles, and for glory to Your people Israel.”

33The Child’s father and mother were amazed at what was spoken about Him.

34Then Simeon blessed them and said to His mother Mary: “Behold, this Child is appointed to cause the rise and fall of many in Israel, and to be a sign that will be spoken against,

35so that the thoughts of many hearts will be revealed—and a sword will pierce your soul as well.”

36There was also a prophetess named Anna, the daughter of Phanuel, of the tribe of Asher, who was well along in years. She had been married for seven years,

37and then was a widow to the age of eighty-four. She never left the temple, but worshiped night and day, fasting and praying.

38Coming forward at that moment, she gave thanks to God and spoke about the Child to all who were waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem.

39When Jesus’ parents had done everything required by the Law of the Lord, they returned to Galilee, to their own town of Nazareth.

40And the Child grew and became strong. He was filled with wisdom, and the grace of God was upon Him.

41Every year His parents went to Jerusalem for the Feast of the Passover.

42And when He was twelve years old, they went up according to the custom of the Feast.

43When those days were over and they were returning home, the boy Jesus remained behind in Jerusalem, but His parents were unaware He had stayed.

44Assuming He was in their company, they traveled on for a day before they began to look for Him among their relatives and friends.

45When they could not find Him, they returned to Jerusalem to search for Him.

46Finally, after three days they found Him in the temple courts, sitting among the teachers, listening to them and asking them questions.

47And all who heard Him were astounded at His understanding and His answers.

48When His parents saw Him, they were astonished. “Child, why have You done this to us?” His mother asked. “Your father and I have been anxiously searching for You.”

49“Why were you looking for Me?” He asked. “Did you not know that I had to be in My Father’s house?”

50But they did not understand the statement He was making to them.

51Then He went down to Nazareth with them and was obedient to them. But His mother treasured up all these things in her heart.

52And Jesus grew in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and man.

1And it came to pass in those days, that there went out a decree from Caesar Augustus, that all the world should be taxed.

2(And this taxing was first made when Cyrenius was governor of Syria.)

3And all went to be taxed, every one into his own city.

4And Joseph also went up from Galilee, out of the city of Nazareth, into Judaea, unto the city of David, which is called Bethlehem; (because he was of the house and lineage of David:)

5To be taxed with Mary his espoused wife, being great with child.

6And so it was, that, while they were there, the days were accomplished that she should be delivered.

7And she brought forth her firstborn son, and wrapped him in swaddling clothes, and laid him in a manger; because there was no room for them in the inn.

8And there were in the same country shepherds abiding in the field, keeping watch over their flock by night.

9And, lo, the angel of the Lord came upon them, and the glory of the Lord shone round about them: and they were sore afraid.

10And the angel said unto them, Fear not: for, behold, I bring you good tidings of great joy, which shall be to all people.

11For unto you is born this day in the city of David a Saviour, which is Christ the Lord.

12And this shall be a sign unto you; Ye shall find the babe wrapped in swaddling clothes, lying in a manger.

13And suddenly there was with the angel a multitude of the heavenly host praising God, and saying,

14Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, good will toward men.

15And it came to pass, as the angels were gone away from them into heaven, the shepherds said one to another, Let us now go even unto Bethlehem, and see this thing which is come to pass, which the Lord hath made known unto us.

16And they came with haste, and found Mary, and Joseph, and the babe lying in a manger.

17And when they had seen it, they made known abroad the saying which was told them concerning this child.

18And all they that heard it wondered at those things which were told them by the shepherds.

19But Mary kept all these things, and pondered them in her heart.

20And the shepherds returned, glorifying and praising God for all the things that they had heard and seen, as it was told unto them.

21And when eight days were accomplished for the circumcising of the child, his name was called JESUS, which was so named of the angel before he was conceived in the womb.

22And when the days of her purification according to the law of Moses were accomplished, they brought him to Jerusalem, to present him to the Lord;

23(As it is written in the law of the Lord, Every male that openeth the womb shall be called holy to the Lord;)

24And to offer a sacrifice according to that which is said in the law of the Lord, A pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons.

25And, behold, there was a man in Jerusalem, whose name was Simeon; and the same man was just and devout, waiting for the consolation of Israel: and the Holy Ghost was upon him.

26And it was revealed unto him by the Holy Ghost, that he should not see death, before he had seen the Lord’s Christ.

27And he came by the Spirit into the temple: and when the parents brought in the child Jesus, to do for him after the custom of the law,

28Then took he him up in his arms, and blessed God, and said,

29Lord, now lettest thou thy servant depart in peace, according to thy word:

30For mine eyes have seen thy salvation,

31Which thou hast prepared before the face of all people;

32A light to lighten the Gentiles, and the glory of thy people Israel.

33And Joseph and his mother marvelled at those things which were spoken of him.

34And Simeon blessed them, and said unto Mary his mother, Behold, this child is set for the fall and rising again of many in Israel; and for a sign which shall be spoken against;

35(Yea, a sword shall pierce through thy own soul also,) that the thoughts of many hearts may be revealed.

36And there was one Anna, a prophetess, the daughter of Phanuel, of the tribe of Aser: she was of a great age, and had lived with an husband seven years from her virginity;

37And she was a widow of about fourscore and four years, which departed not from the temple, but served God with fastings and prayers night and day.

38And she coming in that instant gave thanks likewise unto the Lord, and spake of him to all them that looked for redemption in Jerusalem.

39And when they had performed all things according to the law of the Lord, they returned into Galilee, to their own city Nazareth.

40And the child grew, and waxed strong in spirit, filled with wisdom: and the grace of God was upon him.

41Now his parents went to Jerusalem every year at the feast of the passover.

42And when he was twelve years old, they went up to Jerusalem after the custom of the feast.

43And when they had fulfilled the days, as they returned, the child Jesus tarried behind in Jerusalem; and Joseph and his mother knew not of it.

44But they, supposing him to have been in the company, went a day’s journey; and they sought him among their kinsfolk and acquaintance.

45And when they found him not, they turned back again to Jerusalem, seeking him.

46And it came to pass, that after three days they found him in the temple, sitting in the midst of the doctors, both hearing them, and asking them questions.

47And all that heard him were astonished at his understanding and answers.

48And when they saw him, they were amazed: and his mother said unto him, Son, why hast thou thus dealt with us? behold, thy father and I have sought thee sorrowing.

49And he said unto them, How is it that ye sought me? wist ye not that I must be about my Father’s business?

50And they understood not the saying which he spake unto them.

51And he went down with them, and came to Nazareth, and was subject unto them: but his mother kept all these sayings in her heart.

Summary

A census under Caesar Augustus brings Joseph and the pregnant Mary to Bethlehem, where she gives birth to Jesus and lays him in a feeding trough because there is no room at the inn (vv. 1-7). An angel announces the good news to shepherds in the fields, and a multitude of angels praises God; the shepherds go and see, then spread the word (vv. 8-20). At eight days Jesus is circumcised and named, and at the temple the devout Simeon and the prophetess Anna recognize him as the promised redemption of Israel (vv. 21-38). Twelve years later, on a family trip to Jerusalem for Passover, Jesus stays behind in the temple; his parents find him after three days, astonishing the teachers with his understanding, and he explains he had to be in his Father's house (vv. 41-49). He returns to Nazareth and grows in wisdom and favor with God and people (vv. 51-52).

Authorship & Background

Author: Luke (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 2 contains the most detailed account of Jesus' birth in the New Testament — the Christmas story. It also records the only event from Jesus' childhood preserved in Scripture (the Temple visit at age 12).
Caesar Augustus: The first Roman emperor (27 BC to AD 14), who ordered the census that brought Joseph and Mary to Bethlehem. God used a pagan emperor's decree to fulfill Micah 5:2 — the Messiah would be born in Bethlehem. The most powerful man in the world unknowingly served God's purposes.
The Shepherds: God announced the birth of His Son not to kings, priests, or scholars — but to shepherds. In first-century Palestine, shepherds were considered unclean (unable to keep Sabbath regulations) and their testimony was inadmissible in court. God chose the lowest, most marginalized people as the first witnesses — a pattern throughout Luke's Gospel.
Classification: Gospel — Narrative
Key Themes: a birth dated by an administrative decree rather than by any angel or star, a couple traveling only because a government required it and a firstborn laid in a feeding trough for lack of room, an announcement made not to kings or scholars but to shepherds working a night shift, a sign they can actually go check for themselves, ye shall find the babe wrapped in swaddling clothes, lying in a manger, an offering of two turtledoves at the purification, the very provision Leviticus allows for those who cannot afford a lamb, Simeon's prophecy naming both a light for the Gentiles and a sword through Mary's own soul, and the only glimpse of Jesus between infancy and his baptism, a twelve-year-old telling his parents he must be about his Father's business
The Decree of Caesar Augustus (vv.1-7): Luke dates the birth by an administrative act, a decree from Caesar Augustus that all the world should be taxed, and names the official responsible for the province. The whole point of the paragraph is ordinariness: a couple travelling because a government required it, arriving in a full town, and a first child born where the animals were kept. She brought forth her firstborn son, and wrapped him in swaddling clothes, and laid him in a manger, because there was no room for them in the inn. Luke gives no angels and no star here. He gives a census and a feeding trough.
The Shepherds and the Angels (vv.8-20): The announcement goes to men working a night shift, and the first thing said to them is fear not, because they were sore afraid. The message names the town and gives them a sign they can check, ye shall find the babe wrapped in swaddling clothes, lying in a manger. Then the multitude of the heavenly host, and the song, glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, good will toward men. What the shepherds do with it is practical, let us now go even unto Bethlehem, and see this thing which is come to pass, and afterwards they told everyone. Luke keeps Mary's reaction separate and quieter, she kept all these things, and pondered them in her heart.
Circumcision and Presentation (vv.21-24): Four verses of law being kept. He is circumcised on the eighth day and named as the angel instructed. Then the family goes up to Jerusalem for the purification, and Luke cites the statute twice, and the offering they bring is the one Leviticus allows for people who cannot afford a lamb, a pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons. Luke does not comment on it. The detail tells the reader what kind of household this is.
Simeon and Anna (vv.25-38): Two elderly, faithful believers who had waited their entire lives for the Messiah. Simeon's prophecy includes both joy ("a light to lighten the Gentiles") and sorrow ("a sword shall pierce through thy own soul also"). Anna, an 84-year-old widow, represents lifelong faithfulness in prayer.
Return to Nazareth (vv.39-40): Two verses closing the infancy narrative, and they are deliberately uneventful. When they had performed all things according to the law of the Lord, they returned into Galilee, to their own city Nazareth. Then a summary of about a decade in one sentence, the child grew, and waxed strong in spirit, filled with wisdom, and the grace of God was upon him. Luke is the only Gospel that says anything at all about the years between, and this is most of it.
Jesus at Twelve (vv.41-52): The only glimpse of Jesus between infancy and His baptism at age 30. His statement "I must be about my Father's business" (v.49) is the first recorded words of Jesus — revealing His awareness of His divine identity and mission even as a child.

Map & Geography

  • Bethlehem (vv.4-7): Located about 5 miles south of Jerusalem in Judea. Joseph and Mary traveled here from Nazareth (~80 miles) for the Roman census. Jesus was born here — the city of David.
  • The fields near Bethlehem (vv.8-20): Shepherds keeping watch over their flocks in the fields surrounding Bethlehem heard the angelic announcement.
  • Jerusalem / the Temple (vv.22-38): Jesus was presented at the Temple according to the Law. Simeon and Anna met the holy family here.
  • Nazareth (v.39, 51): The family returned to Nazareth in Galilee where Jesus grew up.

Setting: About 6–4 BC, near the end of Herod the Great's reign. Luke 1 told of two announcements and one birth: Gabriel promised a son to the old priest Zechariah and a Son to the young virgin Mary, and John was born in the hill country of Judea. Now Luke tells of Jesus' birth, set against the backdrop of the Roman Empire at the height of its power. Luke writes as a historian, naming emperors and governors, but his real point is how small and humble God's arrival looks next to them. The chapter moves from Bethlehem to the temple in Jerusalem, then home to Nazareth, and back to the temple twelve years later. It is the only Gospel passage about Jesus between his infancy and his baptism.

The Birth of Jesus Christ (vv1–7)

  • "A decree went out from Caesar Augustus" (v1). Octavian, Julius Caesar's adopted heir, ruled 27 BC–AD 14. The Senate gave him the title "Augustus," meaning "revered." Inscriptions called him "savior" and his birthday "the beginning of good news" for the world. Luke is about to use those same words, "good news," "Savior," "peace," for a baby in a feeding trough. The most powerful man on earth issues a decree, and without knowing it moves a carpenter's family to the town where Micah said the Messiah would be born (Mic 5:2).
  • "This was the first registration when Quirinius was governor of Syria" (v2). This is a well-known puzzle. Roman records place Quirinius as governor of Syria in AD 6, when he held a census that sparked a Jewish revolt (Acts 5:37). That is about ten years too late for Jesus' birth under Herod. Several answers have been offered. Quirinius may have held an earlier post of authority in the region (an inscription has been read that way, though it is debated). The Greek word prōtē, "first," can also mean "before," giving "this registration took place before Quirinius was governor." Or the registration began under Herod and was completed under Quirinius. Luke was careful about such details elsewhere, and he clearly knew of the later census, so it is fair to hold the question open rather than assume he blundered.
  • "Each to his own town… to the city of David, which is called Bethlehem" (vv3–4). In the Old Testament, "the city of David" usually means Jerusalem. Luke uses it for Bethlehem, David's hometown, where he was a shepherd boy (1 Sam 16:1; 17:15). Bethlehem means "house of bread." It lay about five or six miles south of Jerusalem, and the walk from Nazareth was some 90 miles. Going to one's ancestral town for a census is unusual in Roman practice, but it fits Jewish tribal custom, and a papyrus from Egypt in AD 104 orders people to return to their homes for a census.
  • "Her firstborn son" (v7). "Firstborn" carries legal weight: the firstborn son belonged to God and had to be redeemed (v23). It does not in itself say whether other children followed. Protestants usually take the brothers and sisters named in the Gospels as later children of Mary and Joseph (Mark 6:3). Catholic and Orthodox Christians understand them as cousins or Joseph's children from an earlier marriage, holding to Mary's perpetual virginity.
  • "Laid him in a manger" (v7). A manger is a feeding trough for animals, often of stone, sometimes cut into a wall. So the birth happened where animals were kept. Luke never mentions a stable. An early tradition, recorded by Justin Martyr in the second century, says it was a cave, and many Bethlehem homes were built against limestone caves used for animals.
  • "There was no place for them in the inn" (v7). The word is katalyma, a lodging or guest room. Luke uses it again for the "guest room" where Jesus ate the Last Supper (22:11), and uses a different word for a commercial inn (10:34). Many scholars now picture a village house: one main room where the family lived, with a lower area at one end where animals were brought in at night, and perhaps a guest room on the roof or at the side. The guest room was already full, so Mary gave birth in the family's main living space, and the manger served as a crib. This is less of a cold rejection than the Christmas-play innkeeper, but the point stands: he came to his own and there was no room (John 1:11).
  • Commentators: Guzik distinguishes this first registration from the AD 6 census of Acts 5:37. JFB calls v2 "a very perplexing verse," and suggests the rendering "This registration was previous to Cyrenius being governor of Syria," or else a decree made then and carried out later. On "firstborn" JFB says the law "regardeth not whether any were born after or no." Henry draws the lesson that "Christ was born at an inn; he came into the world to sojourn here for awhile," and that so humble a birth rebukes our pride.

The Shepherds and the Angels (vv8–21)

  • "Shepherds out in the field, keeping watch over their flock by night" (v8). Shepherds were poor laborers. Some later rabbinic writings treat them as unreliable, and their work kept them from strict religious observance, though the Old Testament honors shepherds, David above all, and God himself is called Israel's shepherd (Ps 23:1). Being outdoors at night suggests a warmer season, though flocks near Bethlehem could be out much of the year. The real date of Jesus' birth is unknown. December 25 was set by the church centuries later.
  • "The glory of the Lord shone around them" (v9). The glory (Hebrew kavod) is the bright, weighty presence of God that filled the tabernacle and temple (Exod 40:34; 1 Kings 8:11). Ezekiel saw it leave the temple before the exile (Ezek 10:18). Now it appears again, not in the temple but in a field, over working men.
  • "I bring you good news of great joy that will be for all the people" (v10). "Bring good news" is euangelizomai, the verb behind "gospel" and "evangelize." Isaiah used it for heralds announcing God's coming victory (Isa 40:9; 52:7). "All the people" (singular) in Luke means Israel. The good news reaches the nations soon enough (v32).
  • "Unto you is born this day in the city of David a Savior, who is Christ the Lord" (v11). Three titles in one line. "Savior" was a title Augustus' admirers gave him. "Christ" is Greek for the Hebrew mashiach, "Messiah," the anointed King from David's line. "Lord" (kyrios) is the Greek Old Testament's word for God's own name. The combination "Christ the Lord" appears nowhere else in the New Testament. "This day" is the first of Luke's "todays" of salvation (4:21; 19:9; 23:43).
  • "This will be a sign for you" (v12). The sign is not a glow or a star but an ordinary baby in an ordinary trough. No other newborn in Bethlehem that night would be lying in a manger. The shepherds, who knew all about mangers, would find him because he was in the place they felt at home.
  • "Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace among those with whom he is pleased!" (v14). Two lines in parallel: glory in heaven, peace on earth. The famous KJV wording, "good will toward men," follows later manuscripts that read eudokia ("good will"). The oldest manuscripts read eudokias, one letter longer: "men of [God's] good pleasure." The ESV follows them. The peace is not general goodwill among people but God's peace given to those on whom his favor rests. The Dead Sea Scrolls use the same Hebrew phrase, "sons of his good pleasure," for God's chosen people, which supports the reading. In Latin worship this song became the Gloria in excelsis.
  • "But Mary treasured up all these things, pondering them in her heart" (v19). "Pondering" (symballō) means putting things together, turning them over to see how they fit. The others wonder for a moment. Mary keeps thinking. Luke repeats this in v51, and many think it hints that Mary was one of his sources (1:2–3).
  • "At the end of eight days, when he was circumcised, he was called Jesus" (v21). As with John (1:59–63), Luke notes the parents' obedience to the law (Gen 17:12) and to the angel (1:31). From his first week, Jesus is "born under the law" (Gal 4:4). His name, "the LORD saves," is given before he does anything to earn it.
  • Commentators: Guzik cites scholars who note that shepherds had a poor reputation and were barred as witnesses in court, which makes them striking first witnesses to Christ. He also points out the tenses in vv18–19: the crowd's wonder was a passing reaction, while Mary's treasuring was an ongoing habit. JFB argues that flocks out at night make December "too late" for the birth, and calls peace with God the "grand necessity of a fallen world." JFB still reads the KJV text ("good will"), describing it as God's favor "on a new footing." Henry says simply that "Angels were heralds of the new-born Saviour," sent to humble men at their work.

Jesus Presented at the Temple (vv22–38)

  • "When the time came for their purification according to the Law of Moses" (v22). After a son's birth, a mother was ritually unclean for forty days and then brought an offering (Lev 12:1–8). Luke's "their" may include Joseph, who brought Mary, or treat the family as a unit. Some later manuscripts read "her" to tidy it.
  • "To present him to the Lord" (vv22–23). This is a separate law. Every firstborn son belonged to God, because God spared Israel's firstborn at the Passover (Exod 13:2, 12–15). He had to be "redeemed" by paying five shekels to a priest (Num 18:15–16). Luke does not mention the payment. Instead he says they "presented" him, the way Hannah presented Samuel to serve at the tabernacle (1 Sam 1:22–28). Jesus is not bought back. He is given.
  • "A pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons" (v24). The law asked for a lamb and a bird, but allowed two birds if the family could not afford a lamb (Lev 12:8). Mary and Joseph gave the offering of the poor. The wise men's gold (Matt 2:11) had probably not yet arrived. The Lamb of God was brought into his Father's house by parents too poor to bring a lamb.
  • Malachi fulfilled. "The Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to his temple" (Mal 3:1). He comes as a forty-day-old baby, and almost no one notices.
  • "Simeon… righteous and devout, waiting for the consolation of Israel" (v25). "Consolation" (paraklēsis, comfort) recalls Isaiah 40:1: "Comfort, comfort my people, says your God." Simeon was waiting for that promised comfort. Tradition makes him an old priest. Luke says only that he was a devout man in Jerusalem, and his words in v29 suggest he was old.
  • "The Holy Spirit was upon him" (vv25–27). The Spirit is named three times in three verses. He is upon Simeon, reveals the promise to him, and leads him into the temple at the right moment. The long silence since the prophets is over.
  • "Lord, now you are letting your servant depart in peace" (v29). This is the third song in Luke's birth story, called the Nunc Dimittis from its first Latin words ("now you dismiss"). "Lord" here is despotēs, master, and "servant" is doulos, slave. It is a servant asking leave to go off duty because the watch is over. "Depart" is a gentle word for death.
  • "My eyes have seen your salvation" (v30). Simeon looks at a baby and calls him God's salvation. The words echo Isaiah 52:10: "all the ends of the earth shall see the salvation of our God."
  • "A light for revelation to the Gentiles, and for glory to your people Israel" (v32). This is the first time in Luke that the Gentiles are named. Isaiah's Servant was to be "a light for the nations" (Isa 42:6; 49:6). Paul and Barnabas later quote Isaiah 49:6 to explain why they turned to the Gentiles (Acts 13:47). Luke's whole two-volume work, from Jerusalem to Rome, is in seed form here.
  • "Appointed for the fall and rising of many in Israel" (v34). Now the song darkens. Jesus will divide Israel. Isaiah called the LORD "a stone of offense and a rock of stumbling" (Isa 8:14), and the early church applied that to Jesus (Rom 9:33; 1 Pet 2:8). Some will fall over him. Others, the lowly, will be raised. Both may happen to the same person: falling in repentance, rising in faith.
  • "And a sword will pierce through your own soul also" (v35). The word is rhomphaia, a large broad sword. The first and most obvious fulfilment is the cross, where Mary stood watching (John 19:25). But the sword runs through her whole life with him: losing him for three days (v48), hearing him say his true family are those who do God's word (8:21), seeing him rejected. Being the Messiah's mother was a privilege and a wound.
  • "A prophetess, Anna, the daughter of Phanuel, of the tribe of Asher" (v36). Anna is the Greek form of Hannah, the name of Samuel's mother, whose song Mary echoed. Phanuel means "face of God." Asher was one of the northern tribes taken into exile by Assyria in 722 BC. Some families still knew their tribal descent centuries later, as Paul knew he was a Benjaminite (Phil 3:5).
  • "Lived with her husband seven years… and then as a widow until she was eighty-four" (vv36–37). The Greek can mean she was a widow until age eighty-four, as the ESV takes it, or a widow for eighty-four years, which would make her over a hundred. Either way she had spent most of her life as a widow, which in that world often meant poverty. She did not leave the temple, probably meaning she was there whenever it was open, worshiping "with fasting and prayer night and day."
  • "Coming up at that very hour she began to give thanks to God and to speak of him" (v38). Luke pairs a man and a woman, as he often does (Zechariah and Mary, the shepherd and the woman with the coin in ch 15). Two witnesses confirm the matter (Deut 19:15). Anna tells "all who were waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem." There was a whole circle of such people, the faithful remnant who had kept hoping.
  • Commentators: Guzik notes the poor family's offering and says Mary's calling to mother the Messiah was both an honor and a burden. He also says the word "sign" pictures a target people shoot at, which is a fair picture of being "spoken against" though not the meaning of the word itself, which is simply "sign." JFB observes that "Joseph and the Virgin were in poor circumstances," though "not in abject poverty." He sees in Simeon the Spirit returning "after a dreary absence of nearly four hundred years," and reads Mary's sword as including "dreadful alternations of faith and unbelief, of hope and fear." JFB takes the eighty-four years as widowhood, so that Anna "could not at this time be less than a hundred three years old." Henry: "How poor does this world look to one that has Christ in his arms."

The Return to Nazareth (vv39–40)

  • "When they had performed everything according to the Law" (v39). Luke stresses again that Jesus' family kept the law fully. He skips over the wise men and the flight into Egypt, which Matthew tells (Matt 2). Luke compresses; he does not contradict. The return to Nazareth he mentions may follow those events.
  • "The child grew and became strong, filled with wisdom. And the favor of God was upon him" (v40). Almost the same words were used of John (1:80) and of the boy Samuel (1 Sam 2:26). Jesus grew up normally. The Son of God learned to walk, to speak, to read. "Favor" is charis, grace.
  • Commentators: Henry draws from Jesus' circumcision and presentation the call for believers to "present our children to the Lord who gave them to us." Guzik stresses that Jesus kept the law perfectly from infancy.

The Boy Jesus in the Temple (vv41–52)

  • "His parents went to Jerusalem every year at the Feast of the Passover" (v41). The law required men to attend three feasts a year (Deut 16:16). Many who lived far away came only for Passover. That Mary went too shows unusual devotion.
  • "The boy Jesus stayed behind in Jerusalem. His parents did not know it" (v43). Pilgrims traveled in large groups of relatives and neighbors from the same villages. A twelve-year-old would move freely among them, and each parent may have assumed he was with the other. It was a day before they realized.
  • "After three days they found him in the temple" (v46). Probably a day out, a day back and a day searching. Some readers see a hint of the three days between his death and resurrection, when again his followers searched for him (24:5–7). That is a possible echo, not Luke's stated point.
  • "Sitting among the teachers, listening to them and asking them questions" (v46). Rabbis taught by questions and answers. Jesus is not lecturing his elders. He is learning, as a pupil sits at a teacher's feet, but his questions and answers astonish them (v47). The same temple teachers will question him again twenty years later, trying to trap him (20:1–40).
  • "Son, why have you treated us so? Behold, your father and I have been searching for you in great distress" (v48). "Distress" (odynaō) is a strong word for anguish. Luke uses it for the rich man in torment (16:24). Mary's sword (v35) has begun to cut.
  • "Did you not know that I must be in my Father's house?" (v49). These are Jesus' first recorded words. Mary said "your father." Jesus answers "my Father," meaning God. The Greek is literally "in the things of my Father," which can mean "in my Father's house" (ESV) or "about my Father's business" (KJV). Since they found him in the temple, the ESV's reading fits the scene well, and both are true. "I must" (dei) is a key word in Luke for the divine necessity that drives Jesus' life: "I must preach" (4:43), "the Son of Man must suffer" (9:22), "everything written about me… must be fulfilled" (24:44). At twelve he already knows who his Father is.
  • "He went down with them… and was submissive to them" (v51). The one who called God his Father went home and obeyed his human parents for nearly twenty more years. "Submissive" is an ongoing tense: he kept on being subject to them. He kept the fifth commandment perfectly. This is the last time Joseph appears in the Gospels, and many think he died before Jesus' ministry began.
  • "Jesus increased in wisdom and in stature and in favor with God and man" (v52). Wisdom (mind), stature (body, or possibly age), favor with God and with people. Jesus grew as a whole person. This verse matters for the doctrine of the incarnation. Jesus was fully God, but he truly became human, and real humans grow and learn. Hebrews says he "learned obedience through what he suffered" (Heb 5:8). The words echo the boy Samuel again: "the boy Samuel continued to grow both in stature and in favor with the LORD and also with man" (1 Sam 2:26).
  • Commentators: JFB notes that at twelve a boy was counted a "son of the law," and that the temple was where Jesus felt "at home, breathing His own proper air." He allows that v49 can mean either "about My Father's affairs" or "in My Father's courts." Guzik connects twelve with the age a boy began learning a trade and sees in v49 Jesus' growing awareness of his unique relationship with God. Henry stresses that though he was the Son of God, Jesus "was subject to his earthly parents," an example for children, and that young people should early "seek the knowledge of Divine truth." JFB calls the next eighteen silent years a deliberate "pattern of subjection."

Pointing to Christ

Caesar issued a decree, and God brought his Son into the world in a borrowed space, laid in a feeding trough, announced to shepherds. The true Savior, Lord and bringer of peace was not the man in Rome. He was presented in the temple with the offering of the poor, recognized by an old man and an old widow who had waited all their lives, and at twelve he already knew his Father's house. Simeon saw it clearly: this child is salvation for the nations and the glory of Israel, and also a sword and a sign spoken against. He would grow up and go to the cross, so that all who sit in darkness can see the light.

Reflection

  • Sovereignty Behind a Census (vv.1-4) — A Roman emperor's bureaucratic decree unknowingly positioned Joseph and Mary in Bethlehem to fulfill prophecy. Where in your life might God be working through ordinary, even unwelcome, circumstances toward a purpose you can't yet see?
  • No Room for the King (v.7) — The Savior of the world was born in a stable because there was no room for Him elsewhere. What in your schedule, priorities, or attention currently crowds out room for Christ?
  • Good News to the Overlooked (vv.8-16) — God sent His announcement first to shepherds, among the least regarded people in that society, and they went with haste to see for themselves. Who around you might God be reaching in a way you'd overlook, and how quickly do you respond when He reveals something to you?
  • Treasuring What You Don't Understand (v.19) — Mary didn't have every answer, but she held onto these events and reflected on them rather than dismissing them. How do you handle the parts of your faith or your life you can't fully explain?
  • A Lifetime of Faithful Waiting (vv.25-35) — Simeon spent his life waiting to see the Messiah, and when he finally did, he blessed God while also warning Mary that this same child would bring both joy and sorrow. What are you still waiting on God for, and are you prepared for faith to bring pain as well as joy?
  • About the Father's Business (vv.49-52) — At twelve, Jesus already understood His purpose, and He continued growing in wisdom, stature, and favor with God and people. Do you have a clear sense of what God has called you to, and are you still growing toward it?
