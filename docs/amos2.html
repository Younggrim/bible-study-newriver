Summary

The judgment oracles continue against Moab, condemned for desecrating the bones of Edom's king (vv. 1-3), and against Judah for rejecting the LORD's law (vv. 4-5). The cycle then closes on Israel itself: sellers trade the righteous for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals, and trample the poor into the dust (vv. 6-8). God recalls how He destroyed the Amorites before them and brought Israel out of Egypt, raising up prophets and Nazirites, only to have Israel silence the prophets and corrupt the Nazirites with wine (vv. 9-12). As a result, the LORD declares that not even the swiftest or strongest will escape His judgment on that day (vv. 13-16).

Authorship & Background

Author: Amos, a shepherd from Tekoa in Judah who prophesied to the northern kingdom (approximately 760-750 BC). Key theme: social justice — God judges nations (including Israel) for oppressing the poor. "Let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream" (5:24).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — The Trap Springs: Israel Indicted
Key Themes: Judgment on Moab and Judah, the climactic indictment of Israel, selling the poor, oppressing the afflicted, silencing prophets, ingratitude for divine deliverance, inescapable judgment
Historical Context: Chapter 2 completes the spiral begun in chapter 1. After Moab (v.1) and Judah (v.4), the hammer falls on Israel (v.6) — the ultimate target. The audience that cheered judgment on their enemies is now indicted themselves. Israel's sins are specifically social: selling the righteous for silver, trampling the poor, sexual immorality, exploitation through pledge-taking. God then recounts His saving acts (Exodus, wilderness, Amorite conquest, raising prophets/Nazirites) — making Israel's ingratitude inexcusable. The chapter ends with terrifying inevitability: no one escapes. Not the swift, not the strong, not the mighty, not the horseman.
Moab (vv.1-3): The sixth foreign oracle, and the charge is unexpected: burning the bones of the king of Edom into lime. Desecrating a corpse, and the corpse of a third party at that — Edom is not Israel's friend. God is presented as holding nations to account for cruelty that had nothing to do with his own people, which widens the argument considerably.
Judah (vv.4-5): The seventh oracle crosses into the south, and the charge changes character. Where the nations were indicted for atrocities, Judah is indicted for despising the law of the LORD and not keeping his commandments. More is expected where more was given. The northern audience could still applaud this one, since Judah was a rival.
The Trap Closes: Israel (vv.6-8): The eighth oracle is the one the whole sequence was built for, and now the geography has walked all the way round to the listeners' own doorstep. The charges are economic and social: selling the righteous for silver and the poor for a pair of shoes, panting after the dust of the poor, a father and son going in to the same girl, lying on garments taken in pledge beside the altar. The last detail matters — the exploitation is happening in the sanctuary, using pledged clothing the law required be returned by nightfall.
I Brought You Up Out of Egypt (vv.9-12): The indictment turns to history, and every item is something God did for them: destroying the Amorite, bringing them up from Egypt, leading them forty years, raising up prophets and Nazirites. Then what they did with it — they gave the Nazirites wine to drink and told the prophets not to prophesy. The two gifts named are the two they neutralized.
No One Escapes (vv.13-16): The chapter closes with a list of people who would normally survive a rout and will not: the swift, the strong, the mighty, the archer, the horseman, the courageous. Each is named with the thing that usually saves them and told it will fail. The image in v.13, pressed like a cart full of sheaves, is agricultural rather than military, which is where Amos keeps returning.

Deep Dive

Setting: Amos, a sheep-breeder from Tekoa in Judah, is preaching in the northern kingdom of Israel around 760 BC, probably at Bethel, the royal shrine (7:13). Israel is rich, secure and religious under Jeroboam II. Chapter 1 began a sermon of judgment on the nations around Israel, each introduced with the same formula: "For three transgressions… and for four, I will not revoke the punishment." Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom and Ammon have each been condemned for cruelty, slave trading and betrayal, and a crowd at Bethel would have cheered every one. Chapter 2 adds Moab, then turns to Judah, Israel's brother kingdom in the south, and then, unexpectedly, to Israel itself. This is the trap of the sermon, much like the prophet Nathan's story to David: "You are the man" (2 Sam 12:7). The Tyndale notes make that comparison.

Judgment on Israel's Neighbors (vv1–3)

  • "Because he burned to lime the bones of the king of Edom" (v1). Moab lay east of the Dead Sea, and like Ammon it descended from Lot (Gen 19:37). Its crime is strange to modern ears: burning a dead king's bones until they turned to powdered lime. In the ancient world, a proper burial mattered enormously, and destroying a body was a way to destroy a person's memory and, many believed, rob him of rest in the afterlife. The Tyndale notes add that if the lime was used in plaster, the insult was even worse. JFB thinks the king may even have been burned alive. Guzik calls it a sin against the past, as Ammon's was a sin against the future.
  • Not against Israel. Every earlier crime touched Israel or Judah. This one does not. Moab's victim was Edom, Israel's enemy, and God condemns it anyway. His justice is not just loyalty to his own people. He cares about wrongs done to anyone, even the dead. The event itself is not recorded elsewhere. Some connect it with the war in 2 Kings 3, where Israel, Judah and Edom fought Moab, and the king of Moab tried to break through to the king of Edom (2 Kings 3:26–27), but this is a guess.
  • "The strongholds of Kerioth" (v2). Kerioth was a major town of Moab (Jer 48:24). The Moabite Stone, an inscription of King Mesha found in 1868, mentions Kerioth as a shrine of Chemosh, Moab's god. The Tyndale notes suggest the bones may even have been burned there. "Moab shall die amid uproar, amid shouting and the sound of the trumpet." The trumpet is the shophar, the ram's-horn war signal. Moab will fall in the noise of battle.
  • "I will cut off the ruler from its midst" (v3). "Ruler" is literally "judge" (shophet). JFB suggests Moab may have been ruled by a chief magistrate at the time. The Tyndale notes connect fulfillment with Assyrian invasions late in the eighth century, and later with Babylon (Isa 15–16; Jer 48).
  • Commentators: Henry says God has "like controversy with Moab as before with other nations." Guzik sees the desecration of a dead man as a crime God takes seriously, even when nobody else would. JFB reads "die with tumult" as the din of battle.

Judgment on Judah (vv4–5)

  • "Because they have rejected the law of the LORD" (v4). Now Amos turns to his own country, and the charge changes. The nations were judged for crimes against human beings. Judah is judged for rejecting God's torah, his teaching or instruction. The Tyndale notes say God holds people accountable according to what they have been given (3:2; Luke 12:48). Judah had the law, the temple and the line of David, and despised them. A crowd in Israel, which resented Judah's kings (1 Kings 12:16), might have enjoyed this one most of all.
  • "Have not kept his statutes" (v4). "Statutes" (chuqqim) are the fixed decrees of God, things engraved, so to speak. To reject the torah is to stop listening. Not keeping the statutes is the result.
  • "But their lies have led them astray, those after which their fathers walked" (v4). "Lies" (kezabim) here means false gods and false teaching. JFB: "lying idols." What replaces God's truth is never nothing. People who reject it follow lies, and the lies lead them. And they are old lies, handed down from "their fathers." Guzik notes that rejecting truth leaves room for falsehood. Paul says much the same in Romans 1:25.
  • "So I will send a fire upon Judah… Jerusalem" (v5). The same drumbeat of fire that fell on the pagan cities falls on Jerusalem, the city of the temple from which the LORD roars (1:2). Being God's people gave Judah no immunity. The fire came in 586 BC, when the Babylonians burned Jerusalem (2 Kings 25:9). JFB names Nebuchadnezzar.
  • Commentators: Henry says God "shows what quarrel he had with Judah." JFB stresses that Judah's guilt was greater because it was directly against God. Guzik notes how easy it is to condemn outsiders, and draws a gentler application: God's fire also purifies those who are his (1 Cor 3:13–15; 1 Pet 1:7).

Judgment on Israel (vv6–16)

  • "For three transgressions of Israel, and for four" (v6). The eighth oracle, the one nobody at Bethel expected. Seven nations would feel like a complete set. Now the full force of the sermon lands. And this oracle is far longer than the others, with not one sin named but a list.
  • "Because they sell the righteous for silver" (v6). The "righteous" here means the innocent party in a lawsuit, people in the right, and in context they are poor. The Tyndale notes say "righteous needy," faithful people who had fallen into debt. The law of Moses allowed someone who couldn't repay to work off a debt as a servant, but with strict protections, and fellow Israelites were not to be treated as slaves (Lev 25:39–43; Deut 15:7–15). Now people were being sold into bondage, or judges were selling verdicts for a bribe. JFB takes it as judges taking bribes to condemn the righteous.
  • "And the needy for a pair of sandals" (v6). A pair of sandals was cheap. The Tyndale notes say the debts were trivial, or that sandals were used to seal the deal (Ruth 4:7). JFB thinks a cheap bribe. Amos repeats the phrase in 8:6. Either way, a human being was worth less than footwear.
  • "Those who trample the head of the poor into the dust of the earth" (v7). The verb sha'aph can mean "to pant after" (so the KJV: "pant after the dust of the earth on the head of the poor") or "to trample, crush." The KJV picture is of men so greedy they even want the dust the poor have thrown on their heads in mourning. The ESV follows the reading most modern scholars prefer: they grind the poor face down into the dirt. "Turn aside the way of the afflicted" means they push the humble off the road, or twist their legal case (Exod 23:6).
  • "A man and his father go in to the same girl, so that my holy name is profaned" (v7). The word for girl, na'arah, means a young woman, often a servant girl. One view, held by JFB and the Tyndale notes, is that she was a cult prostitute at a shrine, since fertility worship included sacred sex and v8 sets the scene at the altars (Hos 4:13–14). Another view is that she was a household servant used by both father and son, which the law forbade and which made her powerless against two masters (Lev 18:7–8, 15; Exod 21:7–11). Either way, the strong use the weak, and God says it "profanes" his name. The people who carried his name were making it look common and worthless.
  • "They lay themselves down beside every altar on garments taken in pledge" (v8). The law said that if you took a poor man's cloak as security for a loan, you had to give it back before sunset, because it was his blanket (Exod 22:26–27; Deut 24:12–13). These men kept the cloaks and lounged on them at religious feasts. The Tyndale notes call it a sharp irony: they showed off their sins at worship.
  • "In the house of their God they drink the wine of those who have been fined" (v8). They fined the poor through corrupt courts, then used the proceeds to buy wine for religious parties. JFB: wine bought with fines from the unjustly condemned. Note "their God," not "my house." Amos refuses to call Bethel God's house.
  • "Yet it was I who destroyed the Amorite before them" (v9). In Hebrew the "I" is emphatic. Now God reminds Israel of what he had done for them, and Henry says these mercies make their sins worse. "Amorite" here stands for all the peoples of Canaan (Gen 15:16). "Whose height was like the height of the cedars… as strong as the oaks." This recalls the spies' report of giants in the land (Num 13:32–33). Henry: "not only tall, but well-set." "I destroyed his fruit above and his roots beneath" is a picture of a tree destroyed entirely. JFB: utterly destroyed.
  • "I who brought you up out of the land of Egypt and led you forty years in the wilderness" (v10). Notice the order. Amos mentions the conquest first and then the exodus, as if remembering backward. The exodus is the founding act of Israel's story, and God's whole claim on them rests on it (Exod 20:2). Henry: he "rescued thee out of the land of Egypt, where thou wouldst otherwise have perished in slavery." The irony is that a people rescued from slavery were now selling their own poor into it.
  • "I raised up some of your sons for prophets, and some of your young men for Nazirites" (v11). God had not only rescued them. He had kept speaking to them and kept giving them living examples of devotion. A Nazirite took a vow, for a period or for life, to be set apart to God: no wine or grape products, no haircut, no touching the dead (Num 6:1–21). Samson and Samuel were lifelong Nazirites (Judg 13:5; 1 Sam 1:11). Henry calls them men who "bound themselves by a vow to God and his service." "Is it not indeed so, O people of Israel?" God calls them as witnesses.
  • "But you made the Nazirites drink wine, and commanded the prophets… 'You shall not prophesy'" (v12). They pressured the devoted to break their vows and silenced the preachers. Amos will soon experience this himself, when Amaziah the priest tells him to go home and stop prophesying at Bethel (7:12–13, 16). JFB points to that. Henry: "Those have a great deal to answer for that cannot bear faithful preaching, and those much more that suppress it."
  • "Behold, I will press you down in your place, as a cart full of sheaves presses down" (v13). The Hebrew verb is rare and uncertain. The ESV takes it as God pressing Israel down, like the ground sagging under a heavily loaded harvest cart. The KJV, following an older reading, has "I am pressed under you, as a cart is pressed that is full of sheaves," with God as the one weighed down by their sins, which Henry and Guzik follow. Some modern scholars think the word means "to split" or "to make totter," a picture of the ground cracking beneath them, which would point ahead to the earthquake (1:1). The image comes from harvest, as Amos the farmer would know. Either way, the weight of their sin is about to crush them.
  • "Flight shall perish from the swift" (vv14–16). Seven kinds of soldiers are named: the swift, the strong, the mighty warrior, the archer, the fast runner, the horseman, and the bravest of the brave. Not one will escape. The Tyndale notes explain that archers stood at the back, so if they fled, the front lines had already fallen. Every source of military confidence fails. "Shall flee away naked in that day" means throwing off armor and even clothes to run faster. JFB: they could save only their lives. "That day" is what Amos later calls "the day of the LORD" (5:18).
  • How it was fulfilled. About forty years later Assyria swept over Israel. In 722 BC Samaria fell, and its people were deported (2 Kings 17:5–23). The writer of Kings lists the same sins Amos named: idolatry, rejecting the prophets and refusing to listen.
  • Commentators: Henry outlines Israel's sins as injustice, oppression and immorality, aggravated by "the temporal and spiritual mercies God had bestowed upon them." On v14 he warns, "Let not the strong man then glory in his strength, nor trust in it." JFB explains the sandals as a trifling bribe and the wine as bought with unjust fines. On v13 he mentions Calvin's view that God is pressed under them, and notes that Amos's rustic images fit a shepherd. Guzik follows the KJV reading of v13, that sin burdens God, and applies vv14–16 with 1 Corinthians 10:12: let anyone who thinks he stands take heed lest he fall. All three see the list of God's mercies in vv9–11 as what makes Israel's guilt worse than the nations'.

Pointing to Christ

Amos's sermon catches everyone. The cruel nations stand condemned, Judah with the law stands condemned, and Israel, rescued from Egypt and given prophets, stands condemned most of all. Paul makes the same argument at the start of Romans: Gentiles without the law and Jews with it are all "under sin" (Rom 3:9). The answer did not come from a stronger soldier or a faster runner (vv14–16). It came from the righteous one who was sold for silver (Matt 26:15), who took the place of the poor and the trampled, and who was stripped and led away (Mark 15:24). He was wholly set apart to God, as the Nazirites were meant to picture, and he was the true prophet, whom they also tried to silence. In him the God who brought Israel out of Egypt brings people out of every nation named here into a better land.

Reflection

  • Selling People for the Price of Shoes (v.6) — Israel is condemned for selling the needy for the price of a pair of sandals, treating vulnerable people as disposable for trivial gain. How do you treat people who have nothing to offer you in return?
  • Greater Privilege, Greater Accountability (vv.9-12) — Before listing Israel's sins, God recounts everything He did for them — deliverance, provision, victory — making their ingratitude the real indictment. What has God given you that raises your responsibility rather than just your comfort?
  • Silencing the Prophets (v.12) — Israel commanded God's prophets not to speak, refusing correction rather than receiving it. When someone speaks a hard truth into your life, is your instinct to listen or to shut them down?
  • No Strength Escapes Judgment (v.16) — Even the most courageous warrior will flee naked on the day of judgment, showing that no human ability can shield anyone from God's reckoning. What are you quietly trusting to protect you that won't hold up when it matters most?
