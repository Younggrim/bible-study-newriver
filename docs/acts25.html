1Three days after his arrival in the province, Festus went up from Caesarea to Jerusalem,

2where the chief priests and Jewish leaders presented their case against Paul. They urged Festus

3to grant them a concession against Paul by summoning him to Jerusalem, because they were preparing an ambush to kill him along the way.

4But Festus replied, “Paul is being held in Caesarea, and I myself am going there soon.

5So if this man has done anything wrong, let some of your leaders come down with me and accuse him there.”

6After spending no more than eight or ten days with them, Festus went down to Caesarea. The next day he sat on the judgment seat and ordered that Paul be brought in.

7When Paul arrived, the Jews who had come down from Jerusalem stood around him, bringing many serious charges that they could not prove.

8Then Paul made his defense: “I have committed no offense against the law of the Jews or against the temple or against Caesar.”

9But Festus, wishing to do the Jews a favor, said to Paul, “Are you willing to go up to Jerusalem to stand trial before me on these charges?”

10Paul replied, “I am standing before the judgment seat of Caesar, where I ought to be tried. I have done nothing wrong to the Jews, as you yourself know very well.

11If, however, I am guilty of anything worthy of death, I do not refuse to die. But if there is no truth to their accusations against me, no one has the right to hand me over to them. I appeal to Caesar!”

12Then Festus conferred with his council and replied, “You have appealed to Caesar. To Caesar you will go!”

13After several days had passed, King Agrippa and Bernice came down to Caesarea to pay their respects to Festus.

14Since they were staying several days, Festus laid out Paul’s case before the king: “There is a certain man whom Felix left in prison.

15While I was in Jerusalem, the chief priests and elders of the Jews presented their case and requested a judgment against him.

16I told them that it is not the Roman custom to hand a man over before he has had an opportunity to face his accusers and defend himself against their charges.

17So when they came here with me, I did not delay. The next day I sat on the judgment seat and ordered that the man be brought in.

18But when his accusers rose to speak, they did not charge him with any of the crimes I had expected.

19They only had some contentions with him regarding their own religion and a certain Jesus who had died, but whom Paul affirmed to be alive.

20Since I was at a loss as to how to investigate these matters, I asked if he was willing to go to Jerusalem and be tried there on these charges.

21But when Paul appealed to be held over for the decision of the Emperor, I ordered that he be held until I could send him to Caesar.”

22Then Agrippa said to Festus, “I would like to hear this man myself.” “Tomorrow you will hear him,” Festus declared.

23The next day Agrippa and Bernice came with great pomp and entered the auditorium, along with the commanders and leading men of the city. And Festus ordered that Paul be brought in.

24Then Festus said, “King Agrippa and all who are present with us, you see this man. The whole Jewish community has petitioned me about him, both here and in Jerusalem, crying out that he ought not to live any longer.

25But I found he had done nothing worthy of death, and since he has now appealed to the Emperor, I decided to send him.

26I have nothing definite to write to our sovereign about him. Therefore I have brought him before all of you, and especially before you, King Agrippa, so that after this inquiry I may have something to write.

27For it seems unreasonable to me to send on a prisoner without specifying the charges against him.”

Summary

Festus succeeds Felix and travels to Jerusalem, where the chief priests renew their accusations against Paul and ask that he be transferred there, secretly planning to ambush and kill him along the way (vv. 1-3). Festus instead invites the accusers to come to Caesarea, and when the case is heard he asks Paul whether he is willing to stand trial in Jerusalem (vv. 4-9). Paul refuses, insisting he is standing before Caesar's own tribunal, has wronged no one, and formally appeals to Caesar, which Festus grants (vv. 10-12). When King Agrippa and Bernice arrive to pay their respects, Festus lays out Paul's case, admitting he found no charge deserving death and is unsure what to write to the emperor, since Paul's accusers only disputed points about their own religion and someone named Jesus who Paul claims is alive (vv. 13-21). Agrippa asks to hear Paul himself, and the next day, amid great pomp, Paul is brought in so Festus can gather material for his report to Caesar (vv. 22-27).

Authorship & Background

Author: Luke (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 25 records the transition from Felix to Festus as governor, Paul's appeal to Caesar (vv.1-12), and Festus presenting Paul's case to King Agrippa II (vv.13-27). Paul's appeal to Caesar sets in motion the journey to Rome that God had promised (23:11).
Festus: Porcius Festus replaced Felix as governor of Judea (approximately AD 59). He was more competent and fair than Felix but still politically motivated. When he suggested Paul return to Jerusalem for trial (v.9) — where an ambush awaited — Paul exercised his right as a Roman citizen to appeal directly to Caesar.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: a new governor waiting only three days before the prosecution raises Paul's case again, a proposed transfer to Jerusalem that Festus does not realize is actually an ambush, repeated hearings producing the same result as before, no accusation of the things that were supposed, an appeal that removes the case from every court in Judea in eight words, hast thou appealed unto Caesar, unto Caesar shalt thou go, and a king brought in specifically because Festus needs a coherent charge to send Rome and cannot find one on his own
Festus and the Jewish Leaders (vv.1-10): The new governor is in the province three days before the case is raised with him, which is a measure of how much it matters to the prosecution. Their request is that Paul be brought to Jerusalem, and Luke tells the reader what Festus does not know, laying wait in the way to kill him. Festus insists on Caesarea, and the hearing produces the same result as before, they brought none accusation of such things as I supposed. Then Festus offers the transfer as a favor, wilt thou go up to Jerusalem, and there be judged? and Paul refuses it on the record, I stand at Caesar's judgment seat, where I ought to be judged.
Paul's Appeal to Caesar (v.11): "I appeal unto Caesar" — the legal right of every Roman citizen to have their case heard by the emperor. This was irrevocable — once appealed, the case must go to Rome. Paul used Roman law to fulfill God's purpose: bearing witness in Rome (23:11).
Unto Caesar Shalt Thou Go (v.12): Festus confers with his council and answers in eight words, hast thou appealed unto Caesar? unto Caesar shalt thou go. The decision removes the case from every court in Judea and takes it to Rome, which is where Paul said in chapter 19 he must go. Nothing in the remaining chapters is a legal contest any more. It is a journey.
Agrippa II (vv.13-27): The last of the Herod dynasty — great-grandson of Herod the Great. He was considered an expert in Jewish affairs. Festus brought Paul before him because he needed something to write to Caesar about the charges — and couldn't find any legitimate accusation.

Map & Geography

  • Caesarea (v.1): Festus arrived as the new governor in Caesarea. The Jewish leaders came from Jerusalem to bring charges against Paul.
  • Rome (v.11): Paul appealed to Caesar — the supreme court of the Roman Empire. This appeal would send him to Rome, fulfilling God's promise (Acts 23:11).

Deep Dive

Setting: Caesarea and Jerusalem, about AD 59. Paul has waited two years in custody (24:27). Felix has been recalled, and a new governor, Porcius Festus, has arrived. Josephus remembers Festus as a more honest administrator than Felix. He died in office after about two years. A new governor meant a new chance for Paul's enemies, and also, without anyone planning it, the open door to Rome that Jesus had promised (23:11).

Paul Appeals to Caesar (vv1–12)

  • "Three days after Festus had arrived… he went up to Jerusalem" (v1). A sensible new governor goes to meet the Jewish leadership quickly, since keeping peace with them was half the job.
  • "Asking as a favor against Paul… planning an ambush to kill him on the way" (vv2–3). Two years have passed, and the hatred hasn't cooled. In chapter 23 forty zealots made the plot and the leaders went along with it. Now the chief priests themselves propose it. The high priest is a new man, Ishmael son of Phabi, but the agenda is the same.
  • "Paul was being kept at Caesarea… let the men of authority among you go down with me" (vv4–5). Festus refuses, perhaps simply to assert his own authority. Whatever his motive, God uses a pagan official's instinct for proper procedure to keep Paul alive.
  • "Bringing many and serious charges against him that they could not prove" (v7). It is the same case as before Felix, two years older and still without evidence.
  • "Neither against the law of the Jews, nor against the temple, nor against Caesar" (v8). Paul answers the three charges in one sentence. The first two are the charges once brought against Stephen (6:13–14). The third, against Caesar, is the political charge brought against Jesus (Luke 23:2).
  • "Do you wish to go up to Jerusalem and there be tried… before me?" (v9). Festus wants to "do the Jews a favor" (the same phrase used of Felix, 24:27). The question sounds fair. In practice it would hand Paul to the court that wanted him dead, on the road where they had planned to kill him.
  • "I am standing before Caesar's tribunal, where I ought to be tried" (v10). Paul is a Roman citizen and Festus is Caesar's representative, so this is the proper court. "As you yourself know very well." Paul tells the governor plainly that he knows the case is empty.
  • "If… I deserve to die, I do not seek to escape death" (v11). He isn't dodging justice. He is refusing injustice. "No one can give me up to them." The verb (charizomai) means to hand over as a favor. Festus was about to make Paul a political gift.
  • "I appeal to Caesar" (v11). This is the right of provocatio: a Roman citizen could appeal from a provincial governor's court to the emperor. Once the appeal was made and accepted, the governor could no longer decide the case. The emperor in AD 59 was Nero, still in the better, early years of his reign. The terror of AD 64, which Christian tradition holds took Paul's life, was still ahead.
  • "To Caesar you have appealed; to Caesar you shall go" (v12). Festus confers with his legal advisers and accepts. Paul's road to Rome is now settled by Roman law itself (19:21; 23:11).
  • Was the appeal right? Some argue Paul should have trusted God rather than Caesar, since 26:32 says he could have been released. Most see it as wise and faithful. He used the rights God's providence had given him. He could not get justice in Jerusalem, and the Lord had already told him he "must" testify in Rome.
  • Commentators: Guzik draws on Boice to note how the corruption had grown. In chapter 23 the murder plot came from zealots. Now the nation's leaders start it themselves. He also says believers may rightly use lawful means to defend themselves when innocent, rather than rushing toward unnecessary suffering. Henry opens with a proverb: "New lords, new laws, new customs; but here was a new governor, and yet Paul had the same treatment." He describes Festus as not as just as he should have been, yet not as unjust as the Jews wanted. JFB suspects the leaders even hoped Paul would be executed without trial (see v16). It reads the "favor" Festus considered as wanting "to surrender in order to gratify" another. It also catches a dry tone in "to Caesar you shall go," as if adding: "and see if thou fare better."

Paul Before Agrippa and Bernice (vv13–27)

  • "Agrippa the king and Bernice" (v13). Herod Agrippa II was the great-grandson of Herod the Great, the son of Herod Agrippa I (ch 12), and the brother of Drusilla (24:24). He was the last of the Herods. Rome gave him territory north and east of Galilee and the right to appoint the high priest, so he was the natural expert on Jewish questions. Bernice was his sister, widowed young. She lived with him for years amid gossip about the relationship that Josephus and the Roman satirist Juvenal both mention. She later became the mistress of Titus, the general who destroyed Jerusalem in AD 70.
  • "Festus laid Paul's case before the king" (v14). Festus has a problem. He must send a Roman citizen to the emperor with a written report of the charges, and he has none that make sense to him.
  • "Not the custom of the Romans to give up anyone before the accused met the accusers face to face" (v16). This was a real principle of Roman justice, and it shows Roman law at its best. The Jerusalem leaders had asked for "a sentence of condemnation" without trial (v15).
  • "A certain Jesus, who was dead, but whom Paul asserted to be alive" (v19). Festus means it dismissively, but he states the whole matter exactly. Everything turns on whether Jesus is dead or alive (1 Cor 15:14, 17). "Their own religion" (deisidaimonia) can mean religion or superstition. Paul used the same word at Athens (17:22). Festus is probably being polite in front of a Jewish king.
  • "Being at a loss how to investigate these questions" (v20). A Roman judge had no category for a resurrection. Gallio dismissed the same kind of case as "questions about words and names" (18:15).
  • "For the decision of the emperor" (v21). "Emperor" here is Sebastos, the Greek form of "Augustus," which had become an imperial title. Luke's word for it matches Roman usage of the time.
  • "I would like to hear the man myself" (v22). Agrippa is curious. His great-uncle Antipas had once wanted to see Jesus perform a miracle (Luke 23:8).
  • "With great pomp" (v23). The word is phantasia, show or display. Royal purple, military tribunes (five cohorts were stationed at Caesarea) and the city's leading men all gather. Then a chained prisoner is brought in. Luke paints the contrast on purpose: the most important person in the room is the one in chains. Jesus had promised it: "you will be dragged before governors and kings for my sake, to bear witness" (Matt 10:18). The Lord had told Ananias that Paul would carry his name "before the Gentiles and kings" (9:15).
  • "He had done nothing deserving death" (v25). This is the third Roman official to say Paul is innocent, after Lysias (23:29) and implicitly Felix. Agrippa will be the fourth (26:31–32). Luke is building a careful legal record.
  • "I have nothing definite to write to my lord" (v26). "My lord" (kyrios) used of the emperor was becoming common under Nero. It is the title Christians gave to Jesus alone, which would later cost many of them their lives.
  • "Unreasonable, in sending a prisoner, not to indicate the charges" (v27). The governor of Judea admits he has a prisoner and no crime. The stage is set for Paul's great speech in chapter 26.
  • Commentators: Henry notes that Agrippa had come to hear Paul not to judge him but "only to gratify his curiosity." He sees Jesus' promise fulfilled: "in the former part of this chapter Paul was brought before Festus the governor, here before Agrippa the king, for a testimony to both." Henry's concise commentary adds: "How many unjust and hasty judgments the Roman maxim, verse 16, condemn!" JFB says Festus's words about Jesus show "the resurrection of the Crucified One had been the burden, as usual, of Paul's pleading," and that "the insignificance of the whole affair in the eyes of Festus is manifest." It calls this "the most dignified and influential audience Paul had yet addressed," fulfilling 9:15. It explains Agrippa's curiosity by the stir Paul had caused across Jewish life.

Pointing to Christ

Festus's careless summary is the heart of the gospel: "a certain Jesus, who was dead, but whom Paul asserted to be alive." The great men of the province gathered in their finery and thought the prisoner was on trial. In fact they were the ones being given a hearing, before the risen King whose name Paul carried. As at Jesus' own trials before Pilate and Herod, the officials could find no crime. And once again the scheming of men only moved God's plan forward. The road to Rome now ran through Caesar's own court.

Reflection

  • Discerning the Trap Behind the Offer (v.9) — Festus proposes moving Paul's trial to Jerusalem to please the Jewish leaders, unaware or unconcerned that an ambush awaits there. How do you discern when an appealing suggestion is actually a setup, and where do you find the courage to decline it?
  • Using Every Legitimate Tool for God's Purpose (v.11) — Paul invokes his right as a Roman citizen to appeal to Caesar, a legal move that carries him irrevocably toward Rome exactly as the Lord had promised. What legitimate rights, skills, or standing do you have that could be used more deliberately for God's purposes?
  • The Question Everything Hinges On (v.19) — Festus sums up the whole dispute as being about one Jesus, who died, whom Paul insists is alive. Every other question in life is secondary to this one — do you actually believe Jesus is alive, and does your life show it?
  • Declared Innocent, Still in Chains (v.25) — Festus finds nothing in Paul deserving death, yet Paul stays imprisoned because releasing him would cost Festus politically. How do you keep trusting God's justice when the system around you fails to deliver it?
