Summary

Continuing the "words of the wise," this chapter ranges across self-discipline and relationships. It opens with counsel on restraint when dining with a ruler and a warning not to wear yourself out chasing riches, since wealth can sprout wings and fly away like an eagle (vv. 1-5). Readers are told not to eat the bread of a stingy host, whose hospitality is not what it seems (vv. 6-8), and parents are urged to discipline their children and find joy in a wise son or daughter (vv. 13-16, 22-25). The chapter warns against envying sinners and urges continual fear of the Lord instead (v. 17), and it closes with the Bible's most vivid extended portrait of a drunkard, whose eyes see strange things and who wakes only to crave another drink (vv. 29-35).

Authorship & Background

Author: Primarily Solomon (1:1, 10:1, 25:1), with sections by Agur (ch.30) and King Lemuel (ch.31). Compiled over several centuries. Proverbs is wisdom literature — practical instruction for godly living. Hebrew title: 'Mishlei' — 'Proverbs/Comparisons.'
Historical Context: Chapter 23 continues the "Words of the Wise" section (22:17-24:34). The chapter addresses self-control in eating (vv.1-3), the deceptive nature of riches (vv.4-5), the stingy host (vv.6-8), parenting (vv.13-16, 22-25), envy of sinners (v.17), and concludes with the most vivid and extended warning against drunkenness in the Bible (vv.29-35). Verse 7 ("as he thinketh in his heart, so is he") is often quoted about positive thinking, but in context it describes the hypocrite who says "eat and drink" while his heart begrudges every morsel.
Themes in This Chapter: Self-Control in Appetite (vv.1-3, 20-21); The Fleeting Nature of Wealth (vv.4-5); Parenting: Discipline and Joy (vv.13-16, 22-25); Don't Envy Sinners (v.17); The Devastating Effects of Drunkenness (vv.29-35)
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: a meal at a ruler's table treated as a test rather than a courtesy, riches said to make themselves wings and fly away as an eagle toward heaven the moment a man labours to be rich, correction offered from a father's own hope of being gladdened rather than from mere enforcement, a request that becomes the emotional center of the whole chapter, my son, give me thine heart, and the longest sustained scene in all the collected sayings, the drunkard observed from the inside, swimming eyes, an unfelt beating, and a closing line naming the whole point of the portrait, I will seek it yet again
At a Ruler's Table, and Riches That Fly Away (vv.1-11): The instructions continue, and the first is about a meal that is a test rather than a courtesy, when thou sittest to eat with a ruler, consider diligently what is before thee. Then the warning the chapter is best known for, labour not to be rich, followed by its image, riches certainly make themselves wings, they fly away as an eagle toward heaven. The block includes the grudging host whose bread should not be eaten, counsel not to waste words on a fool, and the old landmark again, this time with a reason attached that gives the orphan a legal advocate, their redeemer is mighty, he shall plead their cause with thee.
Correction, and a Father's Gladness (vv.12-18): A short block on discipline given and received. Withhold not correction from the child is set beside the motive that runs under all of it, my son, if thine heart be wise, my heart shall rejoice, even mine. The teacher here is not enforcing rules but hoping to be gladdened. The block closes on patience with the apparent success of the wicked, let not thine heart envy sinners, but be thou in the fear of the LORD all the day long, and on the assurance that there is a latter end and the hope of it shall not be cut off.
Winebibbers, Gluttons and the Deep Ditch (vv.19-28): The chapter gathers its warnings about appetite. Be not among winebibbers, among riotous eaters of flesh, because the drunkard and the glutton shall come to poverty and drowsiness shall clothe a man with rags. Between them stands the line that treats truth as a purchase to be made once and never resold, buy the truth, and sell it not, and the request that is the emotional center of these instructions, my son, give me thine heart. The block ends on the strange woman as a deep ditch and a narrow pit, lying in wait as for a prey.
The Portrait of the Drunkard (vv.29-35): The last seven verses are the longest sustained passage in the collections and the only one built as a scene. It opens with a riddle, who hath woe, who hath sorrow, and answers it, they that tarry long at the wine. Then the instruction, look not thou upon the wine when it is red, when it giveth his colour in the cup, and the reason, at the last it biteth like a serpent, and stingeth like an adder. What follows is observed from inside: the swimming eyes, the ground that will not hold still, the beating not felt, when shall I awake. The closing line is the point of the whole portrait, I will seek it yet again.

Deep Dive

Setting: The "words of the wise" (22:17–24:22) continue. This collection is set out as about thirty sayings (22:20), many of them running two or three verses, and its style is direct command: "Do not…" Several of its early sayings closely resemble the Egyptian Instruction of Amenemope, a teaching text for young officials, especially the advice on eating with a ruler and on wealth that flies away (vv1–5). The ESV has no headings inside the collection, so the headings below follow its paragraph breaks. Chapter 23 moves from table manners at court and money, through care for orphans and discipline of children, to a father's warm appeal ("My son, give me your heart," v26) and ends with the Bible's most vivid portrait of a drunk.

Rulers' Tables, Riches and Landmarks (vv1–11)

  • "When you sit down to eat with a ruler, observe carefully what is before you" (v1). The setting is a young man rising in government service, invited to dine with a powerful official. "What is before you" can mean the food or "who is before you," the ruler himself. Either way, stay alert. A banquet with the powerful is never just a meal. Daniel and his friends faced this at Nebuchadnezzar's table and chose vegetables (Dan 1:8). The Tyndale notes: a newcomer who overeats from lack of self-control puts himself at risk.
  • "Put a knife to your throat if you are given to appetite" (v2). A vivid exaggeration, like Jesus' "if your right eye causes you to sin, tear it out" (Matt 5:29). It means take drastic control of your appetite. JFB: "an Eastern figure for putting restraint on the appetite." Henry: God's restraints on appetite "only say, Do thyself no harm."
  • "Do not desire his delicacies, for they are deceptive food" (v3). "Deceptive food" is literally "bread of lies." The ruler's generosity may have strings attached, a way to test you or buy you. JFB: "though well tasted, injurious." Guzik notes the rich rarely give favors for free.
  • "Do not toil to acquire wealth; be discerning enough to desist" (vv4–5). "Toil" means wear yourself out. Proverbs praises hard work, but not making money the goal of life. "Be discerning enough to desist" is literally "from your understanding, cease": stop relying on your own cleverness to get rich. Then a memorable picture: "When your eyes light on it, it is gone, for suddenly it sprouts wings, flying like an eagle toward heaven." Amenemope has a close parallel, wealth that makes itself wings "like geese" and flies to the sky. Proverbs makes it an eagle. Paul: "those who desire to be rich fall into temptation" (1 Tim 6:9). Jesus: don't lay up treasures on earth where moth and rust destroy (Matt 6:19). Henry: "Be not of those that will be rich."
  • "Do not eat the bread of a man who is stingy" (vv6–8). "Stingy" is literally "evil of eye," the opposite of the "bountiful eye" of 22:9. He says "Eat and drink!" but resents every bite. "Like one who is inwardly calculating" is a hard line. The verb appears only here, and the King James famously rendered it "as he thinketh in his heart, so is he," a line now often quoted as a general truth about thought shaping character. In context it means the miser is counting the cost of every mouthful. Some scholars, reading the Hebrew consonants a little differently, find "like a hair in the throat," something that makes you gag. Either way, the meal ends badly: "You will vomit up the morsels… and waste your pleasant words." JFB: once his true character shows, "all pleasant intercourse will be destroyed." Henry contrasts the heavenly host whose invitation is sincere: believers "may safely partake of the Bread of life" (Isa 55:1–2).
  • "Do not speak in the hearing of a fool, for he will despise the good sense of your words" (v9). Wisdom has a time to keep quiet. JFB and Guzik both link Jesus' "do not throw your pearls before pigs" (Matt 7:6). Compare 9:7–8 and 26:4–5. Henry is careful: speaking of divine things is a duty, but when a fool shows he will only mock, it's time to stop.
  • "Do not move an ancient landmark or enter the fields of the fatherless, for their Redeemer is strong; he will plead their cause against you" (vv10–11). This expands 22:28. Orphans and widows, with no man to defend their land, were easy targets for greedy neighbors. "Redeemer" is go'el, the kinsman-redeemer, the close relative who bought back family land, freed a relative from slavery, or avenged a death (Lev 25:25; Num 35:19). Boaz was Ruth's go'el (Ruth 3:9–13). The fatherless have no human go'el, so God himself takes the role, as he did for Israel in Egypt (Exod 6:6) and as Job hoped: "I know that my Redeemer lives" (Job 19:25). The Tyndale notes favor reading it as God. JFB: "redeemer" means "avenger," and so "advocate." Henry: their Redeemer is "mighty, almighty."
  • Commentators: Guzik passes on Ross's explanation of the go'el and adds Isaiah's many uses of "Redeemer" for God (Isa 41:14; 43:14). JFB says the warning on wealth in vv4–5 doesn't treat riches as evil in themselves. Guzik and Henry both contrast the deceitful tables of vv1–8 with the gospel feast.

Discipline, a Father's Joy and a Future Hope (vv12–18)

  • "Apply your heart to instruction and your ear to words of knowledge" (v12). A fresh call to listen, opening another run of sayings. JFB: "Here begins another series of precepts."
  • "Do not withhold discipline from a child; if you strike him with a rod, he will not die. If you strike him with the rod, you will save his soul from Sheol" (vv13–14). Proverbs takes for granted the ordinary discipline of its day (13:24; 22:15). "He will not die" reassures a soft-hearted parent: firm correction won't kill him, but neglect might. "Sheol" is the realm of the dead, and here it means an early death brought on by a life of folly, and perhaps more. Guzik notes it may mean the grave, eternal death, or both. JFB: "little danger" of bodily harm, "great hope of spiritual good." Guzik and the commentators he cites insist this is never a license for brutality, and the New Testament adds that discipline must not provoke children (Eph 6:4). Faithful Christian parents today differ on physical discipline, but all affirm what the proverb affirms: real love corrects. Henry: correction works best when "accompanied with prayer, and blessed of God."
  • "My son, if your heart is wise, my heart too will be glad. My inmost being will exult when your lips speak what is right" (vv15–16). The father's own feelings come into view. "Inmost being" is literally "kidneys," which Hebrew thought treated as the seat of the deepest emotions (Ps 7:9; 73:21). Wise heart, right words, glad parent. John: "I have no greater joy than to hear that my children are walking in the truth" (3 John 4). JFB: the teacher's joy in the pupil is "a motive to diligence."
  • "Let not your heart envy sinners, but continue in the fear of the LORD all the day. Surely there is a future, and your hope will not be cut off" (vv17–18). Watching the wicked prosper tempts a person to envy (24:1, 19; Ps 73:3). The cure is to keep fearing the LORD "all the day," not just at times of prayer. "A future" is acharit, "an end" or "an afterward." The wicked's success is not the last word. Asaph envied the wicked until he went into God's sanctuary and "discerned their end" (Ps 73:17). JFB: "The prosperity of the wicked is short." Henry: "The believer's expectation shall not be disappointed." 24:14 repeats the promise.
  • Commentators: Guzik passes on Spurgeon's description of the fear of the LORD as childlike trust and loving obedience. JFB reads the "future" of v18 as possibly "hereafter," when present unfairness is put right. The Tyndale notes agree: sinners may succeed for a while, but fearing the Lord brings the true reward.

Drunkards and Gluttons (vv19–21)

  • "Hear, my son, and be wise, and direct your heart in the way" (v19). "The way" is the way of wisdom, the right road.
  • "Be not among drunkards or among gluttonous eaters of meat" (v20). Meat was a feast food, not an everyday meal. "Gluttonous eaters of meat" are people who live for parties. The phrase "a glutton and a drunkard" appears together in the law, in the case of a stubborn, rebellious son (Deut 21:20). The danger is the company: run with them and you become one of them. Jesus was slandered with this very charge, "a glutton and a drunkard" (Matt 11:19), because he ate with sinners, though he never joined their sin.
  • "For the drunkard and the glutton will come to poverty, and slumber will clothe them with rags" (v21). Excess leads to sleeping it off, and sleeping it off leads to rags. JFB: drowsiness is "the dreamy sleep of the slothful." Henry: it is "a shame to make a god of the belly" (Phil 3:19).
  • Commentators: The Tyndale notes link the three dangers here (too much drink, food and sleep) as a lack of discipline. Guzik passes on the reminder that drunkards will not inherit the kingdom of God unless washed and changed (1 Cor 6:10–11).

Listen to Your Father and Mother (vv22–25)

  • "Listen to your father who gave you life, and do not despise your mother when she is old" (v22). The command to honor parents (Exod 20:12) applies all through life, and most of all when they are old and weak. JFB: adults honor the parents they once obeyed as children. Jesus, dying, made sure his mother was cared for (John 19:26–27). The Tyndale notes: unlike other ancient wisdom books, Proverbs credits wisdom to both mother and father (1:8; 6:20).
  • "Buy truth, and do not sell it; buy wisdom, instruction, and understanding" (v23). Truth is worth any price, and once you have it, never trade it away. Jesus told of a man who sold everything to buy a field with hidden treasure (Matt 13:44). The risen Christ counseled a lukewarm church, "buy from me gold refined by fire" (Rev 3:18). Esau sold his birthright for a bowl of stew (Gen 25:33). Henry quotes the line in the King James: "Buy the truth, and sell it not." The verse is missing from the old Greek translation, but it is in the Hebrew text and fits the passage well.
  • "The father of the righteous will greatly rejoice… let her who bore you rejoice" (vv24–25). "Greatly rejoice" doubles the verb for emphasis. A wise child is a parent's deepest joy (10:1; 15:20). Verse 25 turns it into a command: make your parents glad. JFB gives only cross-references here (10:1; 17:21, 25).
  • Commentators: Guzik on v23 recalls the martyrs who paid the full price rather than sell the truth. Henry and the Tyndale notes both stress that a godly father and mother teach together.

The Narrow Well (vv26–28)

  • "My son, give me your heart, and let your eyes observe my ways" (v26). The most personal appeal in the collection. The father doesn't just want obedience but the heart, the center of the person. There are two readings in the Hebrew of the second line: "let your eyes observe" or "let your eyes delight in" my ways. JFB hears divine Wisdom herself speaking. Henry: "The heart is what the great God requires." God's command to Israel was to love him "with all your heart" (Deut 6:5).
  • "For a prostitute is a deep pit; an adulteress is a narrow well" (v27). A pit dug to trap animals, and a narrow well, too tight to climb out of. JFB: "a narrow pit, out of which it is hard to climb." Guzik notes the irony: Proverbs calls a man's own wife his "well" (5:15), the source of real refreshment, while this well only traps. Compare 22:14.
  • "She lies in wait like a robber and increases the traitors among mankind" (v28). She is a predator, and every man she catches becomes a traitor to his wife, family and God (2:17). JFB: this vice is "peculiarly hardening to the heart." Guzik passes on an old line about Samson, who could break every rope his enemies tied but not the cords of his own lust (Judg 16).
  • Commentators: JFB treats vv26–35 as one passage, "A solemn warning against whoredom and drunkenness," citing Hosea 4:11: "whoredom, wine, and new wine take away the understanding." Guzik and the Tyndale notes both connect it to the longer warnings of chapters 5–7.

Wine That Bites Like a Serpent (vv29–35)

  • "Who has woe? Who has sorrow? Who has strife? Who has complaining? Who has wounds without cause? Who has redness of eyes?" (v29). Six questions, like a riddle. "Woe" and "sorrow" are cries of grief. "Wounds without cause" are bruises from fights he can't remember. "Redness of eyes" is bloodshot eyes (Gen 49:12 uses the word). JFB: "This picture is often sadly realized now."
  • "Those who tarry long over wine; those who go to try mixed wine" (v30). The answer to the riddle. "Mixed wine" was wine blended with spices to make it stronger, the drink at Wisdom's feast (9:2) but here the drink of excess. Isaiah: "Woe to those who rise early in the morning, that they may run after strong drink, who tarry late into the evening as wine inflames them!" (Isa 5:11).
  • "Do not look at wine when it is red, when it sparkles in the cup and goes down smoothly" (v31). "Sparkles" is literally "gives its eye," catches the light. The warning starts with looking, before the first sip. Henry: "Those that would be kept from sin, must keep from all the beginnings of it." Song of Songs uses "goes down smoothly" of good wine (Song 7:9). The danger is that it feels pleasant.
  • "In the end it bites like a serpent and stings like an adder" (v32). The smooth drink turns into venom. The "adder" may be a poisonous viper common in Israel. The sting comes later, like the strange woman whose "end is bitter as wormwood" (5:4).
  • "Your eyes will see strange things, and your heart utter perverse things" (v33). Hallucinations and loose talk. "Strange things" can also be read "strange women" (the same word as the adulteress), linking back to vv27–28, as JFB notes: drunkenness inflames passion and strips away restraint.
  • "You will be like one who lies down in the midst of the sea, like one who lies on the top of a mast" (v34). The room spins. The drunk feels like he's lying in the open sea, rising and falling, or clinging to the top of a ship's mast as it sways. An Israelite, living mostly inland and wary of the sea, would hear this as a picture of complete danger. Yet the drunk feels no fear.
  • "'They struck me,' you will say, 'but I was not hurt; they beat me, but I did not feel it. When shall I awake? I must have another drink'" (v35). The saddest line in the chapter. Beaten and numb, he wakes up and his first thought is another drink. This is addiction described three thousand years ago. JFB: "the language rather of acts than of the tongue." The Tyndale notes: the foolishness of drinking too much.
  • Commentators: Guzik passes on Waltke's pairing of the adulteress and wine as two "hidden and deadly traps" sitting side by side in the chapter. Guzik ends with hope: the drunkard can be made sober, and the love of Christ can overpower the love of sin (1 Cor 6:11). Henry stresses avoiding the first steps.

Pointing to Christ

Proverbs 23 promises that the fatherless have a Redeemer who is strong and pleads their cause (v11). That go'el became our kinsman in Christ, sharing our flesh and blood "that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death" (Heb 2:14–15). He bought back what we had lost, "redeemed… not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Pet 1:18–19). Where the ruler's table offers "bread of lies" (v3) and the miser's invitation is hollow (v7), Jesus offers himself as the true bread with a heart wholly for us (John 6:35). He was mocked as "a glutton and a drunkard" (Matt 11:19) for eating with sinners, and he calls drinkers and the enslaved to a better cup, a wine that doesn't bite but is "poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins" (Matt 26:28). And the appeal of v26, "give me your heart," finds its deepest answer in the one who first gave his whole heart for us.

Reflection

  • Riches Are Unstable and Fleeting (v.5) — Wealth is compared to something that grows wings and flies away, no matter how tightly it's held. What are you anchoring your security to that could vanish overnight?
  • Don't Envy the Prosperity of Sinners (v.17) — Their success is temporary, while fearing the LORD carries a lasting future. When you see the wicked flourishing, what keeps your hope fixed on eternity instead of envy?
  • Truth Is Worth Any Price (v.23) — Truth, wisdom, and understanding are valuable enough to acquire at great cost and never worth trading away. What compromise are you being tempted to make that you should refuse?
  • God Wants Your Heart, Not Just Your Behavior (v.26) — The appeal here is for wholehearted devotion, not mere outward compliance. Are you giving God genuine affection, or just going through the motions?
  • The Deceptive Bite of Alcohol (vv.31-32) — What looks appealing going down ends up striking like a venomous snake. Is there a habit in your life that looks harmless now but is quietly gaining control?
