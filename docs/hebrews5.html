Summary

Every high priest is chosen from among people to represent them before God, offering gifts and sacrifices for sin, and dealing gently with the ignorant and wayward because he shares their weakness, offering sacrifices for his own sins as well as theirs (vv. 1-3). No one takes this honor on himself but is called by God, as Aaron was, and Christ likewise did not exalt himself but was appointed by God and declared a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek (vv. 4-6). Though he was God's Son, Christ learned obedience through what he suffered, offering prayers with loud cries and tears, and having been made perfect became the source of eternal salvation for all who obey him (vv. 7-10). The author then rebukes his readers for spiritual immaturity, saying they should be teachers by now but still need to be taught the basics, remaining on milk rather than solid food because they lack practice in distinguishing good from evil (vv. 11-14).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown (see Chapter 1 notes for full discussion).
Historical Context: Chapter 5 begins the extended discussion of Christ's priesthood that runs through chapter 10. The author first describes the qualifications of any high priest (vv.1-4), then shows how Christ meets and exceeds them (vv.5-10). The chapter ends with a rebuke: the readers should be mature enough for this teaching but have become spiritually dull (vv.11-14). The Melchizedek priesthood is introduced here but developed fully in chapter 7.
Classification: Epistle — Sermon or Treatise
Key Themes: Qualifications for a high priest listed before Christ is shown to exceed them, strong crying and tears placed inside a chapter about qualification rather than emotion, obedience learned through suffering by one who was already a Son, an order named after Melchizedek and left unexplained until later, and a rebuke for readers who should already be teachers but need milk again
Qualifications of a High Priest (vv.1-4): Taken from among men (v.1). Appointed to represent men before God (v.1). Able to sympathize with the weak because he shares their weakness (v.2). Must offer sacrifice for his own sins as well as the people's (v.3). Called by God, not self-appointed (v.4).
Christ's Priesthood (vv.5-10): Christ didn't appoint Himself — God declared Him Son (v.5) and priest "after the order of Melchisedec" (v.6). In His earthly life, He prayed with "strong crying and tears" (v.7 — Gethsemane). Though a Son, He "learned obedience by the things which he suffered" (v.8). Made perfect, He became "the author of eternal salvation" (v.9).
Spiritual Immaturity Rebuked (vv.11-14): The readers are "dull of hearing" (v.11). They should be teachers by now but need to relearn basics (v.12). They need milk, not solid food (v.12). Mature believers have "senses exercised to discern both good and evil" (v.14).

Deep Dive

Setting: The unnamed preacher of Hebrews, writing in the 60s to weary Jewish Christians tempted to drift back from Christ, has argued that the Son is God's final word, above the angels (ch 1). He became fully human so that he could be "a merciful and faithful high priest" (2:17). He is greater than Moses (3:1–6), and the readers must not repeat the unbelief that kept the wilderness generation out of God's rest (3:7–4:13). Then the sermon reaches its central theme: "we have a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, Jesus, the Son of God," who sympathizes with weakness and was tempted in every way, "yet without sin," so believers can draw near to the throne of grace with confidence (4:14–16). Chapter 5 carries that thought straight on. It sets out what a high priest is, shows that Jesus qualifies, and then stops to warn the hearers that they have grown too sluggish for what comes next.

Jesus the Great High Priest (vv1–10)

  • The chapter break cuts the argument in half. The ESV heading began at 4:14, and 5:1 opens with "For," explaining 4:15. Jesus can sympathize with weakness because that is what a true high priest must do. Verses 1–10 give the job description and show that Jesus fits it.
  • A mirrored structure. Many commentators notice matching halves. Verses 1–4 describe any high priest: appointed to act for people (v1), gentle because he shares their weakness (vv2–3), and called by God (v4). Verses 5–10 take the same points in reverse for Christ: called by God (vv5–6), sharing human weakness in his sufferings (vv7–8), and appointed for people as the source of their salvation (vv9–10).
  • "Chosen from among men… on behalf of men in relation to God" (v1). A high priest had to be one of the people he represented, which is why the Son had to become human (2:14–17). "Gifts and sacrifices" covers the whole range of offerings. His greatest task came once a year, on the Day of Atonement, when he alone entered the Most Holy Place with blood (Lev 16), a day Hebrews returns to at length (9:7, 11–14).
  • "He can deal gently with the ignorant and wayward" (v2). "Deal gently" is metriopatheō, found only here in the New Testament. The lexicon traces it to "moderating one's passions," and so "to bear gently with." A good priest neither exploded at sinners nor shrugged their sins off. "Ignorant and wayward" echoes the law's line between unintentional sins, for which sacrifices were provided (Lev 4:2; Num 15:27–29), and sins done "with a high hand" in open defiance, for which there was none (Num 15:30–31).
  • "Since he himself is beset with weakness" (v2). "Beset" is perikeimai, "to have around one, to be clothed with," the word Paul uses of the chain he wore (Acts 28:20). Every human priest wore his weakness like a garment, and it made him gentle. Jesus knew weakness too (4:15), but without sin.
  • "Obligated to offer sacrifice for his own sins" (v3). On the Day of Atonement Aaron first offered a bull for himself and his household, and only then the goat for the people (Lev 16:6, 11, 15). Here Christ and Aaron part ways. Jesus "has no need, like those high priests, to offer sacrifices daily, first for his own sins" (7:27).
  • "No one takes this honor for himself, but only when called by God, just as Aaron was" (v4). God chose Aaron (Exod 28:1). When Korah grasped at the priesthood, the earth swallowed him (Num 16), and when King Uzziah burned incense in the temple he was struck with leprosy (2 Chr 26:16–21). The readers knew a sad contrast: in their own century Herod and the Roman governors made and unmade high priests at will, and men like Annas and Caiaphas held office by political favor.
  • "So also Christ did not exalt himself" (v5). Literally, he "did not glorify himself." Jesus said, "It is my Father who glorifies me" (John 8:54). The Father appointed him.
  • "You are my Son, today I have begotten you" (v5). Psalm 2:7, already quoted in 1:5. It was first spoken at the crowning of David's royal heir, and the New Testament applies it to Jesus' resurrection and enthronement (Acts 13:33). It comes first because the one appointed priest is the Son. Sonship comes before priesthood.
  • "You are a priest forever, after the order of Melchizedek" (v6). Psalm 110:4. The sermon has already used this psalm's first verse for the Son seated at God's right hand (1:3, 13). In its fourth verse the same king is made a priest by God's oath. That was startling, because in Israel kings came from Judah and priests from Levi. Melchizedek was king of Salem and "priest of God Most High," and he blessed Abraham (Gen 14:18–20). He is introduced here and left waiting until chapter 7. Two words carry the argument: "forever," because Aaron's priests all died, and "order," because this priesthood does not come through Aaron's family at all.
  • "In the days of his flesh… prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears" (v7). "The days of his flesh" means his mortal, earthly life. Most interpreters see Gethsemane, where Jesus was "greatly distressed and troubled" and prayed, "Remove this cup from me" (Mark 14:33–36). Luke adds sweat like drops of blood (Luke 22:44), though some early manuscripts lack Luke 22:43–44. Some include the cry from the cross as well (Matt 27:46). The Gospels do not mention tears, so Hebrews either draws on a memory they did not record or sums up the anguish they describe. The psalms of the suffering righteous man also lie behind it (Ps 22:1–2, 24). "Supplications" is hiketēria. The lexicon says it once meant the olive branch a suppliant carried, but by this period simply "supplication."
  • "To him who was able to save him from death, and he was heard" (v7). Jesus asked to be spared the cup, and still died. In what sense was he heard? The Greek says God could save him "out of" death. The main answers are that God raised him out of death, the most common view; that God strengthened him to drink the cup (Luke 22:43); and that his deepest prayer, "not what I will, but what you will," was granted exactly as he prayed it. These fit together more than they compete. The Tyndale notes take the answer as God's vindication of Jesus and his appointment as high priest.
  • "Because of his reverence" (v7). "Reverence" is eulabeia. The lexicon gives an older meaning, "caution, discretion," and a later one, "reverence, godly fear," which fits here and in 12:28, its only other New Testament use. The KJV's "heard in that he feared" was sometimes taken to mean he was rescued from fear, but the noun points to reverent submission, not terror. He was heard because his prayer was that of a Son who yielded.
  • "Although he was a son, he learned obedience through what he suffered" (v8). The Greek has a sound-play, emathen ("he learned") and epathen ("he suffered"), echoing a Greek proverb that suffering is schooling. Jesus never moved from disobedience to obedience. He learned by experience what obedience costs when it means a cross (Phil 2:8). A son might expect to be spared, yet this Son was not, and God's other children should not expect to be spared either (12:5–11).
  • "Being made perfect" (v9). "Made perfect" is teleioō, a favorite word in Hebrews (2:10; 7:28). It does not mean Jesus was once flawed. Its core sense is "to bring to completion," to the goal. There is a priestly echo too: in the Greek Old Testament the verb translates the idiom "to fill the hands," the phrase for ordaining a priest (Exod 29:9). The suffering Son finished the course and stands fully installed.
  • "The source of eternal salvation to all who obey him" (v9). "Source" is aitios, "the cause, the author." Aaron's sacrifices had to be repeated every year; this salvation is "eternal" (compare 9:12). The obedience the Son learned (v8) is matched by the obedience of those he saves. That is not salvation by works. In Hebrews, believing and obeying belong together, and the wilderness generation failed through "disobedience" and "unbelief" side by side (3:18–19).
  • "Being designated by God a high priest" (v10). "Designated" is prosagoreuō, found only here, "to address, to call by name." God publicly named him high priest, and the mention of Melchizedek sets up the next section.
  • Commentators: Henry says the high priest must be "one of ourselves, bone of our bones, flesh of our flesh," and of anyone who takes the office uninvited, "He is an intruder who is not called of God, as was Aaron." On v7 he asks, "How many dry prayers, how few wet ones, do we offer up to God!" JFB says the priest's "infirmity" included sinful weakness, which "Christ had not," though he shared the bodily weakness that made him able to suffer and die. JFB prefers "reverent fear" for eulabeia and passes on Trench's picture of carefully handling a fragile, precious vessel. The lexicon supports "caution" and "reverence" but not that image, which is an argument from the word's roots. Guzik lists Korah, Saul and Uzziah as men who seized priestly roles. He links hiketēria to Gethsemane: the olive branch, the olive garden, and the Lamb who supplies the wool. That is a devotional picture rather than first-century usage, since the lexicon says the olive-branch sense had faded. All three agree that "learned obedience" means learning by experience, not moving from sin to obedience; Henry puts it, "By his passive obedience, he learned active obedience."

Warning Against Apostasy (vv11–14)

  • A pause in the sermon. The ESV heading runs from 5:11 to 6:12, and Melchizedek does not return until 6:20. Having reached his deepest material, the preacher stops to ask whether his hearers are ready for it. The Tyndale notes see this as a speaker's way of waking up his audience. "About this" can also be read "about him," meaning Melchizedek or Christ. Either way, the difficulty lies less in the subject than in the listeners.
  • "Since you have become dull of hearing" (v11). "Dull" is nōthros, which the lexicon defines as "sluggish, slothful." The Greek Old Testament uses it for someone slack in his work (Sirach 4:29). It describes a will gone slack more than a weak mind. The Tyndale notes allow both, but the lexicon's weight is on sluggishness. "Have become" matters: they once stood firm under persecution (10:32–34). The same word returns in 6:12, "so that you may not be sluggish," framing the whole warning.
  • "By this time you ought to be teachers" (v12). Not every believer should hold a teaching office (Jas 3:1), but those who have known Christ for years should be able to help newer believers. Instead these believers needed to start again.
  • "The basic principles of the oracles of God" (v12). "Basic principles" is stoicheia, from a root meaning things set in a row. It was used for the letters of the alphabet, and so for the ABCs of any subject. Paul uses it differently for "the elementary principles of the world" (Gal 4:3; Col 2:8), but here the lexicon gives plain "rudiments," listed in 6:1–2. "Oracles" (logia) means God's spoken words, used of the law at Sinai (Acts 7:38) and the Scriptures entrusted to Israel (Rom 3:2). Here it most likely means the Old Testament, the very Scriptures that speak of Melchizedek.
  • "You need milk, not solid food" (v12). Paul uses the same picture with the Corinthians (1 Cor 3:1–2), and Greek teachers used it for beginners and advanced students. Milk is not bad, and Peter tells new believers to long for it (1 Pet 2:2). The problem is an adult who can eat nothing else.
  • "Unskilled in the word of righteousness, since he is a child" (v13). "Child" is nēpios, an infant, the opposite of "mature" in v14. "The word of righteousness" may mean the gospel's message of how God makes people righteous, or teaching about right living. Some take it simply as speech an infant cannot yet follow.
  • "Solid food is for the mature… trained by constant practice" (v14). "Mature" is teleios, from the same root as "made perfect" in v9. Christ reached his goal through suffering, and believers are to grow toward theirs (6:1). The rest of the verse pictures an athlete. "Constant practice" is hexis, habit formed by use. "Trained" is gymnazō, which gives "gymnasium," and it returns for God's discipline in 12:11. "Powers of discernment" is aisthētēria, "the organs of sense." Maturity is spiritual senses strengthened by steady use, like muscles.
  • "To distinguish good from evil" (v14). The phrase recalls the little children who "have no knowledge of good or evil" (Deut 1:39; Isa 7:15–16). A grown believer can tell nourishment from poison: sound teaching from error (13:9), and faithfulness from the drift back to safety that tempted these readers.
  • Commentators: Henry admits the subject is hard, but "generally the fault is in the hearers," and "Dull hearers make the preaching of the gospel a difficult thing." He calls it "a sin and shame" for those long in the church "to be children and babes in understanding," and on v14 writes, "There is a spiritual eye, a spiritual appetite, a spiritual taste." JFB says the Greek for "dull" "by derivation, means hard to move," and that "ye have become dull" implies they were once earnest. On v14 he notes that "the mere child puts into its mouth things hurtful and things nutritious, without discrimination," and Guzik makes the same point. Guzik treats dullness as a heart problem rather than an ear problem and an early warning sign before a believer quits. He also says the original of v13 means the hearer has become a babe. "Have become" is really there in vv11–12, but v13 simply says "he is a child." Guzik and JFB both take the discernment as mainly doctrinal, though "good from evil" seems broad enough to include moral judgment too.

Pointing to Christ

Hebrews 5 sets two pictures side by side. One is the Son of God in a garden, crying out with tears, learning obedience through suffering, and heard because he yielded to his Father. The other is a church grown sluggish and back on milk. The first is the answer to the second. The priest who can "deal gently with the ignorant and wayward" has prayed the hardest prayer ever prayed. He did not take the honor for himself. God named him and installed him forever, and his salvation does not wear off at the end of the year. The cure for dullness is not mainly trying harder. It is looking longer at this high priest, and that is the solid food the preacher wants to serve.

Reflection

  • Honest, Desperate Prayer (v.7) — In His earthly life Jesus prayed with loud cries and tears to the one able to save Him from death. Does your own prayer life carry that kind of honesty and urgency, or do you keep your deepest struggles at a distance from God?
  • Learning Obedience Through Suffering (v.8) — Even as the Son, Christ learned obedience through what He suffered. What is your current season of difficulty teaching you about submitting to God?
  • Stuck on Milk When You Should Be Teaching (v.12) — The readers had been believers long enough to teach others but still needed to be retaught the basics. Given how long you've walked with God, are you growing toward maturity or stuck relearning fundamentals?
  • Maturity Comes Through Practice (v.14) — Solid spiritual food is for the mature, whose senses have been trained by practice to tell good from evil. Are you actually applying what you know, or is your growth stalled because knowledge isn't turning into practice?
