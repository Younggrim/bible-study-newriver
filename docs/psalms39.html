1For the choirmaster. For Jeduthun. A Psalm of David. I said, “I will watch my ways so that I will not sin with my tongue; I will guard my mouth with a muzzle as long as the wicked are present.”

7And now, O Lord, for what do I wait? My hope is in You.

8Deliver me from all my transgressions; do not make me the reproach of fools.

9I have become mute; I do not open my mouth because of what You have done.

10Remove Your scourge from me; I am perishing by the force of Your hand.

11You discipline and correct a man for his iniquity, consuming like a moth what he holds dear; surely each man is but a vapor. Selah

12Hear my prayer, O LORD, and give ear to my cry for help; do not be deaf to my weeping. For I am a foreigner dwelling with You, a stranger like all my fathers.

13Turn Your gaze away from me, that I may again be cheered before I depart and am no more.”

Summary

A meditation on the brevity of life. David resolved to guard his words and stay silent while the wicked were near him, but keeping quiet only intensified his inner anguish until he had to speak (vv. 1-3). He asks God to show him how fleeting his life is, since even a full life amounts to little more than a breath, and everyone goes about like a passing shadow, storing up wealth without knowing who will receive it (vv. 4-6). Placing his hope in the LORD, he asks for deliverance from his transgressions and relief from the weight of God's discipline, comparing his fading strength to a moth-eaten garment (vv. 7-11). The psalm closes asking God to hear his prayer, since he is a temporary resident on earth like his ancestors, and to look away so he may know a measure of gladness before he departs from this life (vv. 12-13).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Individual Lament / Wisdom Reflection
Attributed Author: David ("To the chief Musician, even to Jeduthun, A Psalm of David")
Key Themes: Guarding the tongue, the brevity and vanity of human life, prayer for understanding life's shortness, pilgrimage and sojourning, the discipline of God
Historical Context: The superscription assigns this psalm to Jeduthun (also spelled Ethan), one of David's three chief musicians alongside Asaph and Heman (1 Chronicles 25:1-6). The phrase "to Jeduthun" likely means "for Jeduthun" to perform, or "in the manner/style of Jeduthun." Jeduthun's name may derive from yadah (to praise) — ironic given this psalm's somber tone.
I Will Keep My Mouth with a Bridle (vv.1-3): I said, I will take heed to my ways, that I sin not with my tongue, I will keep my mouth with a bridle, while the wicked is before me. The silence here is a policy rather than a symptom, and the psalm records that it did not work, I was dumb with silence, I held my peace, even from good, and my sorrow was stirred. Then the pressure building, my heart was hot within me, while I was musing the fire burned, then spake I with my tongue. The rest of the psalm is what came out.
Make Me to Know Mine End (vv.4-6): LORD, make me to know mine end, and the measure of my days, what it is, that I may know how frail I am. What he asks for, after three verses of trying not to speak, is a lifespan. The measurements are all diminishing, behold, thou hast made my days as an handbreadth, and mine age is as nothing before thee. And the closing image is the psalter's bleakest assessment of accumulation, he heapeth up riches, and knoweth not who shall gather them, which is the observation Ecclesiastes builds a book on.
My Hope Is in Thee (vv.7-8): And now, Lord, what wait I for, my hope is in thee. Two verses, and the first is a question the psalm answers by elimination: given everything in the previous section, there is nothing else left to be waiting for. Then a petition about reputation, deliver me from all my transgressions, make me not the reproach of the foolish.
Remove Thy Stroke Away from Me (vv.9-11): I was dumb, I opened not my mouth, because thou didst it. The silence of the opening returns and is given a different reason: not discipline but recognition of the source. Then the request, remove thy stroke away from me, and an image of erosion, thou makest his beauty to consume away like a moth.
I Am a Stranger with Thee (vv.12-13): Hear my prayer, O LORD, and give ear unto my cry, hold not thy peace at my tears. Then the phrase the psalm is remembered for and it is a claim about status, for I am a stranger with thee, and a sojourner, as all my fathers were. A resident alien had rights of protection but no land. And the psalm ends on a request that no other psalm makes, O spare me, that I may recover strength, before I go hence, and be no more. He asks God to look away.

Setting: "To the choirmaster: to Jeduthun. A Psalm of David." Jeduthun was one of the three chief musicians David appointed for worship, with Asaph and Heman (1 Chr 16:41–42; 25:1–3). His name appears over Psalms 62 and 77 as well. JFB suggests the psalm was given to him "as a special honor." No occasion is named. Like Psalm 38 it comes from a time of suffering that David connects with God's discipline for sin (vv8, 10–11), and the two psalms share words and mood: in both he keeps silent before the wicked (38:13–14; 39:1–2, 9). But Psalm 39 goes somewhere 38 did not. It wrestles with how short and empty human life is. Its key word is hebel, "breath," the word that runs through Ecclesiastes ("vanity of vanities"). It is also one of the bleakest endings in the Psalter, closer to Job than to the usual turn to praise. Some suggest David's old age as the setting, but that is only a guess.

I Will Guard My Ways (vv1–3)

  • "I said, 'I will guard my ways, that I may not sin with my tongue'" (v1). David knew that what was in his heart, if spoken aloud, could become sin. He was suffering, and he was tempted to complain against God. James calls the tongue "a fire" and "a restless evil" (Jas 3:6–8). David's first instinct was the right one: when you are not sure your words will honor God, keep them in.
  • "I will guard my mouth with a muzzle" (v1). JFB: literally "a muzzle for my mouth." The word is used for a muzzle on an animal. David has to hold himself back the way one restrains a dog that would bite.
  • "So long as the wicked are in my presence" (v1). This is the reason. Complaining about God in front of unbelievers would give them ammunition and dishonor God's name. Guzik passes on Kidner's point that his feelings ran so high that speaking them to the wrong people could look like disloyalty to God. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, warns believers not to broadcast every doubt.
  • "I was mute and silent; I held my peace to no avail" (v2). "To no avail" is literally "from good." The KJV reads "I held my peace, even from good," which JFB takes as silence about everything. The ESV takes it as "without good result." Either way, silence alone did not help.
  • "And my distress grew worse" (v2). Bottled-up pain does not go away. It festers. Holding his tongue kept him from sinning against others but did nothing for the trouble inside.
  • "My heart became hot within me. As I mused, the fire burned" (v3). "Mused" is the same verb as "meditate" in Psalm 1:2, but here the meditation is brooding. The more he turned the trouble over, the hotter it got. Jeremiah describes the same inner fire: "a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I am weary with holding it in" (Jer 20:9). JFB: "a smothered flame" that finally "burst forth."
  • "Then I spoke with my tongue" (v3). But notice whom he speaks to. Not to the wicked, but to God (v4). That is the way out: neither venting to everyone nor stuffing it down, but pouring it out before the LORD.
  • Commentators: Henry: "If an evil thought should arise in the mind, suppress it," but he adds that "sometimes it may be necessary to keep silence, even from good words," and warns against being too slow to speak what would build others up. On v3 he names the inward cause: "Impatience is a sin that has its cause within ourselves, and that is, musing." Guzik notes VanGemeren's view that the fire is anger, and stresses that David broke his silence in prayer, not in public complaint.

Make Me Know My End (vv4–6)

  • "O LORD, make me know my end and what is the measure of my days" (v4). David is not asking for the date of his death. He is asking to see life as it really is, to feel in his bones how short it is. Moses prays the same in Psalm 90:12: "teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom." JFB: to "experientially appreciate" it.
  • "Let me know how fleeting I am!" (v4). JFB gives the literal sense: "when I shall cease." The word for "fleeting" comes from a root meaning to cease or leave off. Isaiah uses it of one "rejected by men" (Isa 53:3).
  • "You have made my days a few handbreadths" (v5). A handbreadth, the width of four fingers, was one of the smallest units of length in Israel, about three inches (Exod 25:25). A life is measured not in miles but in finger-widths.
  • "My lifetime is as nothing before you" (v5). Against God's eternity, a human life does not even register. "A thousand years in your sight are but as yesterday when it is past" (90:4).
  • "Surely all mankind stands as a mere breath!" (v5). "Breath" is hebel, a puff of vapor, like your breath on a cold morning, there and gone. It is the key word of Ecclesiastes, used about 38 times there: "Vanity of vanities, all is vanity" (Eccl 1:2) could be translated "breath of breaths." It is also the name of Abel (Hebrew Hebel), whose short life ended almost as soon as it began (Gen 4:8). "Stands" is a pointed word: even man at his firmest, standing tall, is only breath. The KJV: "every man at his best state is altogether vanity." James uses the same picture: "you are a mist that appears for a little time and then vanishes" (Jas 4:14).
  • "Selah" (v5). The meaning of this word is uncertain. It probably marks a pause, perhaps a musical interlude, a moment to let the line sink in. It comes again at the end of v11 after the same refrain, "surely all mankind is a mere breath."
  • "Surely a man goes about as a shadow!" (v6). "Shadow" is tselem, which normally means "image." It is the word for humanity made "in the image of God" (Gen 1:27). Here the lexicon gives it the sense of a mere semblance or phantom. The irony is sharp: the creature made in God's image walks about as a hollow image, an outline without substance, when he lives without God. JFB: "Their pomp is a mere image."
  • "Surely for nothing they are in turmoil" (v6). People rush about, stressed and busy, and the word for "for nothing" is again hebel. All that noise for a breath.
  • "Man heaps up wealth and does not know who will gather!" (v6). The picture is of piling up grain in heaps at harvest, then dying before it is brought in. Ecclesiastes 2:18–19 complains that a man must leave all his toil to someone who comes after, "and who knows whether he will be wise or a fool?" Jesus told the parable of the rich fool who built bigger barns: "This night your soul is required of you, and the things you have prepared, whose will they be?" (Luke 12:20).
  • Three times "surely" (vv5–6). The Hebrew particle 'ak is repeated, "surely… surely… surely," and comes once more in v11. It hammers the point home. This is not pessimism. It is honesty.
  • Commentators: Henry: "In our greatest health and prosperity, every man is altogether vanity," and people are slow to accept how fragile they are, so we should be ready to die every day. Guzik, following Morgan, reads v4 as a prayer for a true perspective, not for a date. He passes on VanGemeren's note that the handbreadth was among the smallest measures, and Spurgeon's comfort that a short life can still accomplish much. JFB treats vv4–7 as a prayer that is "not essentially fretful," softened because it is spoken to God.

My Hope Is in You (vv7–11)

  • "And now, O Lord, for what do I wait? My hope is in you" (v7). Here is the hinge of the psalm. If life is a breath and wealth slips away, then what is left to hope for? Only God. JFB: the question implies "this world offers nothing to our expectation." Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, pictures David stepping off the sand onto the rock. The breath-like brevity of life, which could lead to despair, drives him instead to God, who does not pass away (102:25–27).
  • "Deliver me from all my transgressions" (v8). His first request is not for healing but for forgiveness. He sees his sin as the deeper problem. "Transgressions" are acts of rebellion, crossing a line God drew.
  • "Do not make me the scorn of the fool!" (v8). The "fool" (nabal) in the Psalms is the person who says in his heart "There is no God" (14:1). David fears that his suffering will make him a joke to people like that, and through him make God a joke. That is what he was guarding against in v1.
  • "I am mute; I do not open my mouth, for it is you who have done it" (v9). The silence returns, but it is a different silence. In v2 it was a clenched, smoldering silence. Now it is the silence of submission. He has stopped arguing because he sees God's hand in his trouble. Eli said much the same when he heard God's judgment on his house: "It is the LORD. Let him do what seems good to him" (1 Sam 3:18). Aaron "held his peace" when his sons died (Lev 10:3). Isaiah's Servant "opened not his mouth" (Isa 53:7).
  • "Remove your stroke from me; I am spent by the hostility of your hand" (v10). "Stroke" is nega', the same "plague" of 38:11. "Hostility" is literally "blow" or "attack." Submission does not mean David stops asking for relief. He accepts God's hand and asks God to lift it, in the same breath.
  • "When you discipline a man with rebukes for sin" (v11). God's discipline is purposeful. It corrects sin (Prov 3:11–12). Hebrews 12:10–11 adds that God disciplines "for our good, that we may share his holiness."
  • "You consume like a moth what is dear to him" (v11). "What is dear to him" can mean his beauty, his strength, everything he treasures. A moth works slowly and silently, and one day the fine garment is full of holes (Job 13:28; Isa 50:9). Sin's consequences wear life away like that. Jesus pointed to treasure "where neither moth nor rust destroys" (Matt 6:20).
  • "Surely all mankind is a mere breath! Selah" (v11). The refrain of v5 comes back, but now David has learned to face it with his hope in God.
  • Commentators: Henry: "When creature-confidences fail, it is our comfort that we have a God to go to," and on vv9–10, "we are beaten with a rod of our own making." On v11 he writes, "The body of man is as a garment to the soul," which sin's moth wears away. Guzik ties v8 to Paul's cry "Who will deliver me?" and its answer in Christ (Rom 7:24–8:4), and passes on Poole's view that pardon is the cure for a clouded relationship with God. JFB says the loss of human enjoyments "is ascribable to sin."

Hear My Prayer, O LORD (vv12–13)

  • "Hear my prayer, O LORD… hold not your peace at my tears!" (v12). David has held his peace (v2). Now he asks God not to hold his. Tears are a prayer God sees: "You have kept count of my tossings; put my tears in your bottle" (56:8).
  • "For I am a sojourner with you, a guest, like all my fathers" (v12). A "sojourner" (ger) was a resident foreigner, living in the land without owning any of it. A "guest" (toshav) was a temporary resident. Abraham called himself "a sojourner and foreigner" when he bought a grave for Sarah, the only land he ever owned in Canaan (Gen 23:4). The Law told Israel that the land belonged to God: "you are strangers and sojourners with me" (Lev 25:23). David uses the same words at the end of his life, praying over the gifts for the temple (1 Chr 29:15).
  • "With you" (v12). This small phrase changes everything. David is not a stranger from God but a stranger with him, a guest in God's house, under God's protection. Guests in the ancient Near East were under their host's care. The Law repeatedly commanded kindness to sojourners because God loves them (Deut 10:18–19).
  • "Like all my fathers" (v12). Hebrews 11:13–16 picks up exactly this idea: the patriarchs "acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth," because they were "seeking a homeland… a better country, that is, a heavenly one." Peter calls believers "sojourners and exiles" (1 Pet 2:11).
  • "Look away from me, that I may smile again" (v13). This is startling. In Psalm 38:21 David begged God not to be far from him. Now he asks God to look away. He does not mean "leave me forever." He means "stop looking at me with the eyes of a judge; lift your discipline." "Smile" (balag) means to brighten up or look cheerful. Job uses both words: "Look away from him and desist" (Job 14:6), and "Leave me alone, that I may find a little cheer" (Job 10:20, the same verb).
  • "Before I depart and am no more!" (v13). The psalm ends there, with no word of praise and no turn to confidence. It is one of only a few psalms that end in the dark (Psalm 88 is the other famous one). Scripture includes it so that believers who feel this way will know they are not outside the faith. Even this prayer is addressed to "O LORD." Psalm 40, which follows, opens: "I waited patiently for the LORD; he inclined to me and heard my cry."
  • Commentators: Henry: "Afflictions are sent to stir up prayer," and "The believer expects weariness and ill treatment on his way to heaven." Guzik stresses the "with you" of v12, so the believer is not alone in a hostile world, and reads v13 as David asking God to stop looking at him with the eyes of correction. He notes that this psalm has been read at burials. JFB reads v13 as a request "to be cheered" rather than held under wrath, so he can regain strength before he dies.

Pointing to Christ

Psalm 39 tells the truth about life without varnish: it is a breath, a shadow, a few handbreadths, and our sin makes it worse. The answer is not found in the psalm itself, only the direction: "My hope is in you." The New Testament completes it. Jesus, the true image of God (Col 1:15), entered our breath-like life and died a real death. He was silent before his accusers, and he took on himself the stroke of God's hand for our transgressions (Isa 53:4–7). By rising, he turned the sojourner's hope into a promise: "In my Father's house are many rooms… I go to prepare a place for you" (John 14:2). Because of him, life is short but not meaningless: "your labor is not in vain in the Lord" (1 Cor 15:58). The guest of God will one day be at home.

Reflection

  • Silence That Couldn't Last (vv.1-3) — David tries to hold his tongue and keep his struggle private, but the pressure inside eventually forces him to speak. What are you holding in that needs to be said honestly to God instead of suppressed?
  • Asking to Number His Days (v.4) — David prays that God would show him just how brief his life really is, so he can live with the right perspective. If you truly grasped how short your time is, what would you stop doing, and what would you finally start?
  • Wealth That Outlives Its Owner (v.6) — People spend their lives accumulating what they will never get to keep, since it all passes to someone else in the end. Are you building something that matters beyond what you own?
  • Hope in God Alone (v.7) — After surveying life's fleeting nature, David lands on a single conclusion: his hope rests in God, not in anything temporary. When the things you've leaned on for security get stripped away, is God actually enough for you?
  • Living as a Sojourner (v.12) — David describes himself as a stranger and temporary resident on earth, just as his ancestors were. How does remembering you're only passing through change the way you hold onto possessions, status, or comfort?
