1As they approached Jerusalem and came to Bethphage on the Mount of Olives, Jesus sent out two disciples,

2saying to them, “Go into the village ahead of you, and at once you will find a donkey tied there, with her colt beside her. Untie them and bring them to Me.

3If anyone questions you, tell him that the Lord needs them, and he will send them right away.”

4This took place to fulfill what was spoken through the prophet:

5“Say to the Daughter of Zion, ‘See, your King comes to you, gentle and riding on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey.’”

6So the disciples went and did as Jesus had directed them.

7They brought the donkey and the colt and laid their cloaks on them, and Jesus sat on them.

8A massive crowd spread their cloaks on the road, while others cut branches from the trees and spread them on the road.

9The crowds that went ahead of Him and those that followed were shouting: “Hosanna to the Son of David!” “Blessed is He who comes in the name of the Lord!” “Hosanna in the highest!”

10When Jesus had entered Jerusalem, the whole city was stirred and asked, “Who is this?”

11The crowds replied, “This is Jesus, the prophet from Nazareth in Galilee.”

12Then Jesus entered the temple courts and drove out all who were buying and selling there. He overturned the tables of the money changers and the seats of those selling doves.

13And He declared to them, “It is written: ‘My house will be called a house of prayer.’ But you are making it ‘a den of robbers.’”

14The blind and the lame came to Him at the temple, and He healed them.

15But the chief priests and scribes were indignant when they saw the wonders He performed and the children shouting in the temple courts, “Hosanna to the Son of David!”

16“Do You hear what these children are saying?” they asked. “Yes,” Jesus answered. “Have you never read: ‘From the mouths of children and infants You have ordained praise’?”

17Then He left them and went out of the city to Bethany, where He spent the night.

18In the morning, as Jesus was returning to the city, He was hungry.

19Seeing a fig tree by the road, He went up to it but found nothing on it except leaves. “May you never bear fruit again!” He said. And immediately the tree withered.

20When the disciples saw this, they marveled and asked, “How did the fig tree wither so quickly?”

21“Truly I tell you,” Jesus replied, “if you have faith and do not doubt, not only will you do what was done to the fig tree, but even if you say to this mountain, ‘Be lifted up and thrown into the sea,’ it will happen.

22If you believe, you will receive whatever you ask for in prayer.”

23When Jesus returned to the temple courts and began to teach, the chief priests and elders of the people came up to Him. “By what authority are You doing these things?” they asked. “And who gave You this authority?”

24“I will also ask you one question,” Jesus replied, “and if you answer Me, I will tell you by what authority I am doing these things.

25What was the source of John’s baptism? Was it from heaven or from men?” They deliberated among themselves and said, “If we say, ‘From heaven,’ He will ask, ‘Why then did you not believe him?’

26But if we say, ‘From men,’ we are afraid of the people, for they all regard John as a prophet.”

27So they answered, “We do not know.” And Jesus replied, “Neither will I tell you by what authority I am doing these things.

28But what do you think? There was a man who had two sons. He went to the first one and said, ‘Son, go and work today in the vineyard.’

29‘I will not,’ he replied. But later he changed his mind and went.

30Then the man went to the second son and told him the same thing. ‘I will, sir,’ he said. But he did not go.

31Which of the two did the will of his father?” “The first,” they answered. Jesus said to them, “Truly I tell you, the tax collectors and prostitutes are entering the kingdom of God before you.

32For John came to you in the way of righteousness, and you did not believe him, but the tax collectors and prostitutes did. And even after you saw this, you did not repent and believe him.

33Listen to another parable: There was a landowner who planted a vineyard. He put a wall around it, dug a winepress in it, and built a tower. Then he rented it out to some tenants and went away on a journey.

34When the harvest time drew near, he sent his servants to the tenants to collect his share of the fruit.

35But the tenants seized his servants. They beat one, killed another, and stoned a third.

36Again, he sent other servants, more than the first group. But the tenants did the same to them.

37Finally, he sent his son to them. ‘They will respect my son,’ he said.

38But when the tenants saw the son, they said to one another, ‘This is the heir. Come, let us kill him and take his inheritance.’

39So they seized him and threw him out of the vineyard and killed him.

40Therefore, when the owner of the vineyard returns, what will he do to those tenants?”

41“He will bring those wretches to a wretched end,” they replied, “and will rent out the vineyard to other tenants who will give him his share of the fruit at harvest time.”

42Jesus said to them, “Have you never read in the Scriptures: ‘The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone. This is from the Lord, and it is marvelous in our eyes’?

43Therefore I tell you that the kingdom of God will be taken away from you and given to a people who will produce its fruit.

44He who falls on this stone will be broken to pieces, but he on whom it falls will be crushed.”

45When the chief priests and Pharisees heard His parables, they knew that Jesus was speaking about them.

46Although they wanted to arrest Him, they were afraid of the crowds, because the people regarded Him as a prophet.

1And when they drew nigh unto Jerusalem, and were come to Bethphage, unto the mount of Olives, then sent Jesus two disciples,

2Saying unto them, Go into the village over against you, and straightway ye shall find an ass tied, and a colt with her: loose them, and bring them unto me.

3And if any man say ought unto you, ye shall say, The Lord hath need of them; and straightway he will send them.

4All this was done, that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophet, saying,

5Tell ye the daughter of Sion, Behold, thy King cometh unto thee, meek, and sitting upon an ass, and a colt the foal of an ass.

6And the disciples went, and did as Jesus commanded them,

7And brought the ass, and the colt, and put on them their clothes, and they set him thereon.

8And a very great multitude spread their garments in the way; others cut down branches from the trees, and strawed them in the way.

9And the multitudes that went before, and that followed, cried, saying, Hosanna to the Son of David: Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord; Hosanna in the highest.

10And when he was come into Jerusalem, all the city was moved, saying, Who is this?

11And the multitude said, This is Jesus the prophet of Nazareth of Galilee.

12And Jesus went into the temple of God, and cast out all them that sold and bought in the temple, and overthrew the tables of the moneychangers, and the seats of them that sold doves,

13And said unto them, It is written, My house shall be called the house of prayer; but ye have made it a den of thieves.

14And the blind and the lame came to him in the temple; and he healed them.

15And when the chief priests and scribes saw the wonderful things that he did, and the children crying in the temple, and saying, Hosanna to the Son of David; they were sore displeased,

16And said unto him, Hearest thou what these say? And Jesus saith unto them, Yea; have ye never read, Out of the mouth of babes and sucklings thou hast perfected praise?

17And he left them, and went out of the city into Bethany; and he lodged there.

18Now in the morning as he returned into the city, he hungered.

19And when he saw a fig tree in the way, he came to it, and found nothing thereon, but leaves only, and said unto it, Let no fruit grow on thee henceforward for ever. And presently the fig tree withered away.

20And when the disciples saw it, they marvelled, saying, How soon is the fig tree withered away!

21Jesus answered and said unto them, Verily I say unto you, If ye have faith, and doubt not, ye shall not only do this which is done to the fig tree, but also if ye shall say unto this mountain, Be thou removed, and be thou cast into the sea; it shall be done.

22And all things, whatsoever ye shall ask in prayer, believing, ye shall receive.

23And when he was come into the temple, the chief priests and the elders of the people came unto him as he was teaching, and said, By what authority doest thou these things? and who gave thee this authority?

24And Jesus answered and said unto them, I also will ask you one thing, which if ye tell me, I in like wise will tell you by what authority I do these things.

25The baptism of John, whence was it? from heaven, or of men? And they reasoned with themselves, saying, If we shall say, From heaven; he will say unto us, Why did ye not then believe him?

26But if we shall say, Of men; we fear the people; for all hold John as a prophet.

27And they answered Jesus, and said, We cannot tell. And he said unto them, Neither tell I you by what authority I do these things.

28But what think ye? A certain man had two sons; and he came to the first, and said, Son, go work to day in my vineyard.

29He answered and said, I will not: but afterward he repented, and went.

30And he came to the second, and said likewise. And he answered and said, I go, sir: and went not.

31Whether of them twain did the will of his father? They say unto him, The first. Jesus saith unto them, Verily I say unto you, That the publicans and the harlots go into the kingdom of God before you.

32For John came unto you in the way of righteousness, and ye believed him not: but the publicans and the harlots believed him: and ye, when ye had seen it, repented not afterward, that ye might believe him.

33Hear another parable: There was a certain householder, which planted a vineyard, and hedged it round about, and digged a winepress in it, and built a tower, and let it out to husbandmen, and went into a far country:

34And when the time of the fruit drew near, he sent his servants to the husbandmen, that they might receive the fruits of it.

35And the husbandmen took his servants, and beat one, and killed another, and stoned another.

36Again, he sent other servants more than the first: and they did unto them likewise.

37But last of all he sent unto them his son, saying, They will reverence my son.

38But when the husbandmen saw the son, they said among themselves, This is the heir; come, let us kill him, and let us seize on his inheritance.

39And they caught him, and cast him out of the vineyard, and slew him.

40When the lord therefore of the vineyard cometh, what will he do unto those husbandmen?

41They say unto him, He will miserably destroy those wicked men, and will let out his vineyard unto other husbandmen, which shall render him the fruits in their seasons.

42Jesus saith unto them, Did ye never read in the scriptures, The stone which the builders rejected, the same is become the head of the corner: this is the Lord’s doing, and it is marvellous in our eyes?

43Therefore say I unto you, The kingdom of God shall be taken from you, and given to a nation bringing forth the fruits thereof.

44And whosoever shall fall on this stone shall be broken: but on whomsoever it shall fall, it will grind him to powder.

45And when the chief priests and Pharisees had heard his parables, they perceived that he spake of them.

46But when they sought to lay hands on him, they feared the multitude, because they took him for a prophet.

Summary

Jesus sends two disciples to bring a donkey and its colt so he can enter Jerusalem riding on them, fulfilling Zechariah's prophecy of a humble king, as the crowds spread their cloaks and branches and shout Hosanna (vv. 1-11). He enters the temple and drives out the money changers and merchants, declaring it a house of prayer that they have made a den of robbers, and heals the blind and lame who come to him there (vv. 12-17). The next morning, finding a fig tree with no fruit, he curses it and it withers at once, using the moment to teach the disciples about faith that does not doubt (vv. 18-22). When the chief priests and elders question his authority, he answers with a counter-question about John's baptism that they refuse to answer (vv. 23-27), then tells the parable of the two sons to expose their false obedience (vv. 28-32) and the parable of the wicked tenants, who kill the vineyard owner's servants and son, warning that the kingdom will be taken from them and given to a people producing its fruit — a parable the chief priests and Pharisees recognize is aimed at them (vv. 33-46).

Authorship & Background

Author: Matthew (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 21 begins the final week of Jesus' life — often called "Passion Week." It records the Triumphal Entry into Jerusalem (vv.1-11), the second cleansing of the Temple (vv.12-17), the cursing of the fig tree (vv.18-22), the challenge to Jesus' authority (vv.23-27), and two parables of judgment (vv.28-46).
The Triumphal Entry: Jesus enters Jerusalem as King, fulfilling Zechariah 9:9. He rides a donkey — a symbol of peace, not a warhorse. The crowd shouts "Hosanna to the Son of David" — a messianic acclamation. Within five days, many of these same people will cry "Crucify Him."
The Temple Cleansing: This is the second time Jesus cleansed the Temple (the first was at the beginning of His ministry, John 2:13-17). The Court of the Gentiles — the only place non-Jews could worship — had been turned into a marketplace. Jesus' anger was directed at those who made worship impossible for outsiders.
The Fig Tree: A prophetic sign-act. The fig tree represents Israel — full of leaves (outward religion) but bearing no fruit (genuine righteousness). Jesus' cursing of the tree is a warning of judgment on fruitless religion.
Classification: Gospel — Narrative
Key Themes: detailed arrangements given in advance for a donkey and colt, fulfilling Zechariah's prophecy of a king who comes in peace rather than in war, a crowd shouting Hosanna to the son of David, a royal acclamation the city itself cannot quite explain, calling him only Jesus the prophet of Nazareth of Galilee, a temple cleared with two quotations paired together, a house of prayer turned into a den of thieves, a fig tree cursed and withered as a sign against fruitless religion, and two parables of judgment, two sons and wicked tenants, that force the religious leaders to pronounce sentence on themselves before they even realize what they have done
The Entry into Jerusalem (vv.1-11): The arrangements are given in detail, including what to say if challenged, and Matthew attaches Zechariah 9:9 to them. He is the only evangelist to mention both an ass and a colt, following the two halves of the Hebrew line. The crowd spreads garments and branches and shouts Hosanna to the son of David, which is a royal acclamation rather than a religious one. Matthew's note on the effect is about the city rather than the procession, all the city was moved, saying, Who is this? and the answer the crowd gives is smaller than the shout, this is Jesus the prophet of Nazareth of Galilee.
The Temple, and the Fig Tree (vv.12-22): The cleansing is immediate and Matthew pairs two quotations, my house shall be called the house of prayer, but ye have made it a den of thieves. What he alone adds is what happened next in the same space: the blind and the lame came to him in the temple, and he healed them, and children were shouting there, which the chief priests object to. The fig tree follows, and Matthew compresses the two days into one scene so that the withering is immediate, and the lesson drawn is about prayer rather than judgment, if ye have faith, and doubt not, ye shall say unto this mountain, Be thou removed.
By What Authority, and Two Sons (vv.23-32): The question is jurisdictional and the counter-question about John's baptism traps them between the crowd and their own record. They answer we cannot tell and get no answer in return. Then a parable only Matthew has: two sons, one who refuses and goes, one who agrees and does not. The question put to them is which of the twain did the will of his father, and they answer correctly, which is what makes the application unanswerable, the publicans and the harlots go into the kingdom of God before you. The reason given is a matter of record, John came unto you, and ye believed him not, but the publicans and the harlots believed him.
The Wicked Husbandmen (vv.33-46): The vineyard of Isaiah 5, servants sent and beaten and stoned and killed, and then the son. The reasoning of the tenants is quoted, this is the heir, come, let us kill him, and let us seize on his inheritance. Then Matthew has him ask them to pass sentence on the story, and they do it, which is the trap: he will miserably destroy those wicked men. The stone which the builders rejected is quoted, with a sentence that only Matthew adds, the kingdom of God shall be taken from you, and given to a nation bringing forth the fruits thereof. They understood that he spake of them, and could not arrest him because the multitude took him for a prophet.

Map & Geography

  • Bethphage and Bethany: Two villages on the eastern slope of the Mount of Olives, about 2 miles east of Jerusalem. The triumphal entry begins here.
  • The Mount of Olives: The hill directly east of Jerusalem, overlooking the Temple. Jesus descended from here into the city.
  • Jerusalem and the Temple: Jesus entered the city and went directly to the Temple, cleansing it of the money changers. The Temple was the religious and geographic center of Jewish life.

Setting: Jerusalem, the week of Passover, spring of AD 30 (some date it AD 33). Jesus has come up from Jericho with crowds of Galilean pilgrims and is staying at Bethany, a village on the far side of the Mount of Olives about two miles from the city, where Lazarus, Martha and Mary lived (John 11:1; 12:1). The city's population swelled several times over at Passover, and Roman soldiers watched closely for unrest. Chapter 21 begins the last section of Matthew's Gospel, which runs to the resurrection. Jesus, who had so often told people to keep quiet about him, now enters the city openly as king, takes charge of the temple, and begins a series of clashes with the leaders that runs through chapter 22. Mark shows these events over several days: the entry on Sunday, the fig tree and the temple on Monday, and the debates on Tuesday (Mark 11). Matthew groups them more tightly.

The Triumphal Entry (vv1–11)

  • "Bethphage, to the Mount of Olives" (v1). Bethphage means "house of unripe figs," a small village on the eastern slope of the Mount of Olives. The Mount of Olives itself carried weight: Zechariah pictured the LORD standing on it on the day he comes to save his people (Zech 14:4).
  • "A donkey tied, and a colt with her" (v2). Only Matthew mentions two animals. Mark and Luke speak of the colt alone, adding that no one had ever ridden it (Mark 11:2). An unbroken colt would be calmer with its mother walking beside it. An animal never used for ordinary work was also fit for sacred use (Num 19:2; 1 Sam 6:7).
  • "The Lord needs them" (v3). Jesus may have arranged this with the owner beforehand, or it may show his foreknowledge, as with the coin in the fish (17:27). Either way he is giving the orders.
  • "This took place to fulfill what was spoken by the prophet" (v4). The quotation joins Isaiah 62:11 ("Say to the daughter of Zion") with Zechariah 9:9. Zechariah goes on to say this king will "cut off the chariot… and the war horse" and "speak peace to the nations" (Zech 9:10). Kings rode horses to war and donkeys in peace. Solomon rode David's mule to his coronation (1 Kgs 1:33–38). Jesus is claiming to be king, but a king of peace, "humble" (praus, the word of 5:5 and 11:29, "gentle and lowly in heart").
  • "He sat on them" (v7). Some read this as Jesus riding both animals at once, which critics mock. "On them" most naturally means on the cloaks laid on the animals, and he rode the colt. Zechariah's two lines describe one animal in the way Hebrew poetry repeats an idea, and Matthew simply reports that two were there.
  • Cloaks and branches (v8). Spreading cloaks on the road was a gesture for a new king (2 Kgs 9:13, when Jehu was proclaimed). John says the branches were palms (John 12:13), which had been a national symbol since the Maccabees cleansed the temple and entered Jerusalem with palms about 160 years earlier (1 Macc 13:51; 2 Macc 10:7).
  • "Hosanna to the Son of David!" (v9). "Hosanna" is Hebrew hoshiʿa-na, "save, please!" from Psalm 118:25. By this time it had also become a shout of praise, something like "God save the king." Psalm 118 was one of the Hallel psalms sung at Passover. Its next line, "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the LORD" (Ps 118:26), was a blessing on pilgrims, but the crowd applies it to Jesus as the coming Son of David.
  • "The whole city was stirred up" (v10). The same verb (seiō) is used of the earthquake at the cross (27:51). The city that "was troubled" when the Magi asked about a newborn king (2:3) is shaken again.
  • "This is the prophet Jesus, from Nazareth of Galilee" (v11). The Galilean crowd is proud of him. "The prophet" may mean the prophet like Moses (Deut 18:15). Yet within days a crowd in this same city will shout "Let him be crucified!" (27:22). The pilgrims from Galilee and the Jerusalem crowd at Pilate's court were probably not entirely the same people, but the city as a whole will reject him.
  • Commentators: Guzik notes the change in Jesus' approach: after long avoiding public attention, he now enters in the most public way possible, knowing it will lead to his death. He follows D. A. Carson in explaining the two animals: Jesus rode the unbroken colt and its mother came along. He mentions those who calculate Daniel's seventy weeks (Dan 9:24–26) to land on this very day, a reckoning many scholars question. Henry draws the lesson, "Those that take Christ for their King, must lay their all under his feet." JFB defers these verses to its notes on Luke 19.

Jesus Cleanses the Temple (vv12–17)

  • "Jesus entered the temple" (v12). Herod's temple was one of the great buildings of the Roman world, with a huge outer court, the Court of the Gentiles, open to anyone. Inside it were the inner courts for Jewish worshipers, with warning signs forbidding Gentiles to go further on pain of death. The trade took place in the outer court, the only place where Gentiles could pray.
  • "All who sold and bought… the money-changers… those who sold pigeons" (v12). Pilgrims needed animals for sacrifice that would pass inspection, and the temple tax had to be paid in Tyrian shekels, a high-quality silver coin (17:24). So money-changers set up tables to exchange Roman and Greek coins, for a fee. Pigeons were the offering of the poor (Lev 5:7; Luke 2:24). The trade had a reasonable purpose, but it filled the one space for Gentile prayer with noise and profit, and it was under the control of the high priestly families.
  • Two cleansings? John places a cleansing of the temple at the start of Jesus' ministry (John 2:13–22). The other three Gospels place one here, in the last week. Many scholars think there was one event that John moved for theological reasons. Many others think Jesus did it twice, once at the start and once at the end, since the details and the words differ, and the trade would surely have returned. Either way the act is a claim to authority over God's house.
  • "My house shall be called a house of prayer… but you make it a den of robbers" (v13). Jesus quotes two prophets. Isaiah 56:7 says God's house will be "a house of prayer for all peoples." Mark keeps those last words (Mark 11:17). Jeremiah 7:11 is from Jeremiah's sermon at the temple gate, where he warned that people who sinned all week then ran to the temple for safety, treating it like a robbers' hideout, and that God would destroy it as he destroyed Shiloh. Jesus is warning of the same thing (24:2).
  • "The blind and the lame came to him in the temple, and he healed them" (v14). Only Matthew records this. Since David's capture of Jerusalem a saying had kept "the blind and the lame" out of "the house" (2 Sam 5:8), and the law barred priests with defects from serving at the altar (Lev 21:18). In the temple courts the Son of David welcomes them and heals them.
  • "The children crying out in the temple, 'Hosanna to the Son of David!'" (v15). The children repeat what they heard from the crowds. The leaders are "indignant," the same word used of the disciples when they resented the brothers' request (20:24).
  • "Out of the mouth of infants and nursing babies you have prepared praise" (v16). Jesus quotes Psalm 8:2 in its Greek form. In that psalm the praise is offered to God himself. Jesus applies it to praise given to him, a quiet but bold claim.
  • "He went out of the city to Bethany and lodged there" (v17). Jerusalem was packed at Passover, and Jesus spent the nights outside (Luke 21:37).
  • Commentators: Guzik says the merchants charged inflated prices for approved animals and exchange, and that their stalls filled the court where Gentiles came to pray. He points out the leaders' hypocrisy: they tolerated profiteering in God's house but objected to children praising God's Son. He treats this as a second cleansing, separate from John 2, and Henry agrees: "Our Lord drove them from the place, as he had done at his entering upon his ministry." JFB defers to Luke 19 and Mark 11.

Jesus Curses the Fig Tree (vv18–22)

  • "He became hungry" (v18). A real human need. Jesus is fully man.
  • "Found nothing on it but only leaves" (v19). Mark adds, "it was not the season for figs" (Mark 11:13). Fig trees around Jerusalem put out leaves in March or April, and with the leaves come small early buds of fruit (in Hebrew paggim), which local people ate. A tree in full leaf with no fruit at all was promising and barren, all show and no fruit. That is the point.
  • "May no fruit ever come from you again!" (v19). This is an acted parable, like the prophets' acted signs (Jer 13; 19). In the Old Testament Israel is often pictured as a fig tree or vine, and the prophets used the barren fig tree as a picture of judgment (Jer 8:13; Hos 9:10, 16; Mic 7:1). Matthew places the curse right after the temple scene. Mark wraps the temple cleansing inside the fig tree story, making the link plain: the temple's busy worship looked like leaves, but there was no fruit. It is the only miracle of Jesus that destroys rather than restores, and it falls on a tree, not on a person.
  • "The fig tree withered at once" (v19). Mark says the disciples noticed the next morning (Mark 11:20). Matthew tells the result together, which is his usual way of shortening stories.
  • "If you say to this mountain, 'Be taken up and thrown into the sea'" (v21). Standing on the Mount of Olives, "this mountain" could point to the mount itself or across to the temple mount. From there the Dead Sea can be seen on a clear day. Jesus may be hinting that the temple system itself will be removed. Moving mountains was a known saying for doing the impossible (17:20).
  • "Whatever you ask in prayer, you will receive, if you have faith" (v22). This is not a blank check. Faith that "does not doubt" trusts God and seeks his will, as Jesus will pray in Gethsemane, "not as I will, but as you will" (26:39; 1 John 5:14).
  • Commentators: Henry: "A false profession commonly withers in this world, and it is the effect of Christ's curse." Guzik sees the tree as a picture of Israel's unfruitfulness and of religion that is all appearance, and he notes that this is one of only two destructive miracles in the Gospels (the other being the pigs at Gadara), neither aimed at people. The Tyndale notes read the tree in the same way, as a picture of the leaders. JFB defers these verses to Mark 11.

The Authority of Jesus Challenged (vv23–27)

  • "The chief priests and the elders of the people" (v23). These were leaders of the Sanhedrin, the ruling council: the high priestly families and the heads of leading families. The temple was their domain, and Jesus had just overturned its trade.
  • "By what authority are you doing these things?" (v23). Rabbis were authorized by other rabbis, and priests by birth. Jesus had neither kind of license. "These things" means the entry, the cleansing and the healings.
  • "I also will ask you one question" (v24). Answering a question with a question was a normal form of rabbinic debate. It is not a dodge. If they answer honestly, they will have their answer: John pointed to Jesus (3:11–14; John 1:29), so if John's authority came from heaven, so did that of the one he announced.
  • "From heaven or from man?" (v25). "Heaven" was a reverent way to say "God." "They discussed it among themselves." Their reasoning shows their real concern: not "what is true?" but "what will this cost us?" They are trapped between their consciences and the crowd.
  • "We do not know" (v27). The nation's religious authorities, whose task was to judge prophets (Deut 18:21–22), admit they cannot tell whether the most popular prophet in a generation came from God. Their answer disqualifies them. "Neither will I tell you." Jesus does not owe an answer to those who refuse the light they already have.
  • Commentators: JFB notes that the question referred "particularly to the expulsion of the buyers and sellers from the temple," and admires the "composure and dignity of wisdom" of Jesus' reply. Guzik observes that the leaders cared more about public opinion than about the truth. The Tyndale notes add that answering a question with a question was common in debates of that time.

The Parable of the Two Sons (vv28–32)

  • "What do you think?" (v28). Jesus now asks the questions, and the next three parables (21:28–22:14) all expose the leaders. Only Matthew has this one.
  • "I will not," but afterward he changed his mind and went (v29). Openly refusing a father was shocking in that culture. The verb for "changed his mind" is metamelomai, to regret and think again, used also of Judas's remorse (27:3). Here it leads to action.
  • "I will, sir," but did not go (v30). The second son is polite, even respectful. "Sir" is kyrie, "lord." He says the right words and does nothing. Jesus has already warned about this: "Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord'… but the one who does the will of my Father" (7:21).
  • A textual note. Manuscripts differ on the order of the two sons, and in some the second son, not the first, is the one who repents, with the leaders' answer changed to match. The ESV follows the order most scholars think original. The meaning is the same either way.
  • "The tax collectors and the prostitutes go into the kingdom of God before you" (v31). These were the two groups most despised in Jewish society: collaborators who served Rome and profited from their own people, and women who sold themselves. They said "no" to God with their lives, but repented when John preached. "Before you" may mean "ahead of you," leaving the door open, or "instead of you." Either way it is a stunning line to say to the chief priests in the temple.
  • "John came to you in the way of righteousness" (v32). John lived and taught the right way, a way of life matching God's will. The leaders saw tax collectors and prostitutes repent and still "did not afterward change your minds," using the same verb as v29. The first son changed his mind. They would not.
  • Commentators: JFB describes the second son as those who "were early called, and all their life long professed obedience to God, but never rendered it." Henry writes that "the daring rebel is brought to repentance and becomes the Lord's servant." Guzik applies the parable to churchgoers who say yes to God and never act on it, and warns that hearing truth again and again without obeying it hardens the heart.

The Parable of the Tenants (vv33–46)

  • "A master of a house who planted a vineyard and put a fence around it and dug a winepress in it and built a tower" (v33). Jesus' listeners would recognize Isaiah's song of the vineyard almost word for word (Isa 5:1–7), where "the vineyard of the LORD of hosts is the house of Israel." There the vineyard yields wild grapes and God judges the vineyard. Here the fault lies with the tenants, the leaders put in charge of it.
  • "Leased it to tenants, and went into another country" (v33). Absentee landlords leasing estates to tenant farmers were common in Galilee, and so was tension between them. A tenant contract gave the owner a share of the crop at harvest.
  • The servants beaten, killed and stoned (vv34–36). The servants stand for the prophets. Israel's history held many examples: Jeremiah beaten and imprisoned (Jer 20:2; 37:15), Zechariah son of Jehoiada stoned in the temple court (2 Chr 24:20–21), and others killed (1 Kgs 19:10; Neh 9:26). Jesus will name this pattern again (23:29–37).
  • "Finally he sent his son to them, saying, 'They will respect my son'" (v37). The son is not one more servant. He is the heir, and in the story he stands for Jesus. Hebrews opens the same way: God spoke "by the prophets, but in these last days… by his Son" (Heb 1:1–2). In telling this parable Jesus quietly claims to be the Son of God, in the temple, to the men who will soon condemn him.
  • "This is the heir. Come, let us kill him and have his inheritance" (v38). The tenants' logic is foolish, though some scholars suggest that an estate with no heir could fall to those in possession of it. Their plan shows what the leaders really want: the vineyard for themselves.
  • "They took him and threw him out of the vineyard and killed him" (v39). In Mark the son is killed and then thrown out (Mark 12:8). Matthew and Luke put the throwing out first, which matches what happened: Jesus was led out of the city to be crucified (John 19:17; Heb 13:12).
  • "He will put those wretches to a miserable death" (v41). The leaders pronounce the sentence themselves, as David did when Nathan told him the story of the poor man's lamb (2 Sam 12:1–7). The Greek has a play on words, "evil men, evilly destroy" (kakous kakōs), which "wretches… miserable death" tries to catch.
  • "The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" (v42). Jesus quotes Psalm 118:22–23, from the same psalm the crowds sang at his entry. The "builders" are the leaders, and the rejected stone is Jesus. A cornerstone (literally "head of the corner") was either the main foundation stone that set the line of the building or the capstone at the top. Either way it is the most important stone. There may also be a wordplay in Hebrew between "son" (ben) and "stone" (eben). Peter later preaches this verse to the same council (Acts 4:11; 1 Pet 2:7).
  • "The kingdom of God will be taken away from you and given to a people producing its fruits" (v43). Only Matthew has this verse. "A people" (ethnos, "nation") is singular. Many read it as the church, made up of Jews and Gentiles, the new people that bears the fruit God was looking for (1 Pet 2:9). Others stress that it is aimed at the leaders, who are losing their place in charge of God's people. Romans 9–11 warns believers against reading it as God being finished with the Jewish people. The first disciples were all Jews.
  • "The one who falls on this stone will be broken to pieces; and when it falls on anyone, it will crush him" (v44). This joins two other stone passages: Isaiah 8:14–15, where the LORD is "a stone of offense" over which many "shall stumble… and be broken," and Daniel 2:34–35, 44–45, where a stone not cut by human hands strikes the great statue and crushes it to dust. Some manuscripts lack v44, and the ESV notes this, since it may have been added from Luke 20:18. Most recent scholars and translations keep it.
  • "They perceived that he was speaking about them… they feared the crowds" (vv45–46). The leaders understand and are not moved to repent. They want to arrest him and wait only because of the crowd. In the parable, the tenants kill the son. Within days the story will come true.
  • Commentators: JFB calls the tenants' plot "the deep aim of the depraved heart" and notes that v43 appears only in Matthew. It applies the crushing stone of v44 both to Jerusalem's destruction and to every individual who rejects Christ. Henry says the builders "threw him aside as a despised stone." Guzik ties the parable to Isaiah 5 and reads the "people producing its fruits" as drawn from every nation. His application of v44 is that everyone will be broken by this stone: either broken in humble surrender to Christ now, or crushed by him in judgment.

Pointing to Christ

Everything in this chapter shows Jesus taking his rightful place. He enters the city as the humble king Zechariah promised, he cleans out his Father's house and fills it with the healed, he receives praise meant for God, and in the parable he names himself the beloved Son sent after all the prophets. He also shows the leaders where their road ends: they are the builders rejecting the stone. Yet the stone they reject becomes the cornerstone, "the Lord's doing, and it is marvelous in our eyes." The cross they are planning will be the very means by which God builds his new house, and the shout of "Hosanna," "save us," will be answered.

Reflection

  • A King Who Enters in Humility (v.5) — Jesus rides into Jerusalem on a donkey, not a warhorse, fulfilling prophecy while revealing a kingdom built on humility rather than force. How do you exercise the authority or influence you have — through humble service, or through control?
  • Worship Corrupted by Commerce (v.13) — Jesus overturns the tables of those who turned God's house of prayer into a marketplace. Is there anything crowding out genuine worship in your life that needs to be overturned?
  • Leaves Without Fruit (v.19) — The fig tree looks alive but produces nothing, and Jesus curses it as a picture of fruitless religion. Is your faith producing real fruit, or just the appearance of it?
  • Obedience Over Empty Promises (v.31) — The son who initially refused but later obeyed is commended over the one who promised to go and didn't. Where are you saying yes to God with your words but no with your actions?
  • Patience Toward Rejection (v.39) — The vineyard owner sends servant after servant, even his own son, despite repeated rejection. How has God's patience with you shaped the way you respond to Him?
  • The Rejected Stone Becomes the Cornerstone (v.42) — The very one the builders discarded becomes the foundation. Have you ever dismissed something God was doing because it didn't fit your expectations?
