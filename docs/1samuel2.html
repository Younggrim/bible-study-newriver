1At that time Hannah prayed: “My heart rejoices in the LORD; my horn is exalted in the LORD. My mouth speaks boldly against my enemies, for I rejoice in Your salvation.

2There is no one holy like the LORD. Indeed, there is no one besides You! And there is no Rock like our God.

3Do not boast so proudly, or let arrogance come from your mouth, for the LORD is a God who knows, and by Him actions are weighed.

4The bows of the mighty are broken, but the feeble are equipped with strength.

5The well-fed hire themselves out for food, but the starving hunger no more. The barren woman gives birth to seven, but she who has many sons pines away.

6The LORD brings death and gives life; He brings down to Sheol and raises up.

7The LORD sends poverty and wealth; He humbles and He exalts.

8He raises the poor from the dust and lifts the needy from the ash heap. He seats them among princes and bestows on them a throne of honor. For the foundations of the earth are the LORD’s, and upon them He has set the world.

9He guards the steps of His faithful ones, but the wicked perish in darkness; for by his own strength shall no man prevail.

10Those who oppose the LORD will be shattered. He will thunder from heaven against them. The LORD will judge the ends of the earth and will give power to His king. He will exalt the horn of His anointed.”

11Then Elkanah went home to Ramah, but the boy began ministering to the LORD before Eli the priest.

12Now the sons of Eli were wicked men; they had no regard for the LORD

13or for the custom of the priests with the people. When any man offered a sacrifice, the servant of the priest would come with a three-pronged meat fork while the meat was boiling

14and plunge it into the pan or kettle or cauldron or cooking pot. And the priest would claim for himself whatever the meat fork brought up. This is how they treated all the Israelites who came to Shiloh.

15Even before the fat was burned, the servant of the priest would come and say to the man who was sacrificing, “Give the priest some meat to roast, because he will not accept boiled meat from you, but only raw.”

16And if any man said to him, “The fat must be burned first; then you may take whatever you want,” the servant would reply, “No, you must give it to me right now. If you refuse, I will take it by force!”

17Thus the sin of these young men was severe in the sight of the LORD, for they were treating the LORD’s offering with contempt.

18Now Samuel was ministering before the LORD—a boy wearing a linen ephod.

19Each year his mother would make him a little robe and bring it to him when she went with her husband to offer the annual sacrifice.

20And Eli would bless Elkanah and his wife, saying, “May the LORD give you children by this woman in place of the one she dedicated to the LORD.” Then they would go home.

21So the LORD attended to Hannah, and she conceived and gave birth to three sons and two daughters. Meanwhile, the boy Samuel grew up in the presence of the LORD.

22Now Eli was very old, and he heard about everything his sons were doing to all Israel and how they were sleeping with the women who served at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting.

23“Why are you doing these things?” Eli said to his sons. “I hear about your wicked deeds from all these people.

24No, my sons; it is not a good report I hear circulating among the LORD’s people.

25If a man sins against another man, God can intercede for him; but if a man sins against the LORD, who can intercede for him?” But they would not listen to their father, since the LORD intended to put them to death.

26And the boy Samuel continued to grow in stature and in favor with the LORD and with man.

27Then a man of God came to Eli and told him, “This is what the LORD says: ‘Did I not clearly reveal Myself to your father’s house when they were in Egypt under Pharaoh’s house?

28And out of all the tribes of Israel I selected your father to be My priest, to offer sacrifices on My altar, to burn incense, and to wear an ephod in My presence. I also gave to the house of your father all the food offerings of the Israelites.

29Why then do you kick at My sacrifice and offering that I have prescribed for My dwelling place? You have honored your sons more than Me by fattening yourselves with the best of all the offerings of My people Israel.’

30Therefore, the LORD, the God of Israel, declares: ‘I did indeed say that your house and the house of your father would walk before Me forever. But now the LORD declares: Far be it from Me! For I will honor those who honor Me, but those who despise Me will be disdained.

31Behold, the days are coming when I will cut off your strength and the strength of your father’s house, so that no one in it will reach old age.

32You will see distress in My dwelling place. Despite all that is good in Israel, no one in your house will ever again reach old age.

33And every one of you that I do not cut off from My altar, I will cause your eyes to fail and your heart to grieve. All your descendants will die by the sword of men.

34And this sign shall come to you concerning your two sons Hophni and Phinehas: They will both die on the same day.

35Then I will raise up for Myself a faithful priest. He will do whatever is in My heart and mind. And I will build for him an enduring house, and he will walk before My anointed one for all time.

36And everyone left in your house will come and bow down to him for a piece of silver or a morsel of bread, pleading, “Please appoint me to some priestly office so that I can eat a piece of bread.”’”

1And Hannah prayed, and said, My heart rejoiceth in the LORD, mine horn is exalted in the LORD: my mouth is enlarged over mine enemies; because I rejoice in thy salvation.

2There is none holy as the LORD: for there is none beside thee: neither is there any rock like our God.

3Talk no more so exceeding proudly; let not arrogancy come out of your mouth: for the LORD is a God of knowledge, and by him actions are weighed.

4The bows of the mighty men are broken, and they that stumbled are girded with strength.

5They that were full have hired out themselves for bread; and they that were hungry ceased: so that the barren hath born seven; and she that hath many children is waxed feeble.

7The LORD maketh poor, and maketh rich: he bringeth low, and lifteth up.

9He will keep the feet of his saints, and the wicked shall be silent in darkness; for by strength shall no man prevail.

10The adversaries of the LORD shall be broken to pieces; out of heaven shall he thunder upon them: the LORD shall judge the ends of the earth; and he shall give strength unto his king, and exalt the horn of his anointed.

11And Elkanah went to Ramah to his house. And the child did minister unto the LORD before Eli the priest.

12Now the sons of Eli were sons of Belial; they knew not the LORD.

13And the priest’s custom with the people was, that, when any man offered sacrifice, the priest’s servant came, while the flesh was in seething, with a fleshhook of three teeth in his hand;

14And he struck it into the pan, or kettle, or caldron, or pot; all that the fleshhook brought up the priest took for himself. So they did in Shiloh unto all the Israelites that came thither.

15Also before they burnt the fat, the priest’s servant came, and said to the man that sacrificed, Give flesh to roast for the priest; for he will not have sodden flesh of thee, but raw.

16And if any man said unto him, Let them not fail to burn the fat presently, and then take as much as thy soul desireth; then he would answer him, Nay; but thou shalt give it me now: and if not, I will take it by force.

17Wherefore the sin of the young men was very great before the LORD: for men abhorred the offering of the LORD.

18But Samuel ministered before the LORD, being a child, girded with a linen ephod.

19Moreover his mother made him a little coat, and brought it to him from year to year, when she came up with her husband to offer the yearly sacrifice.

20And Eli blessed Elkanah and his wife, and said, The LORD give thee seed of this woman for the loan which is lent to the LORD. And they went unto their own home.

21And the LORD visited Hannah, so that she conceived, and bare three sons and two daughters. And the child Samuel grew before the LORD.

22Now Eli was very old, and heard all that his sons did unto all Israel; and how they lay with the women that assembled at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation.

23And he said unto them, Why do ye such things? for I hear of your evil dealings by all this people.

24Nay, my sons; for it is no good report that I hear: ye make the LORD’S people to transgress.

25If one man sin against another, the judge shall judge him: but if a man sin against the LORD, who shall intreat for him? Notwithstanding they hearkened not unto the voice of their father, because the LORD would slay them.

26And the child Samuel grew on, and was in favour both with the LORD, and also with men.

27And there came a man of God unto Eli, and said unto him, Thus saith the LORD, Did I plainly appear unto the house of thy father, when they were in Egypt in Pharaoh’s house?

28And did I choose him out of all the tribes of Israel to be my priest, to offer upon mine altar, to burn incense, to wear an ephod before me? and did I give unto the house of thy father all the offerings made by fire of the children of Israel?

29Wherefore kick ye at my sacrifice and at mine offering, which I have commanded in my habitation; and honourest thy sons above me, to make yourselves fat with the chiefest of all the offerings of Israel my people?

30Wherefore the LORD God of Israel saith, I said indeed that thy house, and the house of thy father, should walk before me for ever: but now the LORD saith, Be it far from me; for them that honour me I will honour, and they that despise me shall be lightly esteemed.

31Behold, the days come, that I will cut off thine arm, and the arm of thy father’s house, that there shall not be an old man in thine house.

32And thou shalt see an enemy in my habitation, in all the wealth which God shall give Israel: and there shall not be an old man in thine house for ever.

33And the man of thine, whom I shall not cut off from mine altar, shall be to consume thine eyes, and to grieve thine heart: and all the increase of thine house shall die in the flower of their age.

Summary

Hannah offers a prayer of praise celebrating the LORD who humbles the proud and exalts the lowly, promising that God will one day give strength to His king and anointed (vv. 1-10). Meanwhile Eli's sons Hophni and Phinehas, serving as priests, abuse their office by seizing meat meant for sacrifice and treating the LORD's offerings with contempt (vv. 12-17). Samuel, by contrast, grows up ministering faithfully before the LORD, wearing a linen ephod, while Hannah visits yearly bringing him a small robe (vv. 18-19). Eli rebukes his sons but they ignore him, so a man of God warns Eli that his sons will die on the same day and his priestly line will lose its honor, while the LORD will raise up a faithful priest in their place (vv. 27-35).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Samuel (for the early chapters), Nathan, and Gad (1 Chronicles 29:29). Originally one book with 2 Samuel in the Hebrew Bible. Written approximately 1050-950 BC. The book covers the transition from the judges to the monarchy — from the birth of Samuel through the death of Saul (approximately 1105-1010 BC).
Historical Context: Chapter 2 presents one of Scripture's sharpest contrasts — Hannah's song of praise alongside the corruption of Eli's sons. Hannah's prayer (vv.1-10) is a prophetic hymn celebrating God's power to reverse human circumstances: the barren bears, the hungry are fed, the poor are exalted. It anticipates Mary's Magnificat (Luke 1:46-55) in both structure and theology. The chapter then exposes the wickedness of Hophni and Phinehas — priests who steal from sacrifices, sleep with women at the tabernacle entrance, and treat God's offerings with contempt. Their sin is not merely personal immorality but institutional corruption: they cause the people to "abhor the offering of the LORD" (v.17). Eli's rebuke is too little, too late — he "honoured his sons above" God (v.29). The unnamed "man of God" delivers a devastating oracle: Eli's priestly line will be cut off, his sons will die on the same day, and God will raise up "a faithful priest" (v.35). This prophecy finds partial fulfillment in Samuel, further fulfillment in Zadok (who replaces Eli's line under Solomon), and ultimate fulfillment in Christ. Meanwhile, young Samuel "grew before the LORD" (v.21) — a quiet counterpoint to the noise of corruption. The chapter establishes the principle that governs the entire book: "them that honour me I will honour, and they that despise me shall be lightly esteemed" (v.30).
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: A song that moves from one woman's relief to a cosmic declaration about how God reverses every human condition, sons called worthless men who knew not the LORD despite serving as priests in His own house, meat stolen from sacrifices before the fat could even be burned to God, a rebuke that is verbal but toothless because Eli never removes his sons from office, and a prophecy naming the exact day both sons will die as its confirming sign
Hannah's Song of Praise (vv.1-10): This is not a simple thank-you for a baby — it is a theological declaration about God's character. Hannah moves from personal experience to cosmic truth. Her "horn is exalted" (v.1) — horn symbolizes strength and dignity. She declares God's holiness (v.2), His omniscience (v.3), and His sovereign power to reverse all human conditions (vv.4-8). The mighty are broken; the weak are strengthened. The full go hungry; the hungry are satisfied. The barren bears seven; the fruitful is forlorn. God kills and makes alive, impoverishes and enriches, humbles and exalts. Verse 8 reaches the theological summit: God raises the poor from the dust to sit with princes — "for the pillars of the earth are the LORD's." Verse 10 introduces the concept of God's "anointed" (mashiach/messiah) — the first reference to a king in this book, spoken before Israel has a king. Hannah prophesies what she cannot yet see: God will establish a king and exalt his horn. This points forward to David and ultimately to Christ.
The Sins of Eli's Sons (vv.11-17): "Sons of Belial" (KJV) / "worthless men" (ESV) — they "knew not the LORD" (v.12). This is not ignorance but willful rejection. Their sins are specific: (1) They took meat from sacrifices before the fat was burned to God — the fat belonged to the LORD (Leviticus 3:16-17), and taking it first was stealing from God. (2) They demanded raw meat instead of boiled — wanting choice cuts for roasting rather than accepting the priest's legitimate portion. (3) They used force if refused (v.16). The result: "men abhorred the offering of the LORD" (v.17). Corrupt leadership does not merely sin privately — it destroys the people's relationship with God. When priests make worship repulsive, the people stop coming. The section opens with the parting: Elkanah goes home to Ramah and the child is left ministering unto the LORD before Eli. That one verse sets the contrast the rest of the chapter runs on, a borrowed child serving faithfully in the same house as two sons of the priest who do not.
Samuel's Growth and Eli's Failure (vv.18-26): The contrast is deliberate. Samuel ministers faithfully in a linen ephod — the garment of priestly service. Hannah brings him a "little coat" yearly — a mother's love expressed in practical care despite the separation. Eli blesses Elkanah and Hannah, and God gives them five more children (v.21). Meanwhile, Eli hears of his sons' sexual sin with women at the tabernacle (v.22). His rebuke (vv.23-25) is verbal but toothless — he does not remove them from office. Verse 25 is sobering: "they hearkened not... because the LORD would slay them." God's judicial hardening has begun. Verse 26 echoes Luke 2:52 — Samuel grows "in favour both with the LORD, and also with men," just as Jesus would.
The Prophecy Against Eli's House (vv.27-36): An unnamed "man of God" delivers God's indictment. The charges: (1) God chose Eli's house for priestly service and gave them the fire offerings (vv.27-28). (2) They have "kicked at" God's sacrifices and honored sons above God (v.29). The sentence: (1) God revokes His promise — "I said indeed... for ever: but now the LORD saith, Be it far from me" (v.30). God's promises are conditional on faithfulness. (2) Eli's house will be cut off — no old men will survive (v.31). (3) Hophni and Phinehas will die on the same day as a sign (v.34). (4) God will raise a "faithful priest" (v.35). The governing principle is stated in v.30: "them that honour me I will honour, and they that despise me shall be lightly esteemed."

Deep Dive

Setting: Shiloh, around 1100 BC, at the close of the judges. Chapter 1 ended with Hannah handing her small son Samuel, perhaps three years old, over to Eli the priest to serve at the LORD's house for life. Chapter 2 sets two households side by side. On one side is Hannah, praising God for turning her life upside down, and her boy quietly growing in God's presence. On the other is Eli, the aging high priest, and his sons Hophni and Phinehas, who are robbing God's altar and abusing God's people at the very door of the tabernacle. The chapter moves back and forth between the two, like a camera cutting between scenes, and ends with a prophet announcing that God will remove Eli's family and raise up "a faithful priest."

Hannah's Prayer (vv1–11)

  • "And Hannah prayed and said" (v1). What follows is a song of praise, but Scripture calls it prayer. Praise is prayer too. She sings it the day she leaves her little boy at Shiloh, which makes her joy all the more striking: it rests on the LORD, not on what she still has in her arms.
  • "My horn is exalted in the LORD" (v1). The horn (qeren) of an ox or wild bull was a picture of strength and dignity. A raised horn is a head lifted high after defeat and shame. "My mouth derides my enemies" is literally "my mouth is wide open over" them. She does not name Peninnah, and the song quickly moves from her own story to God's ways with the whole world.
  • "There is none holy like the LORD… there is no rock like our God" (v2). Holiness here means God is in a class of his own. "Rock" is a favorite Old Testament picture of God as the one fixed, safe place (Deut 32:4; Ps 18:2). David will use the same words when God delivers him from Saul (2 Sam 22:2–3, 32). Hebrew poetry "rhymes" ideas rather than sounds, so each line restates and sharpens the one before.
  • "Talk no more so very proudly" (v3). The proud, like Peninnah, who mocked her year after year, are warned that "the LORD is a God of knowledge, and by him actions are weighed." God sees what is done in the home as well as at the altar, a quiet warning that hangs over Eli's sons in the next scene.
  • The great reversals (vv4–8). The heart of the song is a list of opposites: the mighty broken and the feeble strengthened, the full hiring themselves out for bread and the hungry satisfied, the barren bearing seven and the mother of many left "forlorn." Seven is the number of fullness. Hannah will in fact have five more children (v21), not seven. The point is a complete reversal, not a head count.
  • "The LORD kills and brings to life; he brings down to Sheol and raises up" (v6). Sheol is the Old Testament name for the realm of the dead, the grave. Moses' song says the same thing (Deut 32:39). At the least it means God holds life and death in his hand and can bring someone back from the brink. Many readers, including the early church, have also heard in it a hint of resurrection, which Hannah could not fully have understood but which the New Testament makes plain.
  • "He raises up the poor from the dust… to make them sit with princes" (v8). Psalm 113:7–9 repeats these lines almost word for word and ends with God giving "the barren woman a home." The "ash heap" was the town rubbish mound outside the walls, where beggars and outcasts sat (compare Job 2:8). "The pillars of the earth are the LORD's" pictures the world as a building God himself set on its foundations. He has the right to rearrange the furniture.
  • "Not by might shall a man prevail" (v9). This one line could stand over the whole of 1 and 2 Samuel: a shepherd boy will defeat a giant (ch 17), and a tall king will fall (ch 31).
  • "He will give strength to his king and exalt the horn of his anointed" (v10). This is startling. Israel has no king, and will not for decades. Yet Hannah ends her song with God's king. "Anointed" is mashiach, the word that gives us "Messiah." The song that opened with her own raised horn (v1) closes with the raised horn of God's anointed king. Read in its setting, it looks ahead to the kings Samuel will anoint, Saul and especially David. Read in the light of the whole Bible, it points to the Anointed One above all, Jesus.
  • Mary's song. When Mary learns she will bear the Messiah, her song (Luke 1:46–55) borrows heavily from Hannah's: rejoicing in God her Savior, the mighty brought down and the humble exalted, the hungry filled and the rich sent away empty. Zechariah's song also speaks of God raising "a horn of salvation" in the house of David (Luke 1:69).
  • "Then Elkanah went home to Ramah. And the boy was ministering to the LORD" (v11). The family goes home. The boy stays. "Ministering" is the word used for the service of Levites and priests. A small child could not do much, but he served "in the presence of Eli," learning and helping.
  • Commentators: Henry says Hannah rejoiced "not so much in her son as in her God," and on v6 that God "presides in births and burials." JFB notes that this is "the first place in Scripture where the word 'anointed,' or Messiah, occurs" in this sense, and Guzik makes the same claim. Strictly, priests are called "anointed" earlier (Lev 4:3), so the point is that this is the first time it is used of a king, God's Messiah. Henry reads v10 directly of "the Lord Christ," and Guzik ties the song to Mary's and Zechariah's songs in Luke 1. Guzik's distinctive application is that Hannah could rejoice on the very day she gave up her son, because her joy was in God himself.

Eli's Worthless Sons (vv12–21)

  • "The sons of Eli were worthless men. They did not know the LORD" (v12). "Worthless men" is literally "sons of Belial." The lexicon explains beliyya'al as "worthlessness," good for nothing. Hannah had begged Eli not to count her "a worthless woman," a daughter of Belial (1:16). Now the narrator applies the label to the priest's own sons. Later Jewish writers used Belial as a name for Satan, as Paul does (2 Cor 6:15), but in Samuel it is a word for character, not yet a name. "Did not know the LORD" does not mean they had never heard of him. They handled holy things every day. It means they had no real relationship with him and did not care what he wanted.
  • The three-pronged fork (vv13–14). The law fixed the priest's share of a peace offering: the breast and the right thigh (Lev 7:31–34), taken after the fat had been burned. Eli's sons replaced the law with their own "custom." Their servant stabbed a fork into the boiling pot and the priest kept whatever came up. Whatever the law said, they took what they liked.
  • "Before the fat was burned" (vv15–16). This was worse. The fat belonged to the LORD alone and had to be burned on the altar first (Lev 3:16; 7:23–25). Eli's sons demanded raw meat to roast, before God had received his portion. When a worshiper objected and asked them simply to burn the fat first, the servant threatened, "I will take it by force." Priests who were supposed to protect worship were using intimidation to rob it.
  • "The men treated the offering of the LORD with contempt" (v17). The word can mean to despise, or to cause something to be despised. Both are true. They despised God's offerings, and their greed made ordinary Israelites lose heart about bringing them. Their sin was "very great in the sight of the LORD" because it turned people away from God.
  • "Samuel was ministering before the LORD, a boy clothed with a linen ephod" (v18). The contrast is deliberate: "the young men" in v17, and now "a boy." The linen ephod was a simple priestly apron or vest, worn by ordinary priests (22:18) and by David when he danced before the ark (2 Sam 6:14). It is not the high priest's ornate ephod of Exodus 28.
  • "His mother used to make for him a little robe" (v19). One of the tenderest details in the Old Testament. Every year Hannah sewed a slightly larger robe and brought it to Shiloh. She had kept her vow and given him up, but she never stopped being his mother.
  • "May the LORD give you children by this woman for the petition she asked" (v20). Eli blesses them. "Petition" and "asked" are the same root, sha'al, that ran through chapter 1. Hannah "asked" and gave her asked-for child to the LORD, and now Eli prays that God will repay the gift.
  • "Indeed the LORD visited Hannah… three sons and two daughters" (v21). "Visited" (paqad) means God came to act on her behalf, as he "visited" Sarah (Gen 21:1). She gave one son and received five children. Meanwhile "the boy Samuel grew in the presence of the LORD," a refrain that keeps returning (vv21, 26; 3:19).
  • Commentators: Henry: "Nothing brings a greater reproach upon religion than ministers' covetousness, sensuality, and imperiousness." JFB notes that "pious people revolted at such rapacious and profane encroachments on the dues of the altar." Guzik says Belial was a pagan god, but the lexicon does not support that for this period. The word means "worthlessness," and only later became a name for Satan. He is right that knowing about God cannot be inherited from a godly father, and that the worst part of their sin was making people despise the LORD's offering. On Samuel, Henry pictures Hannah providing his clothes "during his apprenticeship at the tabernacle," and JFB adds that Eli's blessing "had a prophetic virtue." Guzik observes that God gave Hannah far more than she gave up.

Eli Rebukes His Sons (vv22–26)

  • "How they lay with the women who were serving at the entrance" (v22). Women served at the tabernacle entrance as far back as Moses (Exod 38:8). What their service was is not stated: perhaps cleaning, music or other help. Eli's sons used their position to sleep with them. This looks much like the fertility rites of Canaanite religion, brought right into the house of God. Some ancient witnesses, including the main Greek manuscripts, lack this clause. Most translations keep it, and the man of God's charge in vv27–29 focuses on the stolen offerings.
  • "Why do you do such things?" (vv23–24). Eli is "very old" (about 98 at his death, 4:15), and he hears about his sons secondhand, from the people. He speaks to them, and what he says is true, but it is only words. He does not remove them from the priesthood, which the law gave him power to do. God later charges him that "he did not restrain them" (3:13).
  • "If someone sins against a man, God will mediate for him, but if someone sins against the LORD, who can intercede for him?" (v25). There is a wordplay in Hebrew. "Mediate" and "intercede" are two forms of one verb, palal, which the lexicon gives as "intervene" or "interpose": to arbitrate between people, or to pray on someone's behalf. When people wrong each other, God (or, as some translations read elohim here, the judges) can step in and settle it. But when a man sins directly against God, who is left to step in between? Eli's sons were the very priests whose job was to stand between the people and God.
  • "For it was the will of the LORD to put them to death" (v25). This troubles readers. The text does not say God made them sin. They had long refused to listen, and God had now given them over to that refusal, as he hardened Pharaoh after Pharaoh hardened himself. Their deafness to their father was itself a sign that judgment was near.
  • "The boy Samuel continued to grow both in stature and in favor with the LORD and also with man" (v26). Luke uses nearly the same words of the boy Jesus: he "increased in wisdom and in stature and in favor with God and man" (Luke 2:52). Luke is clearly echoing Samuel.
  • Commentators: Henry calls Eli's rebuke "over-mild" and says "it is sometimes necessary that we put an edge upon the reproofs we give." Guzik argues the same way: Eli only talked when he needed to act. On v25 JFB insists it was "not God's preordination, but their own wilful and impenitent disobedience," and Guzik likewise says God gave them what they chose. On v26 Henry reflects that "God's grace is his own; he denied it to the sons of the high priest."

The LORD Rejects Eli's Household (vv27–36)

  • "There came a man of God to Eli" (v27). "Man of God" is an early name for a prophet (9:6). He is not named. In a time when "the word of the LORD was rare" (3:1), God still sent his word. He reminds Eli of God's grace: the LORD revealed himself to "the house of your father," Aaron's family, in Egypt, chose them to serve at the altar, burn incense and wear the ephod, and gave them a share of all Israel's offerings (vv27–28).
  • "Honor your sons above me by fattening yourselves" (v29). The charge includes Eli: "you scorn my sacrifices… fattening yourselves." He was eating from the stolen meat. When he dies, the narrator mentions that he was "heavy" (4:18).
  • "Those who honor me I will honor, and those who despise me shall be lightly esteemed" (v30). The Hebrew plays on weight. "Honor" (kabad) comes from the word for "heavy," and "lightly esteemed" (qalal) means "to be light." Eli made his sons weighty and God light, so God will make Eli's house light. The verse also explains how God can withdraw a promise: "I promised that your house… should go in and out before me forever, but now… far be it from me." God's promise of a lasting priesthood (Exod 29:9; Num 25:13) was given to Aaron's family as a whole. A family that despises God cannot claim it for themselves.
  • "There will not be an old man in your house" (vv31–33). Long life was a sign of God's blessing in the Old Testament. Eli's line will be marked by early deaths. "Cut off your strength" is literally "cut off your arm." One descendant will be left at the altar, but only "to weep his eyes out." This came true in stages: Saul's massacre of the priests at Nob, where only Abiathar escaped (22:17–20), and Solomon's removal of Abiathar from the priesthood, which 1 Kings 2:27 says fulfilled "the word of the LORD… concerning the house of Eli in Shiloh."
  • "Both of them shall die on the same day" (v34). The sign will come soon. Hophni and Phinehas both die in one battle when the ark is captured (4:11).
  • "I will raise up for myself a faithful priest… I will build him a sure house" (v35). "Faithful" and "sure" are the same Hebrew root ('aman, the root of "amen"): reliable, firm. In the short term Samuel, a Levite who served as priest and prophet, fills the gap (3:20 calls him "established," the same word). In the longer term Zadok, of Aaron's line through Eleazar, replaced Abiathar (1 Kgs 2:35), and his family held the high priesthood for centuries. The phrase "a sure house" will appear again when God promises David a lasting house (2 Sam 7:16). The faithful priest will serve "before my anointed forever," priest and king side by side.
  • "Please put me in one of the priests' places, that I may eat a morsel of bread" (v36). Eli's sons grabbed meat from worshipers' pots. Their descendants will beg for a crust.
  • Commentators: Guzik tells the story of Eric Liddell, who was handed a note with v30 before his Olympic race in 1924. He also argues that God did not break his word to Aaron, since Eli's family had no claim to a promise they despised, and he sees the faithful priest fulfilled in stages: Samuel, then Zadok, and finally Jesus, priest forever after the order of Melchizedek. The Tyndale notes point to Zadok. JFB reads v32 as a "successful rival for the office of high priest" rising from another family.

Pointing to Christ

Hannah's song ends with God's anointed king before Israel has a king. The chapter ends with a faithful priest who will do all that is in God's heart, before Israel has one. In Samuel and Zadok and David those promises began to come true, but each was a partial answer, and each man died. Hebrews says the full answer is Jesus, the faithful high priest (Heb 2:17; 3:1–2) who is also the anointed King, "a priest forever" (Heb 7:17). Eli's sons treated God's offerings with contempt and used their office to take. Jesus offered himself and used his office to give. And the boy who "grew in favor with the LORD and also with man" points to the boy in the temple at Jerusalem who did the same, and who would one day bring down the mighty and lift the poor from the dust in a way Hannah could only sing about.

Reflection

  • Worship Rooted in Who God Is (vv.1-8) — Hannah's song moves beyond personal gratitude into a declaration of God's holiness, sovereignty, and power to reverse human circumstances. Does your praise center on God's character, or only on what He's recently done for you?
  • Corrupt Leadership Poisons Worship for Everyone (v.17) — Eli's sons' greed and abuse of the sacrifices caused people to despise coming to the LORD's house at all. Where might your example, for better or worse, be shaping how others feel about approaching God?
  • Quiet Faithfulness Amid Public Corruption (vv.18-21) — While Hophni and Phinehas exploited their office, young Samuel simply grew in humble service, easily overlooked next to the scandal around him. Are you content to be faithful in obscurity while others chase attention?
  • Loving Family More Than God Is Still Idolatry (v.29) — Eli knew his sons' sin and rebuked them verbally, but never removed them from office because he honored them above God. What relationship or loyalty in your life might quietly be outranking your obedience to God?
  • God's Favor Cannot Be Presumed Upon (v.30) — God revoked what looked like a permanent promise to Eli's house because they despised Him. Are you resting on past privilege, or on present faithfulness?
