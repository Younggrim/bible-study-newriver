1Now Naaman, the commander of the army of the king of Aram, was a great man in his master’s sight and highly regarded, for through him the LORD had given victory to Aram. And he was a mighty man of valor, but he was a leper.

2At this time the Arameans had gone out in bands and had taken a young girl from the land of Israel, and she was serving Naaman’s wife.

3She said to her mistress, “If only my master would go to the prophet who is in Samaria, he would cure him of his leprosy.”

4And Naaman went and told his master what the girl from the land of Israel had said.

5“Go now,” said the king of Aram, “and I will send you with a letter to the king of Israel.” So Naaman departed, taking with him ten talents of silver, six thousand shekels of gold, and ten sets of clothing.

6And the letter that he took to the king of Israel stated: “With this letter I am sending my servant Naaman, so that you may cure him of his leprosy.”

7When the king of Israel read the letter, he tore his clothes and asked, “Am I God, killing and giving life, that this man expects me to cure a leper? Surely you can see that he is seeking a quarrel with me!”

8Now when Elisha the man of God heard that the king of Israel had torn his clothes, he sent a message to the king: “Why have you torn your clothes? Please let the man come to me, and he will know that there is a prophet in Israel.”

9So Naaman came with his horses and chariots and stood at the door of Elisha’s house.

10Then Elisha sent him a messenger, who said, “Go and wash yourself seven times in the Jordan, and your flesh will be restored, and you will be clean.”

11But Naaman went away angry, saying, “I thought that he would surely come out, stand and call on the name of the LORD his God, and wave his hand over the spot to cure my leprosy.

12Are not the Abanah and Pharpar, the rivers of Damascus, better than all the waters of Israel? Could I not have washed in them and been cleansed?” So he turned and went away in a rage.

13Naaman’s servants, however, approached him and said, “My father, if the prophet had told you to do some great thing, would you not have done it? How much more, then, when he tells you, ‘Wash and be cleansed’?”

14So Naaman went down and dipped himself in the Jordan seven times, according to the word of the man of God, and his flesh was restored and became like that of a little child, and he was clean.

15Then Naaman and all his attendants went back to the man of God, stood before him, and declared, “Now I know for sure that there is no God in all the earth except in Israel. So please accept a gift from your servant.”

16But Elisha replied, “As surely as the LORD lives, before whom I stand, I will not accept it.” And although Naaman urged him to accept it, he refused.

17“If you will not,” said Naaman, “please let me, your servant, be given as much soil as a pair of mules can carry. For your servant will never again make a burnt offering or a sacrifice to any other god but the LORD.

18Yet may the LORD forgive your servant this one thing: When my master goes into the temple of Rimmon to worship there, and he leans on my arm, and I bow down in the temple of Rimmon, may the LORD forgive your servant in this matter.”

19“Go in peace,” said Elisha. But after Naaman had traveled a short distance,

20Gehazi, the servant of Elisha the man of God, said, “Look, my master has spared this Aramean, Naaman, by not accepting what he brought. As surely as the LORD lives, I will run after him and get something from him.”

21So Gehazi pursued Naaman. And when Naaman saw him running toward him, he got down from the chariot to meet him and asked, “Is everything all right?”

22“Everything is all right,” Gehazi replied. “My master has sent me to say, ‘Look, two young men from the sons of the prophets have just now come to me from the hill country of Ephraim. Please give them a talent of silver and two sets of clothing.’”

23But Naaman insisted, “Please, take two talents.” And he urged Gehazi to accept them. Then he tied up two talents of silver in two bags along with two sets of clothing and gave them to two of his servants, who carried them ahead of Gehazi.

24When Gehazi came to the hill, he took the gifts from the servants and stored them in the house. Then he dismissed the men, and they departed.

25When Gehazi went in and stood before his master, Elisha asked him, “Gehazi, where have you been?” “Your servant did not go anywhere,” he replied.

26But Elisha questioned him, “Did not my spirit go with you when the man got down from his chariot to meet you? Is this the time to accept money and clothing, olive groves and vineyards, sheep and oxen, menservants and maidservants?

27Therefore, the leprosy of Naaman will cling to you and your descendants forever!” And as Gehazi left his presence, he was leprous—as white as snow.

1Now Naaman, captain of the host of the king of Syria, was a great man with his master, and honourable, because by him the LORD had given deliverance unto Syria: he was also a mighty man in valour, but he was a leper.

2And the Syrians had gone out by companies, and had brought away captive out of the land of Israel a little maid; and she waited on Naaman’s wife.

3And she said unto her mistress, Would God my lord were with the prophet that is in Samaria! for he would recover him of his leprosy.

4And one went in, and told his lord, saying, Thus and thus said the maid that is of the land of Israel.

5And the king of Syria said, Go to, go, and I will send a letter unto the king of Israel. And he departed, and took with him ten talents of silver, and six thousand pieces of gold, and ten changes of raiment.

6And he brought the letter to the king of Israel, saying, Now when this letter is come unto thee, behold, I have therewith sent Naaman my servant to thee, that thou mayest recover him of his leprosy.

7And it came to pass, when the king of Israel had read the letter, that he rent his clothes, and said, Am I God, to kill and to make alive, that this man doth send unto me to recover a man of his leprosy? wherefore consider, I pray you, and see how he seeketh a quarrel against me.

8And it was so, when Elisha the man of God had heard that the king of Israel had rent his clothes, that he sent to the king, saying, Wherefore hast thou rent thy clothes? let him come now to me, and he shall know that there is a prophet in Israel.

9So Naaman came with his horses and with his chariot, and stood at the door of the house of Elisha.

10And Elisha sent a messenger unto him, saying, Go and wash in the Jordan seven times, and thy flesh shall come again to thee, and thou shalt be clean.

11But Naaman was wroth, and went away, and said, Behold, I thought, He will surely come out to me, and stand, and call on the name of the LORD his God, and strike his hand over the place, and recover the leper.

12Are not Abana and Pharpar, rivers of Damascus, better than all the waters of Israel? may I not wash in them, and be clean? So he turned and went away in a rage.

13And his servants came near, and spake unto him, and said, My father, if the prophet had bid thee do some great thing, wouldest thou not have done it? how much rather then, when he saith to thee, Wash, and be clean?

14Then went he down, and dipped himself seven times in Jordan, according to the saying of the man of God: and his flesh came again like unto the flesh of a little child, and he was clean.

15And he returned to the man of God, he and all his company, and came, and stood before him: and he said, Behold, now I know that there is no God in all earth, but in Israel: now therefore, I pray thee, take a blessing of thy servant.

16But he said, As the LORD liveth, before whom I stand, I will receive none. And he urged him to take it; but he refused.

17And Naaman said, Shall there not then, I pray thee, be given to thy servant two mules’ burden of earth? for thy servant will henceforth offer neither burnt offering nor sacrifice unto other gods, but unto the LORD.

18In this thing the LORD pardon thy servant, that when my master goeth into the house of Rimmon to worship there, and he leaneth on my hand, and I bow myself in the house of Rimmon: when I bow down myself in the house of Rimmon, the LORD pardon thy servant in this thing.

19And he said unto him, Go in peace. So he departed from him a little way.

20But Gehazi, the servant of Elisha the man of God, said, Behold, my master hath spared Naaman this Syrian, in not receiving at his hands that which he brought: but, as the LORD liveth, I will run after him, and take somewhat of him.

21So Gehazi followed after Naaman. And when Naaman saw him running after him, he lighted down from the chariot to meet him, and said, Is all well?

22And he said, All is well. My master hath sent me, saying, Behold, even now there be come to me from mount Ephraim two young men of the sons of the prophets: give them, I pray thee, a talent of silver, and two changes of garments.

23And Naaman said, Be content, take two talents. And he urged him, and bound two talents of silver in two bags, with two changes of garments, and laid them upon two of his servants; and they bare them before him.

24And when he came to the tower, he took them from their hand, and bestowed them in the house: and he let the men go, and they departed.

25But he went in, and stood before his master. And Elisha said unto him, Whence comest thou, Gehazi? And he said, Thy servant went no whither.

26And he said unto him, Went not mine heart with thee, when the man turned again from his chariot to meet thee? Is it a time to receive money, and to receive garments, and oliveyards, and vineyards, and sheep, and oxen, and menservants, and maidservants?

27The leprosy therefore of Naaman shall cleave unto thee, and unto thy seed for ever. And he went out from his presence a leper as white as snow.

Summary

Naaman, a valued Syrian army commander who has leprosy, learns from a captured Israelite servant girl of a prophet in Samaria who could heal him (vv. 1-4). He travels to Elisha, who sends a messenger telling him simply to wash seven times in the Jordan (vv. 8-10). Naaman is enraged at the lack of ceremony and nearly leaves, but his servants persuade him to try it, and after washing his flesh is restored like a child's (vv. 11-14). He returns to confess the God of Israel and offer a gift, which Elisha refuses, though Naaman asks to take Israelite soil home with him (vv. 15-19). Elisha's servant Gehazi secretly chases Naaman down, lies to obtain silver and clothing, and is struck with Naaman's leprosy as punishment for his greed and deceit (vv. 20-27).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Jeremiah or a prophetic school. Originally one book with 1 Kings. Covers approximately 250 years (850-586 BC) from Elijah's translation through the Babylonian exile.
Historical Context: Chapter 5 contains one of the most beloved narratives in the Old Testament — the healing of Naaman the Syrian. This story is remarkable for several reasons: God heals a Gentile enemy commander; the catalyst is a captive Israelite slave girl; and the healing method deliberately humbles human pride. Syria (Aram) was Israel's primary military adversary during this period, making Naaman's healing an extraordinary act of grace toward an enemy. Jesus specifically referenced this story in Luke 4:27 to demonstrate that God's grace extends beyond Israel: "many lepers were in Israel in the time of Eliseus the prophet; and none of them was cleansed, saving Naaman the Syrian." The chapter also introduces Gehazi's moral failure — greed that transforms a servant of the prophet into a leper, contrasting the Gentile who gains faith with the Israelite who loses integrity.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: A commander introduced with every credential and then one devastating qualifier, but he was a leper, a captive slave girl possessing the one thing her master's whole army lacks, an instruction so simple it insults a man ready to attempt something difficult instead, seven dips in a muddy river restoring flesh like a little child's rather than only improving it, and Gehazi's greed transferring Naaman's leprosy onto himself and his descendants forever
Naaman's Need and the Slave Girl's Faith (vv.1-7): Naaman is introduced with impressive credentials — great, honorable, mighty, victorious. Then the devastating qualifier: "but he was a leper." All his achievements cannot cure his disease. Remarkably, verse 1 states that "by him the LORD had given deliverance unto Syria" — God used a pagan commander for His purposes. The catalyst for healing is a captured Israelite girl — unnamed, enslaved, powerless by every human measure — yet she possesses the one thing Naaman lacks: knowledge of the true God. Her faith is stunning: enslaved by the man whose armies raided her homeland, she still wishes him well. She does not hoard her knowledge or rejoice in his suffering. The king of Syria sends Naaman to the king of Israel with enormous wealth (ten talents of silver = approximately 750 pounds; six thousand shekels of gold). The king of Israel panics — "Am I God, to kill and to make alive?" He sees a political trap rather than a divine opportunity.
Elisha's Simple Command (vv.8-12): Elisha intervenes: "let him come now to me, and he shall know that there is a prophet in Israel" (v.8). When Naaman arrives with his impressive entourage, Elisha does not even come to the door — he sends a messenger with simple instructions: "Go and wash in the Jordan seven times." Naaman is furious. His pride is offended on multiple levels: Elisha did not come out personally, did not perform an impressive ritual, did not acknowledge Naaman's status, and prescribed the muddy Jordan over the clear rivers of Damascus. "Are not Abana and Pharpar, rivers of Damascus, better than all the waters of Israel?" (v.12). Naaman wanted a spectacular cure befitting his rank. God offered a humble obedience that required surrendering pride. This is the pattern of salvation in every age: God's method offends human pride because it requires submission rather than achievement.
Healing Through Obedience (vv.13-14): Naaman's servants reason with him: "if the prophet had bid thee do some great thing, wouldest thou not have done it?" — the problem is not difficulty but simplicity. Pride will attempt the impossible but refuses the humble. Naaman relents, dips seven times in the Jordan, and "his flesh came again like unto the flesh of a little child" — complete restoration, not partial improvement. The healing is total, immediate, and undeniable. Seven dips — the number of completion. Not six (Naaman's effort) but seven (God's perfection).
Naaman's Conversion and Elisha's Refusal (vv.15-19): Naaman returns a changed man: "now I know that there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel" (v.15). He offers payment; Elisha refuses absolutely: "As the LORD liveth, before whom I stand, I will receive none." Grace cannot be purchased. Naaman's request for two mule-loads of Israelite earth reveals his understanding that Yahweh is connected to the land of Israel — he wants to worship on Israelite soil even in Damascus. His confession about bowing in the house of Rimmon (the Syrian storm god) shows honest struggle: he must accompany his king in pagan worship as part of his official duties. Elisha's response — "Go in peace" — is neither explicit permission nor condemnation. It acknowledges the complexity of new faith in a pagan environment.
Gehazi's Greed and Judgment (vv.20-27): Gehazi's internal monologue reveals his heart: "my master hath spared Naaman this Syrian" — he sees grace as waste. He lies to Naaman, inventing a story about prophetic students needing silver and clothing. Naaman generously gives double what was asked. Gehazi hides the goods and lies to Elisha: "Thy servant went no whither." But Elisha's spirit was present: "Went not mine heart with thee?" The judgment is devastating: "The leprosy therefore of Naaman shall cleave unto thee, and unto thy seed for ever." Gehazi gains silver and loses his skin. The disease the Gentile shed through faith, the Israelite inherits through greed. The contrast is absolute: Naaman leaves clean; Gehazi leaves "a leper as white as snow."

Setting: About 850–845 BC, during the reign of Jehoram (Joram) son of Ahab over Israel, and probably Ben-hadad over Syria (Aram). Israel and Syria were enemies, fighting in pitched battles (1 Kgs 20; 22) and in smaller raids across the northern border. Elisha, the successor of Elijah, has become well known. The previous chapter showed him caring for widows, a farming family and hungry prophets. This chapter shows God's mercy reaching even further, to a foreign general, an enemy of Israel, who comes home believing in the LORD. And it shows the opposite: an Israelite, the prophet's own servant, who goes home a leper. Jesus later pointed to this story to show that God's grace has always reached outsiders (Luke 4:27).

Naaman Healed of Leprosy (vv1–14)

  • "Naaman, commander of the army of the king of Syria, was a great man with his master" (v1). Naaman was Syria's top general, the king's right-hand man, wealthy and honored. His name means "pleasant" or "gracious." A Jewish legend, passed on by Trapp and cited by Guzik, made him the archer whose random arrow killed Ahab (1 Kgs 22:34). The text does not say so.
  • "Because by him the LORD had given victory to Syria" (v1). This is a remarkable statement. The LORD gave victory to Israel's enemy. The God of Israel is not a local god. He rules the nations and their battles, even when they do not know him (compare Isa 10:5; 45:1–5; Amos 9:7).
  • "He was a mighty man of valor, but he was a leper" (v1). In Hebrew the last phrase is one word, metsora, "leprous," at the very end of the sentence, after all his titles. Every honor comes with a "but." The Hebrew word for this disease, tsara'at, covered a range of serious skin diseases, not only the condition doctors call leprosy today (Hansen's disease). The law also used it for mold in houses and fabric (Lev 13–14). Naaman could still serve at court, so it was probably not yet advanced, or Syria did not isolate the sick as Israel's law required (Lev 13:45–46). Either way, it was disfiguring, feared and, as far as anyone knew, incurable. Henry: "There is many a sickly, crazy body under rich and gay clothing."
  • "The Syrians on one of their raids had carried off a little girl from the land of Israel" (v2). "Raids" were bands of raiders who struck border villages, taking livestock and people. A little Israelite girl, torn from her family, is now a slave in a Syrian general's house. She is never named.
  • "Would that my lord were with the prophet who is in Samaria! He would cure him" (v3). She could have been bitter. Instead she wishes her master well and points him to the one place help can be found. She speaks with total confidence. There is no record of Elisha curing leprosy before this (Luke 4:27 says no Israelite leper was cleansed in his day), so her faith rests on what she knows of God and his prophet. Henry: "Servants may be blessings to the families where they are, by telling what they know of the glory of God."
  • "I will send a letter to the king of Israel" (v5). The king of Syria assumes a prophet works for the king, so he goes through official channels. Naaman takes an enormous payment: "ten talents of silver, six thousand shekels of gold, and ten changes of clothing." A talent was about 75 pounds, so ten talents was around 750 pounds of silver, and the gold was about 150 pounds. Fine clothing was a form of wealth too. It is a king's ransom.
  • "That you may cure him of his leprosy" (v6). The letter is blunt and, from Israel's side, alarming.
  • "Am I God, to kill and to make alive...?" (v7). Jehoram is right that only God can do this. The words echo Moses' song: "I kill and I make alive; I wound and I heal" (Deut 32:39). But he does not think to send for God's prophet. He tears his clothes in panic and assumes it is a trap: "he is seeking a quarrel with me." If the king cannot cure him, Syria has a pretext for war. JFB reads it as "alarm and suspicion that this was merely made an occasion for a quarrel." The king of Israel has no idea there is a God in Israel who can help. The question of chapter 1, "Is there no God in Israel?", hangs over him too.
  • "Let him come now to me, that he may know that there is a prophet in Israel" (v8). Elisha's message to the king carries a rebuke. The king of Israel acted as if there was no prophet. A foreign general will learn otherwise.
  • "Naaman came with his horses and chariots and stood at the door of Elisha's house" (v9). The scene is almost comic: a general's entire procession parked outside a prophet's front door.
  • "Elisha sent a messenger to him" (v10). Elisha does not even come out. It is a deliberate humbling. Naaman is used to being treated as the great man. Here, he is a leper at the door, like any other. "Go and wash in the Jordan seven times, and your flesh shall be restored, and you shall be clean." "Clean" is the word for ritual purity in the law of Moses, used of lepers healed and declared clean (Lev 14:7–9). Seven is the number of completeness. The law of cleansing for leprosy included sprinkling seven times (Lev 14:7). JFB: "Seven was the symbol of the covenant."
  • "I thought that he would surely come out to me and stand and call upon the name of the LORD his God, and wave his hand over the place" (v11). Naaman had pictured the whole scene in advance: the prophet coming out, a public prayer, a dramatic gesture over the diseased skin. He wanted a ceremony fit for his rank. Henry: "See in Naaman the folly of pride; a cure will not content him, unless he be cured with pomp and parade."
  • "Are not Abana and Pharpar, the rivers of Damascus, better than all the waters of Israel?" (v12). Damascus sits in a fertile oasis watered by rivers flowing from the snows of Mount Hermon. The Abana is usually identified with the Barada, which runs through the city, and the Pharpar with the Awaj to the south. They were clear and cold. The Jordan, by contrast, is a muddy, winding river in a deep valley. To Naaman, washing in it is not just pointless but humiliating. "He turned and went away in a rage."
  • "My father, it is a great word the prophet has spoken to you" (v13). His servants are wiser than he is. "My father" is an affectionate, respectful address. Their argument is simple: if he had told you to do something hard, you would have done it. Why not something easy? The difficulty was not the task but Naaman's pride. Henry: "we are to have our ears open to good advice, though brought by those below us." Twice in this story, Naaman's help comes from servants.
  • "He went down and dipped himself seven times in the Jordan" (v14). "Went down" is literal, down from the hills to the river, and fitting, since he must go down in every sense. "Dipped" (taval) means to plunge or immerse. It is the word used in the Greek translation with baptizō, the word later used for baptism. He does it "according to the word of the man of God." The power is not in the river but in obeying God's word.
  • "His flesh was restored like the flesh of a little child, and he was clean" (v14). The great general, the "great man" (v1), now has skin like a "little child," the same words used of the "little girl" in v2 (na'arah qetannah, and here na'ar qaton). The mighty man is made like the slave girl who pointed him to God. Jesus said, "Unless you turn and become like children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven" (Matt 18:3).
  • Commentators: Guzik notes that "mighty man of valor" is a title given to heroes like Gideon and David, and sees the captive girl as an unwilling missionary God used despite her loss. He passes on Dilday's estimate that Naaman's gifts would be worth over a million dollars today. On Naaman's anger, Guzik says the problem was his expectations: he wanted something grand and was offended by something simple. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's image of Naaman having to defeat two enemies within, pride and arguing. JFB does not know which modern rivers Abana and Pharpar were ("uncertain which"). The Tyndale notes contrast the king of Syria's turn to Israel's prophet with Ahaziah's turn to the god of Ekron in chapter 1.

Gehazi's Greed and Punishment (vv15–27)

  • "He returned to the man of God, he and all his company" (v15). He has to go back up the Jordan Valley, perhaps 25 miles or more. Like the one Samaritan leper who came back to thank Jesus (Luke 17:15–18), the foreigner returns. This time he comes in and "stood before him." This time Elisha sees him.
  • "Behold, I know that there is no God in all the earth but in Israel" (v15). It is one of the great confessions of the Old Testament, from a pagan. He does not say Israel's God is the strongest of the gods. He says there is no other. Compare Rahab (Josh 2:11) and Nebuchadnezzar (Dan 4:34–37). "Accept now a present from your servant." The man who came demanding a cure now calls himself "your servant," five times in four verses.
  • "As the LORD lives, before whom I stand, I will receive none" (v16). Elisha uses Elijah's oath again (1 Kgs 17:1). He refuses because God's grace cannot be paid for. Naaman must go home knowing he was healed for nothing, by the LORD's free kindness. The prophets often did accept gifts (1 Sam 9:7–8; 2 Kgs 4:42), so this is not a general rule. In this case, accepting would have made it look as though the healing could be bought.
  • "Two mule loads of earth" (v17). People in the ancient world commonly believed each god was tied to his own land. Naaman wants Israelite soil to build an altar in Syria, so he can worship the LORD on the LORD's own ground. It is a new believer's mixed understanding, but his heart is clear: "from now on your servant will not offer burnt offering or sacrifice to any god but the LORD." JFB lists several possible motives and concludes "it is not easy to say" which one it was.
  • "When my master goes into the house of Rimmon to worship there, leaning on my arm" (v18). Rimmon was the storm god of Damascus, known in Assyrian texts as Ramman or Hadad-Rimmon, "the thunderer" (Zech 12:11). The Hebrew spelling Rimmon also means "pomegranate," which may be a deliberate Israelite pun mocking the god's name, as with Baal-zebub ("lord of flies") in 1:2. As the king's chief officer, Naaman had to accompany him in state ceremonies. "Leaning on my arm" means serving as the king's chief aide (compare 7:2, 17). When the king bowed, Naaman would have to bow too. He asks the LORD's pardon in advance.
  • "Go in peace" (v19). Elisha neither approves nor condemns. He sends him off with a blessing. Interpreters have long debated this. Some read Elisha as quietly allowing Naaman's outward duty, as long as his heart worshiped only the LORD. Others note that "go in peace" is simply a farewell and does not answer the question, leaving Naaman's conscience in God's hands. Some, including Adam Clarke and Trapp, have suggested Naaman was asking pardon for past idolatry, but the verbs point to the future. JFB says Elisha gives "simply... the parting benediction" without passing judgment. What is clear is that Naaman's conscience is now tender. Before, bowing to Rimmon was nothing. Now it troubles him. Henry: "Those who truly hate evil, will make conscience of abstaining from all appearances of evil."
  • "My master has spared this Naaman the Syrian" (v20). Gehazi's words give him away. "This Naaman the Syrian": the contempt of an Israelite for a foreigner. Elisha's refusal looks to him like wasted opportunity. "As the LORD lives, I will run after him." He swears by the LORD to do something sinful, the same oath Elisha used to refuse the gift (v16), now used to grab it.
  • "He got down from the chariot to meet him" (v21). The general, who had expected the prophet to come out to him (v11), now gets down from his chariot to meet the prophet's servant. The change in Naaman is complete. "Is all well?" (literally shalom?). "All is well," Gehazi lies (v22).
  • "Two young men of the sons of the prophets... a talent of silver and two changes of clothing" (v22). Gehazi's story is clever: a request for others, not himself, in Elisha's name, and a modest sum compared with what Naaman brought. Naaman gladly gives twice as much, "two talents" (v23), about 150 pounds of silver, so heavy it takes two servants to carry.
  • "When he came to the hill, he took them from their hand and put them in the house" (v24). "The hill" (ophel) was probably a raised, fortified part of the city, perhaps Samaria. Gehazi sends Naaman's servants back before they reach Elisha's house, and hides the silver.
  • "Where have you been, Gehazi?" "Your servant went nowhere" (v25). A second lie, to his master's face. Like God's question to Adam, "Where are you?" (Gen 3:9), it gives him the chance to confess.
  • "Did not my heart go when the man turned from his chariot to meet you?" (v26). Elisha saw it all in his spirit. "Was it a time to accept money and garments, olive orchards and vineyards, sheep and oxen, male servants and female servants?" Elisha lists more than Gehazi took, perhaps because he knows what Gehazi planned to buy with it: land, livestock, servants, a comfortable life. At a moment when God was showing a pagan his free grace, Gehazi made it look as if the prophet's God could be paid. He undid the witness of the whole miracle.
  • "The leprosy of Naaman shall cling to you and to your descendants forever" (v27). "Cling" (dabaq) is the word used of Jehoram clinging to Jeroboam's sin (3:3). Gehazi took Naaman's silver, and he gets Naaman's leprosy with it. Henry: "If he will have Naaman's money, he shall have his disease with it." "So he went out from his presence a leper, like snow." "Like snow" describes the white, flaking skin of the disease (Exod 4:6; Num 12:10). The foreigner went home clean. The Israelite servant went out unclean. JFB calls the punishment "not too severe for the crime of Gehazi," which combined greed, lying and misusing the prophet's name. Gehazi appears again in 8:4–5, speaking to the king about Elisha's miracles, so the story there may come from before this, or he may have been allowed some contact despite his disease.
  • Commentators: Guzik compares Naaman's return to the one thankful leper in Luke 17, and reads Elisha's refusal as protecting the free grace of God, not as a rule against supporting ministers. He sees the earth request as a new believer's mixed understanding. On "leaning on my arm," he passes on Wiseman's note that it means the king's chief aide, not literal support. On Gehazi, Guzik passes on Morgan's point that his greed spoiled the free witness of the captive girl and of Elisha, and Dilday's contrast: a foreigner healed by faith, an Israelite cursed by dishonesty. JFB reads Rimmon as a Syrian deity, "probably the sun, or the planetary system," but most modern scholars, like the Tyndale notes, identify him with the storm god Hadad.

Pointing to Christ

Jesus preached his first sermon in Nazareth on this story: "There were many lepers in Israel in the time of the prophet Elisha, and none of them was cleansed, but only Naaman the Syrian" (Luke 4:27). The crowd was so angry they tried to throw him off a cliff. The point they hated is the heart of the gospel: God's grace goes to outsiders and enemies, and it is free. Naaman had to stop trying to pay, stop expecting a ceremony fit for his rank, go down and wash as he was told, and become like a little child. That is how anyone comes to Christ. Jesus did for lepers what Elisha could only send word about: he touched them and said, "Be clean" (Mark 1:41). And in Gehazi's punishment there is a dark mirror of the cross. Gehazi took on another man's leprosy by greed. Jesus took on our uncleanness by love, "made... to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Cor 5:21).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes that Naaman's story is a picture of salvation: the sinner must come God's way, not his own. The Jordan was not chosen for its water quality but for its humbling effect — it required Naaman to abandon his pride and his preferences. Guzik notes that Gehazi's sin was not merely greed but the attempt to put a price on grace, turning God's free gift into a commercial transaction and misrepresenting the prophet's character.

Reflection

  • An Unlikely Witness (vv.2-3) — A captured, unnamed Israelite slave girl tells her captor's wife about the prophet in Samaria who could heal him. What platform are you waiting for before you speak up about what God has done?
  • Pride Refuses the Simple Cure (v.12) — Naaman storms off in a rage because Elisha's cure sounds too plain and the Jordan too unimpressive compared to the rivers of Damascus. What has God made simple that your pride is complicating?
  • Healed Through Obedience (v.14) — Naaman only recovers once he sets aside his expectations and does exactly what he was told, dipping seven times in the Jordan. What command of God are you overthinking instead of simply obeying?
  • Grace That Can't Be Bought (v.16) — Elisha flatly refuses Naaman's payment, even after a miracle worth any price. Are there ways you try to earn or repay what God has already given you freely?
  • Proximity Without Transformation (v.20) — Gehazi has lived beside Elisha through every miracle in this book, yet greed still rules his heart the moment Naaman is out of sight. Is your closeness to godly people and godly things translating into real character, or just familiarity?
