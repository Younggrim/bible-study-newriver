1I speak the truth in Christ; I am not lying, as confirmed by my conscience in the Holy Spirit.

2I have deep sorrow and unceasing anguish in my heart.

3For I could wish that I myself were cursed and cut off from Christ for the sake of my brothers, my own flesh and blood,

4the people of Israel. Theirs is the adoption as sons; theirs the divine glory and the covenants; theirs the giving of the law, the temple worship, and the promises.

5Theirs are the patriarchs, and from them proceeds the human descent of Christ, who is God over all, forever worthy of praise! Amen.

6It is not as though God’s word has failed. For not all who are descended from Israel are Israel.

7Nor because they are Abraham’s descendants are they all his children. On the contrary, “Through Isaac your offspring will be reckoned.”

8So it is not the children of the flesh who are God’s children, but it is the children of the promise who are regarded as offspring.

9For this is what the promise stated: “At the appointed time I will return, and Sarah will have a son.”

10Not only that, but Rebecca’s children were conceived by one man, our father Isaac.

11Yet before the twins were born or had done anything good or bad, in order that God’s plan of election might stand,

12not by works but by Him who calls, she was told, “The older will serve the younger.”

13So it is written: “Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated.”

14What then shall we say? Is God unjust? Certainly not!

15For He says to Moses: “I will have mercy on whom I have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I have compassion.”

16So then, it does not depend on man’s desire or effort, but on God’s mercy.

17For the Scripture says to Pharaoh: “I raised you up for this very purpose, that I might display My power in you, and that My name might be proclaimed in all the earth.”

18Therefore God has mercy on whom He wants to have mercy, and He hardens whom He wants to harden.

19One of you will say to me, “Then why does God still find fault? For who can resist His will?”

20But who are you, O man, to talk back to God? Shall what is formed say to Him who formed it, “Why did You make me like this?”

21Does not the potter have the right to make from the same lump of clay one vessel for special occasions and another for common use?

22What if God, intending to show His wrath and make His power known, bore with great patience the vessels of His wrath, prepared for destruction?

23What if He did this to make the riches of His glory known to the vessels of His mercy, whom He prepared in advance for glory—

24including us, whom He has called not only from the Jews, but also from the Gentiles?

25As He says in Hosea: “I will call them ‘My People’ who are not My people, and I will call her ‘My Beloved’ who is not My beloved,”

26and, “It will happen that in the very place where it was said to them, ‘You are not My people,’ they will be called ‘sons of the living God.’”

27Isaiah cries out concerning Israel: “Though the number of the Israelites is like the sand of the sea, only the remnant will be saved.

28For the Lord will carry out His sentence on the earth thoroughly and decisively.”

Summary

Paul expresses deep sorrow and anguish over his fellow Israelites, even wishing he could be cursed in their place, since they possess the adoption, the covenants, the law, and the promises, and descended from the patriarchs and from Christ himself (vv. 1-5). He argues that God's word has not failed, because not all who descend from Israel are true Israel — God's promise works through his sovereign choice, as shown when he chose Jacob over Esau before either had done anything (vv. 6-13). Paul defends God's justice by pointing to his declaration that he will have mercy on whom he chooses, comparing God to a potter who has the right to shape the clay as he wills, whether for honor or dishonor (vv. 14-23). He explains that God called people from among both Jews and Gentiles, fulfilling the prophecy that those who were not his people would be called his children, though only a remnant of Israel would be saved (vv. 24-29). The chapter ends by noting that Gentiles who did not pursue righteousness attained it by faith, while Israel, pursuing the law, stumbled over the stumbling stone (vv. 30-33).

Authorship & Background

Author: Paul (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapters 9-11 form a unit addressing the question: if justification is by faith, what about Israel? Has God's promise to them failed? Chapter 9 addresses God's sovereign right to choose; chapter 10 addresses Israel's responsibility; chapter 11 addresses God's future plan for Israel.
Classification: Epistle — Pauline
Key Themes: Paul's willingness to be accursed for brothers he has just finished grieving over, Isaac chosen over Ishmael and Jacob over Esau before either had done good or evil, a potter's authority over clay answering an objection about fairness before it is fully raised, Hosea and Isaiah both quoted to include Gentiles among vessels of mercy, and Israel stumbling at a stumblingstone it sought by works rather than by faith
Paul's Grief (vv.1-5): Paul begins with anguish — he would be willing to be "accursed from Christ" for his Jewish brothers (v.3). This is not theological abstraction — it is personal heartbreak. Paul lists Israel's privileges: adoption, glory, covenants, the Law, worship, promises, patriarchs, and Christ Himself (vv.4-5).
God's Sovereign Election (vv.6-18): Paul's argument: God's word has not failed because not all physical descendants of Israel are true Israel (v.6). God chose Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau — before they were born, before they had done good or evil (v.11). God's choice is based on His purpose, not human merit.
The Potter and the Clay (vv.19-23): Paul anticipates the objection: "Why does God still find fault? Who can resist His will?" His answer: "Who art thou that repliest against God?" (v.20). The potter has authority over the clay. God has the right to show mercy to whom He chooses and to harden whom He chooses.
Vessels of Mercy, Called from the Gentiles (vv.24-29): The vessels of mercy are identified, and the identification is the surprise: not of the Jews only, but also of the Gentiles. Two prophets are then made to say it. Hosea is quoted on the reversal of the judgment names, I will call them my people, which were not my people, and in the place where it was said unto them, Ye are not my people, there shall they be called the children of the living God. Then Isaiah on the remnant, though the number of the children of Israel be as the sand of the sea, a remnant shall be saved, with the harder line following it, except the Lord of Sabaoth had left us a seed, we had been as Sodom.
Israel Stumbled at That Stumblingstone (vv.30-33): The chapter closes by stating the outcome as a paradox and then explaining it in one clause. The Gentiles, which followed not after righteousness, have attained to righteousness, and Israel, which followed after the law of righteousness, hath not attained. Wherefore? Because they sought it not by faith, but as it were by the works of the law. The image chosen is a stone in the road rather than a wall, for they stumbled at that stumblingstone, and Isaiah is quoted to make the stone a person, whosoever believeth on him shall not be ashamed.

Deep Dive

Setting: Paul writes from Corinth around AD 57 to a church of Jewish and Gentile believers he has not yet visited. Chapter 8 ended on the letter's highest note: nothing in all creation can separate believers "from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord" (8:39). That raises a painful question. The promises were first made to Abraham's family, yet most of Paul's fellow Jews have not believed in Jesus. If God's word to Israel seems to have failed, how can the church trust his word in chapter 8? Chapters 9–11 answer that question as one unit. Chapter 9 makes the first move: God's word has not failed, because God's people were never defined simply by birth, but by his promise and his free mercy.

Paul's Anguish over Israel (vv1–5)

  • "I am speaking the truth in Christ—I am not lying" (v1). The change of tone is sudden. Paul has just been rejoicing, and now he swears an oath, calling Christ, his conscience and the Holy Spirit as witnesses. Some may have thought the apostle to the Gentiles had written off his own people. The opposite is true.
  • "Great sorrow and unceasing anguish" (v2). The cause becomes plain in 10:1: his prayer for them "is that they may be saved." The man who wrote "no condemnation" (8:1) grieves for those still outside it.
  • "Accursed and cut off from Christ" (v3). "Accursed" is anathema. In the Greek Old Testament it translates the Hebrew herem, something devoted to God for destruction (Lev 27:28–29; Josh 6:17). Paul says he could wish this. The wording expresses a wish that cannot actually be granted, since nothing can separate him from Christ (8:38–39), but it shows how far his love would go. It echoes Moses after the golden calf: "if you will forgive their sin—but if not, please blot me out of your book" (Exod 32:32). In the end only one person was ever made a curse for others (Gal 3:13).
  • "They are Israelites" (v4). Until now Romans has mostly said "Jews." From here through chapter 11 Paul mostly says "Israel," the covenant name, given to Jacob by God himself (Gen 32:28). Paul is speaking of the people as God's chosen family, not only as an ethnic group.
  • Eight privileges (vv4–5). The adoption: God called Israel "my firstborn son" (Exod 4:22), and the word (huiothesia) is the one Paul just used for believers (8:15, 23). The glory: God's presence filling the tabernacle (Exod 40:34). The covenants: with Abraham, at Sinai, with David, and the new covenant promised "with the house of Israel" (Jer 31:31). The law, the worship (latreia, the temple service), the promises, the patriarchs, and greatest of all the Christ, who came from Israel "according to the flesh."
  • "The Christ, who is God over all, blessed forever" (v5). The oldest manuscripts have almost no punctuation, so editors must decide where the sentence breaks. The ESV reads it as calling Christ "God over all." Its footnote gives the other option, which ends the sentence earlier and makes the rest a separate praise to the Father ("God be blessed forever"). Those who prefer that note that Paul seldom calls Christ "God" directly. Those who read it of Christ note that Paul's standalone blessings normally put "blessed" first, that "according to the flesh" leads naturally into a contrast, and that nearly all early church writers read it this way. Most modern translations agree with the ESV. Either way, Christ's deity does not rest on this verse alone (John 1:1; Phil 2:6; Titus 2:13).
  • Commentators: Henry calls Paul's wish "a very high pang of zeal and affection," and notes it is put as a conditional wish, not a request, since it was not a means God had appointed. JFB insists on the full force of the word, saying others "unwarrantably soften the sense of the word 'accursed.'" On v5 Henry calls the phrase "a very full proof of the Godhead of Christ," and JFB rejects the separate-praise reading, concluding there is "no doxology at all, but a naked statement of fact." Guzik agrees, and adds that Paul's love is the more remarkable because his own people were his fiercest persecutors.

God's Sovereign Choice (vv6–18)

  • "It is not as though the word of God has failed" (v6). This is the thesis of chapters 9–11. "Failed" (ekpiptō) means to fall from its place. It is used of flowers dropping off (James 1:11) and of ships running aground (Acts 27:17). God's promise has not run aground.
  • "Not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel" (v6). There is an Israel within Israel. The prophets had long spoken of a faithful remnant inside the larger nation (Isa 10:20–22). Paul is not saying the church replaces Israel, but that birth into the nation never by itself made someone a child of the promise. Chapter 11 shows God's purposes for ethnic Israel continue.
  • "Through Isaac shall your offspring be named" (v7). Paul's first proof is Genesis 21:12. Abraham had two sons, Ishmael and Isaac, but the promise ran through Isaac alone. "Children of the flesh" are simply physical descendants. "Children of the promise" are those God's promise brought into being, and they are the ones "counted as offspring" (v8). "Counted" (logizomai) is the word of chapter 4, righteousness "counted" to Abraham. Isaac was a child of promise in the plainest sense: Sarah was past childbearing, and God's word at Mamre brought him into being (v9; Gen 18:10, 14).
  • Rebekah's twins (vv10–12). An objector could say Ishmael was the son of a slave woman, so of course he was passed over. Paul's second proof removes every human difference. Jacob and Esau had the same father and the same mother, and were conceived together. Before they were born or had done "anything either good or bad," God said, "The older will serve the younger" (Gen 25:23). The choice rested "not because of works but because of him who calls." "Election" (eklogē) means choice or selection. In the New Testament it is always God's choosing.
  • "Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated" (v13). This is Malachi 1:2–3, written more than a thousand years after the twins, about the nations of Israel and Edom. In Hebrew idiom "love" and "hate" in a contrast like this often mean "choose" and "set aside." Jacob "loved Rachel more than Leah," and "the LORD saw that Leah was hated" (Gen 29:30–31). Jesus says his followers must "hate" father and mother (Luke 14:26). The lexicon notes this sense for Romans 9:13. It describes God's choosing, not a grudge, and Esau was richly blessed in this life (Gen 33:9).
  • The main views of election (vv10–13). Here faithful Christian traditions divide. The Reformed (Calvinist) reading, going back to Augustine, says Paul is explaining why some Israelites are saved and others not: God chose particular persons for salvation before birth, without regard to anything in them. The Arminian and Wesleyan reading agrees God's choice is unearned, but holds that it rests on his foreknowledge of faith, or is a choice of a people defined by faith in Christ. It points to vv30–32, where faith divides those who attain righteousness from those who do not. A third reading, held by many scholars across traditions, sees election here as corporate and historical. Genesis 25:23 says "two nations are in your womb," and Malachi speaks of peoples. On this view the question is which line would carry the promise in history, and Paul is defending God's freedom to choose his instruments and now to include Gentiles by faith. Lutherans affirm God's free election to salvation while denying that God chooses anyone for damnation. All of these views agree on what the text plainly says: God's choice is not earned, it rests on "him who calls," and it does not make God's word fail.
  • "Is there injustice on God's part? By no means!" (v14). Paul raises the objection himself (as in 3:5; 6:1) and answers with Scripture, not philosophy.
  • "I will have mercy on whom I have mercy" (v15). This is Exodus 33:19, spoken to Moses right after the golden calf, when the whole nation deserved destruction. The setting matters: it is mercy given to people who had earned only judgment. Mercy that is owed is no longer mercy.
  • "It depends not on human will or exertion" (v16). "Exertion" is literally "running," a runner straining toward the goal. Salvation is not won by wanting it hard enough or trying hard enough. It rests on "God, who has mercy."
  • Pharaoh (v17). The quotation is Exodus 9:16. "Raised you up" probably means "brought you onto the stage of history," or kept him standing through the plagues so far, rather than created him. The purpose was mission: "that my name might be proclaimed in all the earth" (compare Josh 2:10).
  • "He hardens whomever he wills" (v18). "Hardens" (sklērynō) means to make stubborn. Exodus says both things. God announced in advance that he would harden Pharaoh's heart (Exod 4:21), Pharaoh "hardened his heart" himself (8:15, 32), and later "the LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh" (9:12). The Tyndale notes take these together: Pharaoh was responsible for his refusals, even as God worked out his purpose through them. Many interpreters understand God's hardening as judicial, confirming a person in the stubbornness he has chosen, rather than creating resistance in a soft heart. Others take it as a more direct act of God's sovereign will. The text itself does not settle the mechanism. It insists on God's freedom.
  • Commentators: Henry's line on v6 is memorable: "Grace does not run in the blood." He reads Jacob and Esau "not in their own persons, but as ancestors," that is, as two peoples, and calls the choosing "typical," a picture meant "to shadow forth some other election." JFB disagrees sharply. It warns against "hastily inferring" that the election here is national, and reads vv11–13 as personal election to salvation where "the sole ground of distinction lay in the unconditional choice of God." On Pharaoh, JFB says God "only forbore to make him good," and describes hardening as "judicially abandoning them to the hardening influence of sin itself." Henry similarly speaks of God withdrawing "softening grace" and leaving Pharaoh to himself. Guzik reads "hated" as accepted versus rejected for the covenant inheritance, and says God did not push an unwilling Pharaoh into hardness but let his heart go its own way. Guzik explains the name Israel as meaning that one is governed by God. The lexicon does not support that: it gives "God persists" or "God contends," and Genesis 32:28 explains it as "you have striven with God."

God's Wrath and Mercy (vv19–29)

  • "Why does he still find fault? For who can resist his will?" (v19). The second objection is the one still asked: if God hardens whom he wills, how can he blame anyone? Paul neither denies that God blames nor explains away God's will.
  • "Who are you, O man, to answer back to God?" (v20). "O man" sets a mere human against the Creator. Paul is not forbidding honest questions, as the Psalms and Job show. He rebukes putting God on trial. "Will what is molded say to its molder" echoes Isaiah 29:16 and 45:9.
  • The potter and the clay (v21). The image comes from Jeremiah 18:1–6, where the prophet watches a potter reshape a spoiled pot, and Isaiah 64:8. "Vessel" (skeuos) means a container or implement, and Paul uses it of people. Acts 9:15 calls Paul God's "chosen instrument," literally a vessel of choosing. In Jeremiah the potter responds to what a nation does: if it turns from evil, God relents (Jer 18:7–10). Arminian readers stress that context. Reformed readers stress Paul's point that the potter has the right to decide. Both see the image as about God's rights as Creator.
  • "What if God…" (vv22–23). Paul begins a question and, as in 5:12, never finishes it. But God "has endured with much patience" the vessels of wrath. Judgment was real but delayed, the patience that 2:4 says is meant to lead to repentance.
  • "Prepared for destruction"… "prepared beforehand for glory" (vv22–23). The two "prepared" phrases differ in Greek. The vessels of wrath are katērtismena, "made ready" or "fitted," a passive form that names no one as the one who did it. The lexicon lists it as passive, though some read it as "having made themselves ready." The vessels of mercy are those "which he has prepared beforehand" (proetoimazō), an active verb with God as subject, the verb of Ephesians 2:10. Many interpreters think Paul deliberately names God as preparing people for glory but not for destruction. Reformed interpreters often reply that God's will stands behind both, however Paul phrases them.
  • "Even us whom he has called, not from the Jews only but also from the Gentiles" (v24). This is where the argument has been heading. If God was free to choose Isaac over Ishmael and Jacob over Esau, he is free to form a people from Jews and Gentiles together. The word "called" picks up 8:30.
  • Hosea (vv25–26). Paul quotes Hosea 2:23 and 1:10. Hosea named his children "Not My People" and "No Mercy" as signs of judgment on the northern kingdom, then promised God would reverse the names. Paul extends the principle: if God could call unfaithful Israelites back, he can call Gentiles who were never his people. "Sons of the living God" recalls 8:14–16.
  • Isaiah (vv27–28). Isaiah 10:22–23 comes from the Assyrian crisis, when the nation was nearly swept away. Though Israel was "as the sand of the sea," as promised to Abraham (Gen 22:17), "only a remnant of them will be saved." Paul quotes close to the Greek translation of Isaiah. The remnant is the key to chapter 11: "a remnant, chosen by grace" (11:5).
  • "If the Lord of hosts had not left us offspring" (v29). Isaiah 1:9. "Lord of hosts" is the Hebrew title YHWH Sabaoth, the Lord of heaven's armies, which the Greek simply transliterates. Sodom and Gomorrah were wiped out with no survivors (Gen 19). That any of Israel remains is mercy, not entitlement.
  • Commentators: Henry says the vessels of wrath are "fitted by their own sin and self-hardening," and sums up the contrast: "Sinners fit themselves for hell, but it is God that prepares saints for heaven." JFB stays nearer the Reformed reading, explaining "vessels of wrath" as "destined to wrath" and saying God is "not only 'afore preparing,' but in due time effectually 'calling'" his people. Guzik agrees with Henry that Paul does not say God prepared the vessels of wrath, adding that they manage that well enough themselves. On Hosea, JFB notes the passage related "immediately, not to the heathen, but to the kingdom of the ten tribes," the same point the Tyndale notes make, and Henry draws the comfort from it: "Former badness is no bar to God's present grace and mercy."

Israel's Unbelief (vv30–33)

  • "What shall we say, then?" (v30). Paul turns from God's side to the human side. Chapter 9 ends not with God's decree but with faith and unbelief, and Paul holds both together without reducing them to a formula.
  • "Gentiles who did not pursue righteousness have attained it" (v30). The language is from the racetrack. Those who were not even running reached the finish, while the ones running hardest missed it. The difference is "a righteousness that is by faith" (1:17; 3:22).
  • "Israel who pursued a law that would lead to righteousness" (v31). Paul does not fault Israel's zeal (10:2). He faults the direction. They treated the law as a ladder to climb, "as if it were based on works" (v32), when the law itself pointed to faith (3:21; 4:3).
  • "They have stumbled over the stumbling stone" (v32). "Stumbling" is proskomma, and the parallel "rock of offense" uses skandalon, originally the bait-stick of a trap, then a snare or anything that trips. The cause of the fall is not a hidden decree but a person, Christ. A crucified Messiah was the very thing that tripped many (1 Cor 1:23).
  • "Behold, I am laying in Zion a stone" (v33). Paul joins two Isaiah texts. Isaiah 28:16 promises a tested cornerstone laid in Zion. Isaiah 8:14 says the Lord himself will be "a stone of offense and a rock of stumbling" to both houses of Israel. Peter combines the same two texts with Psalm 118:22 (1 Pet 2:6–8), which suggests the early church had a well-known set of "stone" passages about Jesus. The stone that trips some is the foundation for others. And the chapter that began with God's choosing ends with an open invitation: "whoever believes in him will not be put to shame," a line Paul repeats in 10:11.
  • Commentators: All three see vv30–33 as the human side of the chapter. Guzik stresses that Paul here puts responsibility squarely on Israel, and treats this as the other side of God's sovereign choice, not a contradiction of it. Henry says Israel "sought, but not in the right way," and grieves that for many "the rock of salvation" became "a rock of offence." He insists that since they had a fair offer, if they perish "their blood is upon their own heads." JFB identifies the stone as Christ and notes that "Two Messianic predictions are here combined."

Pointing to Christ

Romans 9 begins and ends with Christ. Israel's greatest privilege is that the Christ came from them, "God over all, blessed forever," and their great tragedy is that they stumbled over that very stone. Between those points Paul shows a God who has always worked by promise and mercy, never by birth or effort: Isaac, Jacob, mercy after a golden calf, a remnant kept from becoming Sodom. Christians still disagree about how God's choosing works, but the chapter's own answer to who belongs to God is the cornerstone laid in Zion. Paul, who would have taken the curse for his people, points to the one who actually did, and whoever trusts in him "will not be put to shame."

Reflection

  • Grieving for the Lost (v.3) — Paul says he would be willing to be cut off from Christ himself for the sake of his unsaved kinsmen. How deeply do you grieve over people in your life who don't know Christ?
  • Not All Israel Is Israel (v.6) — Paul insists God's word hasn't failed, because ethnic descent was never the same as true belonging to God. What spiritual privileges might you be relying on instead of genuine faith?
  • Election Rests on God's Purpose, Not Human Merit (v.11) — God chose Jacob over Esau before either had done anything, good or bad. Does the doctrine of election comfort you or unsettle you, and why?
  • Mercy Originates With God (v.16) — Paul says it depends not on human will or effort but on God who shows mercy. How does knowing your salvation began in God's mercy shape your posture toward Him?
  • The Potter's Right Over the Clay (vv.20-21) — When God's ways seem confusing or unfair, Paul's answer isn't an explanation but a call to trust the Potter's right to shape the clay as He wills. Where do you struggle to trust God's sovereignty without full understanding?
  • Faith, Not Works, Reaches the Goal (vv.32-33) — Israel pursued righteousness by works and stumbled; the chapter closes with "whoever believes." Where might you be pursuing God's approval through performance rather than through faith?
