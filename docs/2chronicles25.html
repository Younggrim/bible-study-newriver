1Amaziah was twenty-five years old when he became king, and he reigned in Jerusalem twenty-nine years. His mother’s name was Jehoaddan; she was from Jerusalem.

2And he did what was right in the eyes of the LORD, but not wholeheartedly.

3As soon as the kingdom was firmly in his grasp, Amaziah executed the servants who had murdered his father the king.

4Yet he did not put their sons to death, but acted according to what is written in the Law, in the Book of Moses, where the LORD commanded: “Fathers must not be put to death for their children, and children must not be put to death for their fathers; each is to die for his own sin.”

5Then Amaziah gathered the people of Judah and assigned them according to their families to commanders of thousands and of hundreds. And he numbered those twenty years of age or older throughout Judah and Benjamin and found 300,000 chosen men able to serve in the army, bearing the spear and shield.

6He also hired 100,000 mighty men of valor from Israel for a hundred talents of silver.

7But a man of God came to him and said, “O king, do not let the army of Israel go with you, for the LORD is not with Israel—not with any of the Ephraimites.

8Even if you go and fight bravely in battle, God will overthrow you before the enemy, for God has power to help and power to overthrow.”

9Amaziah asked the man of God, “What should I do about the hundred talents I have given to the troops of Israel?” And the man of God replied, “The LORD is able to give you much more than this.”

10So Amaziah dismissed the troops who had come to him from Ephraim and sent them home. And they were furious with Judah and returned home in great anger.

11Amaziah, however, summoned his strength and led his troops to the Valley of Salt, where he struck down 10,000 men of Seir,

12and the army of Judah also captured 10,000 men alive. They took them to the top of a cliff and threw them down so that all were dashed to pieces.

13Meanwhile the troops that Amaziah had dismissed from battle raided the cities of Judah, from Samaria to Beth-horon. They struck down 3,000 people and carried off a great deal of plunder.

14When Amaziah returned from the slaughter of the Edomites, he brought back the gods of the Seirites, set them up as his own gods, bowed before them, and burned sacrifices to them.

15Therefore the anger of the LORD burned against Amaziah, and He sent him a prophet, who said, “Why have you sought this people’s gods, which could not deliver them from your hand?”

16While he was still speaking, the king asked, “Have we made you the counselor to the king? Stop! Why be struck down?” So the prophet stopped, but he said, “I know that God has determined to destroy you, because you have done this and have not heeded my advice.”

17Then Amaziah king of Judah took counsel and sent word to the king of Israel Jehoash son of Jehoahaz, the son of Jehu. “Come, let us meet face to face,” he said.

18But Jehoash king of Israel replied to Amaziah king of Judah: “A thistle in Lebanon sent a message to a cedar in Lebanon, saying, ‘Give your daughter to my son in marriage.’ Then a wild beast in Lebanon came along and trampled the thistle.

19You have said, ‘Look, I have defeated Edom,’ and your heart has become proud and boastful. Now stay at home. Why should you stir up trouble so that you fall—you and Judah with you?”

20But Amaziah would not listen, for this had come from God in order to deliver them into the hand of Jehoash, because they had sought the gods of Edom.

21So Jehoash king of Israel advanced, and he and Amaziah king of Judah faced each other at Beth-shemesh in Judah.

22And Judah was routed before Israel, and every man fled to his own home.

23There at Beth-shemesh, Jehoash king of Israel captured Amaziah king of Judah, the son of Joash, the son of Jehoahaz. Then Jehoash brought him to Jerusalem and broke down the wall of Jerusalem from the Ephraim Gate to the Corner Gate—a section of four hundred cubits.

24He took all the gold and silver and all the articles found in the house of God with Obed-edom and in the treasuries of the royal palace, as well as some hostages. Then he returned to Samaria.

25Amaziah son of Joash king of Judah lived for fifteen years after the death of Jehoash son of Jehoahaz king of Israel.

26As for the rest of the acts of Amaziah, from beginning to end, are they not written in the Book of the Kings of Judah and Israel?

27From the time that Amaziah turned from following the LORD, a conspiracy was formed against him in Jerusalem, and he fled to Lachish. But men were sent after him to Lachish, and they killed him there.

28They carried him back on horses and buried him with his fathers in the City of Judah.

Summary

Amaziah begins his reign doing what is right, though not wholeheartedly, executing his father's assassins but sparing their children in keeping with the Law of Moses (vv. 1-4). He musters Judah's army and hires a hundred thousand mercenaries from Israel, but a man of God warns him the Lord is not with Israel and he should dismiss them despite the money already paid (vv. 5-10). Trusting God's promise instead, Amaziah defeats Edom at the Valley of Salt, but afterward foolishly adopts the defeated nation's gods and worships them, provoking a prophet's rebuke that he silences by threat (vv. 11-16). Amaziah then rashly challenges Joash of Israel to battle despite Joash's warning parable, and Judah is routed at Beth-shemesh, with Jerusalem's wall broken down and the temple treasures plundered (vv. 17-24). He lives fifteen more years but is eventually assassinated in a conspiracy after he turns away from the Lord (vv. 25-28).

Authorship & Background

Author: Traditionally attributed to Ezra the scribe. Originally one book with 1 Chronicles. Written post-exile (approximately 450-400 BC). 2 Chronicles covers Solomon's reign through the Babylonian exile and Cyrus's decree of return (approximately 970-538 BC). The Chronicler focuses exclusively on Judah (ignoring the northern kingdom) and emphasizes Temple worship, reform movements, and the principle of 2 Chronicles 7:14: "If my people... shall humble themselves, and pray... then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and will heal their land."
Historical Context: Chapter 25 records the reign of Amaziah, son of the assassinated Joash, who ruled Judah for twenty-nine years (approximately 796-767 BC). The Chronicler's verdict is devastating in its precision: he did right "but not with a perfect heart" (v.2). This phrase captures the theme of the entire chapter — partial obedience that eventually collapses into outright rebellion. Amaziah is the king of half-measures, the ruler who knows God's law well enough to cite it (v.4) but not deeply enough to internalize it.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: The book's most exact verdict, right in the sight of the LORD but not with a perfect heart, given before a single event of the chapter unfolds, murderers' children spared in deliberate obedience to a statute the text quotes directly, a hundred talents already spent on hired troops sent home anyway on a prophet's word, gods of the defeated Edomites brought home and worshipped in an act the narrator leaves standing as absurd without comment, and a prophet cut off mid-sentence, art thou made of the king's counsel, before he finishes his diagnosis
The Hired Israelites Sent Home, and the Valley of Salt (vv.1-13): Amaziah gets the book's most exact verdict, he did that which was right in the sight of the LORD, but not with a perfect heart, and the chapter is a demonstration of what that means. He begins well twice over. He executes his father's murderers but not their children, and the Chronicler cites the statute he is obeying, every man shall die for his own sin. And when a man of God tells him to send home the hundred thousand Israelite troops he has hired, he obeys, though his objection is entirely about the money already spent, but what shall we do for the hundred talents. The answer is the chapter's best line, the LORD is able to give thee much more than this. He wins in the valley of salt. The dismissed troops, meanwhile, sack the cities of Judah on their way north, so obedience costs something and the text does not hide it.
The Gods of Edom, and the Challenge to Joash (vv.14-28): What he brings back from the victory is the difficulty: he sets up the gods of the children of Seir and bows to them, which is worship of the losing side's deities and is left standing as absurd without comment. The prophet who says so is cut off mid-sentence, Art thou made of the king's counsel, forbear, and answers with a diagnosis rather than a threat, I know that God hath determined to destroy thee, because thou hast not hearkened unto my counsel. The war with Israel that follows is provoked by Amaziah, and Joash's reply is a fable about a thistle sending to a cedar, with the reading attached, thine heart lifteth thee up to boast, abide now at home. Judah is beaten at Beth-shemesh, the king is captured, four hundred cubits of Jerusalem's wall are broken down and the temple vessels carried to Samaria. He is finally killed at Lachish by his own people, and the Chronicler dates the conspiracy from the turn, after the time that Amaziah did turn away from following the LORD.

Map & Geography

  • Parallels 1-2 Kings but focuses exclusively on Judah (the southern kingdom). Jerusalem and the Temple are the theological center throughout.
  • Key locations include various battle sites, reform locations, and high places destroyed or rebuilt by successive kings.
  • The book ends with exile to Babylon (586 BC) and Cyrus's decree permitting return — the geographic arc moves from Jerusalem to Babylon and back.

Setting: Judah, about 796–767 BC. Joash has been murdered in his bed by his own servants, unburied in the royal tombs, after turning from God and killing the prophet Zechariah (24:20–27). His son Amaziah now takes the throne. In the north, Jehu's grandson Jehoash (spelled Joash in this chapter) rules Israel, and the Syrian threat that crushed Judah under Joash has eased. The parallel account is 2 Kings 14:1–20, which is short. Chronicles adds the hired Israelite army, the man of God's warning, Amaziah's worship of Edom's gods and the prophet he silenced. The chapter follows the pattern of the last two: a reign that begins with obedience and ends in disaster, because the heart was never fully given to God.

Amaziah Reigns in Judah (vv1–4)

  • "Twenty-five years old… twenty-nine years in Jerusalem" (v1). The Tyndale notes date his reign 796–767 BC, with his son Uzziah sharing the throne from about 792. That overlap matters at the end of the chapter. His mother Jehoaddan was "of Jerusalem."
  • "He did what was right in the eyes of the LORD, yet not with a whole heart" (v2). This is the key to the whole chapter. "A whole heart" (lev shalem) is a heart that is complete, undivided. David asked God to give Solomon such a heart (1 Chr 29:19), and it marked the best men in Chronicles (1 Chr 12:38; 2 Chr 16:9). 2 Kings 14:3–4 puts it differently: he did right, "yet not like David his father," and the high places stayed. Amaziah obeyed when it suited him. The rest of the chapter shows what that looks like.
  • "He killed his servants who had struck down the king his father" (v3). The murderers were named in 24:26. Amaziah waited until "the royal power was firmly his," then executed them. This was justice, not a vendetta: they had murdered the king.
  • "But he did not put their children to death" (v4). In the ancient world it was normal to wipe out a traitor's whole family so no one would seek revenge later. Amaziah followed the law instead: "each one shall die for his own sin" (Deut 24:16; compare Ezek 18:20). After three generations of royal family massacres (21:4; 22:10), a king who spares children because God's word says so is a bright moment. It is also the clearest sign of Amaziah's good start.
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry sums up the king in one line: "Many do what is good, but not with a perfect heart." Guzik quotes G. Campbell Morgan, who describes a divided heart as one that has not fully surrendered. Guzik also points out how unusual it was in the ancient world to spare a traitor's children, and that it took faith and courage to do it.

Amaziah's Victories (vv5–13)

  • "300,000 choice men" (v5). Amaziah organized the army "by fathers' houses," that is, by clans. JFB notes the effect: a unit was made up of brothers, relatives and friends who would fight for one another. The Tyndale notes compare the totals: Asa had 580,000 (14:8) and Jehoshaphat over a million (17:14–18). Judah's strength had fallen sharply after the losses under Joash, which may explain what Amaziah did next.
  • "He hired also 100,000 mighty men of valor from Israel for 100 talents of silver" (v6). A talent was about 75 pounds (34 kg), so this was roughly three and a half tons of silver. Mercenaries were normal in the ancient world. The problem was not money but trust: the king went to the north for strength.
  • "The LORD is not with Israel, with all these Ephraimites" (v7). "A man of God" is an old title for a prophet (Deut 33:1; 1 Kings 13:1). "Ephraim," the largest northern tribe, often stands for the whole northern kingdom. Under Jehu's family, Israel still worshiped at the golden calves (2 Kings 10:29), and the Chronicler has warned again and again against Judah's kings allying with the north (19:2; 20:37). The prophet's point is simple: an army God is not with will not help you.
  • "For God has power to help or to cast down" (v8). The Hebrew of this verse is hard. It literally reads something like "But if you go, act, be strong for the battle, God will cast you down before the enemy." It may be sarcasm ("go ahead, be strong, and God will throw you down"), or a word may have dropped out. JFB suggests reading "not" ("God will not cast you down"). The ESV turns it into a question. Every reading lands in the same place: victory depends on God, not on the size of the army.
  • "But what shall we do about the hundred talents?" (v9). It is an honest and very human question. Obeying God would mean losing money already spent. "The LORD is able to give you much more than this." That one sentence answers the question for every age. Obedience can be costly, but God is never in anyone's debt (compare Prov 3:9–10; Mark 10:29–30; Phil 4:19).
  • "They became very angry with Judah and returned home in fierce anger" (v10). Mercenaries were paid partly in plunder. Sent home with their wages but no chance to loot, they felt insulted.
  • "Amaziah took courage… the Valley of Salt… 10,000 men of Seir" (v11). "Took courage" is the same verb used of Jehoiada in 23:1, "strengthened himself." Seir is Edom, which had broken free under Jehoram (21:8–10). The Valley of Salt was probably near the salt flats south of the Dead Sea. David had beaten Edom there (2 Sam 8:13; Ps 60 title). The Tyndale notes add that Amaziah's conquest reached only northern Edom. His son would later retake the port at Elath (26:2).
  • "Took them to the top of a rock and threw them down" (v12). "The rock" is ha-sela in Hebrew. 2 Kings 14:7 says Amaziah "took Sela by storm" and renamed it Joktheel. Older commentators, JFB among them, identify Sela with Petra, the later rock city of the Nabateans. Many scholars now prefer a mountain fortress called es-Sela near the Edomite capital Bozrah. Either way, the execution of 10,000 prisoners was brutal, and the text records it without praise. Ancient warfare was savage, and later prophets condemned this kind of cruelty even when done by Israel's neighbors (Amos 1:11; 2:1).
  • "Raided the cities of Judah, from Samaria to Beth-horon" (v13). While Amaziah was in Edom, the angry mercenaries struck the undefended north of Judah. Beth-horon guarded the pass up from the coastal plain into the hills northwest of Jerusalem. "From Samaria" probably means they set out from Samaria. The price of obeying God included 3,000 lives and much plunder. The text does not explain this. It simply records that obedience did not make Amaziah immune from loss, and the man of God's promise of "much more" still stood.
  • Commentators: Henry gives the prophet's line the weight it deserves: "The Lord is able to give thee much more than this," and urges that a good conscience is worth more than any money lost. Guzik makes the same point in his own way: obedience always costs less in the end than disobedience. JFB calls a hundred talents "a very paltry pay" for so many men, says the dismissed troops "plundered all the towns in their way," and identifies the rock with Selah or Petra. Guzik, following Selman, notices that Amaziah won the victory but no thanks to God is recorded.

Amaziah's Idolatry (vv14–16)

  • "He brought the gods of the men of Seir and set them up as his gods and worshiped them" (v14). Conquerors often carried off the images of a defeated people's gods as trophies, and the Tyndale notes add that some victors worshiped them, hoping to gain their favor. For Amaziah to do so was absurd. Edom's chief god was Qaus, known from inscriptions, though the text names no god.
  • "Why have you sought the gods of a people who did not deliver their own people from your hand?" (v15). The prophet's logic is almost humorous. These gods could not save Edom from Amaziah. Why would they save Amaziah? The prophets used the same argument against idols for centuries (Isa 44:9–20; Jer 10:1–16). "Sought" is the Chronicler's key verb, darash. Amaziah had "sought" the wrong gods.
  • "Have we made you a royal counselor? Stop! Why should you be struck down?" (v16). The king threatens the prophet with death, the same thing his father did to Zechariah (24:21). This time the prophet is not killed.
  • A play on "counsel" (v16). The Hebrew word for counsel (ya'ats) runs through these verses. The king sneers: "Have we made you a royal counselor?" The prophet answers: "God has determined (literally counseled) to destroy you… because you have not listened to my counsel." Then in v17 Amaziah "took counsel" and challenged Israel. The king refused God's counsel, so God's counsel was set against him.
  • Commentators: Henry calls worshiping the gods of a beaten enemy absurd, and notes that the king ordered the prophet to say not one word more. JFB says the victory "had elated and blinded him," and insists that prophets "were entitled to counsel kings." Guzik sees God's patience in sending a prophet at all, a final warning before judgment.

Israel Defeats Amaziah (vv17–28)

  • "Come, let us look one another in the face" (v17). It is a challenge to battle. Amaziah, flushed with victory over Edom and perhaps angry about the mercenaries' raid, now picks a fight with Israel. Joash son of Jehoahaz, son of Jehu had beaten the Syrians three times, as Elisha foretold on his deathbed (2 Kings 13:14–25). He was a seasoned soldier.
  • The thistle and the cedar (v18). This is a fable, like Jotham's story of the trees choosing a king (Judg 9:8–15). The cedars of Lebanon were the grandest trees in the ancient world. A thistle, or thornbush, asks a cedar for a marriage alliance as if they were equals, and a passing wild animal tramples it without noticing. Joash casts himself as the cedar and Amaziah as the thistle. The insult is deliberate.
  • "Your heart has lifted you up in boastfulness. But now stay at home" (v19). The words came from a king who himself worshiped at the golden calves, but they were true. Pride makes a person overestimate what one victory means. Proverbs had said it long before: "Pride goes before destruction" (Prov 16:18).
  • "But Amaziah would not listen, for it was of God" (v20). Only Chronicles adds this. The same phrase is used for Rehoboam's refusal to listen (10:15) and Ahaziah's fatal visit (22:7). Amaziah's own stubbornness and God's judgment work together. He would not listen because he had already refused to listen to God's prophet, and God gave him over to the consequences "because they had sought the gods of Edom."
  • "At Beth-shemesh, which belongs to Judah" (v21). Beth-shemesh, "house of the sun," was a town on the Sorek Valley route from the coast up to Jerusalem, where the ark had once returned from the Philistines (1 Sam 6). The Tyndale notes suggest Joash may have been after control of that trade route. "Which belongs to Judah" makes the point that the battle was fought on Judah's own soil. Amaziah's challenge brought the war to his own land.
  • "Every man fled to his home" (v22). It was a rout. And it ended where Joash had told Amaziah to stay: at home.
  • "Joash king of Israel captured Amaziah" (v23). The king of Judah was taken prisoner. His full line is given, "son of Joash, son of Ahaziah," which reminds the reader of where this family's choices have led.
  • "Broke down the wall of Jerusalem for 400 cubits, from the Ephraim Gate to the Corner Gate" (v23). A cubit was about 18 inches (45 cm), so this was about 600 feet (180 m). The Tyndale notes place the Ephraim Gate on the north side and the Corner Gate on the west, the side facing Israel. A broken wall meant the city was defenseless and humiliated. Uzziah would later rebuild towers at the Corner Gate (26:9).
  • "All the vessels… in the care of Obed-edom" (v24). The family of Obed-edom were Levite gatekeepers whom David assigned to guard the temple storehouse (1 Chr 26:4–8, 15). Joash also took the palace treasuries and "hostages," probably sons of leading families, to guarantee Judah's good behavior. The gold Amaziah had trusted was gone, and so was the temple's.
  • "Amaziah… lived fifteen years after the death of Joash" (v25). Some scholars think Amaziah was held captive in Samaria for a time, which would explain why his son Uzziah began to reign alongside him around 792 BC. The text doesn't say so, but it fits the dates. Either way, Amaziah outlived his conqueror, without regaining his standing.
  • "From the time when he turned away from the LORD they made a conspiracy against him" (v27). The Chronicler traces the plot back to the day he turned to Edom's gods. Like his father, Amaziah died by conspiracy. He fled to Lachish, the great fortress city about 30 miles southwest of Jerusalem, but was killed there. Micah later calls Lachish "the beginning of sin to the daughter of Zion" (Mic 1:13), though the link with Amaziah is a guess.
  • "They brought him upon horses, and he was buried with his fathers" (v28). He was carried home, perhaps on a horse-drawn carriage, and buried in the city of David. Unlike his grandfather Jehoram and his father Joash, he was given a royal burial (compare 21:20; 24:25).
  • Commentators: Henry warns, "A man's pride shall bring him low; his ruin may be dated from his turning from the Lord," and adds that "Never was a proud prince more thoroughly mortified than Amaziah by Joash king of Israel." Guzik observes that one leader's foolish decision brought loss to the whole kingdom, and he passes on Knapp's suggestion that Lachish had picked up the north's idolatry (Mic 1:13). JFB refers vv17–28 to its notes on 2 Kings 14 and does not comment on them here. The Tyndale notes link v20 to God's judicial hardening, comparing Ahab being lured to his death in ch 18.

Pointing to Christ

Amaziah's story turns on two kinds of counsel. He listened to God's counsel when it cost a hundred talents, and God gave him victory. He refused it when it touched his pride, and God's counsel turned against him. "Not with a whole heart" sums up his life. Jesus is the King whose heart was whole: "I always do the things that are pleasing to him" (John 8:29). He did not trust in hired strength or captured gods, and he did not cling to his position, but "humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death" (Phil 2:8). And the promise of v9 is fulfilled most fully in him. Whatever obedience costs, "the LORD is able to give you much more than this." In Christ, God has given "every spiritual blessing" (Eph 1:3).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik highlights the irrationality of Amaziah's idolatry — adopting the gods of a people he just conquered — as illustrative of sin's fundamental irrationality. He notes that the "hundred talents" exchange reveals a common spiritual test: will we obey when obedience is costly? Guzik also emphasizes that Amaziah's pride after the Edomite victory led directly to his humiliation before Israel — a consistent biblical pattern.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • A Heart That's Right but Not Whole (v.2) — Amaziah does what's right, yet the Chronicler notes it wasn't with a perfect heart, a partial devotion that eventually collapses. Where in your life is your obedience only partial, and what would it look like to close that gap?
  • Restraint Even in Justice (v.4) — Amaziah executes the men who murdered his father but deliberately spares their children, following God's law rather than the more common practice of collective punishment. Where do you need to exercise restraint instead of taking justice further than God's word allows?
  • Trusting God Over a Financial Loss (v.9) — Told to send home mercenaries he'd already paid for, Amaziah is reminded the Lord can give him far more than the money he'd lose by obeying. Is there a cost to obedience right now that you're weighing against God's ability to provide?
  • Worshiping What Already Failed (vv.14-15) — After defeating Edom, Amaziah bizarrely adopts the very gods that couldn't save their own worshipers. What are you still turning to for security or comfort that has already proven it can't deliver?
  • Refusing to Hear Correction (v.16) — When the prophet challenges his idolatry, Amaziah threatens him into silence instead of listening. Is there a voice of correction in your life right now that you're trying to quiet rather than consider?
  • Pride Rising After a Win (v.19) — Fresh off his victory over Edom, Amaziah's swelling confidence pushes him into a reckless and humiliating challenge against Israel. After a recent success, are you staying humble, or letting it convince you you're untouchable?
