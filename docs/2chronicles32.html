1After all these acts of faithfulness, Sennacherib king of Assyria came and invaded Judah. He laid siege to the fortified cities, intending to conquer them for himself.

2When Hezekiah saw that Sennacherib had come to make war against Jerusalem,

3he consulted with his leaders and mighty men about stopping up the waters of the springs outside the city, and they helped him carry it out.

4Many people assembled and stopped up all the springs and the stream that flowed through the land. “Why should the kings of Assyria come and find plenty of water?” they said.

5Then Hezekiah worked resolutely to rebuild all the broken sections of the wall and to raise up towers on it. He also built an outer wall and reinforced the supporting terraces of the City of David, and he produced an abundance of weapons and shields.

6Hezekiah appointed military commanders over the people and gathered the people in the square of the city gate. Then he encouraged them, saying,

7“Be strong and courageous! Do not be afraid or discouraged before the king of Assyria and the vast army with him, for there is a greater One with us than with him.

8With him is only the arm of flesh, but with us is the LORD our God to help us and to fight our battles.” So the people were strengthened by the words of Hezekiah king of Judah.

9Later, as Sennacherib king of Assyria and all his forces besieged Lachish, he sent his servants to Jerusalem with a message for King Hezekiah of Judah and all the people of Judah who were in Jerusalem:

10“This is what Sennacherib king of Assyria says: What is the basis of your confidence, that you remain in Jerusalem under siege?

11Is not Hezekiah misleading you to give you over to death by famine and thirst when he says, ‘The LORD our God will deliver us from the hand of the king of Assyria?’

12Did not Hezekiah himself remove His high places and His altars and say to Judah and Jerusalem, ‘You must worship before one altar, and on it you shall burn sacrifices’?

13Do you not know what I and my fathers have done to all the peoples of the lands? Have the gods of these nations ever been able to deliver their land from my hand?

14Who among all the gods of these nations that my fathers devoted to destruction has been able to deliver his people from my hand? How then can your God deliver you from my hand?

15So now, do not let Hezekiah deceive you, and do not let him mislead you like this. Do not believe him, for no god of any nation or kingdom has been able to deliver his people from my hand or from the hand of my fathers. How much less will your God deliver you from my hand!”

16And the servants of Sennacherib spoke further against the LORD God and against His servant Hezekiah.

17He also wrote letters mocking the LORD, the God of Israel, and saying against Him: “Just as the gods of the nations did not deliver their people from my hand, so the God of Hezekiah will not deliver His people from my hand.”

18Then the Assyrians called out loudly in Hebrew to the people of Jerusalem who were on the wall, to frighten and terrify them in order to capture the city.

19They spoke against the God of Jerusalem as they had spoken against the gods of the peoples of the earth—the work of human hands.

20In response, King Hezekiah and the prophet Isaiah son of Amoz cried out to heaven in prayer,

21and the LORD sent an angel who annihilated every mighty man of valor and every leader and commander in the camp of the king of Assyria. So he withdrew to his own land in disgrace. And when he entered the temple of his god, some of his own sons struck him down with the sword.

22So the LORD saved Hezekiah and the people of Jerusalem from the hands of King Sennacherib of Assyria and all others, and He gave them rest on every side.

23Many brought offerings to Jerusalem for the LORD and valuable gifts for Hezekiah king of Judah, and from then on he was exalted in the eyes of all nations.

24In those days Hezekiah became mortally ill. So he prayed to the LORD, who spoke to him and gave him a sign.

25But because his heart was proud, Hezekiah did not repay the favor shown to him. Therefore wrath came upon him and upon Judah and Jerusalem.

26Then Hezekiah humbled the pride of his heart—he and the people of Jerusalem—so that the wrath of the LORD did not come upon them during the days of Hezekiah.

27Hezekiah had very great riches and honor, and he made treasuries for his silver, gold, precious stones, spices, shields, and all kinds of valuable articles.

28He also made storehouses for the harvest of grain and new wine and oil, stalls for all kinds of livestock, and pens for the flocks.

29He made cities for himself, and he acquired herds of sheep and cattle in abundance, for God gave him very great wealth.

30It was Hezekiah who blocked the upper outlet of the Spring of Gihon and channeled it down to the west side of the City of David. And Hezekiah prospered in all that he did.

31And so when ambassadors of the rulers of Babylon were sent to him to inquire about the wonder that had happened in the land, God left him alone to test him, that He might know all that was in Hezekiah’s heart.

32As for the rest of the acts of Hezekiah and his deeds of loving devotion, they are indeed written in the vision of the prophet Isaiah son of Amoz in the Book of the Kings of Judah and Israel.

33And Hezekiah rested with his fathers and was buried in the upper tombs of David’s descendants. All Judah and the people of Jerusalem paid him honor at his death. And his son Manasseh reigned in his place.

1After these things, and the establishment thereof, Sennacherib king of Assyria came, and entered into Judah, and encamped against the fenced cities, and thought to win them for himself.

2And when Hezekiah saw that Sennacherib was come, and that he was purposed to fight against Jerusalem,

3He took counsel with his princes and his mighty men to stop the waters of the fountains which were without the city: and they did help him.

4So there was gathered much people together, who stopped all the fountains, and the brook that ran through the midst of the land, saying, Why should the kings of Assyria come, and find much water?

7Be strong and courageous, be not afraid nor dismayed for the king of Assyria, nor for all the multitude that is with him: for there be more with us than with him:

8With him is an arm of flesh; but with us is the LORD our God to help us, and to fight our battles. And the people rested themselves upon the words of Hezekiah king of Judah.

10Thus saith Sennacherib king of Assyria, Whereon do ye trust, that ye abide in the siege in Jerusalem?

11Doth not Hezekiah persuade you to give over yourselves to die by famine and by thirst, saying, The LORD our God shall deliver us out of the hand of the king of Assyria?

12Hath not the same Hezekiah taken away his high places and his altars, and commanded Judah and Jerusalem, saying, Ye shall worship before one altar, and burn incense upon it?

13Know ye not what I and my fathers have done unto all the people of other lands? were the gods of the nations of those lands any ways able to deliver their lands out of mine hand?

14Who was there among all the gods of those nations that my fathers utterly destroyed, that could deliver his people out of mine hand, that your God should be able to deliver you out of mine hand?

15Now therefore let not Hezekiah deceive you, nor persuade you on this manner, neither yet believe him: for no god of any nation or kingdom was able to deliver his people out of mine hand, and out of the hand of my fathers: how much less shall your God deliver you out of mine hand?

16And his servants spake yet more against the LORD God, and against his servant Hezekiah.

17He wrote also letters to rail on the LORD God of Israel, and to speak against him, saying, As the gods of the nations of other lands have not delivered their people out of mine hand, so shall not the God of Hezekiah deliver his people out of mine hand.

18Then they cried with a loud voice in the Jews’ speech unto the people of Jerusalem that were on the wall, to affright them, and to trouble them; that they might take the city.

19And they spake against the God of Jerusalem, as against the gods of the people of the earth, which were the work of the hands of man.

20And for this cause Hezekiah the king, and the prophet Isaiah the son of Amoz, prayed and cried to heaven.

21And the LORD sent an angel, which cut off all the mighty men of valour, and the leaders and captains in the camp of the king of Assyria. So he returned with shame of face to his own land. And when he was come into the house of his god, they that came forth of his own bowels slew him there with the sword.

Summary

After Hezekiah's faithful reforms, Sennacherib of Assyria invades Judah's fortified cities, so Hezekiah blocks the outside water sources, repairs the walls, arms the people, and assures them that a greater power is with Judah than the arm of flesh backing Assyria (vv. 1-8). Sennacherib's messengers mock Hezekiah's trust in the Lord, boasting that no god of any nation has delivered its people from Assyria and insulting God directly in letters and shouted taunts (vv. 9-19). Hezekiah and the prophet Isaiah cry out to heaven, and the Lord sends an angel who destroys the Assyrian camp, forcing Sennacherib to retreat home in shame, where his own sons later kill him (vv. 20-22). Hezekiah grows wealthy from the gifts that follow, but later becomes proud during an illness and the visit of Babylonian envoys, and God tests him before he humbles himself (vv. 24-31). The chapter closes noting his building projects and honored death, succeeded by his son Manasseh (vv. 27-33).

Authorship & Background

Author: Traditionally attributed to Ezra the scribe. Originally one book with 1 Chronicles. Written post-exile (approximately 450-400 BC). 2 Chronicles covers Solomon's reign through the Babylonian exile and Cyrus's decree of return (approximately 970-538 BC). The Chronicler focuses exclusively on Judah (ignoring the northern kingdom) and emphasizes Temple worship, reform movements, and the principle of 2 Chronicles 7:14: "If my people... shall humble themselves, and pray... then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and will heal their land."
Historical Context: Chapter 32 records one of the most dramatic events in Judah's history — the Assyrian invasion under Sennacherib in 701 BC and God's miraculous deliverance of Jerusalem. The chapter opens with a sobering note: "After these things, and the establishment thereof" (v.1) — after all Hezekiah's faithful reforms (chapters 29-31), crisis still came. Faithfulness does not guarantee immunity from trial; it guarantees God's presence within trial.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: An invasion arriving after all Hezekiah's reforms rather than in place of them, faithfulness proving no immunity from trial, an Assyrian argument allowed to run at length because it is a genuinely good one, why should this god succeed where every other nation's god has failed, a religious reform turned into evidence against the very king who carried it out, four verses disposing of the entire siege once Hezekiah and Isaiah pray, and pride after healing corrected only by humbling before the wrath actually arrives, the same structure as Uzziah's fall with a different ending
The Invasion, the Water Stopped and the Wall Built (vv.1-8): Sennacherib comes after all this, which is the Chronicler's way of saying that a reformed kingdom is not an unthreatened one. Hezekiah's preparations are practical and are listed as such: the springs outside the city stopped, with the reasoning given in his own words, why should the kings of Assyria come, and find much water, then the wall raised, another wall built outside it, the towers strengthened, weapons made and captains appointed. The engineering behind verse 4 is described again at verse 30 and can still be walked through, the tunnel cut to bring the Gihon spring inside the walls, with an inscription at its midpoint recording the two teams of quarrymen meeting. Then the speech, which puts the whole preparation in proportion, with him is an arm of flesh, but with us is the LORD our God to help us, and to fight our battles.
The Assyrian Argument at the Wall (vv.9-19): The Assyrian case is set out at length because it is a good argument, and the Chronicler lets it run. It begins with the question that matters, whereon do ye trust. It reasons from an unbroken record, no god of any nation Assyria has taken has delivered his people, so why should this one. And it turns Hezekiah's reform into evidence against him, hath not the same Hezekiah taken away his high places and his altars, which is how a religious purge looks to an observer who assumes more altars mean more help. The flaw is named in the last verse of the section rather than answered in it, they spake against the God of Jerusalem, as against the gods of the people of the earth, which are the work of the hands of man. The category mistake is the whole reply.
The Angel, and the Gifts Brought to Jerusalem (vv.20-23): Four verses dispose of the campaign. Hezekiah and Isaiah prayed and cried to heaven, and the LORD sent an angel which cut off all the mighty men of valour, and the leaders and captains, in the camp of the king of Assyria. Sennacherib goes home with shame of face, and is killed in the house of his own god by his own sons, which answers the argument of the previous section on its own terms. The consequence is reputational, many brought gifts unto the LORD to Jerusalem, and Hezekiah was magnified in the sight of all nations from thenceforth.
The Sickness, the Envoys, and What God Left Him to Learn (vv.24-33): Hezekiah's illness, prayer and sign are given in a single verse, and then the difficulty: he rendered not again according to the benefit done unto him, for his heart was lifted up. The structure is the same as Uzziah's at 26:16, and the outcome differs only because of what he does next, notwithstanding Hezekiah humbled himself, so the wrath came not upon them in the days of Hezekiah. The wealth is then inventoried, the conduit noted again, and the Babylonian ambassadors arrive to enquire of the wonder that was done in the land. What the Chronicler says about that visit is the most searching sentence in the chapter, God left him, to try him, that he might know all that was in his heart. He is buried in the chiefest of the sepulchres of the sons of David, and all Judah did him honour at his death.

Map & Geography

  • Parallels 1-2 Kings but focuses exclusively on Judah (the southern kingdom). Jerusalem and the Temple are the theological center throughout.
  • Key locations include various battle sites, reform locations, and high places destroyed or rebuilt by successive kings.
  • The book ends with exile to Babylon (586 BC) and Cyrus's decree permitting return — the geographic arc moves from Jerusalem to Babylon and back.

Deep Dive

Setting: Judah, 701 BC, with Hezekiah's later years after that. Hezekiah has cleansed the temple, held a Passover for all Israel, and organized the priests and their support (chs 29–31). He also stopped paying tribute to Assyria (2 Kings 18:7). Sennacherib, who ruled Assyria 705–681 BC, spent his first years putting down revolts, and in 701 BC he marched west to crush a rebel alliance that included Judah. His own record of the campaign survives on clay prisms, and its carved stone reliefs of the siege of Lachish now hang in the British Museum. The same events fill 2 Kings 18–20 and Isaiah 36–39. The Chronicler tells them much more briefly, leaving out Hezekiah's attempt to buy Sennacherib off with temple gold (2 Kings 18:14–16), and keeps the focus on one question: whom will Judah trust?

Sennacherib Invades Judah (vv1–8)

  • "After these things and these acts of faithfulness, Sennacherib… came" (v1). The Chronicler often shows God blessing the faithful, so this opening is a jolt. The reward for faithfulness is an invasion by the strongest army in the world. Faithfulness does not mean trouble stays away. It means God is present when it comes. Guzik makes the same point: trials often come when believers are walking closest to God.
  • "Encamped against the fortified cities, thinking to win them for himself" (v1). Sennacherib's prism boasts that he took "46 of his strong walled cities" and countless villages, and carried off 200,150 people. That number is probably inflated, but archaeology confirms widespread destruction across Judah in this period. Lachish, Judah's second city, fell after a brutal siege.
  • "To stop the water of the springs that were outside the city" (v3). Water decided sieges. An army camped around a city needed huge amounts of it. Hezekiah's plan was to deny the Assyrians water while keeping it for Jerusalem (v30). "Why should the kings of Assyria come and find much water?" (v4).
  • "The brook that flowed through the land" (v4). Probably the overflow channel from the Gihon spring in the Kidron Valley. JFB argues instead for a stream in the Gihon valley on the west, citing the Greek translation. Either way, the water outside the walls was hidden or cut off.
  • "Built up all the wall… and outside it he built another wall" (v5). Archaeologists in Jerusalem have uncovered a section of city wall about 7 meters (23 feet) thick, now called the Broad Wall, dated to Hezekiah's time. It cut through houses, and Isaiah mentions this: "you counted the houses of Jerusalem, and you broke down the houses to fortify the wall" (Isa 22:10). The ESV footnote notes that the Hebrew for "raised towers upon it" is difficult.
  • "He strengthened the Millo in the city of David" (v5). The Millo, "the filling," was probably a large stepped stone terrace supporting buildings on the steep slope of the old city. David and Solomon both worked on it (1 Chr 11:8; 1 Kings 9:15).
  • Preparing and trusting. Isaiah 22:8–11 gives a sharp angle on these same preparations. He rebukes the people because they looked to the weapons, the walls and the water, "but you did not look to him who did it." The Chronicler shows Hezekiah doing both: preparing hard and trusting God (vv7–8). The danger Isaiah names is preparing instead of trusting.
  • "Be strong and courageous. Do not be afraid or dismayed" (v7). These are the words God gave Joshua before the conquest (Josh 1:9) and David gave Solomon (1 Chr 22:13). Hezekiah stands in that line.
  • "For there are more with us than with him" (v7). This echoes Elisha at Dothan, when his servant saw the hills full of horses and chariots of fire: "those who are with us are more than those who are with them" (2 Kings 6:16). Guzik draws the same link.
  • "With him is an arm of flesh, but with us is the LORD our God" (v8). The "arm" was a picture of power. "Flesh" in the Old Testament means human weakness. Jeremiah later says, "Cursed is the man who trusts in man and makes flesh his strength" (Jer 17:5). Assyria's might, however huge, is only human. Isaiah describes Egypt the same way: "their horses are flesh, and not spirit" (Isa 31:3).
  • "The people took confidence from the words of Hezekiah" (v8). "Took confidence" is literally "leaned on." The people leaned on the king's words because the king leaned on God.
  • Commentators: Henry outlines this section as "the care he took to fortify himself, his city, and the minds of his people." Guzik notes that the Chronicler skips Hezekiah's payment of tribute, and passes on Selman's report of the massive Iron Age wall. JFB describes the Millo as "the lower portion of Zion," and says Hezekiah covered the spring heads and brought the water into the city.

Sennacherib Blasphemes (vv9–19)

  • "Besieging Lachish with all his forces" (v9). Lachish was about 30 miles southwest of Jerusalem and guarded the road from Egypt. Sennacherib was so proud of taking it that he had the siege carved on the walls of his palace at Nineveh: siege ramps, battering rams, defenders on the walls and captives led away. At Lachish itself, archaeologists found the Assyrian siege ramp, arrowheads and a mass grave.
  • "On what are you trusting?" (v10). This is the question of the whole chapter. In 2 Kings the Assyrian field commander gives a longer speech, mocking trust in Egypt as "a broken reed" (2 Kings 18:21). The Chronicler cuts that out and keeps the spiritual attack: trust in the LORD.
  • "Is not Hezekiah misleading you… by famine and by thirst?" (v11). Turn the people against their king, and the city opens itself.
  • "Has not this same Hezekiah taken away his high places and his altars?" (v12). A clever twist. The Assyrian claims Hezekiah offended the LORD by removing worship sites. To a pagan, more altars meant more piety. The Tyndale notes point out that the opposite was true: removing the high places was Hezekiah's greatest act of loyalty. Guzik sees the devil's kind of reasoning here, taking obedience and calling it sin. He also passes on Grogan's view that the muddled theology fits an Assyrian speaker, a sign the speech is genuine.
  • "Were the gods of the nations… able to deliver their lands out of my hand?" (vv13–15). By Assyrian thinking a nation's god was only as strong as its army. Assyria had beaten everyone, so the LORD would be no different. Kings and Isaiah list the fallen cities, including Samaria (2 Kings 18:34). "How much less will your God deliver you" (v15) is the sentence that seals Sennacherib's fate. He has moved from attacking Judah to attacking God.
  • "He wrote letters to cast contempt on the LORD" (v17). In 2 Kings 19:14 Hezekiah takes one of these letters into the temple and spreads it "before the LORD."
  • "They shouted it with a loud voice in the language of Judah" (v18). That is, in Hebrew, not Aramaic, the diplomatic language. Hezekiah's officials had asked them to speak Aramaic so the people on the wall would not understand (2 Kings 18:26–28). The Assyrians refused, because frightening the crowd was the point.
  • "They spoke of the God of Jerusalem as… the work of men's hands" (v19). This is the climax of the blasphemy and the Chronicler's own comment. The LORD is not an idol carved by craftsmen. Isaiah mocks those idols at length (Isa 44:9–20). Treating the living God as one of them is the fatal insult.
  • Commentators: Henry describes "the insolent blasphemous letters and messages which Sennacherib sent." Guzik points to the Lachish reliefs and the mass grave as confirmation, and notes that Isaiah had warned Hezekiah against trusting Egypt (Isa 30–31). JFB, on v18, observes that Hezekiah's envoys had "answered him in their own tongue." JFB otherwise refers the reader to 2 Kings for vv9–20.

The LORD Delivers Jerusalem (vv20–23)

  • "Hezekiah the king and Isaiah the prophet… prayed… and cried to heaven" (v20). One verse here. In 2 Kings 19 and Isaiah 37 we have Hezekiah's actual prayer, "that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that you, O LORD, are God alone" (2 Kings 19:19), and Isaiah's long answer. King and prophet together turn the crisis over to God.
  • "The LORD sent an angel, who cut off all the mighty warriors" (v21). 2 Kings 19:35 gives the number: 185,000 died in one night. The Greek historian Herodotus, writing about 250 years later, preserves an Egyptian story that Sennacherib's army was overrun by mice that ate their bowstrings. Some scholars see a garbled memory of a plague in it, since mice carry disease. Scripture says only that the LORD's angel did it.
  • What Sennacherib's own record says. His prism boasts that he shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage," and lists the tribute Hezekiah paid. But it never claims he took the city. For an Assyrian royal record, that silence is striking. Guzik notes the same.
  • "He returned with shame of face to his own land" (v21). He came to mock and went home humiliated.
  • "Some of his own sons struck him down there with the sword" (v21). This happened in 681 BC, about twenty years later. 2 Kings 19:37 names two sons. Babylonian records confirm he was murdered by a son. The Chronicler sets it next to the retreat to make a point: the "god" Sennacherib trusted could not protect him in his own temple. The man who said no god could deliver from his hand was not delivered by his own.
  • "Many brought gifts to the LORD to Jerusalem… so that he was exalted in the sight of all nations" (v23). Gifts to the LORD come first, then to the king. The deliverance made Jerusalem's God famous, the very thing Hezekiah prayed for.
  • Commentators: Henry calls this "the total rout of the Assyrian army, to the shame of Sennacherib and the honour of Hezekiah." Guzik quotes Payne ranking the deliverance alongside the Red Sea, mentions Herodotus's mice through Grogan, and passes on Wiseman's suggestion of a fast-acting disease as the means God used. He also notes the Assyrian annals' silence about Jerusalem's capture. JFB simply points to the fuller account in 2 Kings.

Hezekiah's Pride and Achievements (vv24–33)

  • "In those days Hezekiah became sick and was at the point of death" (v24). This is earlier than the invasion, despite its place here. God added fifteen years to his life (2 Kings 20:6), and since he died in 686 BC, the illness was about 702 BC, just before or during the Assyrian crisis. Isaiah 38 preserves the psalm Hezekiah wrote after he recovered.
  • "He answered him and gave him a sign" (v24). The sign was the shadow going back ten steps on the stairway of Ahaz (2 Kings 20:8–11). The Chronicler assumes his readers know the story.
  • "But Hezekiah did not make return according to the benefit done to him, for his heart was proud" (v25). Literally "his heart was high." After deliverance from death and from Assyria, the danger was no longer the enemy but success. Guzik observes that people who withstand failure are often undone by success. The same was said of Uzziah: "when he was strong, he grew proud" (26:16).
  • "Hezekiah humbled himself… so that the wrath of the LORD did not come upon them in the days of Hezekiah" (v26). He did what 7:14 calls for. The judgment was delayed, not cancelled. Isaiah told him Babylon would one day take everything, and Hezekiah answered, "There will be peace and security in my days" (2 Kings 20:19). That reply is open to more than one reading, either humble acceptance or a troubling relief that it would not happen to him.
  • "Very great riches and honor" (vv27–29). Treasuries, storehouses, stalls and cities. The Tyndale notes say this portrait makes Hezekiah a king like Solomon, whose wealth is described in similar terms (9:13–28). It also explains what he showed the Babylonians.
  • "Closed the upper outlet of the waters of Gihon and directed them down to the west side of the city of David" (v30). This is Hezekiah's Tunnel, one of the most famous finds in biblical archaeology. Workers cut through solid rock from the Gihon spring, outside the city wall in the Kidron Valley, to the Pool of Siloam inside the city. It winds about 533 meters (1,750 feet), though the straight-line distance is barely half that. Two teams dug from opposite ends and met in the middle. An inscription carved near the Siloam end, found in 1880, describes the moment the two crews heard each other's picks through the rock and broke through, "pick against pick." People can still walk through it, ankle-deep in water. This is the Pool of Siloam where Jesus later sent a blind man to wash (John 9:7).
  • "In the matter of the envoys of the princes of Babylon" (v31). 2 Kings 20:12–19 tells the story. Merodach-baladan, a Babylonian rebel against Assyria, sent envoys, probably hoping to recruit Hezekiah into his alliance. Hezekiah showed them everything in his treasuries. Isaiah then told him that all of it, and some of his own sons, would one day be carried to Babylon. The Chronicler adds what no other account says: they came "to inquire about the sign that had been done in the land." Babylonians studied the sky closely, and a shadow moving backward would have fascinated them. Hezekiah had a chance to tell them about the God who gave the sign. He showed them his wealth instead. JFB makes this point: his failure was not giving God the glory.
  • "God left him to himself, in order to test him and to know all that was in his heart" (v31). God already knew Hezekiah's heart. The testing was for Hezekiah to learn what was in it, and for the record to show it. Deuteronomy uses the same language for the wilderness years: God tested Israel "to know what was in your heart" (Deut 8:2). Henry concludes that God let Hezekiah see he was not as strong in grace as he thought, so that believers learn their own weakness and lean on grace.
  • "Written in the vision of Isaiah the prophet" (v32). This probably refers to the material now in Isaiah 36–39, one of the Chronicler's named sources.
  • "They buried him in the upper part of the tombs of the sons of David" (v33). The phrase may mean the most honored spot, or the higher slope because the older tombs were full. Guzik passes on Wiseman's view that it was the latter. "All Judah… did him honor at his death," unlike Jehoram, for whom no one grieved (21:20).
  • "And Manasseh his son reigned in his place" (v33). A dark last line. Manasseh was born in Hezekiah's added fifteen years, and he would undo nearly everything his father did (ch 33).
  • Commentators: Henry outlines these verses as Hezekiah's "sickness and his recovery from that, his sin and his recovery from that." On v31 he says God left Hezekiah to his pride in his wealth, so that he would discover his weakness. Guzik distinguishes Hezekiah's pride in possessions from Uzziah's pride in claiming priestly rights, and adds that the extra years did not make him more godly, since time matters only by how it is used. He also gives the tunnel's measurements from Wiseman. JFB calls the tunnel a great engineering feat and says Hezekiah failed to point the envoys to God.

Pointing to Christ

"With him is an arm of flesh, but with us is the LORD our God." Hezekiah's words still frame every crisis of faith. Jerusalem was saved not by its walls or its water, but because God answered the prayer of his king. Yet the same king who stood firm against Assyria grew proud after he was saved, and God left him to discover his own heart. The chapter shows both the power of a praying king and the limit of even the best one. Jesus faced a far greater enemy for his people and did not grow proud in victory. He humbled himself "to the point of death, even death on a cross" (Phil 2:8). When he is tested, nothing is found in his heart but faithfulness, and in him the promise "the LORD our God, to help us and to fight our battles" is kept for good.

Reflection

  • Trials Come Even After Faithfulness (v.1) — The Assyrian invasion arrives right after Hezekiah's greatest reforms, not as punishment but as a test that faithfulness doesn't exempt him from. Do you expect obedience to guarantee an easy life, or are you prepared to trust God through hardship too?
  • Confidence Rooted in Who Is With You (v.8) — Hezekiah tells the people that their enemy has only human strength, while they have the Lord to help and fight for them. When you feel outmatched by a problem, where does your confidence actually come from?
  • Refusing to Let God Be Reduced (v.19) — Sennacherib's messengers speak of Jerusalem's God as if he were just another manmade idol like every other nation's gods. Do you ever unconsciously treat God as just one more option among many rather than as who he actually is?
  • Pride Rising After Deliverance (v.25) — Following God's dramatic rescue from Assyria, Hezekiah's heart grows proud instead of grateful. After God clearly comes through for you, does your heart tend toward humility or toward self-congratulation?
  • Humility That Turns Away Wrath (v.26) — When Hezekiah humbles himself for his pride, the coming judgment is held back. Is there a proud attitude you're currently aware of that needs humbling before it produces consequences?
  • Being Tested to Reveal What's Really There (v.31) — God allows the Babylonian envoys' visit specifically to see what is truly in Hezekiah's heart. In seasons when God seems to step back and let you choose, what does your response reveal about what's actually inside you?
