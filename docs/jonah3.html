Summary

The LORD's message comes to Jonah a second time, commissioning him again to go to Nineveh, and this time Jonah obeys, entering the enormous city and proclaiming that it will be overthrown in forty days (vv. 1-4). Remarkably, the Ninevites believe God, and from the greatest to the least they declare a fast and put on sackcloth (v. 5). The king steps down from his throne, exchanges his robe for sackcloth, and issues a decree calling everyone, even the animals, to fast and cry out to God while turning from their evil ways, in hope that he might relent (vv. 6-9). Seeing their repentance, God relents from the judgment he had threatened and does not destroy the city (v. 10).

Authorship & Background

Author: Jonah son of Amittai (2 Kings 14:25). Set approximately 780-760 BC. Key theme: God's mercy extends beyond Israel to all nations — even Israel's enemies (Nineveh/Assyria). Jonah resists; God persists. "Salvation is of the LORD" (2:9). Jesus references Jonah as a sign (Matthew 12:39-40).
Classification: Prophetic Narrative
Key Themes: The second chance, obedience after failure, the shortest sermon and the largest revival, corporate repentance from king to cattle, and God relenting from declared judgment
Historical Context: Chapter 3 is the shortest and most powerful revival account in Scripture. God speaks "a second time" (v.1) — grace after failure. Jonah finally obeys. His sermon is only eight words in Hebrew: "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown." No persuasion, no altar call, no follow-up plan. Yet the response is total: from the greatest to the least, including the king himself, the entire city repents — humans and animals covered in sackcloth. This pagan city responds more deeply than Israel ever did to its prophets. The chapter ends with God "repenting" (relenting) of the threatened judgment. This will infuriate Jonah (ch.4) — but it vindicates God's character: He is "gracious and merciful" (4:2).
The Second Call: God Speaks Again (vv.1-2): “And the word of the LORD came unto Jonah the second time.” The commission is not withdrawn and not given to someone else. The wording nearly repeats 1:2, as though the intervening chapters had not happened — grace that resumes rather than grace that renegotiates. One phrase does shift: Jonah is now to preach “the preaching that I bid thee”, the message specified as God's rather than the prophet's. After Tarshish, the terms are clearer.
Obedience: Jonah Goes (v.3): “So Jonah arose, and went unto Nineveh, according to the word of the LORD.” The same verb that began his flight now begins his obedience. The narrator adds that Nineveh was “an exceeding great city of three days' journey”, which most likely describes the greater administrative district rather than a walled circumference. Whatever the measure, it is large enough that the response recorded next is genuinely remarkable.
The Sermon: Eight Words (v.4): Jonah's sermon is five words in Hebrew and eight in English: “Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown.” No mention of the LORD by name, no call to repent, no offer of terms, no appeal. It is an announcement of demolition with a date on it. Whether Jonah preached it hoping to be believed or hoping to be ignored, the text does not say. The word translated “overthrown” is the one used of Sodom, and it can also carry the sense of being turned around — which is what actually happens.
The Response: Total Repentance (vv.5-9): The city believes God, and the repentance moves upward from the people to the king rather than being imposed from the throne down. The king leaves his seat, removes his robe, sits in ashes and issues a decree extending the fast to the animals — a detail that sounds strange until you realize it is how the ancient world expressed grief that admitted no exceptions. His reasoning is careful and theologically humble: “Who can tell if God will turn and repent?” He claims no promise. He orders his people to turn from violence, naming the specific sin Assyria was known for, and leaves the outcome with God.
God's Response: Judgment Averted (v.10): “God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way; and God repented of the evil.” God's relenting is not a change of character but the consistent application of it — Jeremiah 18:7-8 states the principle plainly: a declared judgment answers to repentance. What God saw was not their sackcloth but their turning. This one verse is what will send Jonah into the fury of chapter 4, and it is worth sitting with the fact that the prophet's complaint is about a successful mission.

Map & Geography

  • Joppa (modern Jaffa/Tel Aviv) — Mediterranean port city where Jonah boards a ship fleeing God's call.
  • Tarshish (likely Tartessus in modern Spain) — the farthest west one could sail; the exact OPPOSITE direction from Nineveh.
  • Nineveh (modern Mosul, Iraq) — capital of the Assyrian Empire, located on the Tigris River, approximately 500 miles northeast of Israel.

Reflection

  • A Second Call After Failure (vv.1-2) — God speaks to Jonah again instead of writing him off after his flight to Tarshish. Where do you need to believe that God is still calling you even after you've already said no once?
  • The Simplicity of a Faithful Message (v.4) — Jonah's entire sermon is only a handful of words, with no strategy or persuasion behind it, yet it moves an entire city to repentance. Are you holding back from sharing what God has put in front of you because you don't feel eloquent or prepared enough?
  • A Whole City Turns Together (v.5) — From the greatest to the least, the Ninevites respond to the warning with genuine humility and fasting. What would it look like for your own household or community to respond that wholeheartedly to conviction?
  • Repentance Proven by a Change of Direction (v.10) — God doesn't just see Nineveh's sackcloth; He sees their actual turning from evil, and He relents from the judgment He had planned. Is your own repentance visible in changed behavior, or does it stop at feeling sorry?
