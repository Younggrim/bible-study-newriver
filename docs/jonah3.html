Summary

The LORD's message comes to Jonah a second time, commissioning him again to go to Nineveh, and this time Jonah obeys, entering the enormous city and proclaiming that it will be overthrown in forty days (vv. 1-4). Remarkably, the Ninevites believe God, and from the greatest to the least they declare a fast and put on sackcloth (v. 5). The king steps down from his throne, exchanges his robe for sackcloth, and issues a decree calling everyone, even the animals, to fast and cry out to God while turning from their evil ways, in hope that he might relent (vv. 6-9). Seeing their repentance, God relents from the judgment he had threatened and does not destroy the city (v. 10).

Authorship & Background

Author: Jonah son of Amittai (2 Kings 14:25). Set approximately 780-760 BC. Key theme: God's mercy extends beyond Israel to all nations — even Israel's enemies (Nineveh/Assyria). Jonah resists; God persists. "Salvation is of the LORD" (2:9). Jesus references Jonah as a sign (Matthew 12:39-40).
Classification: Prophetic Narrative
Key Themes: The second chance, obedience after failure, the shortest sermon and the largest revival, corporate repentance from king to cattle, and God relenting from declared judgment
Historical Context: Chapter 3 is the shortest and most powerful revival account in Scripture. God speaks "a second time" (v.1) — grace after failure. Jonah finally obeys. His sermon is only eight words in Hebrew: "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown." No persuasion, no altar call, no follow-up plan. Yet the response is total: from the greatest to the least, including the king himself, the entire city repents — humans and animals covered in sackcloth. This pagan city responds more deeply than Israel ever did to its prophets. The chapter ends with God "repenting" (relenting) of the threatened judgment. This will infuriate Jonah (ch.4) — but it vindicates God's character: He is "gracious and merciful" (4:2).
The Second Call: God Speaks Again (vv.1-2): “And the word of the LORD came unto Jonah the second time.” The commission is not withdrawn and not given to someone else. The wording nearly repeats 1:2, as though the intervening chapters had not happened — grace that resumes rather than grace that renegotiates. One phrase does shift: Jonah is now to preach “the preaching that I bid thee”, the message specified as God's rather than the prophet's. After Tarshish, the terms are clearer.
Obedience: Jonah Goes (v.3): “So Jonah arose, and went unto Nineveh, according to the word of the LORD.” The same verb that began his flight now begins his obedience. The narrator adds that Nineveh was “an exceeding great city of three days' journey”, which most likely describes the greater administrative district rather than a walled circumference. Whatever the measure, it is large enough that the response recorded next is genuinely remarkable.
The Sermon: Eight Words (v.4): Jonah's sermon is five words in Hebrew and eight in English: “Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown.” No mention of the LORD by name, no call to repent, no offer of terms, no appeal. It is an announcement of demolition with a date on it. Whether Jonah preached it hoping to be believed or hoping to be ignored, the text does not say. The word translated “overthrown” is the one used of Sodom, and it can also carry the sense of being turned around — which is what actually happens.
The Response: Total Repentance (vv.5-9): The city believes God, and the repentance moves upward from the people to the king rather than being imposed from the throne down. The king leaves his seat, removes his robe, sits in ashes and issues a decree extending the fast to the animals — a detail that sounds strange until you realize it is how the ancient world expressed grief that admitted no exceptions. His reasoning is careful and theologically humble: “Who can tell if God will turn and repent?” He claims no promise. He orders his people to turn from violence, naming the specific sin Assyria was known for, and leaves the outcome with God.
God's Response: Judgment Averted (v.10): “God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way; and God repented of the evil.” God's relenting is not a change of character but the consistent application of it — Jeremiah 18:7-8 states the principle plainly: a declared judgment answers to repentance. What God saw was not their sackcloth but their turning. This one verse is what will send Jonah into the fury of chapter 4, and it is worth sitting with the fact that the prophet's complaint is about a successful mission.

Deep Dive

Setting: Jonah, a prophet of the northern kingdom under Jeroboam II (about 793–753 BC), ran from God's call to preach against Nineveh, was thrown into the sea, swallowed by a great fish and spat up on dry land (chs 1–2). Now the book starts over. Chapter 3 opens almost exactly as chapter 1 did, and this time Jonah goes. Nineveh, on the east bank of the Tigris across from modern Mosul in Iraq, was one of the chief cities of Assyria, an empire famous for its cruelty: its own royal inscriptions boast of flaying, impaling and piling up the heads of rebels. Assyria in the first half of the eighth century BC was, however, in a weak patch. Records from the reign of Ashur-dan III (about 773–755 BC) mention revolts, outbreaks of plague and a total eclipse of the sun in 763 BC, which Assyrians read as an evil omen. Many scholars think this unsettled time helps explain why a city might take a foreign prophet's warning seriously. The text itself gives one reason only: "the people of Nineveh believed God" (v5).

Jonah Goes to Nineveh (vv1–5)

  • "Then the word of the LORD came to Jonah the second time" (v1). The words of 1:1–2 are repeated nearly word for word. The Tyndale notes point out that the second half of the book is built to mirror the first. God does not start a new plan with a new prophet. He gives the same man the same job again. Guzik calls this pure grace: God owed Jonah nothing, yet he came back to him. Peter, after his denials, would know the same kind of second call (John 21:15–19).
  • "Call out against it the message that I tell you" (v2). In 1:2 God said to "call out against it, for their evil has come up before me." Now the emphasis falls on the message itself: Jonah is to say what God gives him, no more and no less. JFB notes that the message is left open, so Jonah must speak "whatever God may please."
  • "So Jonah arose and went to Nineveh, according to the word of the LORD" (v3). In 1:3 "Jonah rose" too, but to flee. Same verb, opposite direction. Nineveh was roughly 500 miles northeast of Israel, a journey of a month or more on foot.
  • "Nineveh was an exceedingly great city" (v3). Literally "a great city to God" ('ir gedolah le'lohim). This can simply be a Hebrew way of saying "very great," much as "mountains of God" means very high mountains (Ps 36:6). JFB takes it as greatness "before God." Many readers hear both: the city is huge, and it matters to God. The book's last verse will make that plain (4:11).
  • "Three days' journey in breadth" (v3). This phrase has puzzled readers. Excavated Nineveh, at its largest under Sennacherib a few decades later, had walls about seven and a half miles around, so a person could walk around the city proper in well under a day. The Tyndale notes suggest the phrase describes the time Jonah needed to go through the city preaching, stopping in its squares, or that it includes nearby towns. Guzik takes it to mean "Greater Nineveh," the whole district, which in Genesis 10:11–12 is grouped with Rehoboth-Ir, Calah and Resen as "the great city." JFB estimates a circuit of about sixty miles. An ancient diplomatic custom has also been suggested: a visit to a great city took three days, a day to arrive, a day for business and a day to leave. The point either way is that this was a huge task for one man.
  • "Jonah began to go into the city, going a day's journey" (v4). One day in, he is preaching. The text hints that the city responded before he had even finished.
  • "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown!" (v4). In Hebrew this is just five words. There is no call to repent, no mention of the LORD's name, no offer of mercy. Guzik thinks this was Jonah's headline rather than his whole sermon. Forty in Scripture is often a period of testing or waiting: the flood rains (Gen 7:4), Moses on the mountain (Exod 24:18), Israel's forty years, Elijah's forty days (1 Kings 19:8), Jesus' forty days in the wilderness (Matt 4:2). The Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) reads "three days," probably influenced by the "three days" earlier in the verse. The Hebrew "forty" is almost certainly original.
  • "Overthrown" (v4). The verb is haphak, "to overturn." It is the word used for the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah (Gen 19:21, 25; Deut 29:23; Amos 4:11), so a Hebrew reader would hear fire from heaven. But haphak can also mean "turned" or "changed," as when the heart is "turned" (Hos 11:8) or mourning is "turned" into dancing (Ps 30:11). Many scholars see a deliberate double meaning. Nineveh was overturned in forty days, just not the way Jonah expected. The city was turned upside down by repentance.
  • "And the people of Nineveh believed God" (v5). This is the heart of the chapter. The Hebrew phrase "believed in" (he'emin be) is the one used of Abraham, who "believed the LORD, and he counted it to him as righteousness" (Gen 15:6), and of Israel at the Red Sea (Exod 14:31). Notice it says they believed God (Elohim), not just Jonah. Henry: "they gave credit to the word which Jonah spoke to them in the name of God." It is the second time in the book that pagans respond rightly to God, as the Tyndale notes observe. First the sailors, now a whole city.
  • "They called for a fast and put on sackcloth, from the greatest of them to the least of them" (v5). Sackcloth was coarse cloth, usually goat's hair, worn in mourning or deep sorrow. Fasting went with it as a sign of humbling oneself before God (2 Sam 12:16; Neh 1:4; Joel 2:12–13). The Tyndale notes say the Assyrians had similar customs. Notice the order: the people act first, before the king's decree. Repentance began from the street up.
  • Commentators: Henry outlines the chapter as the second commission, the warning, the repentance and "God's gracious revocation of the sentence." JFB says the forty-day sentence was more definite and more severe than the first message, and that fasting and sackcloth were the outward signs of inward humility. Guzik stresses that repentance begins with believing God's word, and that this required someone to preach it, even a reluctant prophet. The Tyndale notes add that Jesus used this scene to rebuke his own generation (Matt 12:41).

The People of Nineveh Repent (vv6–10)

  • "The word reached the king of Nineveh" (v6). "King of Nineveh" is an unusual title. Assyrian rulers called themselves "king of Assyria," and Nineveh was not always the capital (it became the main royal city under Sennacherib around 700 BC). The Tyndale notes say this may be a local ruler or governor, or the king of Assyria himself, who at times ruled from Nineveh (2 Kings 19:36). The book never names him. Some have suggested Ashur-dan III or Adad-nirari III, but no Assyrian record mentions a mass repentance, and this is a guess.
  • "He arose from his throne, removed his robe, covered himself with sackcloth, and sat in ashes" (v6). Four actions, each a step down. He rose from the throne, took off the royal robe, put on sackcloth and sat in the dust. Sitting in ashes was the posture of deepest grief (Job 2:8; Isa 58:5). Henry: "The king set them a good example of humiliation." It is a picture of what Jonah also needed to learn: stepping down from where he sat.
  • "By the decree of the king and his nobles" (v7). Joining the nobles to the decree fits what is known of Assyrian government in this period, when powerful provincial governors shared real power with a weak king. Some scholars see this as a small sign of the book's historical setting.
  • "Let neither man nor beast, herd nor flock, taste anything" (v7). A total fast, no food and no water. And the animals too.
  • "Let man and beast be covered with sackcloth" (v8). This strikes modern readers as odd, even comic. The Tyndale notes say including the animals showed how serious the moment was: normal life stopped completely. The Greek historian Herodotus records Persians cutting the manes of their horses in mourning for a general, so the custom of animals sharing in mourning was known in the region. JFB: "The brute creatures share in the evil effects of man's sin." Joel also speaks of beasts groaning and crying to God (Joel 1:18, 20). The animals return in the book's final words (4:11).
  • "Let them call out mightily to God" (v8). "Call out" (qara') again, the key word of the book. Jonah was told to call out (1:2; 3:2). The captain told him to call out (1:6). The sailors called out (1:14). Jonah called from the deep (2:2). Now a pagan king tells his city to call out, and to do it "with strength."
  • "Let everyone turn from his evil way and from the violence that is in his hands" (v8). Repentance is not only fasting and sackcloth. It is turning from sin. The king names it: "evil" (ra'ah, the word God used in 1:2) and "violence" (chamas), the sin that brought the flood (Gen 6:11, 13). The Tyndale notes say violence was the main problem in this society. A century later Nahum calls Nineveh "the bloody city, all full of lies and plunder" (Nah 3:1). "In his hands" is vivid: violence held and used. Guzik notes the king offers no excuses: he names the sin and calls everyone to leave it.
  • "Who knows? God may turn and relent and turn from his fierce anger" (v9). This is exactly the hope Joel offers Judah: "Who knows whether he will not turn and relent?" (Joel 2:14). It echoes David too (2 Sam 12:22). The king makes no claim on God. He has no promise to stand on, as the Tyndale notes say, because Jonah offered none. JFB calls it "a vague possibility of God's mercy," and yet it was enough to act on. It is a striking contrast with the pagan captain's "Perhaps the god will give a thought to us" (1:6). Both times, pagans hope in a mercy they cannot demand.
  • "So that we may not perish" (v9). The sailors' prayer was "that we may not perish" (1:6, 14). The repeated phrase ties the two scenes together.
  • "When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil way" (v10). God saw what they did. Not just the sackcloth, but the turning. Genuine faith shows itself in a changed life (Matt 3:8; James 2:18).
  • "God relented of the disaster that he had said he would do to them" (v10). "Relented" is nacham, older translations "repented." "Disaster" is again ra'ah: Nineveh turned from its evil, and God turned from the evil, meaning the calamity, he had threatened. The wordplay is deliberate. Does God change his mind? The Bible says both that God "is not a man, that he should… change his mind" (Num 23:19; 1 Sam 15:29) and that he relents when people repent (Exod 32:14; Joel 2:13). Jeremiah 18:7–8 gives the principle: when God announces judgment on a nation and it turns from evil, he relents. The warning carried an unspoken "unless." The Tyndale notes say God's response was part of his plan all along, since he always intended mercy for those who repent, and that this does not mean God did not know the future. JFB says the change was "really a change in them and in God's corresponding dealings," described in human terms for our understanding. Some modern theologians (open theists) read the verse as God genuinely learning and changing, but most of the church through history has read it as the faithful response of an unchanging God to a changed people.
  • "And he did not do it" (v10). The judgment did not come in forty days. It came about 150 years later, in 612 BC, when the Babylonians and Medes destroyed Nineveh so completely that its site was lost for centuries. Nahum's whole book is about that fall. Guzik notes that God delayed judgment, but a later generation returned to the old violence. Henry, cautious, says only that "for some days they lived better." No Assyrian record of this repentance has been found, which is not surprising, since kings did not carve their humiliations into stone.
  • How deep was it? Readers ask whether this was saving faith or a passing fright. Jesus answered part of that: "The men of Nineveh will rise up at the judgment with this generation and condemn it, for they repented at the preaching of Jonah" (Matt 12:41; Luke 11:32). In his eyes their repentance was real enough to stand in judgment over people who had heard far more. Nothing in the text says they gave up their gods or were circumcised. They believed God's warning, humbled themselves and turned from violence, and God received it.
  • Commentators: Henry's lesson from vv6–9 is that "Even the beasts must do penance as well as man," and that Nineveh's hope rested only on "Who can tell," yet God answered it. Guzik sees the king's decree as a model of repentance, earnest prayer and action, and ties God's relenting to Jeremiah 18:7–8. JFB explains God's "repentance" as language that stoops to human understanding. All three, and the Tyndale notes, insist that God's mercy here does not mean his word failed. His warning did exactly what he meant it to do.

Pointing to Christ

Jonah preached five words of judgment with no offer of grace, and a city of pagans still believed God and turned. Jesus said that "something greater than Jonah is here" (Matt 12:41). He did not come grudgingly. He wept over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41). He did not merely threaten "forty days." He bore the overthrow himself, so that turning to God could rest not on "Who knows?" but on a promise: "everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" (Rom 10:13). Nineveh hoped that God might relent. In Christ, those who repent know that he has.

Reflection

  • A Second Call After Failure (vv.1-2) — God speaks to Jonah again instead of writing him off after his flight to Tarshish. Where do you need to believe that God is still calling you even after you've already said no once?
  • The Simplicity of a Faithful Message (v.4) — Jonah's entire sermon is only a handful of words, with no strategy or persuasion behind it, yet it moves an entire city to repentance. Are you holding back from sharing what God has put in front of you because you don't feel eloquent or prepared enough?
  • A Whole City Turns Together (v.5) — From the greatest to the least, the Ninevites respond to the warning with genuine humility and fasting. What would it look like for your own household or community to respond that wholeheartedly to conviction?
  • Repentance Proven by a Change of Direction (v.10) — God doesn't just see Nineveh's sackcloth; He sees their actual turning from evil, and He relents from the judgment He had planned. Is your own repentance visible in changed behavior, or does it stop at feeling sorry?
