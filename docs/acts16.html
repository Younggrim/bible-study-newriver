1Paul came to Derbe and then to Lystra, where he found a disciple named Timothy, the son of a believing Jewish woman and a Greek father.

2The brothers in Lystra and Iconium spoke well of him.

3Paul wanted Timothy to accompany him, so he took him and circumcised him on account of the Jews in that area, for they all knew that his father was a Greek.

4As they went from town to town, they delivered the decisions handed down by the apostles and elders in Jerusalem for the people to obey.

5So the churches were strengthened in the faith and grew daily in numbers.

6After the Holy Spirit had prevented them from speaking the word in the province of Asia, they traveled through the region of Phrygia and Galatia.

7And when they came to the border of Mysia, they tried to enter Bithynia, but the Spirit of Jesus would not permit them.

8So they passed by Mysia and went down to Troas.

9During the night, Paul had a vision of a man of Macedonia standing and pleading with him, “Come over to Macedonia and help us.”

10As soon as Paul had seen the vision, we got ready to leave for Macedonia, concluding that God had called us to preach the gospel to them.

11We sailed from Troas straight to Samothrace, and the following day on to Neapolis.

12From there we went to the Roman colony of Philippi, the leading city of that district of Macedonia. And we stayed there several days.

13On the Sabbath we went outside the city gate along the river, where it was customary to find a place of prayer. After sitting down, we spoke to the women who had gathered there.

14Among those listening was a woman named Lydia, a dealer in purple cloth from the city of Thyatira, who was a worshiper of God. The Lord opened her heart to respond to Paul’s message.

15And when she and her household had been baptized, she urged us, “If you consider me a believer in the Lord, come and stay at my house.” And she persuaded us.

16One day as we were going to the place of prayer, we were met by a slave girl with a spirit of divination, who earned a large income for her masters by fortune-telling.

17This girl followed Paul and the rest of us, shouting, “These men are servants of the Most High God, who are proclaiming to you the way of salvation!”

18She continued this for many days. Eventually Paul grew so aggravated that he turned and said to the spirit, “In the name of Jesus Christ I command you to come out of her!” And the spirit left her at that very moment.

19When the girl’s owners saw that their hope of making money was gone, they seized Paul and Silas and dragged them before the authorities in the marketplace.

20They brought them to the magistrates and said, “These men are Jews and are throwing our city into turmoil

21by promoting customs that are unlawful for us Romans to adopt or practice.”

22The crowd joined in the attack against Paul and Silas, and the magistrates ordered that they be stripped and beaten with rods.

23And after striking them with many blows, they threw them into prison and ordered the jailer to guard them securely.

24On receiving this order, he placed them in the inner cell and fastened their feet in the stocks.

25About midnight Paul and Silas were praying and singing hymns to God, and the other prisoners were listening to them.

26Suddenly a strong earthquake shook the foundations of the prison. At once all the doors flew open and everyone’s chains came loose.

27When the jailer woke up and saw the prison doors open, he drew his sword and was about to kill himself, presuming that the prisoners had escaped.

28But Paul called out in a loud voice, “Do not harm yourself! We are all here!”

29Calling for lights, the jailer rushed in and fell trembling before Paul and Silas.

30Then he brought them out and asked, “Sirs, what must I do to be saved?”

31They replied, “Believe in the Lord Jesus and you will be saved, you and your household.”

32Then Paul and Silas spoke the word of the Lord to him and to everyone in his house.

33At that hour of the night, the jailer took them and washed their wounds. And without delay, he and all his household were baptized.

34Then he brought them into his home and set a meal before them. So he and all his household rejoiced that they had come to believe in God.

35When daylight came, the magistrates sent their officers with the order: “Release those men.”

36The jailer informed Paul: “The magistrates have sent orders to release you. Now you may go on your way in peace.”

37But Paul said to the officers, “They beat us publicly without a trial and threw us into prison, even though we are Roman citizens. And now do they want to send us away secretly? Absolutely not! Let them come themselves and escort us out!”

38So the officers relayed this message to the magistrates, who were alarmed to hear that Paul and Silas were Roman citizens.

39They came to appease them and led them out, requesting that they leave the city.

40After Paul and Silas came out of the prison, they went to Lydia’s house to see the brothers and encourage them. Then they left the city.

1Then came he to Derbe and Lystra: and, behold, a certain disciple was there, named Timotheus, the son of a certain woman, which was a Jewess, and believed; but his father was a Greek:

2Which was well reported of by the brethren that were at Lystra and Iconium.

3Him would Paul have to go forth with him; and took and circumcised him because of the Jews which were in those quarters: for they knew all that his father was a Greek.

4And as they went through the cities, they delivered them the decrees for to keep, that were ordained of the apostles and elders which were at Jerusalem.

5And so were the churches established in the faith, and increased in number daily.

6Now when they had gone throughout Phrygia and the region of Galatia, and were forbidden of the Holy Ghost to preach the word in Asia,

7After they were come to Mysia, they assayed to go into Bithynia: but the Spirit suffered them not.

8And they passing by Mysia came down to Troas.

9And a vision appeared to Paul in the night; There stood a man of Macedonia, and prayed him, saying, Come over into Macedonia, and help us.

10And after he had seen the vision, immediately we endeavoured to go into Macedonia, assuredly gathering that the Lord had called us for to preach the gospel unto them.

11Therefore loosing from Troas, we came with a straight course to Samothracia, and the next day to Neapolis;

12And from thence to Philippi, which is the chief city of that part of Macedonia, and a colony: and we were in that city abiding certain days.

13And on the sabbath we went out of the city by a river side, where prayer was wont to be made; and we sat down, and spake unto the women which resorted thither.

14And a certain woman named Lydia, a seller of purple, of the city of Thyatira, which worshipped God, heard us: whose heart the Lord opened, that she attended unto the things which were spoken of Paul.

15And when she was baptized, and her household, she besought us, saying, If ye have judged me to be faithful to the Lord, come into my house, and abide there. And she constrained us.

16And it came to pass, as we went to prayer, a certain damsel possessed with a spirit of divination met us, which brought her masters much gain by soothsaying:

17The same followed Paul and us, and cried, saying, These men are the servants of the most high God, which shew unto us the way of salvation.

18And this did she many days. But Paul, being grieved, turned and said to the spirit, I command thee in the name of Jesus Christ to come out of her. And he came out the same hour.

19And when her masters saw that the hope of their gains was gone, they caught Paul and Silas, and drew them into the marketplace unto the rulers,

20And brought them to the magistrates, saying, These men, being Jews, do exceedingly trouble our city,

21And teach customs, which are not lawful for us to receive, neither to observe, being Romans.

22And the multitude rose up together against them: and the magistrates rent off their clothes, and commanded to beat them.

23And when they had laid many stripes upon them, they cast them into prison, charging the jailer to keep them safely:

24Who, having received such a charge, thrust them into the inner prison, and made their feet fast in the stocks.

25And at midnight Paul and Silas prayed, and sang praises unto God: and the prisoners heard them.

26And suddenly there was a great earthquake, so that the foundations of the prison were shaken: and immediately all the doors were opened, and every one’s bands were loosed.

27And the keeper of the prison awaking out of his sleep, and seeing the prison doors open, he drew out his sword, and would have killed himself, supposing that the prisoners had been fled.

28But Paul cried with a loud voice, saying, Do thyself no harm: for we are all here.

29Then he called for a light, and sprang in, and came trembling, and fell down before Paul and Silas,

30And brought them out, and said, Sirs, what must I do to be saved?

31And they said, Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved, and thy house.

32And they spake unto him the word of the Lord, and to all that were in his house.

33And he took them the same hour of the night, and washed their stripes; and was baptized, he and all his, straightway.

34And when he had brought them into his house, he set meat before them, and rejoiced, believing in God with all his house.

35And when it was day, the magistrates sent the serjeants, saying, Let those men go.

36And the keeper of the prison told this saying to Paul, The magistrates have sent to let you go: now therefore depart, and go in peace.

37But Paul said unto them, They have beaten us openly uncondemned, being Romans, and have cast us into prison; and now do they thrust us out privily? nay verily; but let them come themselves and fetch us out.

38And the serjeants told these words unto the magistrates: and they feared, when they heard that they were Romans.

39And they came and besought them, and brought them out, and desired them to depart out of the city.

40And they went out of the prison, and entered into the house of Lydia and when they had seen the brethren, they comforted them, and departed.

Summary

Paul begins his second missionary journey and adds Timothy to the team in Lystra, circumcising him out of consideration for the Jews in that region (vv. 1-3). Forbidden by the Spirit from preaching in Asia and Bithynia, Paul receives a night vision of a Macedonian man pleading for help, and the group sets sail for Macedonia (vv. 6-10). In Philippi, Lydia, a seller of purple goods, has her heart opened by the Lord and is baptized with her household (vv. 13-15). Paul casts a spirit of divination out of a slave girl, and when her owners lose their profit they drag Paul and Silas before the magistrates, who have them beaten and jailed (vv. 16-24). At midnight, as Paul and Silas pray and sing, an earthquake shakes the prison doors open; the jailer, about to take his own life, is stopped by Paul and asks what he must do to be saved, and he and his household believe and are baptized that night (vv. 25-34). The next day the magistrates try to release them quietly, but Paul insists on his rights as a Roman citizen before finally departing (vv. 35-40).

Authorship & Background

Author: Luke (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details). The first "we" passage begins in v.10 — indicating Luke joined Paul's team at Troas.
Historical Context: Chapter 16 begins Paul's second missionary journey and records the Gospel crossing into Europe for the first time. It contains Timothy joining the team (vv.1-5), the Macedonian call (vv.6-10), Lydia's conversion (vv.11-15), the slave girl's deliverance (vv.16-18), and the Philippian jailer's conversion (vv.25-34).
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: Timothy circumcised by Paul, of all people, right after the Jerusalem council settled that circumcision cannot be required, a tactic rather than a principle chosen to remove an obstacle among local Jews, the Spirit blocking Asia and Bithynia twice before a vision finally redirects Paul toward Macedonia, a businesswoman named Lydia becoming the first convert in Europe, her heart opened by the Lord rather than by the argument itself, a slave girl's true but unbearable shouting leading to a beating whose real cause is economic loss rather than religious objection, and a midnight earthquake answered by a jailer's question, what must I do to be saved, met with the simplest gospel statement in the whole book
Timothy Joins Them (vv.1-5): Timothy is introduced by his parentage, the son of a certain woman, which was a Jewess, and believed, but his father was a Greek, and by his reputation, well reported of by the brethren. Then the decision that looks like a contradiction of the chapter before it: Paul took and circumcised him because of the Jews which were in those quarters. Having just won the argument that circumcision cannot be required, he performs it where it removes an obstacle, which is the distinction between a principle and a tactic. The council's letters are delivered as they go, and the churches were established in the faith, and increased in number daily.
The Macedonian Call (vv.6-10): The Spirit blocked Paul from going to Asia and Bithynia — twice redirecting him. Then a vision: "Come over into Macedonia, and help us" (v.9). God's "no" to one direction was preparation for His "yes" to another. The Gospel entered Europe because Paul was willing to be redirected.
Lydia at Philippi (vv.11-15): The first convert in Europe is a businesswoman. On the sabbath they go outside the city to the riverside, where prayer was wont to be made, which suggests Philippi had no synagogue, and speak to the women gathered there. Lydia is a seller of purple, an expensive trade, and Luke credits the outcome to God rather than to the argument, whose heart the Lord opened, that she attended unto the things which were spoken of Paul. Her household is baptized and she presses them to stay in her house, and Luke keeps the form of the invitation, if ye have judged me to be faithful to the Lord, come into my house, and abide there. And she constrained us.
The Damsel and the Beating (vv.16-24): The girl with a spirit of divination follows them for many days shouting something true, these men are the servants of the most high God, which shew unto us the way of salvation, and Paul eventually finds it unbearable rather than useful. The charge brought after her deliverance is economic and is stated without embarrassment by her owners, they saw that the hope of their gains was gone, though the accusation they make in court is about religion and public order. Then the beating, and Luke gives the details that make the next scene legally explosive: they were rent of their clothes and beaten with many stripes without trial, thrust into the inner prison, and their feet made fast in the stocks.
The Philippian Jailer (vv.25-34): Paul and Silas, beaten and imprisoned, sang hymns at midnight. An earthquake opened the doors. The jailer, about to kill himself, asked "What must I do to be saved?" (v.30). Paul's answer: "Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved" (v.31) — the clearest, simplest statement of the Gospel in Acts.
Roman Citizens, Uncondemned (vv.35-40): In the morning the magistrates send word to let them go quietly, and Paul refuses to be released quietly. The objection is legal and precise: they have beaten us openly uncondemned, being Romans, and have cast us into prison, and now do they thrust us out privily? nay verily, but let them come themselves and fetch us out. Beating an uncondemned Roman citizen was a serious offense, and Luke records the reaction, they feared, when they heard that they were Romans. The magistrates come in person. Paul's reason for insisting is left implicit and it is not personal, because the young church at Lydia's house has to live in this city after he leaves.

Map & Geography

  • Lystra and Derbe (v.1): Paul picks up Timothy in Lystra (southern Asia Minor). Timothy's mother was Jewish, his father Greek.
  • Troas (v.8): Paul traveled through Phrygia, Galatia, and Mysia to the port city of Troas on the Aegean coast. Here he received the Macedonian vision.
  • Philippi (v.12): The first European city to receive the Gospel — a Roman colony in Macedonia (northern Greece). Lydia was converted and the jailer was saved here.

Deep Dive

Setting: Paul's second missionary journey begins, about AD 49–50. Paul and Barnabas have parted over John Mark (15:36–41), and Paul has set out overland with Silas, a Jerusalem prophet and Roman citizen, carrying the Jerusalem council's letter. They travel west from Syria through Cilicia and over the Taurus Mountains to the churches Paul planted on his first journey in what is now south-central Turkey. Then the Spirit closes door after door until Paul crosses the Aegean into Macedonia (northern Greece today). This is the gospel's first recorded church in Europe. In this chapter Luke also begins to say "we," quietly joining the story himself.

Timothy Joins Paul and Silas (vv1–5)

  • Derbe and Lystra (v1). Coming from the east, Paul reaches Derbe first, then Lystra, where he had been stoned and left for dead (14:19). He comes back to the place where he nearly died.
  • "A disciple… named Timothy" (v1). He was probably converted on Paul's first visit, since Paul calls him "my true child in the faith" (1 Tim 1:2). His mother Eunice and grandmother Lois were believing Jews who taught him the Scriptures from childhood (2 Tim 1:5; 3:15).
  • "His father was a Greek" (v1). A mixed marriage like this went against Jewish law, and Timothy had not been circumcised as a baby, probably because his father would not allow it. Later Jewish law, and probably custom already in Paul's day, counted the child of a Jewish mother as Jewish. So Timothy was a Jew who lacked the sign of the covenant.
  • "Well spoken of by the brothers" (v2). Timothy had a good name in two towns, Lystra and Iconium, about twenty miles apart. Paul wants a man with a proven character, not only gifts (1 Tim 3:7). Later Paul says of him, "I have no one like him" (Phil 2:20).
  • "Took him and circumcised him because of the Jews" (v3). This can look like a contradiction right after the Jerusalem council. But the council ruled that Gentiles need not be circumcised to be saved. Timothy was Jewish by birth, and an uncircumcised Jew would have been seen as a traitor to the covenant. That would have shut synagogue doors on him and on Paul. Contrast Titus, a full Gentile, whom Paul refused to have circumcised when others demanded it as necessary (Gal 2:3–5). The issue was never the rite itself but what it was made to mean. Paul's principle: "To the Jews I became as a Jew, in order to win Jews" (1 Cor 9:20).
  • "Delivered to them for observance the decisions" (v4). "Decisions" is dogmata, the source of "dogma," here meaning official rulings. The Galatian churches received the council's letter directly. Paul did not keep the Jerusalem decision to himself, even though he had been the one arguing for it.
  • "Strengthened in the faith, and they increased in numbers daily" (v5). This is another of Luke's progress reports (6:7; 9:31). The churches grew stronger and larger together.
  • Commentators: Guzik and JFB both make the Timothy and Titus contrast the key. Guzik notes that God replaced Barnabas and Mark with Silas and Timothy, so no worker is beyond replacing. JFB says circumcision was "a rite which every Israelite might perform," and so Paul could do it himself. JFB also thinks Timothy's ordination with the laying on of hands (1 Tim 4:14; 2 Tim 1:6) took place around this time, which is a reasonable guess. Guzik adds that churches grow most healthily when they are strong in faith, not when they rely on clever methods.

The Macedonian Call (vv6–10)

  • "Forbidden by the Holy Spirit to speak the word in Asia" (v6). "Asia" here is the Roman province in western Turkey, with Ephesus as its capital, not the continent. Paul's natural next step was west on the main road to Ephesus. The Spirit blocked it. Luke does not say how, whether by prophecy, circumstance or an inner conviction. Paul would get to Ephesus later (19:1).
  • "The region of Phrygia and Galatia" (v6). The wording is debated. Many take it to mean the area around Iconium and Antioch in Pisidia already visited. Others think Paul turned north into the ethnic Galatian territory around modern Ankara. Which one is chosen affects which churches Paul's letter to the Galatians was written to.
  • "The Spirit of Jesus did not allow them" (v7). Next they tried north toward Bithynia, on the Black Sea. This title, "the Spirit of Jesus," appears only here in the New Testament. The Holy Spirit (v6) is the Spirit of Jesus (v7). The risen Lord is personally directing his mission (1:1–2; Rom 8:9). Peter's first letter later greets churches in Bithynia (1 Pet 1:1), so the gospel reached it in God's time.
  • "Passing by Mysia, they went down to Troas" (v8). With west and north closed, they went northwest to Troas, a port on the Aegean near ancient Troy. They had reached the edge of Asia and the sea.
  • "A man of Macedonia… 'Come over to Macedonia and help us'" (v9). The vision explains the closed doors. Macedonia was the homeland of Philip II and Alexander the Great. Paul's later letters show how close he became to churches in Philippi, Thessalonica and Berea. Some have guessed that the man in the vision was Luke, but nothing in the text says so.
  • "Immediately we sought to go on into Macedonia" (v10). This is the first "we" passage in Acts (16:10–17; 20:5–21:18; 27:1–28:16). The simplest explanation, held by most scholars and by early church tradition, is that Luke, the author, joined the team at Troas. The "we" stops at Philippi (after v17) and starts again in Philippi years later (20:5), so Luke may have stayed to care for the new church.
  • "Concluding that God had called us" (v10). "Concluding" means putting things together. They weighed the closed doors and the vision together and reached a decision as a team.
  • Commentators: Guzik observes that the Spirit often guides by closing doors as well as opening them, and that Paul's team kept moving instead of waiting passively. He also treats this as the gospel's first planned mission into Europe. JFB explains that "Asia" meant only the western coastal province, and takes the "we" as proof that "the historian himself had now joined the missionary party." Guzik suggests Luke the doctor might have joined because Paul needed medical care, a guess he offers as such. The Tyndale notes list the many ways the Spirit guides in Acts: visions, inner promptings, counsel, prayer, Scripture and prophecy.

The Conversion of Lydia (vv11–15)

  • "A direct voyage to Samothrace, and the following day to Neapolis" (v11). About 150 miles in two days, which means a strong following wind. Later the same trip the other way takes five days (20:6). Neapolis (modern Kavala) was the port for Philippi.
  • Philippi (v12). It was named for Philip II of Macedon, Alexander's father. In 42 BC Octavian and Mark Antony defeated Brutus and Cassius, the assassins of Julius Caesar, nearby. Afterward Rome settled army veterans there and made it "a Roman colony," a little Rome abroad with Roman law and Latin speech. Its citizens were proud of it (v21), and that pride shapes the whole chapter. Paul later reminds this church that "our citizenship is in heaven" (Phil 3:20). It stood on the Via Egnatia, the great Roman road across the Balkans.
  • "A leading city of the district" (v12). The Greek here is uncertain. The ESV follows most manuscripts: a "leading" or "first" city. Some think the original read "a city of the first district," since Rome had divided Macedonia into four districts and Philippi lay in the first. The ESV footnote mentions this.
  • "Outside the gate to the riverside, where we supposed there was a place of prayer" (v13). Jewish tradition required ten men to form a synagogue. Apparently Philippi did not have them, so the Jews and God-fearers met outdoors by the river, probably the Gangites, a little over a mile west. Paul's habit was the synagogue first. Here he finds only a group of women and sits down with them to teach, as a rabbi would.
  • Lydia (v14). Her name may mean "the Lydian woman," since Thyatira was in the region of Lydia in western Asia Minor. That was the very province of Asia where the Spirit had stopped Paul from preaching. God brought a woman from Asia to meet Paul in Europe. Thyatira was famous for its dyers, and a dyers' guild there is known from inscriptions. Thyatira later received one of the seven letters in Revelation (Rev 2:18–29).
  • "A seller of purple goods" (v14). Purple cloth was a luxury, dyed with an expensive dye from shellfish or, in Lydia's region, from madder root. Lydia was a businesswoman with a household and a house big enough to host guests. "A worshiper of God" means a Gentile who worshiped Israel's God and had not fully converted, like Cornelius (10:2).
  • "The Lord opened her heart to pay attention" (v14). Paul spoke, and the Lord opened. Luke gives both sides plainly: human preaching and listening, and God's prior work. The same verb is used of Jesus opening the disciples' minds to understand the Scriptures (Luke 24:45).
  • "She was baptized, and her household as well" (v15). A household in the Roman world included family, servants and dependents. Christians differ over whether household baptisms in Acts included infants (also v33; 11:14; 18:8; 1 Cor 1:16). The text does not say, and each tradition reads it in light of its wider theology of baptism.
  • "Come to my house and stay" (v15). Her first act as a believer is hospitality. "She prevailed upon us" suggests Paul hesitated, perhaps not wanting to be a burden (1 Thess 2:9). Her home became the church's first meeting place in Philippi (v40). The Philippians later supported Paul with gifts again and again (Phil 4:15–16), and Lydia's generosity may have set that pattern.
  • Commentators: Henry and JFB both stress that grace came first in Lydia's heart. JFB: "The first disposition to turn to the Gospel is a work of grace." Guzik draws from v14 that evangelism depends on God opening hearts, so prayer belongs with preaching. The Tyndale notes count Lydia as the first of three people Luke shows changed in Philippi: a wealthy woman, an exploited slave girl and a middle-class Roman official. JFB thinks children were probably included in the household baptisms, but says the question of infant baptism "must be determined on other grounds."

Paul and Silas in Prison (vv16–24)

  • "A spirit of divination" (v16). The Greek is literally "a spirit, a python." In Greek myth Python was the serpent that guarded the oracle at Delphi, slain by Apollo, who was then called "Pythian." By Paul's day "python" was also a name for fortune-tellers believed to be inspired by Apollo, sometimes speaking like ventriloquists. Luke reports what people called her, but he treats it as a real evil spirit.
  • "Brought her owners much gain" (v16). "Owners" is plural, so a group owned her and made money from her. She was exploited twice over, by a spirit and by her owners.
  • "These men are servants of the Most High God, who proclaim to you the way of salvation" (v17). What she said was true, yet dangerous. "Most High God" was a title pagans also used for Zeus. And the Greek has no article: she proclaims "a way of salvation," which in Philippi could sound like one more path among many gods. Jesus also silenced demons who announced him (Mark 1:24–25, 34). The gospel was not to be endorsed by the powers it came to defeat.
  • "Having become greatly annoyed" (v18). The word means deeply troubled or worn down. Paul waited "many days," which suggests he did not act lightly.
  • "I command you in the name of Jesus Christ to come out of her" (v18). Paul does not invoke his own power. He speaks in Jesus' name, and the spirit leaves "that very hour." It is the opposite of the sons of Sceva, who tried to use the name without knowing its owner (19:13–16).
  • "When her owners saw that their hope of gain was gone" (v19). Luke plays on words. The same verb, "came out," is used of the spirit in v18 and the hope of profit in v19: when the spirit left, the money went with it. The girl's freedom meant nothing to them.
  • "These men are Jews… not lawful for us as Romans" (vv20–21). The charge leaves out the real reason (money) and appeals to prejudice and civic pride. Jews were officially tolerated but disliked, and only a few years earlier Emperor Claudius had expelled Jews from Rome (18:2). Romans were not supposed to adopt foreign cults without approval. "Customs" sounds like law-abiding concern, but it hides greed.
  • "The magistrates tore the garments off them and gave orders to beat them with rods" (v22). The magistrates were the two chief officials of a Roman colony, here called by the Greek title stratēgoi. They were attended by lictors who carried bundles of rods, the "police" of v35, literally "rod-bearers." The beating was quick, public and humiliating. Paul later writes, "Three times I have been beaten with rods" (2 Cor 11:25), and remembers how they "suffered and been shamefully treated at Philippi" (1 Thess 2:2).
  • "The inner prison… their feet in the stocks" (v24). The jailer took no chances. The innermost cell was the darkest and dampest, and stocks could stretch the legs painfully apart. Paul and Silas sat with fresh wounds and no way to lie down.
  • Commentators: Henry says it troubled Paul to see the girl "made an instrument of Satan to deceive people," and on the owners: "See here what evil the love of money is the root of!" JFB explains the spirit as one "supposed to be inspired by the Pythian Apollo," and calls the possession "as undeniable as that of any in the Gospel history." Guzik follows a commentator who links the word to the Python of Apollo's shrine, and the lexicon supports that. He adds that demons can read people and make guesses but cannot truly know the future as God does.

The Philippian Jailer Converted (vv25–40)

  • "About midnight Paul and Silas were praying and singing hymns to God" (v25). With bleeding backs, in the dark, they sang. "The prisoners were listening" uses a word for listening closely. It was not a sound anyone expected from that cell. Paul later writes to this church from another prison: "Rejoice in the Lord always" (Phil 4:4).
  • "A great earthquake… all the doors were opened, and everyone's bonds were unfastened" (v26). Northern Greece is earthquake country, but Luke presents this as God's act. Doors swinging open and every chain falling loose go beyond a natural tremor, much as Peter's chains fell off (12:7). Earlier in Acts, the place where the believers prayed was shaken too (4:31).
  • "He drew his sword and was about to kill himself" (v27). Under Roman practice a guard who lost his prisoners could suffer their penalty (12:19; 27:42). He preferred a quick death to disgrace and execution. Philippi was a colony of retired soldiers, and Roman tradition saw suicide as a way to escape shame.
  • "Do not harm yourself, for we are all here" (v28). Perhaps Paul could see the jailer outlined in the doorway by the light outside, while the jailer could not see into the dark cell. The surprise is not only that the doors opened. It is that no one ran. The man who had just locked their feet in the stocks is the first one Paul cares for.
  • "Sirs, what must I do to be saved?" (v30). "Sirs" is kyrioi, "lords," the polite word. Paul's answer uses the same word for its real owner: "Believe in the Lord Jesus." The jailer's question may at first have meant "How do I get out of this mess?" But he had heard the slave girl's cry about "the way of salvation" (v17), and perhaps the hymns at midnight. Paul answers on the deepest level.
  • "Believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household" (v31). Salvation comes through trusting Jesus as Lord, not by keeping the law, with no rite added (15:11). The verb "believe" (pisteuō) means to trust, to rely on. "You and your household" does not mean his family is saved by his faith. Verse 32 shows they too heard the word, and v34 shows the whole household believed. The offer reached them all.
  • "Washed their wounds; and he was baptized" (v33). Probably at a well or fountain in the prison courtyard, in the same water. He washes their stripes and they wash him in baptism, two washings at the same hour of the night.
  • "Rejoiced along with his entire household that he had believed in God" (v34). A pagan Roman official, at a table in the middle of the night, with two Jews who were his prisoners an hour before. Joy runs through Luke's story of salvation (8:8, 39; 13:48, 52).
  • "Let those men go" (v35). The magistrates may have decided a beating and a night in jail was lesson enough, or the earthquake may have unsettled them. Either way they wanted the problem gone quietly.
  • "Men who are Roman citizens… Let them come themselves and take us out" (v37). Roman law strictly forbade beating a citizen without a trial. The claim "I am a Roman citizen" was meant to stop such treatment, and magistrates who ignored it could lose office. Why Paul did not speak up the day before is not said. He may not have been heard over the mob. Now he insists on a public release, not to get revenge but to protect the new church: the believers left behind must not be seen as followers of convicted criminals.
  • "They came and apologized to them" (v39). The same officials who stripped and beat them now come in person and plead with them. They "asked them to leave the city" because they could not guarantee their safety, or did not want more trouble.
  • "They visited Lydia… encouraged them and departed" (v40). Paul, just out of prison, encourages the church, not the other way round. "Them" and "departed" are third person: the "we" has stopped. Luke apparently stayed behind in Philippi.
  • Commentators: Henry says the jailer's salvation "is now his great concern, and lies nearest his heart, which before was the furthest thing from his thoughts." JFB suggests they may have sung the Passover psalms (Pss 113–118), which is possible but only a guess. It calls Paul's answer "singularly beautiful" for its "brevity, simplicity, and directness." JFB notes that colony citizens were exempt "from scourging," and that the public beating was "the most stinging feature" for Paul. Guzik holds that the jailer's trembling already showed a turned heart, so "believe" was all he needed to hear. On v37 he observes that Paul seems to have chosen the right time to use his citizenship, and that rights served the gospel, not Paul's pride.

Pointing to Christ

Three people, about as unlike as Philippi could produce, are each freed by the same Lord: a rich merchant whose heart he opens, a slave girl he releases from a spirit and her owners' grip, and a soldier jailer he spares from his own sword. Paul and Silas sing in the dark because the Jesus who suffered unjustly and rose again holds them. The jailer's question is the oldest question, and the answer is still the same: "Believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved."

Reflection

  • God's "No" Prepares the Way for a Bigger "Yes" (v.9) — The Spirit blocks Paul from Asia and Bithynia twice before a vision redirects him to Macedonia. Where has a closed door in your life turned out to be redirecting you toward something better?
  • Salvation Is God's Work on the Heart (v.14) — Luke notes that it was the Lord who opened Lydia's heart to respond to Paul's words. How does that truth shape the way you pray for the people in your life who don't yet believe?
  • When the Gospel Threatens Someone's Bottom Line (v.19) — The slave girl's owners turn violently against Paul once his ministry costs them money. Where has following Christ cost you something financially, and are you willing to accept that cost?
  • Worship in the Worst Circumstances (v.25) — Beaten and imprisoned, Paul and Silas are found praying and singing hymns at midnight. How do you respond when life is at its hardest — with complaint, or with praise?
  • The Simplest Statement of the Gospel (v.31) — Paul tells the desperate jailer that believing in the Lord Jesus is all that's required to be saved. Could you explain the Gospel that simply if someone asked you right now?
  • Instant, Total Transformation (v.34) — In one night the jailer moves from planning to end his life to rejoicing with his whole household. Who in your life seems beyond hope, and how does this story challenge that assumption?
