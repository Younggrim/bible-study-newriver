1It is for freedom that Christ has set us free. Stand firm, then, and do not be encumbered once more by a yoke of slavery.

2Take notice: I, Paul, tell you that if you let yourselves be circumcised, Christ will be of no value to you at all.

3Again I testify to every man who gets himself circumcised that he is obligated to obey the whole law.

4You who are trying to be justified by the law have been severed from Christ; you have fallen away from grace.

5But by faith we eagerly await through the Spirit the hope of righteousness.

6For in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision has any value. What matters is faith expressing itself through love.

7You were running so well. Who has obstructed you from obeying the truth?

8Such persuasion does not come from the One who calls you.

9A little leaven works through the whole batch of dough.

10I am confident in the Lord that you will take no other view. The one who is troubling you will bear the judgment, whoever he may be.

11Now, brothers, if I am still preaching circumcision, why am I still being persecuted? In that case the offense of the cross has been abolished.

12As for those who are agitating you, I wish they would proceed to emasculate themselves!

13For you, brothers, were called to freedom; but do not use your freedom as an opportunity for the flesh. Rather, serve one another in love.

14The entire law is fulfilled in a single decree: “Love your neighbor as yourself.”

15But if you keep on biting and devouring one another, watch out, or you will be consumed by one another.

16So I say, walk by the Spirit, and you will not gratify the desires of the flesh.

17For the flesh craves what is contrary to the Spirit, and the Spirit what is contrary to the flesh. They are opposed to each other, so that you do not do what you want.

18But if you are led by the Spirit, you are not under the law.

19The acts of the flesh are obvious: sexual immorality, impurity, and debauchery;

20idolatry and sorcery; hatred, discord, jealousy, and rage; rivalries, divisions, factions,

21and envy; drunkenness, orgies, and the like. I warn you, as I did before, that those who practice such things will not inherit the kingdom of God.

22But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness,

23gentleness, and self-control. Against such things there is no law.

24Those who belong to Christ Jesus have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires.

25Since we live by the Spirit, let us walk in step with the Spirit.

26Let us not become conceited, provoking and envying one another.

Summary

Paul opens by declaring that Christ has set believers free and urges them to stand firm rather than submit again to slavery, warning that accepting circumcision as a requirement severs them from Christ and obligates them to keep the whole law (vv. 1-6). He rebukes whoever has hindered their progress, comparing false teaching to leaven spreading through the whole batch (vv. 7-12), and calls them to use their freedom to serve one another in love, since the whole law is fulfilled in loving one's neighbor (vv. 13-15). Walking by the Spirit rather than the flesh keeps believers from sinful desires; he contrasts the works of the flesh with the fruit of the Spirit — love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control — noting that those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh (vv. 16-26).

Authorship & Background

Author: Paul (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 5 is the practical turning point of Galatians. Having established the theological argument for justification by faith (chs. 1-4), Paul now addresses the practical question: If we are free from the Law, how should we live? His answer: Walk in the Spirit. This chapter contains two of the most famous passages in all of Paul's letters — the works of the flesh (vv.19-21) and the fruit of the Spirit (vv.22-23).
Classification: Epistle — Pauline
Key Themes: Liberty declared before any instruction on how to use it, circumcision accepted as salvation shown to obligate the whole law, freedom fulfilled in serving one another rather than the flesh, works of the flesh and fruit of the Spirit listed as two harvests rather than two rule-books, and a flesh already crucified alongside its affections and lusts
Stand Fast in Liberty (vv.1-12): Paul opens with a ringing declaration of freedom: "Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free" (v.1). He warns that accepting circumcision as necessary for salvation means accepting the entire Law — and falling from grace (v.4). The only thing that counts is "faith which worketh by love" (v.6).
Liberty, Not License (vv.13-15): Freedom is not an excuse for sin. True liberty expresses itself in love: "By love serve one another" (v.13). The entire Law is fulfilled in one command: "Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself" (v.14).
Walk in the Spirit (vv.16-26): The flesh and Spirit are at war (v.17). Paul lists the works of the flesh — 15 sins covering sexual immorality, idolatry, relational conflict, and excess (vv.19-21). Then the fruit of the Spirit — 9 qualities that describe the character of Christ (vv.22-23). Those who belong to Christ "have crucified the flesh with the affections and lusts" (v.24).

Deep Dive

Setting: Paul writes to the churches of Galatia, in the middle of modern Turkey. Many scholars think these are the churches of his first journey (Acts 13–14) and date the letter about AD 48–49. Others place them farther north and date it to the mid-50s. Either way the crisis is the same. After Paul left, teachers arrived (usually called Judaizers) insisting that Gentile believers must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to be fully right with God (Acts 15:1). Chapters 1–4 argued that a person is justified by faith in Christ, not by works of the law, and chapter 4 ended with the picture of Hagar and Sarah: believers are "children, not of the slave but of the free woman" (4:31). Chapter 5 turns that argument into a call to action. Stand in that freedom, and then use it the way the Spirit leads.

Christ Has Set Us Free (vv1–15)

  • "For freedom Christ has set us free" (v1). The sentence almost repeats itself on purpose. Freedom is both the gift and the goal. Christ did not free believers so they could pick up a new set of chains. JFB points out that the Greek has no "in" here, so it reads "It is FOR freedom that" Christ freed us. "Stand firm" means freedom can be lost by drifting back, not only by being dragged.
  • "A yoke of slavery" (v1). A yoke (zygos) was the wooden bar that harnessed oxen for work. Jewish teachers spoke with honor of taking "the yoke of the law." Peter used the same word at the Jerusalem Council for a yoke "that neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear" (Acts 15:10). Jesus offered a different one, "easy" and "light" (Matt 11:30). "Again" is pointed: these Gentiles had once served idols (4:8–9), and earning favor by the law would be one more form of the same slavery.
  • "If you accept circumcision, Christ will be of no advantage to you" (v2). Paul circumcised Timothy, who had a Jewish mother, so he could minister among Jews (Acts 16:3), and he says plainly in v6 that neither circumcision nor uncircumcision "counts for anything". The issue is circumcision taken as a requirement for being accepted by God. Once something is added to Christ as a condition, Christ is no longer trusted as enough.
  • "Obligated to keep the whole law" (v3). Circumcision was the doorway into the whole covenant of Moses. Choosing the law as the path to righteousness means taking on all of it, with no partial credit (3:10; James 2:10).
  • "You are severed from Christ… you have fallen away from grace" (v4). "Severed" translates katargeō, which means to make idle or ineffective, and with "from" means to be released or cut loose from something. Paul uses the same construction in Romans 7:2 and 7:6 for a widow released from the law of her husband and believers released from the law. Here it is turned around: those trying to be justified by the law have cut themselves loose from Christ. "Fallen away" (ekpiptō) means to fall out of a place, and Luke uses it of a ship driven off course (Acts 27:17, 29). In context "falling from grace" does not mean committing a bad sin. It means leaving the ground of grace to stand on the ground of law.
  • Can a believer lose salvation? Arminian and Wesleyan readers see in v4 a real warning that true believers can fall away. Reformed readers see a warning God uses to keep his people, or a description of those who professed faith without resting on Christ. All agree on Paul's point: a person cannot stand on law and grace at once.
  • "Through the Spirit, by faith, we ourselves eagerly wait for the hope of righteousness" (v5). The "hope of righteousness" is God's final verdict at the last day. Believers already have it by faith (2:16) and wait eagerly for it to be announced. The Judaizers work to earn it; believers wait for it.
  • "Only faith working through love" (v6). Faith is what joins a person to Christ, and real faith does not sit still. It goes to work, and the way it works is love. Christians have long discussed this verse. Medieval Catholic theology spoke of faith "formed by love" as what justifies. The Reformers answered that faith alone justifies, but the faith that justifies is never alone, because it always works through love. Paul's point in Galatians is that circumcision adds nothing, and love is the evidence that faith is alive.
  • "You were running well" (v7). The race picture is a favorite of Paul's (1 Cor 9:24; Phil 3:14). "Hindered" (enkoptō) means to obstruct or thwart. There is a play on words between "obeying" (peithō, to be persuaded) the truth and the "persuasion" (peismonē) of v8. Someone had talked them out of the truth, and it did not come from God who called them.
  • "A little leaven leavens the whole lump" (v9). This was a proverb, and Paul uses it again in 1 Corinthians 5:6. A pinch of yeast spreads through the whole batch of dough. Adding one small requirement to the gospel, such as "Christ plus circumcision", changes the whole thing.
  • "Whoever he is" (v10). Paul either did not know the agitators' names or did not care how important they were (compare 2:6). God would judge them. About the Galatians themselves he is hopeful.
  • "If I… still preach circumcision, why am I still being persecuted?" (v11). Apparently someone claimed Paul himself preached circumcision when it suited him, perhaps pointing to Timothy. Paul's reply: then why do Jewish opponents keep hunting him? If he taught that Gentiles must become Jews, "the offense of the cross has been removed." "Offense" is skandalon, originally the trigger-stick of a trap, then anything that trips people up. A crucified Messiah who saves sinners by grace offends human pride (1 Cor 1:23). Add circumcision, and the offense disappears, because people can contribute something again. "Removed" is the same verb as "severed" in v4.
  • "I wish those who unsettle you would emasculate themselves!" (v12). Paul at his bluntest. "Unsettle" (anastatoō) is a strong word for stirring up revolt, used of the Egyptian rebel in Acts 21:38. "Emasculate themselves" translates apokoptō in the middle voice, which the lexicon gives as to mutilate oneself or have oneself castrated. It points to Deuteronomy 23:1, which kept such men out of the assembly. The priests of the goddess Cybele, whose great shrine at Pessinus was in Galatian territory, castrated themselves in her service. The bitter irony: if cutting the body could please God, they should go all the way, like the pagan priests, and so cut themselves off from God's people. Paul's anger is for the troublemakers, not the troubled. He is protecting young believers from being led back into slavery.
  • "For you were called to freedom, brothers" (v13). Here Paul faces the obvious objection: if believers are not under the law, won't they just do as they please? His answer is no. "Opportunity" (aphormē) was a military word for a base of operations, a foothold from which an army launches attacks. Freedom must not become a beachhead for the flesh. Instead, "through love serve one another." The word for serve is douleuō, to be a slave. Paul has just told them not to submit to a yoke of slavery (v1). Now he tells them to be slaves to one another in love. The freedom of the gospel is freedom for love.
  • "The whole law is fulfilled in one word" (v14). Paul quotes Leviticus 19:18, the same verse Jesus called the second great commandment (Mark 12:31), and makes the same point in Romans 13:8–10. "Is fulfilled" is a perfect tense: the law has been brought to its full meaning in love. This is not a back door to law-keeping. Those who walk in love end up doing what the law was aiming at all along.
  • "If you bite and devour one another" (v15). The pictures are of wild animals tearing at each other. The circumcision debate seems to have split the churches into quarrelling camps, and a church at war with itself consumes itself.
  • Commentators: All three agree that the problem in v2 is circumcision as a condition of salvation, not the rite itself, and JFB adds that circumcision stands for legal works in general. Guzik draws out the logic: anyone who adds a requirement to Christ ends up losing Christ, a point he backs with a line from Calvin about wanting half a Christ and losing the whole. Henry: "Faith, where it is true, is a working grace." Guzik and JFB both say "hindered" (v7) pictures breaking up a road to block an advance. The lexicon gives the word only as "hinder, thwart," so that image should not be pressed. On v12 JFB rejects the castration reading and takes it to mean being cut off from the church, but the lexicon supports the ESV: the middle voice means to mutilate oneself, and it cites Deuteronomy 23:1. Guzik and the Tyndale notes both connect the verse to the Cybele cult. Guzik's note that aphormē (v13) was a military term is supported by the lexicon. Henry on v15: "those that devour one another are in a fair way to be consumed one of another."

Keep in Step with the Spirit (vv16–26)

  • "Walk by the Spirit" (v16). "Walk" (peripateō) is everyday Bible language for how a person lives. Its Hebrew counterpart (halak) gave later Judaism halakha, the rules for walking under the law. Paul offers a different way to walk. The promise is strong. The Greek uses a double negative: such a person will by no means carry out the flesh's desires. Paul does not promise the desires will vanish, only that they will not have the last word.
  • "The flesh" (v16). Paul's word is sarx. It can mean physical flesh or the human body, but in Paul's letters it often carries a moral sense: the lexicon describes it as the seat and vehicle of sinful desires, set against the Spirit (5:16–17, 19; Rom 8:4–9). It does not mean the body is evil: jealousy and rivalry are "works of the flesh" too (v20). "Flesh" is human nature turned away from God and trying to live without him, which explains why law-keeping in one's own strength can be just as fleshly as open sin (3:3).
  • "These are opposed to each other" (v17). Christians have read this verse in two main ways. Some take it as the believer's daily struggle, close to Romans 7:14–25: the Spirit and the flesh pull in opposite directions, so neither the good nor the evil a person wants is ever done completely. Others stress the context, which is about victory (vv16, 18): the Spirit opposes the flesh precisely to stop a believer from doing whatever the flesh wants. Both readings agree that the Christian life is a real conflict, and that the answer is the Spirit, not more rules.
  • "If you are led by the Spirit, you are not under the law" (v18). The law could name sin but not change the heart. The Spirit writes God's will on the heart, as the prophets promised (Jer 31:33; Ezek 36:27).
  • "The works of the flesh are evident" (v19). Notice "works," plural, and "fruit," singular, in v22. Works are things people produce. Fruit is something that grows. The list is not complete ("and things like these," v21), and it falls into four rough groups.
  • Sexual sins (v19). "Sexual immorality" (porneia) covers any sexual activity outside marriage. "Impurity" is moral uncleanness. "Sensuality" (aselgeia) is licentiousness or wantonness, sin with no sense of shame left, done openly. Some pagan worship included them.
  • Religious sins (v20). "Idolatry" is worshiping anything other than the true God. "Sorcery" is pharmakeia. The root has to do with drugs and potions, and English gets "pharmacy" from it. But the lexicon gives its New Testament sense as sorcery or witchcraft, which fits its place right beside idolatry. Magic often used drugs and potions in spells (Acts 19:19; Rev 18:23). Paul is condemning occult practice; drug abuse is related, but it is not the main point of the word here.
  • Relational sins (vv20–21). This is the longest group, eight words, in a church that was biting and devouring (v15). "Enmity" is settled hostility. "Strife" is quarrelling. "Jealousy" (zēlos) can be good zeal elsewhere, but here it is resentful rivalry. "Fits of anger" are sudden outbursts. "Rivalries" (eritheia) originally meant working for pay, then canvassing for office, and so came to mean selfish, party-minded ambition. "Dissensions" (dichostasia) is literally "standing apart." "Divisions" (haireseis, from a verb meaning "to choose") are factions, people choosing sides. It later gave us "heresy," but here it means parties within the church rather than false doctrine as such. "Envy" is resentment at someone else's good.
  • Sins of excess (v21). "Drunkenness" and "orgies" (kōmos) belong together. A kōmos was a drunken revel, often a noisy street procession after a feast.
  • A textual note. The King James Version lists "adultery" in v19 and "murders" in v21. Neither word appears in the oldest manuscripts, so most modern translations, including the ESV, leave them out. "Envy" and "murders" look almost the same in Greek (phthonoi, phonoi), which may explain how one crept in.
  • "Will not inherit the kingdom of God" (v21). "Those who do such things" describes people whose lives are characterized by these things, not believers who stumble and repent. 1 Corinthians 6:9–11 gives the same warning and adds, "such were some of you. But you were washed."
  • "But the fruit of the Spirit" (v22). One fruit, nine qualities. The Spirit does not hand out love to one believer and patience to another, the way he distributes gifts (1 Cor 12:11). He grows the whole character in every believer. It is the fruit of the Spirit: Jesus said, "apart from me you can do nothing" (John 15:5).
  • Love, joy, peace (v22). Love (agapē) heads the list, and some read the other eight as its description (compare 1 Cor 13:4–7). Joy (chara) is gladness in God that does not depend on circumstances. Peace (eirēnē) carries the Hebrew sense of shalom, wholeness, first with God (Rom 5:1) and then with others.
  • Patience, kindness, goodness (v22). Patience is makrothymia, literally "long-tempered," the opposite of a short fuse. In the Greek Old Testament it is the family of words for the Hebrew phrase "slow to anger," used of God himself (Exod 34:6). Kindness (chrēstotēs) is goodness of heart, the kindness of God that leads to repentance (Rom 2:4). Goodness (agathōsynē) is close to it. It is goodness that acts.
  • Faithfulness, gentleness, self-control (vv22–23). "Faithfulness" is pistis, usually translated "faith." Here, in a list of character qualities, it means being trustworthy and reliable, as God is faithful. Gentleness (praotēs) is not weakness. Moses was called the meekest man on earth (Num 12:3), and Jesus called himself "gentle and lowly in heart" (Matt 11:29). It is strength under control. Self-control (enkrateia) is mastery over one's desires, and it answers directly the sins of excess at the end of the flesh list.
  • "Against such things there is no law" (v23). A deliberate understatement. No law forbids love or patience. Those who walk by the Spirit end up living above what any law could demand.
  • "Have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires" (v24). The tense looks back to something already done, when believers came to Christ by faith and were joined to his death (2:20; Rom 6:6). Yet Paul says they did it. Crucifixion was a slow death, but a certain one. Believers keep it there by refusing to feed it (Rom 8:13; Col 3:5).
  • "Let us also keep in step with the Spirit" (v25). The verb changes from peripateō in v16 to stoicheō. The lexicon gives it as walking in a row or line, especially soldiers marching in file into battle. "Keep in step" captures it well. The Spirit sets the pace and direction, and the believer falls in line.
  • "Let us not become conceited, provoking one another, envying one another" (v26). The chapter ends where the trouble began, with church relationships. Note the "us." Paul includes himself. Spiritual people can become proud of being spiritual. Some who think themselves strong provoke others, and some who feel weaker envy them. It leads straight into 6:1.
  • Commentators: Henry: "The best antidote against the poison of sin is to walk in the Spirit." He describes v17 as "a struggle between the old nature and the new nature," and calls grace "the fruit of the Spirit, because it wholly proceeds from the Spirit." JFB says the works are plural because they divide and conflict, while the fruit is singular because the graces form "one harmonious whole." It explains haireseis as "Self-constituted parties" and heresy as "a schism become inveterate," and it reads the provoking of v26 as the fault of the stronger, the envying as that of the weaker. Guzik groups the works of the flesh much as above, and argues that the present tense of v21 refers to settled, unrepentant practice, not occasional falls like David's or Peter's. On pharmakeia Guzik links it to drug use and potions tied to the occult. The lexicon agrees on the root but gives Paul's meaning here as sorcery. His readings of eritheia (first "work for hire") and stoicheō ("keep in step") match the lexicon. The Tyndale notes add that the fruit list answers the Galatian church's own troubles.

Pointing to Christ

Galatians 5 is really about where a person stands, and Christ is the ground. Freedom is his gift, and adding anything to him means losing him. The fruit of the Spirit describes his own character: love, patience, kindness and gentleness, shown to the full when he went to the cross. The flesh is dealt with by being crucified with him, not by a fresh set of rules. And the whole law is fulfilled in love because he first loved, the Son of God "who loved me and gave himself for me" (2:20).

Reflection

  • Standing Fast in Freedom (v.1) — Paul urges believers to hold firmly to the freedom Christ purchased rather than slide back into bondage. What pulls you toward trading that freedom for rules or performance?
  • Faith Expressed Through Love (v.6) — What matters isn't outward ritual but faith that actively works itself out in love. Is your faith visibly loving toward others, or mostly private and passive?
  • Freedom as Service, Not Indulgence (v.13) — Christian liberty isn't license to please yourself but an opportunity to serve others in love. Where could you trade self-focus for serving someone else this week?
  • Walking by the Spirit (v.16) — Paul's answer to the pull of the flesh is a daily, moment-by-moment walking in step with the Spirit. What does staying sensitive to the Spirit's leading actually look like in your everyday life?
  • Naming the Works of the Flesh (vv.19-21) — Paul's list includes not just obvious sins but also things like strife, jealousy, and quarreling. Which items on this list are you most prone to excuse in yourself?
  • Fruit That Grows, Not Works You Manufacture (vv.22-23) — The fruit of the Spirit describes character that grows naturally from abiding in Christ rather than being forced. Which fruit is most evident in your life right now, and which is least developed?
