Summary

God recalls his tender fatherly love for Israel from its infancy, calling his son out of Egypt, yet the more he called them the more they wandered off to sacrifice to the Baals (vv. 1-2). He describes teaching Ephraim to walk, healing them, and leading them with cords of kindness and bands of love, though they never recognized his care (vv. 3-4). Because they refuse to return to him, they will instead return to bondage under Assyria, and the sword will devastate their cities as a result of their own schemes (vv. 5-7). Yet God's compassion overrules his anger: he cannot bring himself to give Ephraim up or destroy them like Admah and Zeboiim, since he is God and not man, the Holy One who will not come in wrath (vv. 8-9). The chapter ends with a promise that the people will one day follow the LORD as he roars like a lion, and his children will come trembling home from Egypt and Assyria to settle again in their houses (vv. 10-11).

Authorship & Background

Author: Hosea son of Beeri. Prophesied to the northern kingdom (Israel) approximately 755-715 BC. Contemporary with Isaiah and Micah. Key theme: God's unfailing love for unfaithful Israel, illustrated through Hosea's marriage to Gomer (a prostitute). Despite Israel's spiritual adultery, God says: "I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely" (14:4).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — The Father's Love for His Rebellious Child
Key Themes: God as Father, the pain of unrequited parental love, divine compassion overcoming divine wrath, "I am God and not man," the call from Egypt, future restoration
Historical Context: Chapter 11 is often considered the emotional climax of Hosea — shifting from husband and wife imagery to father and child. God recalls teaching infant Israel to walk, holding him by the arms, feeding him, leading with "cords of kindness." Yet the more God called, the more Israel ran away. This leads to God's most anguished self-conflict: "How can I give you up?" (v.8). The answer reveals God's very nature: "I am God, and not man" — His compassion is divine, not human. A human father might give up; God cannot. This chapter quotes the Exodus ("out of Egypt I called my son") — applied by Matthew to Jesus (Matthew 2:15).
God's Fatherly Love Recalled (vv.1-4): The metaphor changes here from husband to father, and the tone changes with it. When Israel was a child, then I loved him, and called my son out of Egypt. What follows is domestic and physical: I taught Ephraim to go, taking them by their arms, I drew them with cords of a man, I laid meat unto them. This is a parent teaching a toddler to walk and lifting the feeding trough so the animal can reach, and against it stands the refusal, they knew not that I healed them. Matthew 2:15 takes the phrase about Egypt and applies it to Jesus.
The Consequence: Exile Under Assyria (vv.5-7): He shall not return into the land of Egypt, but the Assyrian shall be his king, because they refused to return. The refusal and the exile are set side by side using the same verb, they would not turn back, so they will be taken away. The sword shall abide on his cities. My people are bent to backsliding, and they called on the idols rather than on the one who was healing them, though they called him to the most High none at all would exalt him.
God's Heart Torn: How Can I Give You Up (vv.8-9): How shall I give thee up, Ephraim? how shall I deliver thee, Israel? Judgment has been pronounced and the pronouncer is now arguing against it, and the cities named, Admah and Zeboim, were destroyed with Sodom and Gomorrah, so what is being refused is that precedent. Mine heart is turned within me, my repentings are kindled together. Then the reason, and it is not Israel's improvement: I will not execute the fierceness of mine anger, for I am God, and not man. The difference between divine and human love is offered as the explanation, a man at the end of his patience is at the end of it, and God is not.
Future Restoration: The Lion Roars (vv.10-11): They shall walk after the LORD, he shall roar like a lion. The lion appeared in chapter 5 as the predator none could escape, and here the same roar is a summons the scattered come toward, the children shall tremble from the west. They come as birds out of Egypt and as a dove out of Assyria, the two empires that had taken them, and the dove is the same bird that was silly and heartless in chapter 7, now flying home.
Ephraim's Current State: Lies (v.12): Ephraim compasseth me about with lies, and the house of Israel with deceit. After the most tender passage in the book, the chapter closes on the present tense and nothing has changed on the ground. Judah is mentioned in the same verse, and the Hebrew is difficult enough that translations split over whether it is being commended or accused. The restoration in verses 10 and 11 is real and it is not yet.

Setting: Hosea is preaching to the northern kingdom of Israel (Ephraim) in its last years before the Assyrian conquest of 722 BC. Chapters 4–10 have been one long indictment: idolatry, murdered kings, false treaties, empty sacrifice, and judgment coming like a whirlwind. Chapter 11 opens a new ESV section, "The LORD's Love for Israel," and changes the picture. In chapters 1–3 God was a betrayed husband. Here he is a father remembering his son's childhood. The chapter climbs from memory (vv1–4) through judgment (vv5–7) to one of the most moving passages in the Old Testament, where God seems to argue with himself (vv8–9), and then to a promise of homecoming (vv10–11). Verse 12 is a hinge. In Hebrew Bibles it is 12:1, and it introduces the charges of chapter 12.

The LORD's Love for Israel (vv1–12)

  • "When Israel was a child, I loved him" (v1). God's love came first, before Israel had done anything. It was not earned by a strong or impressive nation (Deut 7:7–8). "Child" (na'ar) is the word for a boy or a youth, and it takes the reader back to the exodus, when the nation was young.
  • "Out of Egypt I called my son" (v1). At the exodus God told Pharaoh, "Israel is my firstborn son… Let my son go" (Exod 4:22–23). In Hosea this is history, looking back. Matthew quotes this line when Joseph brings the infant Jesus back from Egypt (Matt 2:15). Matthew is not claiming Hosea was predicting a single event. He sees Jesus as the true Son who relives Israel's story: out of Egypt, through the waters, tested in the wilderness forty days, and faithful where Israel failed (Matt 3–4).
  • "The more they were called, the more they went away" (v2). The Hebrew reads "they called them," meaning God's messengers, the prophets, kept calling, and Israel kept walking off. The Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) reads "the more I called them," which the ESV's passive "were called" also allows. Either way, every call is answered with a step in the other direction: "they kept sacrificing to the Baals." The plural "Baals" means the many local forms of the Canaanite storm god worshipped at different shrines.
  • "It was I who taught Ephraim to walk" (v3). One of the tenderest pictures of God in Scripture: a parent bending over a toddler, holding the child's arms and helping it take its first steps. Moses used similar language: God carried Israel "as a man carries his son" (Deut 1:31). "But they did not know that I healed them": they never realised who was holding them up. The same blindness runs through the book: Israel "did not know that it was I who gave her the grain, the wine, and the oil" (2:8).
  • "I led them with cords of kindness, with the bands of love" (v4). "Cords of kindness" is literally "cords of a man" (chavle adam), human ropes, the gentle ties by which a person leads a child, as opposed to the ropes used to drive cattle. Some read it as "leading strings," used to keep a toddler from falling. God drew them; he did not drag them.
  • "As one who eases the yoke on their jaws, and I bent down to them and fed them" (v4). The picture seems to shift to a farmer caring for his ox. He lifts the yoke forward off its neck so it can eat, and bends down to set food in front of it. This recalls God feeding Israel with manna in the wilderness (Exod 16). Some interpreters keep the parent picture throughout and read it as lifting a baby to one's cheek. The Hebrew words for "yoke" and "infant" are very close, which is why versions differ.
  • "They shall not return to the land of Egypt, but Assyria shall be their king" (v5). This seems to contradict 9:3 ("Ephraim shall return to Egypt"). There are a few solutions. Some read it as a question ("Shall they not return to Egypt?"). Some attach the Hebrew word lo ("not") to the end of v4 as lo ("to him"). The simplest reading: they will not go back to the literal Egypt they keep asking for help (7:11; 12:1); Assyria will be their master instead, a new Egypt. "Because they have refused to return to me": Hosea plays on shuv, "return." They will not return to Egypt because they would not return to God.
  • "The sword shall rage against their cities… because of their own counsels" (v6). "Bars of their gates" may also be translated "their boasters" or "their oracle-priests." Their "counsels" are their schemes: alliances, plots and policies made without God (10:13).
  • "My people are bent on turning away from me" (v7). "Turning away" is meshuvah, from the same root shuv: apostasy, backsliding. Israel is "hung up on" turning away, fixed in it. "Though they call out to the Most High, he shall not raise them up at all": the Hebrew is hard, but the point is that their calls to God are hollow (compare 7:14; 8:2). JFB reads it as "none would exalt Him."
  • "How can I give you up, O Ephraim?" (v8). Suddenly God breaks into four anguished questions. By the law, a stubborn and rebellious son could be put to death (Deut 21:18–21), and Israel was that son. Justice says hand him over. But God cannot bring himself to do it.
  • "How can I make you like Admah? How can I treat you like Zeboiim?" (v8). These were the two lesser-known cities destroyed with Sodom and Gomorrah (Gen 14:2; Deut 29:23). Moses warned that covenant breaking would turn the land into the same smoking ruin. God has every right to do it.
  • "My heart recoils within me" (v8). The verb is haphak, "turn over, overthrow," the very word used for the overthrow of Sodom and the cities of the plain (Gen 19:25; Deut 29:23). Instead of overthrowing Ephraim, God's own heart is overthrown. The judgment falls inside God rather than on his people. "My compassion grows warm and tender": his compassions are stirred and kindled together. In Hebrew thought the heart is the seat of will and decision, not only feeling, so this is a resolve, not a passing mood.
  • "I will not execute my burning anger; I will not again destroy Ephraim" (v9). Three times God says "I will not." This does not cancel the exile. Assyria still came, as the chapter has said (vv5–6). It means God will not wipe them out completely, as he did Admah and Zeboiim. There will be a future.
  • "For I am God and not a man, the Holy One in your midst" (v9). This is the reason, and it is surprising. We might expect God's holiness to be the reason he must destroy. Instead his holiness is why he will not. A wronged human being lashes out, holds a grudge or gives up. God is not like that (compare Num 23:19; Isa 55:8–9). His holiness includes a love that cannot be exhausted. "I will not come in wrath" can also be read "I will not come into the city," but the ESV's reading fits the parallel lines.
  • "He will roar like a lion" (v10). Earlier God was a lion tearing his prey (5:14). Now the roar is a summons. A lion's roar calls its cubs, and "his children shall come trembling from the west." "Trembling" here is not terror but eager, fluttering haste. "The west" probably means the Mediterranean coasts and islands.
  • "Like birds from Egypt, and like doves from the land of Assyria" (v11). Ephraim had been "like a dove, silly and without sense," flitting between Egypt and Assyria for help (7:11). Now the same doves fly home from those same places. "I will return them to their homes": the final word of the section is restoration. Later prophets take up the same hope (Isa 11:11–12; 60:8, "Who are these that fly… like doves to their windows?").
  • "Ephraim has surrounded me with lies… but Judah still walks with God" (v12). This verse looks forward to chapter 12. Israel's worship is wrapped in deceit. The second half is uncertain in Hebrew. The ESV, following JFB and others, reads it as praise of Judah, which under kings like Uzziah, Jotham and later Hezekiah still kept the temple worship. Others translate it "Judah still wanders from God," which fits 12:2, where Judah is also charged. The ESV's "the Holy One" follows the marginal sense JFB prefers.
  • Commentators: All three see vv8–9 as the heart of the chapter. Henry: "God is slow to anger, and is loth to abandon a people to utter ruin, who have been called by his name," and "God knows how to pardon poor sinners." Guzik sees the cross behind v8: God did not give Israel up because one day he would give up his Son in their place. He sets God's forgiveness against ours, which keeps grudges and demands probation. On v4, Guzik reads the "cords" as drawing rather than driving, and JFB agrees: "Not such cords as oxen are led by." JFB also says God bore Israel "as a parent does an infant." On v1 JFB notes Matthew's use: "even from His infancy, partly spent in Egypt, God called Him His son." Henry on v10: "Holy trembling at the word of Christ will draw us to him, not drive us from him." On v12 JFB argues the verse belongs to chapter 11 as praise of Judah, while the Tyndale notes take it with chapter 12 as part of the charge of deceit.

Pointing to Christ

Hosea 11 is where the Old Testament comes closest to showing God's heart breaking. Matthew saw the first verse fulfilled in Jesus, the Son called out of Egypt, who lived Israel's story without Israel's failure. But the chapter points to the cross too. God asks, "How can I give you up?" and answers that he will not. Paul tells us how that could be just: God "did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all" (Rom 8:32). The overthrow that should have fallen on the rebellious son fell on the faithful Son instead, and the lion's roar now calls scattered children home.

Reflection

  • Unrecognized Care Along the Way (v.3) — God recalls teaching Israel to walk and healing them, yet says they didn't recognize it was Him doing it. Looking back over your life, can you identify moments of God's care that you attributed to something or someone else at the time?
  • Led by Kindness, Not Coercion (v.4) — God describes drawing Israel with cords of kindness and bands of love, stooping down to feed and care for them rather than forcing compliance. Does your picture of God match this gentle, drawing love, or do you tend to imagine Him as harsher than this passage describes?
  • God's Internal Anguish Over Us (v.8) — God's question "how can I give you up" reveals genuine inner conflict, His compassion wrestling visibly with the justice Israel's sin deserves. Does it change how you see your own failures to know they cause God real anguish rather than cold indifference?
  • Divine Love That Doesn't Run Out (v.9) — God explains His refusal to fully destroy Israel by pointing to His own nature: He is God, not a man whose patience eventually runs dry. When you've exhausted the patience of people around you, can you rest in a love that isn't limited the way human love is?
