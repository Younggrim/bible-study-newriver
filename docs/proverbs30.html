Summary

This chapter records the sayings of Agur son of Jakeh, opening with a humble confession that he lacks understanding of God's ways (vv. 1-4) and an affirmation that every word of God is trustworthy, not to be added to (vv. 5-6). Agur prays for honesty and modest provision — neither poverty nor riches — so he is not tempted to deny God or to steal (vv. 7-9). The rest of the chapter is built from numerical proverbs grouped in sets of "three, even four": generations marked by pride and cruelty to the poor (vv. 11-14); things never satisfied, like the grave and a barren womb (vv. 15-16); mysteries too wonderful to grasp, including the way of a man with a young woman, likened to an adulterous woman who denies wrongdoing (vv. 18-20); things the earth cannot bear, such as a well-fed fool or an unloved woman who marries (vv. 21-23); small creatures that are remarkably wise, like ants and spiders (vv. 24-28); and things stately in bearing, like a lion and a king (vv. 29-31). It closes warning that stirring up wrath produces strife, just as churning milk produces butter (vv. 32-33).

Authorship & Background

Author: Primarily Solomon (1:1, 10:1, 25:1), with sections by Agur (ch.30) and King Lemuel (ch.31). Compiled over several centuries. Proverbs is wisdom literature — practical instruction for godly living. Hebrew title: 'Mishlei' — 'Proverbs/Comparisons.'
Historical Context: Chapter 30 is unique in Proverbs — it is not attributed to Solomon but to Agur the son of Jakeh (v.1). Agur is otherwise unknown in Scripture; his identity remains a mystery. Some Jewish traditions identify him as Solomon under a pseudonym, but there is no strong evidence for this. The chapter's opening is a "massa" (oracle/burden) — the same term used for prophetic utterances (Isaiah 13:1; Nahum 1:1). Agur addresses his words to "Ithiel and Ucal" — possibly students or companions, though some translations treat these as Hebrew words meaning "God is with me" and "I am able." What makes this chapter extraordinary is its humility. Unlike Solomon's authoritative declarations, Agur begins with radical self-abasement: "Surely I am more brutish than any man" (v.2). This is not false modesty but genuine epistemological humility — the recognition that human wisdom is insufficient without divine revelation. The chapter moves from confession of ignorance (vv.1-4) to the sufficiency of God's Word (vv.5-6), to a prayer for moderation (vv.7-9), to a series of brilliant numerical proverbs that observe creation with wonder and moral seriousness.
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: an unknown author, Agur son of Jakeh, opening not with instruction but with a confession of ignorance, surely I am more brutish than any man, questions no answer in the collection has actually addressed, who hath gathered the wind in his fists, the only prayer in the entire book, give me neither poverty nor riches, feed me with food convenient for me, a run of numerical sayings admiring ants, conies, locusts and spiders for competence that outruns their size, and a closing image of pressure producing what it must, the churning of milk bringeth forth butter, so the forcing of wrath bringeth forth strife
Agur's Confession of Ignorance (vv.1-4): The chapter changes author and the new one opens by disqualifying himself, surely I am more brutish than any man, and have not the understanding of a man. That is an odd thing to find in a wisdom book, and the questions that follow explain it: who hath gathered the wind in his fists? who hath bound the waters in a garment? who hath established all the ends of the earth? what is his name, and what is his son's name, if thou canst tell? A collection of answers is interrupted by a man listing what nobody knows.
The Sufficiency and Purity of Scripture (vv.5-6): Two verses that follow the confession directly and answer it. Every word of God is pure, he is a shield unto them that put their trust in him. If the previous section says human understanding cannot reach, this one says something else has come down. The instruction attached is about restraint, add thou not unto his words, lest he reprove thee, and thou be found a liar.
The Prayer for Contentment (vv.7-9): The only prayer in Proverbs, and it asks for two things and then explains both. Remove far from me vanity and lies, and give me neither poverty nor riches, feed me with food convenient for me. The reasons are symmetrical and both are about God rather than comfort: lest I be full, and deny thee, and say, Who is the LORD? or lest I be poor, and steal, and take the name of my God in vain. A request to be kept in the middle, on the grounds that either end is a spiritual risk.
Warnings Against Wickedness (vv.10-14): A short run of sayings about accusation and arrogance, beginning with an instruction not to inform on a servant to his master, lest he curse thee, and thou be found guilty. Then a generation described in four parallel verses, and the charges escalate: cursing father and mother, pure in their own eyes, lofty of eyelid, and finally a generation whose teeth are as swords, to devour the poor from off the earth, and the needy from among men.
Things Never Satisfied (vv.15-16): The first of the numerical sayings, and the form is a riddle rather than a lesson. The horseleach hath two daughters, crying, Give, give. Then four things that say not, It is enough: the grave, the barren womb, the earth that is not filled with water, and the fire. The list is not moral. It is an observation about appetite that has no bottom, and the reader is left to apply it.
The Eye That Mocks a Father (v.17): One verse standing between two numerical sayings and belonging to neither. The eye that mocketh at his father, and despiseth to obey his mother, the ravens of the valley shall pick it out, and the young eagles shall eat it. The punishment is aimed at the organ that did the mocking, and the picture is of a body left unburied in open country, which for the ancient reader is the worst end available. It is the harshest verse in the chapter and it is about contempt at home.
Things Too Wonderful (vv.18-19): Four things which I know not: the way of an eagle in the air, the way of a serpent upon a rock, the way of a ship in the midst of the sea, and the way of a man with a maid. Each of the first three leaves no track, which is what holds the list together, and the fourth is placed last so that the reader supplies the connection. The saying admires rather than explains.
The Adulterous Woman (v.20): A single verse appended to the previous saying and reading as a sour comment on it. Such is the way of an adulterous woman, she eateth, and wipeth her mouth, and saith, I have done no wickedness. The image is deliberately domestic and casual, and what it describes is not pleasure but the absence of any sense that anything happened.
Things the Earth Cannot Bear (vv.21-23): Four for which the earth is disquieted, and all four are inversions of status rather than crimes: a servant when he reigneth, a fool when he is filled with meat, an odious woman when she is married, and an handmaid that is heir to her mistress. The saying is about social order under strain and it does not pretend to be kind. It records what the writer thought insupportable.
The Four Small but Wise Creatures (vv.24-28): Four things which are little upon the earth, but they are exceeding wise, and each is credited with a competence that outruns its size. The ants are a people not strong, yet they prepare their meat in the summer. The conies are but a feeble folk, yet make they their houses in the rocks. The locusts have no king, yet go they forth all of them by bands. And the spider taketh hold with her hands, and is in kings' palaces. Preparation, shelter, organization without leadership, and access to places above her station.
Things Stately in Going (vv.29-31): Three things which go well, yea, four are comely in going: a lion which is strongest among beasts and turneth not away for any, a greyhound, a he goat, and a king against whom there is no rising up. The saying is purely about bearing, and it is the only one in the chapter that admires something without qualifying it.
Final Warning Against Pride (vv.32-33): The chapter ends with an instruction that is physical, if thou hast thought evil, lay thine hand upon thy mouth. Then a proverb about process, and the three clauses run in parallel: surely the churning of milk bringeth forth butter, and the wringing of the nose bringeth forth blood, so the forcing of wrath bringeth forth strife. Each is a thing produced by pressure, and the third is offered as the same mechanism as the first two.

Setting: The Hezekiah collection ended with chapter 29, and chapter 30 starts a new one under a new name: "The words of Agur son of Jakeh." Agur and his father appear nowhere else in Scripture. Jewish tradition sometimes made "Agur" a nickname for Solomon, but most interpreters, including Guzik, reject that: the book itself presents these as another man's words. If "oracle" in v1 should be read as the place name "Massa," as some translations have it, Agur came from an Arab tribe descended from Ishmael (Gen 25:14), and the Tyndale notes add that Massa is known from Assyrian records. Like "the sayings of the wise" (22:17–24:22), his words were included because they are true and God-given, wherever they came from. The chapter is unlike the rest of Proverbs. It opens with a confession of ignorance and a string of questions about God, moves to a short prayer, and then gives a series of numbered sayings ("three things… four") drawn from close observation of people and animals. It reads like the notebook of a humble man who has looked hard at the world and found God at the edges of everything he cannot explain. The ESV has no headings here, so the headings below follow its groupings.

Agur's Confession and Five Questions (vv1–6)

  • "The words of Agur son of Jakeh. The oracle" (v1). "Oracle" (massa') is the word used for a prophet's message, a "burden" laid on him (Isa 13:1; Mal 1:1). JFB: it means any divine instruction, "not necessarily a prediction."
  • "I am weary, O God; I am weary, O God, and worn out" (v1). The KJV and other older translations read this line as names: "the man spake unto Ithiel, even unto Ithiel and Ucal," and JFB suggests they were Agur's pupils. The Hebrew letters can be divided either way. The ESV, like many modern translations, reads them as words: la'iti 'el, "I am weary, O God." This fits what follows: Agur has worn himself out trying to understand God by his own effort. Both readings are honest attempts at a difficult line.
  • "Surely I am too stupid to be a man… nor have I knowledge of the Holy One" (vv2–3). "Stupid" is the word for a brute beast, used by Asaph: "I was brutish and ignorant; I was like a beast toward you" (Ps 73:22), as the Tyndale notes point out. This is not false modesty. Agur has measured his mind against God and found it tiny. The Tyndale notes: the first step toward wisdom is admitting our ignorance. "The Holy One" is plural in Hebrew, a plural of majesty, as in 9:10: "the knowledge of the Holy One is insight."
  • "Who has ascended to heaven and come down?" (v4). Five questions follow, in the style God uses with Job (Job 38–39). Who has gone up to heaven to learn its secrets and come back to tell? Who holds the wind in his fists, wraps the oceans in his cloak like a bundle (Gen 1:6–7; Job 26:8), and has set the limits of the earth? The answer is: no human being. Only God.
  • "What is his name, and what is his son's name? Surely you know!" (v4). The last question presses further: if someone did these things, who is he? "His son" in Proverbs usually means a pupil or child, and Israel is called God's son (Exod 4:22). Agur may simply mean "who is he, and who are his people?" But Christians have long heard more. Jesus said, "No one has ascended into heaven except he who descended from heaven, the Son of Man" (John 3:13). Guzik, with Garrett, links v4 to that verse. JFB reads the questions as denying these powers to any creature and does not discuss the son's name. The line is a question, not a prophecy in so many words, but the New Testament answers it.
  • "Every word of God proves true; he is a shield to those who take refuge in him" (v5). "Proves true" is the word for refining metal: tested in the fire and found pure. This line is almost word for word from David's psalm (Ps 18:30; 2 Sam 22:31). Agur can't climb to heaven, but God has come down in his word, and that word can be trusted. The Tyndale notes: God's strength and wisdom encourage Agur, because God protects all who seek him.
  • "Do not add to his words, lest he rebuke you and you be found a liar" (v6). Moses gave the same warning (Deut 4:2; 12:32), and Scripture ends with it (Rev 22:18–19). Adding human ideas to God's word and calling them his is lying in God's name. JFB: the warning implies relying on God's teaching as sufficient.
  • Commentators: Guzik notes Clarke's view that Agur was a public teacher with Ithiel and Ucal as his students, and Bridges' advice to attend to the teaching rather than speculate about the teacher. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that philosophy had failed Agur and only God's revelation answered him. Henry: "The more enlightened people are, the more they lament their ignorance," and "it becomes us all to have low thoughts of ourselves."

Agur's Prayer (vv7–9)

  • "Two things I ask of you; deny them not to me before I die" (v7). This is the only prayer in the book of Proverbs. Agur asks for two things, and both are about keeping him faithful.
  • "Remove far from me falsehood and lying" (v8). First, truth. "Falsehood" is literally "emptiness," worthless things, and JFB takes it as "all sorts of sinful acts." Having just said God's word is true (v5), Agur asks to be kept from lies, his own and other people's. The Tyndale notes list the many places Proverbs condemns lying (6:16–19; 14:5).
  • "Give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with the food that is needful for me" (v8). Second, enough. "The food that is needful" is literally "my portion of bread," the allotted share. It is the prayer Jesus taught: "Give us this day our daily bread" (Matt 6:11). Israel ate manna one day at a time (Exod 16:4).
  • "Lest I be full and deny you and say, 'Who is the LORD?'" (v9). Agur knows his own heart. Plenty breeds forgetfulness. Moses warned Israel that when they had eaten and were full, they would forget God (Deut 8:11–14). Pharaoh's proud "Who is the LORD?" (Exod 5:2) is the voice of a full man. "Or lest I be poor and steal and profane the name of my God." Desperation tempts too. A believer caught stealing brings shame on God's name. The Tyndale notes: wealth and poverty are not sinful in themselves, but Agur wisely wants to avoid the dangers of both. Paul learned "the secret of facing plenty and hunger" (Phil 4:12), and "godliness with contentment is great gain" (1 Tim 6:6).
  • Commentators: Guzik, with Waltke, stresses that the motive of the prayer is God's honor, not comfort. Agur is afraid of what wealth or poverty might do to his faithfulness. Guzik passes on Meyer's point that this is also a prayer for daily spiritual bread. JFB: the prayer is to be kept from wickedness and from both extremes, which implies contentment.

Generations That Go Wrong (vv10–14)

  • "Do not slander a servant to his master, lest he curse you, and you be held guilty" (v10). A servant had little power to defend himself. Running him down to his master is cowardly, and if the servant is innocent, his cry to God against you will stick. JFB: he may turn and expose the accuser. Guzik passes on Kidner's point that an undeserved curse has no force (26:2), but a deserved one does.
  • "There are those who curse their fathers and do not bless their mothers" (v11). "There are those" is literally "a generation," and it begins each of the next four verses. Agur sketches four kinds of people, or perhaps a whole generation gone wrong. First, children who despise their parents, breaking the fifth commandment (Exod 20:12; 21:17). Henry: "In every age there are monsters of ingratitude who ill-treat their parents."
  • "Clean in their own eyes but are not washed of their filth" (v12). Second, the self-righteous. "Filth" is a strong word, used of human excrement in Isaiah 4:4 and 28:8. They think they are clean, but they are filthy and don't know it. Jesus called such people "whitewashed tombs" (Matt 23:27). Compare 20:9: "Who can say, 'I have made my heart pure'?" Henry: many "persuade themselves they are holy persons, whose hearts are full of sin."
  • "How lofty are their eyes, how high their eyelids lift!" (v13). Third, the proud, with their noses in the air. "Haughty eyes" head God's list of things he hates (6:17).
  • "Whose teeth are swords, whose fangs are knives, to devour the poor" (v14). Fourth, the predators. Their mouths are weapons, and they eat up the poor. The four form a downward line: contempt for parents, self-righteousness, pride, and finally open cruelty. Micah describes leaders who "eat the flesh of my people" (Mic 3:2–3). The Tyndale notes list what these verses reject: disrespect, self-deception, pride, cruel words and harm to the poor.

Never Enough (vv15–17)

  • "The leech has two daughters: Give and Give" (v15). The leech ('aluqah, found only here) is the bloodsucking worm common in the pools and streams of Israel. Its "two daughters" are its two suckers, one at each end, or simply its offspring, who have one word between them: "Give! Give!" Guzik follows Waltke's description of the horseleech's two sucking organs. The KJV's "horseleach" is the same creature. JFB passes on an older suggestion that it meant a mythical vampire, but the simple worm fits. It is the picture of greed: always taking, never full.
  • "Three things are never satisfied; four never say, 'Enough'" (vv15–16). This is the first of the numbered sayings. "Three… four" is a Hebrew poetic pattern meaning "here are several, and here is the one I'm building to" (compare 6:16; Amos 1:3). The Tyndale notes explain it as a way of listing poetic examples. The four: Sheol, the grave, which always has room for more (27:20). The barren womb, which aches for a child, like Sarah, Rachel ("Give me children, or I shall die!" Gen 30:1) and Hannah. The dry land, which soaks up every rain. Fire, which consumes all it is given. Death, longing, drought and fire are the natural pictures, and the leech is the human one. Henry: "Those are never rich that are always coveting."
  • "The eye that mocks a father and scorns to obey a mother will be picked out by the ravens of the valley and eaten by the vultures" (v17). This returns to the "generation" of v11. The eye is the window of the heart, and a sneering look at one's parents shows contempt inside. The punishment is grim: dying in disgrace, unburied, the body left for scavengers, the worst end imaginable in Israel (1 Sam 17:44; Jer 22:19). Absalom, who rebelled against his father, died hanging in a tree and was thrown into a pit (2 Sam 18:9–17). JFB: dying unnaturally or left unburied, or both. Henry: many who came to ruin began by despising their parents.

Things Too Wonderful (vv18–20)

  • "Three things are too wonderful for me; four I do not understand" (v18). "Too wonderful" is the word for marvels, things beyond explanation (Ps 139:6). Agur stands in awe.
  • The eagle, the serpent, the ship, and "the way of a man with a virgin" (v19). An eagle soars with no visible support, a snake glides across bare rock with no legs, a ship finds its path across the trackless sea. Each moves by a power that can't be seen and leaves no trail behind. The fourth is the climax: the mystery of a man and a young woman falling in love and coming together. "Virgin" is 'almah, a young woman of marriageable age, the same word as in Isaiah 7:14. The Tyndale notes: the writer is amazed at how one thing affects another. Guzik, with Ross and Van Leeuwen, takes the fourth as a celebration of God's design in romantic and married love.
  • "This is the way of an adulteress: she eats and wipes her mouth and says, 'I have done no wrong'" (v20). The verse is set against v19. Love's mystery is beautiful, but here is its counterfeit, and it leaves no trace either. She sins as casually as finishing a meal, wipes her mouth, and feels nothing. The tone has shifted from wonder to shock. The Tyndale notes say the writer is shocked by sex outside marriage. JFB: she "hides the evidences of her shame and professes innocence."
  • Commentators: Guzik lists what the four ways may share, following Waltke and Kidner: each is easy to see but hard to explain, and each leaves no trace. Henry adds that the adulteress's way of hiding wrong is as hard to trace as the rest, so her denials can't be trusted. JFB reads all four as "the concealment of all methods or traces of action," which links them directly to v20.

The Earth Trembles (vv21–23)

  • "Under three things the earth trembles; under four it cannot bear up" (v21). The humor here is deliberate: the whole earth shakes because some people can't handle their good fortune.
  • "A slave when he becomes king, and a fool when he is filled with food" (v22). A slave suddenly in power, untrained for it, may rule as harshly as he was ruled (19:10; 28:3). A fool with a full belly becomes even more unbearable. The Tyndale notes: in the ancient world, plenty of food was a sign of prosperity. These are not complaints about anyone rising in life. Joseph went from slave to ruler and ruled well. The problem is the character that comes with them.
  • "An unloved woman when she gets a husband, and a maidservant when she displaces her mistress" (v23). "Unloved" is literally "hated," a woman no one wanted. When at last she marries, she may lord it over others. A maid who replaces her mistress may gloat. Hagar, once pregnant by Abraham, "looked with contempt on her mistress" (Gen 16:4), the example JFB and Guzik both give. Peninnah, who had children, taunted the childless Hannah (1 Sam 1:6). The Tyndale notes: these four disrupt the natural order.

Small but Wise, Stately in Stride (vv24–31)

  • "Four things on earth are small, but they are exceedingly wise" (v24). After four people who can't handle rising, here are four creatures who make the most of being small. "Exceedingly wise" is literally "wise, made wise": they have wisdom built in.
  • "The ants… provide their food in the summer" (v25). Weak but forward-looking (6:6–8). Henry applies these to people who are small in the world but wise for their souls and the life to come.
  • "The rock badgers… make their homes in the cliffs" (v26). The rock badger or rock hyrax is a small, furry animal about the size of a rabbit, still common around En-gedi by the Dead Sea. It is defenseless, but it lives among the rocks where nothing can get at it (Ps 104:18). Its wisdom is knowing where to hide. "The LORD is my rock and my fortress" (Ps 18:2).
  • "The locusts have no king, yet all of them march in rank" (v27). No leader, yet a locust swarm moves like an army (Joel 2:7–8). Guzik, with Waltke, sees teamwork here: strength in order and unity.
  • "The lizard you can take in your hands, yet it is in kings' palaces" (v28). The Hebrew word is uncertain. The KJV read "spider," and JFB follows it. Most modern scholars, as Guzik notes from Waltke and Kidner, think it is the gecko, a small lizard that climbs walls and ceilings and gets into the finest houses. Easy to catch, yet at home where kings live.
  • "Three things are stately in their tread; four are stately in their stride" (vv29–31). The lion, which "does not turn back before any." Then a creature described in Hebrew as "girded at the loins" (zarzir motnayim), a phrase found only here. The KJV guessed "greyhound," the Greek translation and the ESV "strutting rooster." The he-goat leading the flock. And "a king whose army is with him," where the Hebrew is again uncertain. Each walks with confidence and dignity. JFB: these are similes "for whatever is majestic or comely." The point builds to the king: real authority carries itself with calm assurance, which leads into the warning of vv32–33.
  • Commentators: Guzik draws a lesson from each small creature: the ants' diligence (Bridges), the badgers' safe refuge (Trapp), the locusts' order (Waltke), and the gecko's quiet persistence. JFB says the creatures succeed by instinct and activity rather than strength.

Hand Over the Mouth (vv32–33)

  • "If you have been foolish, exalting yourself… put your hand on your mouth" (v32). After the stately king, a warning against pretending to be one. If you have puffed yourself up or been plotting, stop talking. Job did exactly this when God spoke: "I lay my hand on my mouth" (Job 40:4). Agur ends where he began, humbled before God (vv2–3).
  • "For pressing milk produces curds, pressing the nose produces blood, and pressing anger produces strife" (v33). The same Hebrew word, "pressing" (churning, wringing), is used three times. It is also a play on words: "nose" ('af) and "anger" ('appayim, literally "nostrils") come from the same root, because in Hebrew anger is pictured as hot breath through the nose. Churn milk and you get butter. Squeeze a nose and you get a nosebleed. Stir up anger and you get a fight. It is as predictable as cause and effect. JFB says strife follows from devising evil "as surely" as natural effects follow natural causes. Henry: "Let nothing be said or done with violence, but every thing with softness and calmness."

Pointing to Christ

Agur asked, "Who has ascended to heaven and come down?… What is his name, and what is his son's name?" (v4). He knew the answer was beyond him. The New Testament gives it: "No one has ascended into heaven except he who descended from heaven, the Son of Man" (John 3:13). The one who gathers the wind in his fists stood in a boat and stilled it (Mark 4:39–41). The Father's name is made known through the Son (John 17:6), and his name is Jesus. Agur's prayer for daily bread became the Lord's Prayer, and the one who taught it is himself the "bread of life" (John 6:35). The word of God that "proves true" and is a shield (v5) is finally the Word made flesh (John 1:14). And the humility that made Agur put his hand on his mouth is the humility of the Son who "emptied himself" (Phil 2:7).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik highlights Agur's humility as the foundation of the entire chapter: "Agur is the anti-philosopher. Where Greek thinkers boasted of wisdom, Agur confesses ignorance. This is not anti-intellectualism — it is the recognition that human reason cannot reach God without revelation." On v.4, Guzik notes the extraordinary Christological implication: "Agur asks about God's Son. In the context of the Old Testament, this is remarkable. It implies that even before the Incarnation, there was an awareness that God had a Son involved in creation." On vv.8-9, Guzik emphasizes that Agur's prayer is radical because almost no one prays it: "We pray for more, never for 'enough.' Agur understood that both wealth and poverty carry spiritual dangers most people never consider."
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Confessing What You Don't Know (vv.2-3) — Agur opens not with authority but with a stark admission that he lacks understanding and has not attained the knowledge of the Holy One. Where does pretending to have it all figured out keep you from the deeper wisdom that starts with admitting your limits?
  • The Question Only God Can Answer (v.4) — Agur asks who has ascended to heaven, gathered the wind, and bound the waters, and what that Creator's Son is named. When you're confronted with the sheer scale of God's power, does it move you toward worship or just wonder?
  • Trusting a Word That Needs No Editing (vv.5-6) — Agur calls God's word flawless and warns against adding to it. Where are you tempted to adjust Scripture's teaching to fit your preferences rather than letting it shape you?
  • Praying for Enough, Not More (vv.8-9) — Agur asks God for neither poverty nor riches, aware that plenty breeds self-sufficiency and scarcity breeds desperation. Could you pray sincerely for "just enough," or does that feel like settling for less than you want?
  • Wisdom From Small Creatures (vv.24-28) — Ants, rock badgers, locusts, and lizards are small and unimpressive yet each survives through preparation, wise positioning, cooperation, or persistence. Which of these small-creature strategies is missing from how you're approaching your current challenges?
