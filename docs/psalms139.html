Summary

David marvels that the LORD has searched him and knows him completely — his sitting, rising, and every thought before he speaks it (vv. 1-6). He reflects on God's inescapable presence, noting there is nowhere he could flee, whether to the heavens, the depths, or the far side of the sea, where God's hand would still lead him (vv. 7-12). He praises God for forming him in his mother's womb, calling himself fearfully and wonderfully made, with every one of his days written in God's book before they began (vv. 13-18). After voicing his hatred for those who oppose God (vv. 19-22), he closes by inviting God to search his own heart and lead him in the way everlasting (vv. 23-24).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Hymn of Praise / Wisdom Psalm
Attributed Author: David (superscription: "To the chief Musician, A Psalm of David")
Key Themes: God's omniscience (all-knowing), omnipresence (everywhere), creation of human life in the womb, the inescapability of God, being fully known and fully loved, invitation for God to search the heart
Historical Context: Psalm 139 is one of the most theologically rich and personally intimate psalms in the Bible. It addresses three divine attributes — omniscience (vv.1-6), omnipresence (vv.7-12), and sovereign creative power (vv.13-18) — not as abstract theology but as intensely personal realities. David doesn't discuss God's knowledge generally; he says "Thou hast searched me." This psalm has been called the most complete expression of the individual's relationship to God in the Old Testament. The imprecatory section (vv.19-22) has puzzled some, but it flows logically: the God who knows David so intimately also sees the wicked, and David aligns himself with God's purposes. The psalm then returns full circle to the opening theme: "Search me" (v.23).
Thou Hast Searched Me, and Known Me (vv.1-6): O LORD, thou hast searched me, and known me. The psalm opens with the fact and spends six verses on how thorough it is, and the examples chosen are deliberately ordinary, my downsitting and mine uprising, my path and my lying down. Speech is covered before it happens, for there is not a word in my tongue, but, lo, O LORD, thou knowest it altogether. Thou hast beset me behind and before, where beset is a siege word and the being known is not yet obviously comfortable. Such knowledge is too wonderful for me; it is high, I cannot attain unto it.
Whither Shall I Go from Thy Spirit (vv.7-12): Whither shall I go from thy spirit? or whither shall I flee from thy presence? The question is asked as a man looking for an exit, and the psalm then closes every one. Height and depth first, if I ascend up into heaven, thou art there: if I make my bed in hell, behold, thou art there, where hell is Sheol, the place of the dead. Then distance, if I take the wings of the morning, and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea, and the answer changes tone, even there shall thy hand lead me, and thy right hand shall hold me, so what began as pursuit is described as escort. Last, darkness, and the psalm dismisses it in one line, the darkness and the light are both alike to thee.
Fearfully and Wonderfully Made (vv.13-18): For thou hast possessed my reins: thou hast covered me in my mother's womb. The psalm's answer to the inescapability of God is not resignation but the reason for it, which is that he was there first. I will praise thee; for I am fearfully and wonderfully made. My substance was not hid from thee, when I was made in secret, and curiously wrought in the lowest parts of the earth, where curiously wrought is the word used of embroidery. Then verse 16, thine eyes did see my substance, yet being unperfect; and in thy book all my members were written, which in continuance were fashioned, when as yet there was none of them. These verses are the ones most often cited in arguments about the unborn, and they do say that God attended to a person before that person existed to be seen. It is worth being clear what the psalm is doing with them: the subject under discussion is God's knowledge of this particular man, and the womb is produced as the earliest available proof of it rather than as a definition of when life begins. The verses will bear the weight put on them more steadily if that is kept in view. How precious also are thy thoughts unto me, O God, and they are counted like sand, and the section ends in the morning, when I awake, I am still with thee.
I Hate Them with Perfect Hatred (vv.19-22): Surely thou wilt slay the wicked, O God: depart from me therefore, ye bloody men. The turn is abrupt and many readers find it spoils the psalm, arriving four verses after the sand and the waking. The ground given is not personal injury, for they speak against thee wickedly, and thine enemies take thy name in vain, and the questions that follow ask whether hating God's enemies is not the right response, do not I hate them, O LORD, that hate thee. I hate them with perfect hatred, where perfect is the word for complete or whole, so the claim is of an undivided attitude rather than an intense one. Whether that claim is one a reader should make is a real question, and the psalm's own next two verses suggest the writer was not certain either.
Search Me, O God, and Know My Heart (vv.23-24): Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts. This is the same verb the psalm opened with, and the difference is everything: verse 1 reported that the searching had happened, and verse 23 asks for it. Between them stand the four verses of hatred, which is very likely why the request is made. And see if there be any wicked way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting. Having just described his enemies' way, the writer asks whether he is on it, and the psalm ends with that question open rather than answered.

Setting: Psalm 139 is one of the best-loved psalms, and one of the deepest. Its heading reads "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David," so it was meant for public worship, even though it is intensely personal. Matthew Henry notes that some Jewish teachers counted it the most excellent of David's psalms. It is the second of the two thanksgiving and trust psalms (138–139) that open the last collection of David's psalms, before the five laments of 140–144. No event is named. The last part of the psalm (vv19–24) suggests David was surrounded by enemies who spoke against God, and perhaps accused David himself. If so, the psalm is in part an appeal to the one Judge who truly knows him: search me and see. The psalm moves through four great truths about God, each made personal. God knows me completely (vv1–6). God is with me everywhere (vv7–12). God made me (vv13–16). God's thoughts toward me are beyond counting (vv17–18). Then it turns sharply against the wicked (vv19–22) and ends with a humble prayer to be searched and led (vv23–24). Theologians use big words for these truths: omniscience (God knows all things), omnipresence (God is everywhere) and omnipotence (God can do all things). The psalm never uses abstract terms. It speaks of sitting and rising, of darkness and dawn, of a mother's womb. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanzas as the ESV spaces them.

You Have Searched Me and Known Me (vv1–6)

  • "O LORD, you have searched me and known me!" (v1). "Searched" (chaqar) means to dig, to explore, to examine thoroughly, the word for searching out a matter or exploring a land (Judg 18:2; Prov 25:2). God has not merely glanced at David. He has investigated him. The psalm ends by asking God to do the same again: "Search me, O God" (v23). The whole psalm sits between those two "searches."
  • "You know when I sit down and when I rise up" (v2). Sitting and rising, together with "my path and my lying down" (v3), cover a whole day. The study notes compare Deuteronomy 6:7, where God's words are to be talked about "when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise." God's knowledge covers the ordinary hours of life, not only the dramatic moments. In the Hebrew of v2 the "you" is emphatic: you know.
  • "You discern my thoughts from afar" (v2). "Thoughts" (rea') is a rare word, meaning purposes or intentions. "From afar" can mean from heaven, or long before. God knows a person's thoughts before they are fully formed.
  • "Even before a word is on my tongue, behold, O LORD, you know it altogether" (v4). God knows the sentence before it is spoken. Jesus said the Father knows what his people need before they ask (Matt 6:8).
  • "You hem me in, behind and before, and lay your hand upon me" (v5). "Hem in" (tsur) is a word for besieging a city or enclosing something. It can sound threatening, like being trapped. The study notes point out that for the wicked, God's knowledge feels like a prison, while for the godly it is a comfort. Job felt it as pressure (Job 13:27). Here it is closer to a parent's arms around a child: God is on every side, and his hand rests on David.
  • "Such knowledge is too wonderful for me; it is high; I cannot attain it" (v6). "Wonderful" (pil'iyah) is from the same root as "wonders," something beyond human ability. The angel of the LORD told Samson's father his name was "wonderful," beyond understanding (Judg 13:18). David does not try to explain how God can know everything. He bows before it.
  • Commentators: Henry outlines vv1–6 as the doctrine stated: God knows all of a person, inward and outward, thoughts and words. Guzik stresses that this is not distant knowledge but personal, and he reads "hem me in" as protective care, like a loving parent's watchfulness rather than surveillance. He passes on Trapp's line contrasting people who search without knowing with God, who does both. JFB's commentary on this psalm gives only a brief overview, describing its "sublime doctrines of God's omnipresence and omniscience," and offers no verse notes.

Where Shall I Go from Your Spirit? (vv7–12)

  • "Where shall I go from your Spirit? Or where shall I flee from your presence?" (v7). The two lines say the same thing in two ways, as Hebrew poetry often does. God's "Spirit" (ruach) and his "presence" (literally his "face," panim) both mean God himself. The question is not a wish to escape. It is a way of saying: there is nowhere God is not. Jonah tried to flee "from the presence of the LORD" (Jonah 1:3) and found God in the storm and in the sea.
  • "If I ascend to heaven, you are there! If I make my bed in Sheol, you are there!" (v8). The psalm reaches the farthest points up and down. "Sheol" was the Old Testament name for the realm of the dead, pictured as deep below the earth. Elsewhere the psalmists say the dead are cut off from praising God (Ps 6:5; 88:10–12). This verse goes further: even there, God is present. Amos uses the same picture for judgment: "If they dig into Sheol, from there shall my hand take them" (Amos 9:2).
  • "If I take the wings of the morning and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea" (v9). Now the psalm reaches east and west. "The wings of the morning" is the dawn, spreading across the sky from the east. "The uttermost parts of the sea" is the far west, the Mediterranean, where the sun sets. The study notes read it this way. Up, down, east, west: the whole universe is covered.
  • "Even there your hand shall lead me, and your right hand shall hold me" (v10). The surprise is that God's presence at the ends of the earth is not a threat but guidance. His hand leads and holds.
  • "If I say, 'Surely the darkness shall cover me'" (v11). People have always thought darkness hides them (Job 24:13–17; Isa 29:15). But "even the darkness is not dark to you; the night is bright as the day" (v12). God does not need light to see. That is a warning to anyone hiding sin, and a comfort to anyone afraid in the dark.
  • Commentators: Henry says, "No veil can hide us from God's eye, no, not that of the thickest darkness," and "secret haunts of sin are as open before God as the most open and barefaced villanies." He argues that God's being everywhere is the proof that he knows everything. Guzik makes the same link and passes on Spurgeon's citation of a careful distinction: God is everywhere, but he is not everything. That guards the psalm from being read as if God and creation were the same thing. Guzik also notes Clarke's suggestion that the plural Hebrew word for "presence" (panim, "faces") might hint at the Trinity. The word is plural in form everywhere it appears, so that should not be pressed.

You Knitted Me Together (vv13–16)

  • "For you formed my inward parts" (v13). "For" explains why God knows David so well: he made him. "Inward parts" is literally "kidneys" (kilyot). In Hebrew thought the kidneys were the seat of the deepest feelings and the hidden self, much as English uses "heart" (Ps 16:7; Jer 11:20). "Formed" (qanah) can mean "create" or "acquire," and it is used of God as "Possessor" or "Maker" of heaven and earth (Gen 14:19). The study notes say the inward parts include a person's very being.
  • "You knitted me together in my mother's womb" (v13). The verb (sakak) means to weave or interlace. Job uses the same picture: "You clothed me with skin and flesh, and knit me together with bones and sinews" (Job 10:11). The psalm sees God at work in the hidden growth of a baby before birth.
  • "I praise you, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made" (v14). "Fearfully" (nora'ot) means in a way that inspires awe. "Wonderfully" (nifleti) is from the same root as "too wonderful" in v6. The human body is something to marvel at. The ancient Greek and Syriac translations read the line slightly differently, "for you are fearfully wonderful," praising God rather than the body. The ESV follows the Hebrew text. Either way, it ends in praise of the Maker: "Wonderful are your works; my soul knows it very well."
  • "When I was being made in secret, intricately woven in the depths of the earth" (v15). "Intricately woven" (ruqqamti) is the word for embroidery, the colored needlework used in the tabernacle curtains and the priests' garments (Exod 26:36; 28:39). The picture is of the body's veins, sinews and organs worked together like a many-colored tapestry. "The depths of the earth" is a poetic picture for the hidden darkness of the womb. Some connect it with Adam formed from the dust (Gen 2:7). Maclaren, as Guzik reports, takes it as a figure for the mysterious womb.
  • "Your eyes saw my unformed substance" (v16). "Unformed substance" translates golem, a word found only here in the Bible. It means something rolled up or shapeless: an embryo. God saw David when he was still only that.
  • "In your book were written, every one of them, the days that were formed for me, when as yet there was none of them" (v16). Scripture speaks of God keeping a book (Exod 32:32; Ps 56:8; 69:28; Dan 12:1). Here it holds the days of David's life, planned before any of them happened. The Hebrew of v16 is difficult, and "every one of them" could refer either to the days or to the body's parts. The ESV and most translations take it as the days. The verse does not mean life is a script with no real choices in it. It means no day takes God by surprise, and no life is unplanned in his eyes.
  • Life before birth. Christians have long drawn from these verses that God knows and values human life in the womb. Guzik makes that argument directly. The psalm itself is a hymn of praise, not a medical or legal text. Yet it plainly treats the unborn David as "me," the same person who now prays, known and cared for by God before birth (Jer 1:5; Luke 1:41–44).
  • Commentators: Henry draws the plain conclusion: "God made us, and therefore no doubt he knows us." Guzik passes on Poole's note that the verb in v15 means "embroidered," and VanGemeren's point that the "book" stands for God's knowledge and blessing of each day. The lexicon confirms both: raqam is used for embroidery, and golem means an embryo or shapeless mass. JFB gives no verse notes here.

How Precious to Me Are Your Thoughts (vv17–18)

  • "How precious to me are your thoughts, O God!" (v17). "Precious" (yaqar) means costly, weighty, of great value. God's thoughts are his purposes and his care. The study notes read it as God's thoughts about the psalmist: the investigation that began in v1 turns out to be loving care. What could have felt like being watched turns out to be being loved.
  • "How vast is the sum of them! If I would count them, they are more than the sand" (vv17–18). Sand was the standard picture for something beyond counting (Gen 22:17). God promised Abraham descendants like the sand. Here the countless things are God's thoughts toward one person.
  • "I awake, and I am still with you" (v18). The simplest reading is that David falls asleep counting God's thoughts and wakes up still in God's presence. Some take it further, as a hint of waking from death (Ps 17:15; Dan 12:2). Guzik notes that Kidner and Maclaren see that possibility. Either way, nothing, not even sleep, breaks the bond.
  • Commentators: Henry sees vv17–18 as the first inference from the doctrine: admiration and gratitude. Guzik passes on Maclaren's note that "precious" comes from a root meaning "weighty," and Spurgeon's description of God's thoughts as a treasure. The lexicon gives "precious, costly, weighty" for yaqar.

Oh That You Would Slay the Wicked (vv19–22)

  • "Oh that you would slay the wicked, O God!" (v19). The turn is sudden, and many readers find it jarring. Kidner, as Guzik reports, calls it disturbing yet realistic. But it follows from what came before. If God sees everything, he sees evil too. David's prayer is for God, the Judge who knows all, to deal with those who do violence.
  • "O men of blood, depart from me!" (v19). "Men of blood" means murderers, violent men. David wants no fellowship with them (Ps 26:9; 119:115).
  • "They speak against you with malicious intent; your enemies take your name in vain" (v20). The Hebrew of v20 is difficult. "Take your name in vain" is the ESV's reading of a phrase that recalls the third commandment (Exod 20:7). Their offense is not first against David but against God.
  • "Do I not hate those who hate you, O LORD?... I hate them with complete hatred" (vv21–22). This is covenant language. To "hate" here means to reject and refuse to side with, not to nurse personal spite. A loyal servant counts his king's enemies as his own. The study notes say that rejecting evil is a mark of wisdom (Ps 1:1; 5:4–6). The New Testament adds a deeper note. Jesus calls his followers to love their enemies (Matt 5:44), and Paul to "abhor what is evil" (Rom 12:9). Christians have often put it this way: hate the sin, and pray for the sinner. David's own life shows something like this. He spared Saul twice (1 Sam 24; 26).
  • Commentators: Henry says, "Sin is hated, and sinners are lamented, by all that fear God." He reads David's hatred as grounded in love for God, not personal malice. Guzik passes on Horne's point that a servant shares his master's enemies, while also distinguishing between hating sins and hating persons. He warns against a love for God that has no hatred of evil in it.

Search Me, O God (vv23–24)

  • "Search me, O God, and know my heart! Try me and know my thoughts!" (v23). The psalm returns to its opening words. Verse 1 said "you have searched me and known me." Now David asks for it. Right after declaring his hatred of the wicked, he turns the light on himself. The study notes observe that he does not presume he is righteous. "Try" (bachan) is the word for testing metal in a fire. "Thoughts" (sar'appay) means troubled or anxious thoughts (Ps 94:19). David asks God to see not only his sins but his worries.
  • "And see if there be any grievous way in me" (v24). "Grievous" translates 'otsev, which means pain or sorrow. The KJV reads "wicked way." The same consonants also spell the word for an idol ('otsev, Isa 48:5), and some think there is a play on words: a way of pain, or a way of idolatry. The ESV's "grievous way" covers a path that brings grief to God and to oneself.
  • "And lead me in the way everlasting!" (v24). "Way everlasting" (derek 'olam) could also be "the ancient way," as in Jeremiah 6:16, "ask for the ancient paths, where the good way is." Guzik notes Kidner's mention of this option. Most translations take it as "everlasting." The study notes connect it with the path of wisdom that leads to life (Ps 1:6; Prov 4:18). The hand that leads in v10 is asked to lead forever.
  • Commentators: Henry says that the sincere believer "could wish he had a window in his breast" so that his heart could be seen. He takes the prayer as an appeal for sincerity. Guzik calls the prayer a dangerous one to pray, because God may answer it. He passes on Boice's image of inviting surgery and Maclaren's warning that zeal against evil in others can hide secret sin in oneself, which is exactly why v23 follows v22.

Pointing to Christ

Psalm 139 marvels at a God who knows every thought, fills every place, and formed each person in the womb. In Jesus that God came close. The eternal Son was himself "knitted together" in a mother's womb (Luke 1:31; Gal 4:4). In his earthly life he showed that he knew people's hearts: "he himself knew what was in man" (John 2:25). He saw Nathanael under the fig tree (John 1:48) and knew Peter would deny him (Luke 22:34). He went where the psalm imagined going, down into death, and God was there with him and did not leave him in the grave (Acts 2:27). Because of him, the God who searches every heart is not only a judge to fear. He is a Father who knows his children by name. "If God is for us, who can be against us?" (Rom 8:31). The prayer "lead me in the way everlasting" finds its answer in the one who said, "I am the way, and the truth, and the life" (John 14:6), and who promised, "I am with you always, to the end of the age" (Matt 28:20).

Reflection

  • Fully Known (vv.1-6) — God knows every thought, word, and movement before David acts on them, a knowledge David calls too wonderful to grasp. Does being fully known by God feel more like exposure or like relief to you?
  • Nowhere to Hide, Nowhere You're Alone (vv.7-12) — Whether in the heights of heaven or the depths of the grave, David finds God already there, even darkness is not dark to Him. In your loneliest moments, do you actually believe God is present, or does it just feel like empty theology?
  • Fearfully and Wonderfully Made (vv.13-16) — God formed David in the womb and wrote out the days of his life before any of them existed. Do you extend that same value to your own body and story, or do you quietly believe you were an accident?
  • Thoughts Beyond Counting (vv.17-18) — David marvels that God's thoughts toward him outnumber the grains of sand, an idea he still can't fully grasp even when he wakes. What would change if you woke up believing God's thoughts toward you were that abundant and that kind?
  • Inviting God to Search Your Heart (vv.23-24) — The psalm ends with David asking God to search him, know his anxious thoughts, and lead him in the way everlasting. Are you willing to invite that same searching, or do you keep certain rooms of your heart closed off even from God?
