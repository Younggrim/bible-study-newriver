11Our holy and beautiful temple, where our fathers praised You, has been burned with fire, and all that was dear to us lies in ruins.

12After all this, O LORD, will You restrain Yourself? Will You keep silent and afflict us beyond measure?

Summary

The prayer begun in 63:15 continues with a passionate plea for God to tear open the heavens and come down in power as he has in the past (vv. 1-3), for no eye has ever seen a God like him who acts on behalf of those who wait for him (v. 4). The prayer turns to confession: the people are all unclean, their righteous deeds like filthy rags, and they wither like leaves carried off by their sin (vv. 5-7). Yet they appeal to God as Father and themselves as clay in the hands of their Potter (v. 8), pleading with him not to remember their iniquity forever or stay angry, but to look on the ruined cities that bear his name (vv. 9-12).

Authorship & Background

Author: Isaiah son of Amoz (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Communal Lament and Confession
Key Themes: Prayer for God to rend the heavens, the incomparable God who acts for those who wait, universal uncleanness, filthy rags righteousness, fading like autumn leaves, God as Father and Potter, appeal for mercy
Historical Context: This chapter continues the prayer begun in 63:15. It combines desperate longing for God's intervention with honest confession of Israel's unworthiness. The prayer moves from bold request ("rend the heavens!") to humble confession ("we are all as an unclean thing") to trusting appeal ("thou art our Father"). The language of v.6 ("filthy rags") became one of Protestantism's key texts on human inability to achieve self-righteousness. The chapter ends with a question hanging in the air: "Wilt thou refrain thyself for these things?" (v.12) — God's answer comes in chapters 65-66.
Oh That Thou Wouldest Rend the Heavens (vv.1-3): Oh that thou wouldest rend the heavens, that thou wouldest come down, that the mountains might flow down at thy presence. The prayer of chapter 63 continues and it asks for a tearing rather than an opening, and the model cited is Sinai, when thou didst terrible things which we looked not for, thou camest down, the mountains flowed down at thy presence. What is being requested is a repeat of the most frightening event in the national memory, on the grounds that it would at least be unmistakable.
Neither Hath the Eye Seen (v.4): One verse, and for since the beginning of the world men have not heard, nor perceived by the ear, neither hath the eye seen, O God, beside thee, what he hath prepared for him that waiteth for him. Paul quotes it in 1 Corinthians 2:9, and in both places the point is the same: the thing being waited for has no precedent in anybody's experience, so nobody's imagination is the measure of it. And the condition attached is the one this book returns to more than any other, for him that waiteth for him.
All Our Righteousnesses Are as Filthy Rags (vv.5-7): Behold, thou art wroth, for we have sinned, in those is continuance, and we shall be saved. Then the confession that the chapter is known for, and it does not exempt the good conduct, but we are all as an unclean thing, and all our righteousnesses are as filthy rags. The image after it is autumnal, and we all do fade as a leaf, and our iniquities, like the wind, have taken us away. And the last verse describes the state of religious practice, and there is none that calleth upon thy name, that stirreth up himself to take hold of thee, with the cause assigned to God in the same breath, for thou hast hid thy face from us, and hast consumed us, because of our iniquities.
We Are the Clay (v.8): One verse, and it is the turn of the whole prayer. But now, O LORD, thou art our father, we are the clay, and thou our potter, and we all are the work of thy hand. Where the potter image is used in 45:9 and Jeremiah 18 to silence an objection, here it is picked up by the objectors themselves and used as a plea: if we are your work, the state we are in is your concern. It is the strongest argument in the prayer and it is made in eight words.
Wilt Thou Hold Thy Peace (vv.9-12): Be not wroth very sore, O LORD, neither remember iniquity for ever, behold, see, we beseech thee, we are all thy people. Then the state of the buildings, which dates the prayer, thy holy cities are a wilderness, Zion is a wilderness, Jerusalem a desolation, our holy and our beautiful house, where our fathers praised thee, is burned up with fire. And the chapter, and the prayer, ends on a question that is left standing: wilt thou refrain thyself for these things, O LORD, wilt thou hold thy peace, and afflict us very sore. Chapter 65 is the answer, and it does not begin the way the prayer expected.

Map & Geography

  • Jerusalem (v.10): Capital of Judah; the holy city where the Temple stood.
  • Zion (v.10): The hill on which Jerusalem/the Temple stood; often used poetically for God's dwelling.

Reflection

  • Rend the Heavens and Come Down (v.1) — The prayer opens with desperate boldness, pleading for God to tear open the heavens and act visibly. Do you ever pray with that kind of urgency, or does your prayer life stay polite and restrained even in hard seasons?
  • What No Eye Has Seen (v.4) — No one has ever heard or seen a God like this, who acts on behalf of those who wait for Him. Whatever you're currently waiting on, do you trust God is preparing something beyond what you can currently imagine?
  • Our Righteousness Is Like Filthy Rags (v.6) — Even the people's righteous acts are described as filthy garments, and they fade like a leaf carried off by the wind. Do you rely on your best efforts to feel acceptable to God, or on a righteousness that isn't your own?
  • We Are the Clay, You Are the Potter (v.8) — Having confessed their unworthiness, the people appeal to the relationship itself: God is their Father and they are the work of His hands. When you have nothing left to offer God, do you still trust He won't abandon what He shaped?
  • Will You Restrain Yourself Forever? (v.12) — The prayer ends hanging in the air, asking whether God will keep silent and let His people suffer indefinitely. When your own prayers feel unanswered, can you trust the answer is still coming, even if it arrives further down the road than you'd like?
