Summary

The prayer begun in 63:15 continues with a passionate plea for God to tear open the heavens and come down in power as he has in the past (vv. 1-3), for no eye has ever seen a God like him who acts on behalf of those who wait for him (v. 4). The prayer turns to confession: the people are all unclean, their righteous deeds like filthy rags, and they wither like leaves carried off by their sin (vv. 5-7). Yet they appeal to God as Father and themselves as clay in the hands of their Potter (v. 8), pleading with him not to remember their iniquity forever or stay angry, but to look on the ruined cities that bear his name (vv. 9-12).

Authorship & Background

Author: Isaiah son of Amoz (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Communal Lament and Confession
Key Themes: Prayer for God to rend the heavens, the incomparable God who acts for those who wait, universal uncleanness, filthy rags righteousness, fading like autumn leaves, God as Father and Potter, appeal for mercy
Historical Context: This chapter continues the prayer begun in 63:15. It combines desperate longing for God's intervention with honest confession of Israel's unworthiness. The prayer moves from bold request ("rend the heavens!") to humble confession ("we are all as an unclean thing") to trusting appeal ("thou art our Father"). The language of v.6 ("filthy rags") became one of Protestantism's key texts on human inability to achieve self-righteousness. The chapter ends with a question hanging in the air: "Wilt thou refrain thyself for these things?" (v.12) — God's answer comes in chapters 65-66.
Oh That Thou Wouldest Rend the Heavens (vv.1-3): Oh that thou wouldest rend the heavens, that thou wouldest come down, that the mountains might flow down at thy presence. The prayer of chapter 63 continues and it asks for a tearing rather than an opening, and the model cited is Sinai, when thou didst terrible things which we looked not for, thou camest down, the mountains flowed down at thy presence. What is being requested is a repeat of the most frightening event in the national memory, on the grounds that it would at least be unmistakable.
Neither Hath the Eye Seen (v.4): One verse, and for since the beginning of the world men have not heard, nor perceived by the ear, neither hath the eye seen, O God, beside thee, what he hath prepared for him that waiteth for him. Paul quotes it in 1 Corinthians 2:9, and in both places the point is the same: the thing being waited for has no precedent in anybody's experience, so nobody's imagination is the measure of it. And the condition attached is the one this book returns to more than any other, for him that waiteth for him.
All Our Righteousnesses Are as Filthy Rags (vv.5-7): Behold, thou art wroth, for we have sinned, in those is continuance, and we shall be saved. Then the confession that the chapter is known for, and it does not exempt the good conduct, but we are all as an unclean thing, and all our righteousnesses are as filthy rags. The image after it is autumnal, and we all do fade as a leaf, and our iniquities, like the wind, have taken us away. And the last verse describes the state of religious practice, and there is none that calleth upon thy name, that stirreth up himself to take hold of thee, with the cause assigned to God in the same breath, for thou hast hid thy face from us, and hast consumed us, because of our iniquities.
We Are the Clay (v.8): One verse, and it is the turn of the whole prayer. But now, O LORD, thou art our father, we are the clay, and thou our potter, and we all are the work of thy hand. Where the potter image is used in 45:9 and Jeremiah 18 to silence an objection, here it is picked up by the objectors themselves and used as a plea: if we are your work, the state we are in is your concern. It is the strongest argument in the prayer and it is made in eight words.
Wilt Thou Hold Thy Peace (vv.9-12): Be not wroth very sore, O LORD, neither remember iniquity for ever, behold, see, we beseech thee, we are all thy people. Then the state of the buildings, which dates the prayer, thy holy cities are a wilderness, Zion is a wilderness, Jerusalem a desolation, our holy and our beautiful house, where our fathers praised thee, is burned up with fire. And the chapter, and the prayer, ends on a question that is left standing: wilt thou refrain thyself for these things, O LORD, wilt thou hold thy peace, and afflict us very sore. Chapter 65 is the answer, and it does not begin the way the prayer expected.

Deep Dive

Setting: This chapter is the second half of a single long prayer that began at 63:15 ("Look down from heaven and see"). The prophet speaks for God's people in the ruins after the Babylonian exile. Jerusalem's cities lie empty and Solomon's temple has been burned (v11), as happened in 586 BC (2 Kings 25:9). Chapter 63 recalled how God carried Israel through the Red Sea, asked where his zeal and compassion had gone, and appealed to him as "our Father" (63:16). Chapter 64 presses the same appeal: come down as you did at Sinai, look at our sin honestly, and remember we are yours. In Hebrew Bibles the first verse here is the last line of 63:19, so Hebrew verse numbers in this chapter run one behind the English. The ESV keeps the whole chapter under the heading that began at 63:15.

Prayer for Mercy (vv1–12)

  • "Oh that you would rend the heavens and come down" (v1). "Rend" means to tear, the word used for tearing clothes in grief (2 Sam 13:31). The prayer pictures the sky as a curtain between God and his people and asks God to rip it open and step through. Psalm 18:9 says of David's rescue, "He bowed the heavens and came down," and Psalm 144:5 prays, "Bow your heavens, O LORD, and come down!" At Jesus' baptism Mark uses this very picture: he "saw the heavens being torn open and the Spirit descending on him like a dove" (Mark 1:10). At his death "the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom" (Mark 15:38). Mark uses the same Greek verb (schizō) both times.
  • "That the mountains might quake at your presence" (v1). This recalls Sinai, where "the whole mountain trembled greatly" when the LORD descended in fire (Exod 19:18; Judg 5:4–5; Ps 68:8). Guzik draws the same link. The prayer wants that God back, the God who comes in person.
  • "As when fire kindles brushwood and the fire causes water to boil" (v2). Two everyday pictures of how fast and how completely fire works. Dry brush catches instantly. A pot over a flame cannot stay still. When God comes, nothing stays the same.
  • "To make your name known to your adversaries, and that the nations might tremble" (v2). The goal is not only Israel's relief but God's reputation. Nations who mocked Israel's God (52:5) would learn who he is.
  • "When you did awesome things that we did not look for, you came down" (v3). The prayer argues from the past: you have done this before, beyond anything we expected. The plagues, the Red Sea, Sinai. Moses asked, "has any god ever attempted to go and take a nation for himself… by signs, by wonders, and by war?" (Deut 4:34).
  • "From of old no one has heard or perceived by the ear, no eye has seen a God besides you, who acts for those who wait for him" (v4). Israel's God is unique. No other god acts for his worshippers. Idols have ears that do not hear and eyes that do not see (Ps 115:5–6); this God hears and sees and acts. "Wait" (chakah) means to wait expectantly, as in 30:18: "blessed are all those who wait for him."
  • Paul's use of v4. Paul quotes this verse freely in 1 Corinthians 2:9: "What no eye has seen, nor ear heard, nor the heart of man imagined, what God has prepared for those who love him." He seems to blend it with the language of 65:17 ("not… come into mind") and to change "wait for" to "love," which in Scripture go together. Paul applies it to the gospel of Christ crucified, a wisdom hidden from the world and revealed by the Spirit (1 Cor 2:7–10). The verse is often quoted of heaven, and that fits too, but Paul's point is that God has already revealed it to believers through his Spirit. JFB says the gospel can be grasped only "through the inward teaching of the Holy Ghost."
  • "You meet him who joyfully works righteousness, those who remember you in your ways" (v5). God comes to meet those who walk with him gladly. That is the problem, because Israel has not. "Behold, you were angry, and we sinned." The order sounds backward, but the Hebrew can be read "you were angry because we sinned," and the ESV text simply sets the two facts side by side: anger and sin together, a long, settled pattern.
  • "In our sins we have been a long time, and shall we be saved?" (v5). This line is one of the hardest in Isaiah to translate, and versions differ widely. The Hebrew reads roughly "in them [is] forever, and we shall be saved." The KJV took "them" as God's ways: "in those is continuance, and we shall be saved," a statement of faith. JFB follows a similar reading: the people "infer by faith" that they will be saved because God's covenant lasts. The ESV takes "them" as the sins and reads the end as a question. Both are possible. One is hope rising through confession; the other is honest despair asking whether there is any way out. Either way, the next verse is the confession.
  • "We have all become like one who is unclean" (v6). Under the law, uncleanness kept a person out of worship and community. A leper had to cry "Unclean, unclean" and live outside the camp (Lev 13:45–46). Isaiah himself cried, "I am a man of unclean lips" (6:5). Now the whole nation stands in that place.
  • "All our righteous deeds are like a polluted garment" (v6). The KJV's "filthy rags" is famous. The Hebrew is blunt: beged ʿiddim, literally "a garment of menstruation," a cloth stained with menstrual blood, which made a person ceremonially unclean under the law (Lev 15:19–24). The lexicon confirms that meaning, and JFB and Guzik both state it. The point is not that women's bodies are shameful. It is that even Israel's best acts, their "righteous deeds," were stained when offered by a people living in rebellion. Isaiah 1:11–15 already said God hated their sacrifices and festivals because their hands were full of blood. Paul says something similar in Philippians 3:8–9, counting his own righteousness as rubbish next to "the righteousness from God that depends on faith."
  • What it does and doesn't mean. This verse is sometimes used to say that nothing a Christian does can please God. That goes beyond it. The prayer is the confession of a people under judgment, describing how their religious performance looked while their hearts were far from God. Scripture elsewhere says that believers' obedience, done in faith, does please God (Heb 13:16; Col 1:10). What the verse does teach clearly is that no one can be saved by their own goodness (Rom 3:20; Titus 3:5).
  • "We all fade like a leaf, and our iniquities, like the wind, take us away" (v6). In autumn a dying leaf loses its color, loosens and is blown off. Sin works that way: first it drains life, then it carries people away, as wind blew Judah off into exile. Psalm 1:4 pictures the wicked as "chaff that the wind drives away." Henry: "we not only wither and lose our beauty, but we fall and drop off." Isaiah has used the fading leaf before (1:30; 40:6–8).
  • "There is no one who calls upon your name, who rouses himself to take hold of you" (v7). Prayer had dried up. "Rouses himself" means waking up and making the effort. JFB: rousing oneself "from spiritual drowsiness." "Take hold of you" is the language of clinging, as Jacob held on to God and would not let go until he was blessed (Gen 32:26), and as God invited, "let them lay hold of my protection" (27:5).
  • "For you have hidden your face from us, and have made us melt in the hand of our iniquities" (v7). "Hidden your face" means withdrawn favor, the opposite of the priestly blessing, "the LORD make his face to shine upon you" (Num 6:25). Sin made Israel's prayers go unheard (59:2). "Made us melt in the hand of our iniquities" pictures sin as a master holding them, and God handing them over to it. The Tyndale notes compare Romans 1:24: "God gave them up… to impurity." Sin's punishment is often more of the sin.
  • "But now, O LORD, you are our Father" (v8). "But now" is the turn of the whole prayer. After the honest confession of vv5–7, the people do not argue their worth. They appeal to relationship. They have said it once already (63:16), and they say it again. A father does not stop being a father when his children fail.
  • "We are the clay, and you are our potter; we are all the work of your hand" (v8). Potters worked wet clay on a spinning wheel, and a ruined pot could be pressed down and reshaped while still soft. Earlier Isaiah used this picture to rebuke people who argued with God (29:16; 45:9), and Paul uses it the same way (Rom 9:20–21). Here it is turned into a plea. Clay cannot fix itself, but the potter can remake it. Jeremiah watched a potter rework a spoiled vessel and heard God say, "Like the clay in the potter's hand, so are you in my hand" (Jer 18:1–6). JFB: unable to shape themselves rightly, they ask God to form them again.
  • "Be not so terribly angry, O LORD, and remember not iniquity forever" (v9). They do not ask God to pretend there was no sin, only not to hold it against them forever. God promised exactly that: "I, I am he who blots out your transgressions for my own sake, and I will not remember your sins" (43:25; Jer 31:34).
  • "Behold, please look, we are all your people" (v9). "Please look" picks up the opening plea, "Look down from heaven and see" (63:15). The argument is simple: whatever we have done, we are still yours.
  • "Your holy cities have become a wilderness; Zion… Jerusalem a desolation" (v10). The Tyndale notes explain that all of Judah's towns were considered holy because the land belonged to God. Isaiah's own day had seen the start of this when Assyria's Sennacherib captured "all the fortified cities of Judah" (36:1; 2 Kings 18:13). Babylon finished it. Lamentations is a whole book of this grief.
  • "Our holy and beautiful house, where our fathers praised you, has been burned by fire" (v11). "Our house" is what they call the temple, the place their fathers sang the psalms. Babylon's commander burned it in 586 BC (2 Kings 25:9; Ps 74:7). JFB adds that its destruction under Nebuchadnezzar "prefigured that under Titus," when the Romans burned the second temple in AD 70. "All our pleasant places have become ruins." JFB reads "pleasant places" as "objects of desire," homes and city and everything they loved.
  • "Will you restrain yourself at these things, O LORD? Will you keep silent, and afflict us so terribly?" (v12). The prayer ends with questions, not answers. "Restrain yourself" is the word used of Joseph holding back his tears before his brothers until he could not any longer (Gen 43:31; 45:1). The people cannot believe God will hold back his compassion forever. "Keep silent" answers 62:1 and 62:6–7, where God's watchmen were told to give him no rest. Chapter 65 is God's reply.
  • Commentators: Henry outlines the chapter as a model prayer for any time of affliction: "Let them pray, let them thus pray." The people ask God to appear "in some remarkable and surprising manner," plead his past works, then "confess themselves to be sinful and unworthy of God's favour" before throwing themselves on his mercy as Father. Guzik and JFB both explain "filthy rags" as a menstrual cloth, which the lexicon supports. JFB extends the uncleanness "even to their prayers and praises" and applies it to the best works of the unconverted, while Guzik quotes Spurgeon that sin stains even our best actions. On v5, JFB follows the hopeful reading, while the ESV, which Guzik notes, keeps the despairing question. Guzik closes by arguing that the prayer's unanswered question finds its answer in a righteous intercessor, and points to praying in Jesus' name (John 14:13–14).

Pointing to Christ

The prayer "Oh that you would rend the heavens and come down" was answered more fully than its first readers could imagine. God did come down: the heavens were torn open at Jesus' baptism, and the temple curtain was torn at his death, opening the way to God for unclean people. The people of v6, with their stained garments, are given "the robe of righteousness" (61:10) that Christ provides. And the plea "You are our Father… we are the clay" became the prayer Jesus taught: "Our Father in heaven." The potter took the ruined clay into his hands and made a new creation (2 Cor 5:17).

Reflection

  • Rend the Heavens and Come Down (v.1) — The prayer opens with desperate boldness, pleading for God to tear open the heavens and act visibly. Do you ever pray with that kind of urgency, or does your prayer life stay polite and restrained even in hard seasons?
  • What No Eye Has Seen (v.4) — No one has ever heard or seen a God like this, who acts on behalf of those who wait for Him. Whatever you're currently waiting on, do you trust God is preparing something beyond what you can currently imagine?
  • Our Righteousness Is Like Filthy Rags (v.6) — Even the people's righteous acts are described as filthy garments, and they fade like a leaf carried off by the wind. Do you rely on your best efforts to feel acceptable to God, or on a righteousness that isn't your own?
  • We Are the Clay, You Are the Potter (v.8) — Having confessed their unworthiness, the people appeal to the relationship itself: God is their Father and they are the work of His hands. When you have nothing left to offer God, do you still trust He won't abandon what He shaped?
  • Will You Restrain Yourself Forever? (v.12) — The prayer ends hanging in the air, asking whether God will keep silent and let His people suffer indefinitely. When your own prayers feel unanswered, can you trust the answer is still coming, even if it arrives further down the road than you'd like?
