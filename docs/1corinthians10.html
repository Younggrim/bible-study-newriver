1I do not want you to be unaware, brothers, that our forefathers were all under the cloud, and that they all passed through the sea.

2They were all baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea.

3They all ate the same spiritual food

4and drank the same spiritual drink; for they drank from the spiritual rock that accompanied them, and that rock was Christ.

5Nevertheless, God was not pleased with most of them, for they were struck down in the wilderness.

6These things took place as examples to keep us from craving evil things as they did.

7Do not be idolaters, as some of them were. As it is written: “The people sat down to eat and drink and got up to indulge in revelry.”

8We should not commit sexual immorality, as some of them did, and in one day twenty-three thousand of them died.

9We should not test Christ, as some of them did, and were killed by snakes.

10And do not complain, as some of them did, and were killed by the destroying angel.

11Now these things happened to them as examples and were written down as warnings for us, on whom the fulfillment of the ages has come.

12So the one who thinks he is standing firm should be careful not to fall.

13No temptation has seized you except what is common to man. And God is faithful; He will not let you be tempted beyond what you can bear. But when you are tempted, He will also provide an escape, so that you can stand up under it.

14Therefore, my beloved, flee from idolatry.

15I speak to reasonable people; judge for yourselves what I say.

16Is not the cup of blessing that we bless a participation in the blood of Christ? And is not the bread that we break a participation in the body of Christ?

17Because there is one loaf, we who are many are one body; for we all partake of the one loaf.

18Consider the people of Israel: Are not those who eat the sacrifices fellow partakers in the altar?

19Am I suggesting, then, that food sacrificed to an idol is anything, or that an idol is anything?

20No, but the sacrifices of pagans are offered to demons, not to God. And I do not want you to be participants with demons.

21You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons too; you cannot partake in the table of the Lord and the table of demons too.

22Are we trying to provoke the Lord to jealousy? Are we stronger than He?

23“Everything is permissible,” but not everything is beneficial. “Everything is permissible,” but not everything is edifying.

24No one should seek his own good, but the good of others.

25Eat anything sold in the meat market without raising questions of conscience,

26for, “The earth is the Lord’s, and the fullness thereof.”

27If an unbeliever invites you to a meal and you want to go, eat anything set before you without raising questions of conscience.

28But if someone tells you, “This food was offered to idols,” then do not eat it, for the sake of the one who told you and for the sake of conscience—

29the other one’s conscience, I mean, not your own. For why should my freedom be determined by someone else’s conscience?

30If I partake in the meal with thankfulness, why am I denounced because of that for which I give thanks?

31So whether you eat or drink or whatever you do, do it all to the glory of God.

32Do not become a stumbling block, whether to Jews or Greeks or the church of God—

33as I also try to please everyone in all I do. For I am not seeking my own good, but the good of many, that they may be saved.

Summary

Paul points to Israel's wilderness generation as a warning: though they were all under the cloud, passed through the sea, and shared spiritual food and drink from the Rock, which was Christ, most of them were still struck down by God for their disobedience (vv. 1-5). He lists their failures — idolatry, sexual immorality, testing Christ, and grumbling — as examples written down to warn believers living in the last days, concluding that anyone who thinks they are standing firm should be careful not to fall (vv. 6-12). He assures them that God is faithful and will not let them be tempted beyond what they can bear, always providing a way out (v. 13). From there Paul warns against idolatry directly, arguing that sharing the Lord's cup and bread at communion creates a real spiritual participation that cannot be mixed with participation in pagan sacrifices (vv. 14-22). He closes with practical guidance on food bought in the market or served by an unbeliever: it may generally be eaten without question, but should be avoided if it would offend someone's conscience, since the overriding goal in eating, drinking, or anything else is to do all things for God's glory and for the good of others (vv. 23-33).

Authorship & Background

Author: Paul (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 10 continues the idol-meat discussion (chapters 8-10) with a warning from Israel's history (vv.1-13), a command to flee idolatry (vv.14-22), and practical guidelines for eating (vv.23-33). Paul uses Israel's wilderness failures as a warning: privilege doesn't guarantee perseverance.
Classification: Epistle — Pauline
Key Themes: Israel's wilderness privileges shown insufficient to prevent its wilderness failures, let him that thinketh he standeth take heed lest he fall, a way of escape promised through temptation rather than around it, one loaf making many eaters one body used to argue what sharing an idol's table also does, and liberty limited by another man's conscience rather than by the eater's own
Israel's Warning (vv.1-12): All Israel experienced God's blessings — the cloud, the sea, the manna, the water from the rock. Yet "with many of them God was not well pleased" (v.5). They fell through lust, idolatry, fornication, testing God, and grumbling. Paul's point: spiritual privilege doesn't make you immune to falling. "Let him that thinketh he standeth take heed lest he fall" (v.12).
The Faithfulness of God (v.13): One of the most quoted and comforting verses in the Bible. God is faithful — He won't allow temptation beyond your ability, and He always provides "a way to escape." Not escape from temptation but escape through it — the ability to bear it.
Flee from Idolatry (vv.14-22): The argument returns to idol meat with a command rather than a concession, flee from idolatry. Then the reasoning, and it runs through the Lord's supper: the cup of blessing is the communion of the blood of Christ, and the bread the communion of his body, and because there is one bread, we being many are one body. If eating together makes a body, then eating at an idol's table does the same thing there. Paul is careful about what he is not saying, the idol is nothing, but he identifies what is actually present, the things which the Gentiles sacrifice, they sacrifice to devils. The closing question is about loyalty rather than about digestion, ye cannot be partakers of the Lord's table, and of the table of devils.
All to the Glory of God (vv.23-33): The practical guidance is set out with two liberties and one limit. All things are lawful for me, but all things edify not, and the limit is somebody else, let no man seek his own, but every man another's wealth. Meat in the market may be eaten without asking questions, for the earth is the Lord's, and the fulness thereof. Meat at a private dinner may be eaten until somebody points out where it came from, and then it may not, and the reason given is not the eater's conscience but the other man's. Then the sentence that sets the standard for the whole discussion, whether therefore ye eat, or drink, or whatsoever ye do, do all to the glory of God.

Deep Dive

Setting: Paul writes from Ephesus in about AD 53–55 (16:8). This chapter finishes the long answer, begun in 8:1, to the Corinthians' question about food offered to idols. The "strong" in Corinth knew an idol was nothing and felt free to eat temple meat, even at banquets inside pagan temples. In chapter 8 Paul told them to give way for the sake of weaker believers, and in chapter 9 he showed how he gives up his own rights for the gospel. Chapter 9 ended with Paul disciplining himself so that he would not be "disqualified" (9:27). Chapter 10 opens with "For," and shows what disqualification looks like: a whole generation of God's people who started well and fell in the desert.

Warning Against Idolatry (vv1–22)

  • "Our fathers" (v1). Paul is writing to a mostly Gentile church, yet he calls Israel's wilderness generation "our fathers." Those who belong to Christ have been grafted into Israel's family story (Rom 11:17; Gal 3:29), so the exodus is their history too.
  • "All… all… all… all… all" (vv1–4). "All" comes five times: all under the cloud, all through the sea, all baptized, all ate, all drank. Then v5 turns on one word: "Nevertheless, with most of them God was not pleased." Every one of them shared the privileges. Almost none of them reached the land. Of the adults who left Egypt, only Joshua and Caleb entered Canaan (Num 14:29–30).
  • "Under the cloud… through the sea" (v1). The pillar of cloud was God's own presence leading them (Exod 13:21–22), and the Red Sea crossing was their rescue from Egypt (Exod 14).
  • "Baptized into Moses" (v2). Paul is drawing a parallel with Christian baptism. Christians are baptized "into Christ" (Rom 6:3) and joined to him. Israel, passing between walls of water under the cloud, was joined to Moses as their God-given leader. They stayed dry, so the point is not the method of baptism but what it means: belonging to a deliverer.
  • "Spiritual food… spiritual drink" (vv3–4). The manna (Exod 16) and the water from the rock (Exod 17:1–7; Num 20:2–13). "Spiritual" means given by God's Spirit and pointing beyond itself. The parallel is plain: baptism (v2) and the Lord's Supper (vv3–4, picked up again in vv16–17). Apparently some Corinthians thought their baptism and their place at the Lord's Table made them safe whatever else they did. Paul says Israel had the same kind of signs, and it did not save those who turned to idols.
  • "The spiritual Rock that followed them, and the Rock was Christ" (v4). Water came from a rock near the start of the wilderness years (Exod 17) and again near the end (Num 20). Later Jewish tradition joined the two into a single rock or well that rolled along beside Israel for forty years (it appears in the Tosefta and in Pseudo-Philo's Biblical Antiquities). Scholars disagree on whether Paul is nodding to that legend. Either way, he is not interested in a moving boulder. He calls it a "spiritual Rock" and says the one who really went with Israel and gave them drink was Christ himself. Behind this stands the Song of Moses, where God is "the Rock" again and again (Deut 32:4, 15, 18, 30–31). Paul quietly puts Christ in that place, which assumes his presence with Israel long before Bethlehem (compare John 1:1–3; 8:58; Jude 5).
  • "They were overthrown in the wilderness" (v5). The verb pictures bodies strewn across the ground, an echo of Numbers 14:16, where the bodies of the faithless generation fall in the wilderness.
  • "These things took place as examples for us" (v6). "Examples" is typoi, from typos, the mark left by a blow or the stamp pressed by a die. It can mean a pattern to copy or, as here, a warning pattern. The same word stands behind our word "type" in Bible study: earlier events that foreshadow later ones. Israel's story is stamped with a shape that the church must not repeat.
  • Five sins (vv6–10). Paul lists five, each from the wilderness story, and each one a live danger in Corinth.
  • "That we might not desire evil as they did" (v6). This points to Numbers 11:4–34, where the people craved the meat and fish of Egypt and died at a place called "Graves of Craving." Craving is the root of the rest.
  • "Do not be idolaters" (v7). Paul quotes Exodus 32:6, the golden calf: "The people sat down to eat and drink and rose up to play." The setting matters. Israel ate a sacrificial meal in front of an idol, which is exactly what the strong in Corinth were doing in temple dining rooms. "Play" is the Hebrew tsachaq, which can mean laugh, jest or play, and in Genesis 26:8 is used of Isaac caressing Rebekah. In Exodus 32 it describes revelry, and the context (dancing, v19; "the people had broken loose," v25) suggests it got out of hand.
  • "Twenty-three thousand fell in a single day" (v8). This points to Numbers 25, where Israel joined the feasts of the Moabite god Baal of Peor and slept with Moabite women. Numbers 25:9 gives 24,000 dead. Interpreters have suggested several honest explanations: Paul counts those who died "in a single day" while Numbers counts the whole plague, or both figures are round numbers, or Paul's memory blended Numbers 25:9 with the 23,000 of Numbers 26:62 nearby. Sexual immorality and temple feasts went together in Corinth too (6:12–20).
  • "We must not put Christ to the test" (v9). This points to Numbers 21:4–9, when the people complained against God and Moses and were bitten by fiery serpents. To "test" God is to push him, demanding he prove himself on our terms (Deut 6:16; Matt 4:7). Some manuscripts read "the Lord" instead of "Christ," as the ESV footnote shows. Most scholars think "Christ" is original and that copyists changed it because it seemed strange in an Old Testament setting. It fits v4: the Christ who went with Israel is the one they provoked.
  • "Nor grumble… destroyed by the Destroyer" (v10). Grumbling runs all through Numbers, and Paul probably means the rebellion of Korah and the plague that followed (Num 16:41–49). "The Destroyer" recalls the destroying angel of the Passover night (Exod 12:23). Grumbling sounds minor next to idolatry and immorality, but in Numbers it is unbelief out loud: the claim that God has brought his people into the desert to kill them.
  • "Written down for our instruction, on whom the end of the ages has come" (v11). Paul repeats v6, this time adding that God had these stories written with later believers in view (Rom 15:4). "The end of the ages" is plural, literally "the ends of the ages." The ages before Christ have reached their goal in him, and the church lives in the last chapter of the story.
  • "Let anyone who thinks that he stands take heed lest he fall" (v12). This is aimed at the strong, who were sure their knowledge made them safe (8:1–2). Paul does not tell them to doubt God. He tells them to doubt themselves. Christians have long differed on how such warnings relate to the security of true believers. Some read them as real warnings that a person can fall away. Others read them as one of the means God uses to keep his people from falling. All agree the warning is meant to be taken seriously, not explained away.
  • "No temptation has overtaken you that is not common to man" (v13). The word peirasmos means both testing and temptation: the same pressure can be a trial God allows for our good or a pull toward sin. "Common to man" is literally "human." Corinth's pressures, as Paul goes on to show, were real, but they were not unique, and they were not superhuman.
  • "God is faithful" (v13). The hinge of the verse is not human strength but God's character, the same words as 1:9. He sets limits on the trial and, with it, provides "the way of escape." The Greek ekbasis means simply "a way out," and in classical Greek could mean a landing place or a way off a ship. Notice the purpose clause: the way out exists "that you may be able to endure it." God does not promise a life without testing. He promises it will never be more than he will carry his people through. And here the way of escape is close at hand: v14 says what it is.
  • "Therefore, my beloved, flee from idolatry" (v14). "Therefore" ties the promise to the command. The way out of temptation to idolatry is not to stand near it testing one's strength, but to run. Paul says the same about sexual sin (6:18). The Greek has "the idolatry," most likely meaning the temple meals in question.
  • "I speak as to sensible people; judge for yourselves" (v15). The Corinthians prided themselves on being wise (4:10). Paul takes them at their word and asks them to think the matter through.
  • "The cup of blessing that we bless" (v16). "Cup of blessing" was the Jewish name for a cup of wine over which a blessing was spoken at a meal, and especially the third cup of the Passover meal. That is very likely the cup Jesus took "after supper" (Luke 22:20; 1 Cor 11:25). Notice that Paul mentions the cup first here, probably because he is about to contrast it with the drink offerings poured to idols.
  • "A participation in the blood of Christ… in the body of Christ" (v16). "Participation" is koinōnia, the word for fellowship, sharing, partnership. It is the word in 1:9: "called into the fellowship of his Son." At the Lord's Table believers really share in Christ and all that his death won for them. Christians have long differed over how Christ is present at the Supper. Roman Catholics, Lutherans, Reformed, Baptists and others all read this verse, and all of them read it as more than a bare memory. Paul's own point here is simpler: eating at a table joins people to the one whose table it is.
  • "Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body" (v17). One loaf was broken and shared. Paul draws the line from union with Christ to union with each other. This comes back strongly in chapter 11 (divisions at the Supper) and chapter 12 (one body, many members).
  • "Are not those who eat the sacrifices participants in the altar?" (v18). In Israel, worshippers ate part of their peace offerings in God's presence (Lev 7:15; Deut 12:17–18). Eating the meal was taking part in the worship.
  • "What pagans sacrifice they offer to demons and not to God" (vv19–20). Paul is careful. He has not changed his mind from 8:4: an idol is nothing, a lump of carved stone. But real spiritual powers stand behind idolatry and feed on the worship people pour into it. His wording echoes Deuteronomy 32:17: "They sacrificed to demons that were no gods." So the strong were right about the idol and wrong about the meal.
  • "You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons" (v21). Temple banquets in Corinth began with a drink offering to the god, and dinner invitations on papyrus from this period ask guests to dine "at the couch of the lord Sarapis," a meal hosted by the god himself. Paul's phrase "the table of the Lord" (compare Mal 1:7, 12) answers that directly. A believer cannot sit at both tables.
  • "Shall we provoke the Lord to jealousy? Are we stronger than he?" (v22). Deuteronomy 32 again: "They have made me jealous with what is no god" (Deut 32:21). God's jealousy is not petty envy but the burning love of a husband who will not share his bride. The second question is pointed irony for people who called themselves "the strong."
  • Commentators: All three read vv1–5 as a warning against trusting outward privileges. Guzik notes that "sacrament" comes from the Latin for a soldier's oath of loyalty, and argues the Corinthians had wrongly treated baptism and communion as guarantees. Matthew Henry says the passage warns the Corinthians "against security, by the example of the Jews." JFB counts five privileges in vv1–4 set against five sins in vv6–10, renders "followed" as "accompanied," and plainly rejects the rabbis' traveling rock: the one who went with Israel was Christ. Guzik leaves the rock tradition more open. On v13 Guzik passes on William Barclay's picture of ekbasis as a mountain pass, an escape route out of a trap. The lexicon does not support that particular sense. It gives "a way out," with nautical uses like landing place, so the picture is a fair sermon illustration but not the word's meaning. Guzik and JFB agree on the main thing: the escape is a way to bear up under the trial, not a way out of all trials. On vv16–22, Henry says that partaking of the one loaf means "we coalesce into one body, become members of him and one another," and that "God cannot endure a rival in matter of worship." JFB stresses that the Lord's Supper is "a feast on a table," not a sacrifice on an altar, a point Guzik also presses against the Roman Catholic view of the Mass.

Do All to the Glory of God (vv23–33)

  • "'All things are lawful,' but not all things are helpful" (v23). Paul quotes the Corinthian slogan again, as in 6:12, and again answers it. Two tests now: is it "helpful" (does it do good?) and does it "build up" (does it strengthen others?). The second picks up 8:1: "knowledge puffs up, but love builds up."
  • "Let no one seek his own good, but the good of his neighbor" (v24). This is the principle under the whole section. "Good" is not in the Greek, which says simply "his own… the other's." Paul's own life is the model (9:19; 10:33).
  • "Eat whatever is sold in the meat market" (v25). The makellon, from the Latin macellum, was the city's food market. Excavators in Corinth found an inscription naming such a market near the forum. Some of the meat came from temple sacrifices, but once sold it was simply food. Paul tells believers to buy and eat without anxious questions. This is a remarkable freedom for a former Pharisee who had once kept a strict kosher diet.
  • "The earth is the Lord's, and the fullness thereof" (v26). Psalm 24:1. Everything edible belongs to God, not to Apollo or Aphrodite. Jewish teachers used this very verse to teach that one should bless God before eating, since all food is his gift. Paul uses it to free believers to eat with thanks (v30).
  • "If one of the unbelievers invites you to dinner" (v27). A private meal in a home is different from a banquet in a temple dining room (8:10). Paul says go if you wish, and eat what is served without asking.
  • "But if someone says to you, 'This has been offered in sacrifice'" (v28). Here Paul uses a different word. Christians and Jews said eidōlothyton, "sacrificed to an idol," the word in v19 and chapter 8. Here the speaker says hierothyton, "sacrificed in a temple," the polite word a pagan would use. So the one speaking up may well be the unbelieving host or a guest, pointing it out out of courtesy or curiosity. (Later Greek manuscripts, followed by the King James, use the Christian word here too and repeat Psalm 24:1, which the earlier manuscripts lack.) Once the meal has been labeled religious, eating it would say the wrong thing to the one who told you, so do not eat.
  • "I do not mean your conscience, but his" (v29). The believer's own conscience is clear. The concern is what the other person will think the act means.
  • "Why should my liberty be determined by someone else's conscience?" (vv29–30). These two questions are notoriously hard. Some read them as an objection Paul is quoting and then answering. Others read them as Paul's own voice continuing from v27, with vv28–29a as an aside: the believer's freedom itself is not cancelled by another's conscience, even if its use gives way. Either way the practical rule is clear, and v30 adds that thanksgiving makes ordinary food holy (1 Tim 4:4–5).
  • "Whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do all to the glory of God" (v31). Paul widens the lens from meat to all of life. Eating and drinking are the most ordinary acts there are. If they can be done for God's glory, anything can. This is not a rule that settles every case, but a question that tests every case: does this make God look as good as he is?
  • "Give no offense to Jews or to Greeks or to the church of God" (v32). Three groups: Jews, Gentiles, and the church, which belongs to neither and includes both. "Offense" here means a stumbling block, something that trips another person up in their walk with God, not merely something that annoys them. Paul was happy to annoy legalists (Gal 2:3–5).
  • "Not seeking my own advantage, but that of many, that they may be saved" (v33). Paul ties it all back to chapter 9: "I have become all things to all people, that by all means I might save some" (9:22). The next verse, 11:1, belongs with this paragraph: "Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ."
  • Commentators: All three see the section as liberty limited by love. Henry: "Christians should take care not to use their liberty to the hurt of others, nor their own reproach." On v31 he calls this "the fundamental principle of practical godliness" and adds that "the great end of all practical religion must direct us where particular and express rules are wanting." Guzik links the quotation of Psalm 24:1 to the Jewish blessing before meals and stresses that Paul's apparent back-and-forth is one principle applied to two situations. JFB notes the oldest manuscripts drop the repeated Psalm quote in v28 and gives both readings of v29. On v32 JFB draws a careful line that Guzik also draws: give way freely in things that do not matter, but never on the truth of the gospel. The Tyndale notes, like JFB, mention that some translations put vv28–29a in parentheses.

Pointing to Christ

Paul finds Christ in the wilderness, giving water from the rock to a thirsty people who did not deserve it. The same Christ offers living water now (John 7:37–38), and the rock had to be struck for it to flow (Exod 17:6). At his table believers share in his blood and body, given once for all, and are made one loaf together. That is why there can be no second table. And when Paul asks believers to give up their rights and seek "the good of many, that they may be saved," he is describing Jesus, who "did not please himself" (Rom 15:3) and who faced every temptation common to man without sin, so that God's faithfulness would never be in doubt for those who flee to him.

Reflection

  • Spiritual Privilege Isn't a Guarantee (vv.1-5) — Israel experienced the cloud, the sea, the manna, and the water, yet God was not pleased with most of them and they still fell. What are you leaning on besides ongoing faith and obedience to keep you spiritually secure?
  • The Danger of Overconfidence (v.12) — Paul warns that anyone who thinks they're standing firm should be careful not to fall. Where are you most confident in your own spiritual strength — and could that be exactly where you're most vulnerable?
  • God's Faithfulness in Temptation (v.13) — Paul promises that God won't allow temptation beyond what you can bear and will always provide a way through it. When you're in the middle of temptation, are you actively looking for that way of escape, or assuming there isn't one?
  • Fleeing Rather Than Flirting With Idolatry (v.14) — Paul's command is to flee from idolatry entirely, not negotiate with it. What competes with God for your worship, trust, or affection right now — money, approval, comfort, control?
  • You Cannot Serve Two Tables (v.21) — Paul says believers cannot share in the Lord's table and a table devoted to what opposes Him. Where might you be trying to hold onto both Christ and something incompatible with Him?
  • Every Action as an Act of Worship (v.31) — Paul's summary command is that whatever believers do — even eating and drinking — should be done for God's glory. How would your ordinary daily choices change if you actually evaluated them by this standard?
