1On the twenty-first day of the seventh month, the word of the LORD came through Haggai the prophet, saying:

2“Speak to Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel, governor of Judah, and to Joshua son of Jehozadak, the high priest, and also to the remnant of the people. Ask them,

3‘Who is left among you who saw this house in its former glory? How does it look to you now? Does it not appear to you like nothing in comparison?’

4But now be strong, O Zerubbabel, declares the LORD. Be strong, O Joshua son of Jehozadak, the high priest. And be strong, all you people of the land, declares the LORD. Work! For I am with you, declares the LORD of Hosts.

5This is the promise I made to you when you came out of Egypt. And My Spirit remains among you; do not be afraid.”

6For this is what the LORD of Hosts says: “Once more, in a little while, I will shake the heavens and the earth, the sea and the dry land.

7I will shake all the nations, and they will come with all their treasures, and I will fill this house with glory, says the LORD of Hosts.

8The silver is Mine, and the gold is Mine, declares the LORD of Hosts.

9The latter glory of this house will be greater than the former, says the LORD of Hosts. And in this place I will provide peace, declares the LORD of Hosts.”

10On the twenty-fourth day of the ninth month, in the second year of Darius, the word of the LORD came to Haggai the prophet, saying,

11“This is what the LORD of Hosts says: ‘Ask the priests for a ruling.

12If a man carries consecrated meat in the fold of his garment, and it touches bread, stew, wine, oil, or any other food, does that item become holy?’” “No,” replied the priests.

13So Haggai asked, “If one who is defiled by contact with a corpse touches any of these, does it become defiled?” “Yes, it becomes defiled,” the priests answered.

14Then Haggai replied, “So it is with this people and this nation before Me, declares the LORD, and so it is with every work of their hands; whatever they offer there is defiled.

15Now consider carefully from this day forward: Before one stone was placed on another in the temple of the LORD,

16from that time, when one came expecting a heap of twenty ephahs of grain, there were but ten. When one came to the winepress to draw out fifty baths, there were but twenty.

17I struck you—all the work of your hands—with blight, mildew, and hail, but you did not turn to Me, declares the LORD.

18Consider carefully from this day forward—from the twenty-fourth day of the ninth month, the day the foundation of the LORD’s temple was laid—consider carefully:

19Is there still seed in the barn? The vine, the fig, the pomegranate, and the olive tree have not yet yielded fruit. But from this day on, I will bless you.”

20For the second time that day, the twenty-fourth day of the month, the word of the LORD came to Haggai, saying,

21“Tell Zerubbabel governor of Judah that I am about to shake the heavens and the earth:

22I will overturn royal thrones and destroy the power of the kingdoms of the nations. I will overturn chariots and their riders; horses and their riders will fall, each by the sword of his brother.

23On that day, declares the LORD of Hosts, I will take you, My servant, Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel, declares the LORD, and I will make you like My signet ring, for I have chosen you, declares the LORD of Hosts.”

Summary

The LORD asks the discouraged builders who remembers the temple's former glory and now sees it as nothing by comparison, then commands them to be strong and to work, for He is with them just as He promised at the exodus (vv. 1-5). He promises to shake the heavens and earth and the nations, so the treasures of all nations will come and fill this house with glory greater than the former, and He will give peace in that place (vv. 6-9). Haggai then teaches through the priests that just as uncleanness is contagious while holiness is not, the people's defiled offerings have kept God's blessing from their crops, but from this day forward He promises to bless them (vv. 10-19). Finally, God promises to overthrow the kingdoms of the nations, and to make Zerubbabel like a signet ring, chosen and treasured by the LORD, a note of messianic hope pointing toward the line of David (vv. 20-23).

Authorship & Background

Author: Haggai the prophet. Prophesied in 520 BC (precisely dated). Key theme: rebuilding the Temple — the returned exiles have neglected God's house while building their own. "Is it time for you... to dwell in your ceiled houses, and this house lie waste?" (1:4). "The glory of this latter house shall be greater than of the former" (2:9).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — The Greater Glory and the Chosen Signet
Key Themes: Encouragement to the discouraged, the latter glory surpassing the former, the shaking of nations, holiness and defilement, the turning point of blessing, Zerubbabel as God's signet ring (Messianic type)
Historical Context: Chapter 2 contains three distinct prophetic messages, each precisely dated. The first (vv.1-9) comes on the twenty-first day of the seventh month — during the Feast of Tabernacles, when the people would remember wilderness wandering and God's faithfulness. Some elderly people who remembered Solomon's Temple (destroyed 586 BC) were weeping at the comparison (Ezra 3:12). The new Temple seemed pitiful. God's response to their discouragement is not rebuke but encouragement: "Be strong... I am with you... My Spirit remains among you." Then comes the stunning prophecy: the latter glory of this house will exceed the former. How? "The desire of all nations shall come" — traditionally understood as a Messianic reference. Jesus Christ Himself would walk in this Temple (the expanded Herodian Temple built on this same foundation). The third oracle (vv.20-23) elevates Zerubbabel to extraordinary status: God's "signet ring." A signet ring carried the king's authority. God had removed His signet (Jehoiachin, Jer 22:24) but now restores it through Zerubbabel — an ancestor of Christ in both Matthew 1:12 and Luke 3:27.
Encouragement to the Discouraged Builders (vv.1-5): Seven weeks into the work, morale has fallen. The question in v.3 is put to the oldest men present: who remembers the first house, and is this not nothing by comparison? Some of them had seen Solomon's temple before 586 BC, and the honest answer was yes. The encouragement does not dispute the comparison. It changes the subject to presence — be strong, work, for I am with you — and grounds it in the covenant made at the exodus.
The Shaking of Nations and the Greater Glory (vv.6-9): “Yet once, it is a little while, and I will shake” extends the horizon past the building site to heaven, earth, sea and all nations. The promise that “the desire of all nations shall come” has been read both as the Messiah and as the wealth of the nations arriving, and the following verse about silver and gold supports the second while not excluding the first. The claim in v.9 is the point: the latter glory of this house will exceed the former. Measured in stonework that was false, which is why the measure has changed.
The Lesson of Holiness and Defilement (vv.10-14): Haggai puts two questions to the priests, and their answers are the argument. Does consecrated meat make what it touches holy? No. Does a corpse make what it touches unclean? Yes. Holiness does not transmit by contact; defilement does. The application in v.14 is unsparing — the people's offerings had been unclean because the people were — and it explains why sixteen years of sacrifice at a neglected site had not helped them.
The Turning Point: From This Day I Will Bless (vv.15-19): The date is given again, the twenty-fourth day of the ninth month, and it is treated as a hinge. Haggai reviews the barren years, then points forward: from this day, consider. Verse 19 concedes that nothing is yet visible — the seed is still in the ground, the vine and the fig have not yet borne — and promises blessing anyway. The promise is dated before the evidence, which is the shape of most of what the book asks of them.
Zerubbabel: God's Chosen Signet Ring (vv.20-23): The final oracle comes the same day and addresses one man. Kingdoms will be overthrown and Zerubbabel will be made “as a signet” — a signet ring, the instrument that carried a king's authority and was never lent casually. The weight of it is that his grandfather Jehoiachin had been told the opposite in Jeremiah 22:24, that God would pluck him off as a signet from His hand. The reversal is deliberate, and Zerubbabel appears in both genealogies of Jesus.

Setting: In Haggai 1 the prophet rebuked the people of Jerusalem for leaving God's house in ruins while they finished their own, and within twenty-three days they started work on the temple (1:14–15). It was late summer of 520 BC, the second year of the Persian king Darius I. Haggai 2 holds the book's last three messages, all dated, over the next three months. The first (vv1–9) came after about a month of work, when discouragement set in because the new temple looked so small. The second (vv10–19) came two months later, in December, and dealt with the people's uncleanness and God's promise of blessing. The third (vv20–23), on the same day, was a private word to Zerubbabel, the governor descended from David. The prophet Zechariah began preaching between the first and second of these messages (Zech 1:1). Together the two prophets kept the people building until the temple was finished in 515 BC (Ezra 6:14–15).

The Coming Glory of the Temple (vv1–9)

  • "In the seventh month, on the twenty-first day" (v1). That is October 17, 520 BC. The date is significant. It was the seventh day of the Feast of Booths, the great autumn harvest festival (Lev 23:34–36, 39–43). Solomon had dedicated the first temple at this same feast (1 Kgs 8:2). The people would have been reminded of that glorious day, and the harvest they were celebrating was poor (1:6, 10–11). Both facts made the new building look all the more pitiful.
  • "Who is left among you who saw this house in its former glory?" (v3). Solomon's temple had been destroyed sixty-six years earlier, in 586 BC. A few people in their seventies or older could remember it. They had wept at the foundation-laying sixteen years before (Ezra 3:12). Solomon's temple was covered in gold inside (1 Kgs 6:20–22) and took seven years to build with vast resources. The new one was built by a poor community with what they could find.
  • "Is it not as nothing in your eyes?" (v3). God does not pretend. He says aloud what they were thinking. Honest comparison with the past was crushing them, and God names it before he answers it.
  • "Yet now be strong… be strong… be strong" (v4). Three times, to the governor, the high priest and "all you people of the land." The words echo God's charge to Joshua as he entered the land: "Be strong and courageous" (Josh 1:6–9), and David's charge to Solomon before the first temple: "Be strong and courageous and do it" (1 Chr 28:20). The new builders stand in that line.
  • "Work, for I am with you" (v4). The command is plain: work. The reason is God's presence, the same promise as in 1:13. Strength for the work does not come from the size of the building or the budget but from who is with them.
  • "According to the covenant that I made with you when you came out of Egypt" (v5). The Hebrew is literally "the word that I cut with you," an unusual phrase built on the idiom "to cut a covenant." God ties this moment to the exodus. The return from Babylon was a second exodus, and the same God who brought Israel out of Egypt and dwelt among them in the tabernacle was with them now (Exod 29:45–46).
  • "My Spirit remains in your midst. Fear not" (v5). Isaiah recalled the exodus as the time when God "put in the midst of them his Holy Spirit" (Isa 63:11). The Spirit who guided Israel then was "standing" among them still. Zechariah will make the same point a few months later: the temple will be finished "not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit" (Zech 4:6).
  • "Yet once more, in a little while, I will shake the heavens and the earth" (v6). At Sinai the earth shook when God came down (Exod 19:18; Judg 5:4–5; Ps 68:8). God promises to shake the whole created order again, and the nations with it (v7). Hebrews 12:26–27 quotes this verse and reads "yet once more" as pointing to the final shaking at the end of the age, when everything that can be shaken will be removed "in order that the things that cannot be shaken may remain." "In a little while" speaks the language of prophecy, where God's timetable is certain even when it is long by human reckoning.
  • "The treasures of all nations shall come in" (v7). The Hebrew is chemdat kol-haggoyim, literally "the desire of all nations." Chemdah means "desire" or, more often, "a desirable thing," and the lexicon gives it here as "the desirable, precious things of all nations." The verb "shall come" is plural, which fits a collective noun like "treasures" rather than one person. So most modern translations, including the ESV, read "treasures": the nations will bring their wealth to God's house, as Isaiah foresaw (Isa 60:5–11). The KJV's "the desire of all nations shall come" was read by many Christians, including Henry and JFB, as a title of the Messiah, the one all nations long for. That reading rests on the Latin Vulgate and is less likely from the Hebrew, but the two are not far apart. The nations' greatest treasure, the one who would fill the house with glory, was Christ.
  • "I will fill this house with glory" (v7). The cloud of God's glory had filled the tabernacle (Exod 40:34–35) and Solomon's temple (1 Kgs 8:10–11). Ezekiel had seen it leave the temple before its destruction (Ezek 10:18–19; 11:23) and return in a vision (Ezek 43:1–5). The Old Testament never records that glory cloud filling the second temple.
  • "The silver is mine, and the gold is mine" (v8). The builders had no gold for the walls. God reminds them that all the world's wealth belongs to him. Within a few years Darius ordered the costs paid from the royal treasury (Ezra 6:8), and later Persian kings sent silver and gold (Ezra 7:15–22).
  • "The latter glory of this house shall be greater than the former" (v9). "This house" is one house, the temple on the same site, first built by Solomon and now rebuilt. The second temple never matched Solomon's in gold, and it lacked the ark. Herod the Great greatly enlarged and beautified it from about 20 BC. But its greater glory came from something else: the Son of God walked in its courts. Jesus was presented there as a baby (Luke 2:22–32), taught there (John 7:14) and cleansed it (Mark 11:15–17). "Something greater than the temple is here" (Matt 12:6).
  • "In this place I will give peace" (v9). "Peace" is shalom: wholeness, well-being and right relationship with God, not only the absence of war. Jerusalem's name was popularly linked with shalom (Ps 122:6–8; Heb 7:2).
  • Commentators: Henry warns that "it is sometimes the fault of old people to discourage the services of the present age," and says those who work for God "ought to exert themselves with vigour." He reads v7 as Christ, whom the nations will call "all their salvation and all their desire," and finds the latter glory in "the presence of the Messiah in it, the Son of God." JFB also defends the messianic reading, and calls earthly kingdoms "but the scaffolding for God's spiritual temple." Guzik holds the messianic reading but grants that the word can mean "treasures." All three agree that the greater glory was Christ's presence. Guzik adds that under the old covenant the Spirit was among God's people, and under the new he dwells in them.

Blessings for a Defiled People (vv10–19)

  • "On the twenty-fourth day of the ninth month" (v10). That is December 18, 520 BC, three months to the day after work began (1:15). The early rains had come and the winter grain was being sown.
  • "Ask the priests about the law" (v11). The priests were responsible for teaching the Law and deciding questions of clean and unclean (Lev 10:10–11; Deut 33:10; Mal 2:7). Haggai asks them for a ruling, the Hebrew word torah, "instruction."
  • "If someone carries holy meat in the fold of his garment" (v12). Meat from a sacrifice was "holy," set apart for God. Leviticus 6:27 says that whatever touches it becomes holy. But here the holy meat touches a garment, and the garment touches food. Does holiness pass on to the food at second hand? The priests answer correctly: "No."
  • "If someone who is unclean by contact with a dead body touches any of these, does it become unclean?" (v13). Contact with a corpse made a person unclean for seven days, and whatever the unclean person touched became unclean too (Num 19:11, 22). The priests answer: "It does become unclean."
  • "So is it with this people… and so with every work of their hands" (v14). The lesson is that uncleanness spreads more easily than holiness. Being in the holy land and offering sacrifices on the altar (Ezra 3:3) did not make the people holy. Their neglect of God's house had defiled everything they did, including their offerings. "There" probably means the altar. Some take "this people" and "this nation" to be the Samaritans of Ezra 4, but most interpreters, including the Tyndale notes, apply it to the Jews themselves, which fits the rest of the passage.
  • "Consider from this day onward" (v15). Haggai uses his favorite call again, "set your heart" (1:5, 7). The Hebrew can mean looking back from this day ("upward," to the past) or forward. Verses 15–17 look back. Verses 18–19 look forward.
  • "A heap of twenty measures, there were but ten" (v16). A farmer went to his grain pile expecting twenty measures and found ten. He went to the wine vat expecting fifty and drew twenty. Every expectation fell short.
  • "I struck you… with blight and with mildew and with hail, yet you did not turn to me" (v17). This line almost quotes Amos 4:9, where the same refrain, "yet you did not return to me," follows each of God's disciplines on the northern kingdom. Blight and mildew are the covenant curses of Deuteronomy 28:22. Hardship by itself had not brought repentance. It took the prophet's word to explain what the hardship meant.
  • "Since the day that the foundation of the LORD's temple was laid" (v18). This is probably the day of the renewed foundation in 520, not the first one under Cyrus (Ezra 3:10). Some scholars think it was this very day, December 18, when work had progressed to a formal foundation ceremony (compare Zech 8:9).
  • "Is the seed yet in the barn?" (v19). The seed had just been sown, and the vines and fruit trees had not borne. Nothing could yet be seen. "But from this day on I will bless you." Before any harvest came in, God gave his word. They would need faith to believe it until the spring.
  • Commentators: Henry draws from the priests' answers that the people's sin spoiled their work, and he urges them to notice how blessing returned once they took up God's work. JFB explains that sacrifice alone could not sanctify disobedient people, notes that v17 is "appropriated from Am 4:9," and observes that affliction hardens unless it is traced to God's hand. On v19 he writes that "we may trust God's promise to bless us" before there is anything to see. Guzik makes the same point as JFB: hardship does not automatically draw people closer to God.

Zerubbabel Chosen as a Signet (vv20–23)

  • "The word of the LORD came a second time to Haggai on the twenty-fourth day" (v20). On the same December day, a second message came, this time for Zerubbabel alone.
  • "I am about to shake the heavens and the earth, and to overthrow the throne of kingdoms" (vv21–22). The shaking of v6 returns, now aimed at the world's powers. "Throne of kingdoms" may mean every throne, or the one empire that ruled them all, Persia. Darius had just spent two years crushing revolts across his empire. Haggai says God will one day overturn all such thrones.
  • "Overthrow the chariots and their riders… every one by the sword of his brother" (v22). This is exodus language. God threw "the horse and his rider" into the sea (Exod 15:1, 21). The picture of enemies killing one another echoes God's victories in the days of Gideon (Judg 7:22) and Jehoshaphat (2 Chr 20:23).
  • "I will take you, O Zerubbabel my servant" (v23). "My servant" is a title God gave to David (2 Sam 7:5; Ezek 34:23–24) and to the coming Servant in Isaiah (Isa 42:1). "I have chosen you" is also royal language (1 Kgs 11:34).
  • "And make you like a signet ring" (v23). A signet ring carried the owner's seal, used to sign and authorize documents, so it was the sign of a king's authority (Gen 41:42; Esth 8:8). This reverses a curse. Jeremiah had said of Zerubbabel's grandfather, King Jehoiachin (also called Coniah): "Though Coniah… were the signet ring on my right hand, yet I would tear you off," and that none of his offspring would sit on David's throne (Jer 22:24, 30). Now God takes Jehoiachin's grandson and makes him a signet again. The line of David is not finished.
  • What happened to Zerubbabel? He vanishes from the record after the temple work. He never became king, and Judah stayed a Persian province. Some scholars think Persia removed him, though there is no evidence either way. The promise was not fulfilled in Zerubbabel himself. It passed through him. He appears in both genealogies of Jesus (Matt 1:12–13; Luke 3:27). The signet promise was a pledge that God would keep his covenant with David, and he kept it in Zerubbabel's greater descendant.
  • Commentators: Henry reads the word to Zerubbabel as pointing to Christ, God's chosen servant, likened to a signet. JFB calls the Messiah the "antitypical Zerubbabel," with Zerubbabel as the type and Christ as the fulfilment, both chosen by God. Guzik says the promise showed the returnees they were on the winning side, and he notes that Zerubbabel was a legitimate ruler of the returned exiles but not a king, and that Jesus' descent passes through him in both genealogies.

Pointing to Christ

Haggai 2 makes three promises, and each finds its fulfilment in Jesus. The latter glory of the plain second temple would exceed Solomon's, and it did, when the Lord whom Malachi said would "suddenly come to his temple" (Mal 3:1) walked into it. Holiness could not spread from holy meat to common food, but uncleanness spread everywhere. Jesus reversed that rule. When he touched lepers and the dead, he did not become unclean. They became clean (Mark 1:41; Luke 8:54). And the signet ring promise to David's grandson was kept in David's greatest Son, whom God chose and sealed (John 6:27). The shaking of heaven and earth is not yet over. Hebrews draws the conclusion from Haggai's words: "Let us be grateful for receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken" (Heb 12:28).

Reflection

  • Comparing Present to Past Glory (v.3) — Those who remembered Solomon's temple looked at the new foundation and saw it as nothing by comparison. Are you discouraged about something you're building today because you keep measuring it against a former, more impressive season?
  • A Greater Glory Still to Come (v.9) — God promises that the latter glory of this house will exceed the former, and He will give peace in that place. Can you trust that what God is building in your life now may surpass what you've lost, even if it doesn't look that way yet?
  • Holiness Doesn't Transfer, but Defilement Does (vv.12-14) — The priests confirm that holy contact doesn't make ordinary things holy, but unclean contact does spread uncleanness. Are you assuming your religious activity purifies areas of compromise you haven't actually dealt with?
  • The Turning Point of Blessing (v.19) — God marks a specific day — the day the temple's foundation work resumed — as the point from which He will bless the people. Is there a decisive step of obedience you could take today that would mark your own turning point?
  • Chosen as God's Signet Ring (v.23) — God singles out Zerubbabel, restoring him to the place of authority once removed from Judah's royal line, and calls him chosen. Do you see yourself as chosen and entrusted by God, or as too ordinary for Him to use?
