1This is the list of the Israelites—the heads of families, the commanders of thousands and of hundreds, and their officers who served the king in every matter concerning the divisions on rotating military duty each month throughout the year. There were 24,000 men in each division:

2Jashobeam son of Zabdiel was in charge of the first division, which was assigned the first month. There were 24,000 men in his division.

3He was a descendant of Perez and chief of all the army commanders for the first month.

Summary

This chapter catalogs David's military and administrative structure. Twelve army divisions of 24,000 men each rotate monthly guard duty under commanders including Jashobeam, Benaiah, and Asahel's son Zebadiah (vv. 1-15). A ruler is named over each of the twelve tribes (vv. 16-22), followed by a note that David never finished numbering men under twenty, trusting God's promise to multiply Israel like the stars, and that Joab's earlier census was left out of the official record because it brought divine wrath (vv. 23-24). Royal stewards are then listed over the king's treasuries, storehouses, farmland, vineyards, olive and sycamore groves, herds, camels, donkeys, and flocks (vv. 25-31). The chapter closes with David's inner circle of counselors and officials, including Jonathan, Ahithophel, Hushai, and Joab as army commander (vv. 32-34).

Authorship & Background

Author: Traditionally attributed to Ezra the scribe. Originally one book with 2 Chronicles. Written post-exile (approximately 450-400 BC) for the returned remnant. The Chronicler retells Israel's history from a priestly/worship perspective, emphasizing the Davidic covenant, Temple worship, and God's faithfulness.
Historical Context: Chapter 27 shifts from Temple personnel to the civic and military organization of David's kingdom. It records twelve monthly military divisions of 24,000 men each (vv.1-15), tribal leaders over Israel's twelve tribes (vv.16-22), a theological note about David's census failure (vv.23-24), royal stewards over the king's property (vv.25-31), and David's personal advisors (vv.32-34). The monthly rotation system meant 288,000 men served annually — a standing militia that provided national defense without maintaining a permanent army that would drain resources. Each division served one month and returned to civilian life the remaining eleven months — a citizen-soldier model that preserved economic productivity while ensuring military readiness.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: An army organized as a rotating militia of twenty-four thousand a month rather than a standing force, commanders drawn directly from the mighty men who once took Jerusalem, two tribes missing from the list of princes with no explanation offered, a census admitted to have been left unfinished and deliberately excluded from the official record because wrath fell on Israel for it, and an inventory of royal estates naming a man over every vineyard, herd and storehouse down to the sycomore trees
The Twelve Monthly Courses (vv.1-15): The army is organized as a rota rather than a standing force: twelve divisions of twenty-four thousand, each serving one month of the year, so at any time one twelfth is on duty. The commanders are drawn from the mighty men of chapter 11, which ties the militia to the men who took Jerusalem. One entry records a succession within a family, and another names Asahel with the note that his son succeeded him, which is the Chronicler quietly acknowledging that Asahel died young.
The Rulers of the Tribes (vv.16-24): The tribal princes are listed, and two omissions in the list are conspicuous: Gad and Asher are absent, and the Chronicler offers no explanation. Then the census of chapter 21 is returned to, and the note about it is unusually candid. David took not the number of them from twenty years old and under, because the LORD had said he would increase Israel like to the stars. Joab began to number, and finished not, because there fell wrath for it against Israel, neither was the number put in the account of the chronicles of king David. An official record admitting that a record was deliberately left incomplete.
The Stewards of the King's Substance (vv.25-34): Ten verses on estate management, and they are the closest thing in scripture to an inventory of a royal economy: officers over the treasures, the storehouses in the fields, the cities and villages, the tillage, the vineyards, the wine cellars, the olive trees, the sycomore trees, the herds in Sharon, the camels, the asses and the flocks. Each has a named man over it, and two of them are foreigners. Then the counselors, Ahithophel and Hushai, and Joab last, general of the king's army.

Setting: Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. Chapters 23–26 set out the religious side of David's kingdom: Levites, priests, singers, gatekeepers, treasurers. Chapter 27 turns to the civil and military side: the army's twelve monthly divisions, the leaders of the tribes, the managers of the king's farms and herds, and his inner circle of advisers. It is the last list before David's great farewell speeches in chapters 28–29. For readers with no king and no army of their own, it was a picture of what an ordered, God-honoring kingdom had looked like, and a reminder that the throne promised to David still waited for its heir.

The Army of King David (vv1–15)

  • "The divisions that came and went, month after month" (v1). David did not keep a huge full-time army. He organized a citizen militia in twelve divisions of 24,000, each serving one month a year. That gave a large trained force, 288,000 in all, while each man spent eleven months at home farming. The duties probably included guarding borders, manning forts and keeping order. The Tyndale notes point out that the divisions were not organized by tribe, so the army helped bind the tribes into one kingdom.
  • The numbers. 24,000 is a round, stylized figure, twelve times two thousand, and it matches the "24" pattern of the priests, singers and gatekeepers (chs 24–26). Some scholars think the Hebrew word for "thousand" (elef) can also mean a military unit or clan, which would make the divisions smaller. Either way, the point is an orderly, rotating national force.
  • The twelve commanders are David's mighty men. Every name matches the list of heroes in 11:10–47 (and 2 Sam 23). The men who stood with David in the cave of Adullam and at the well of Bethlehem became the leaders of his kingdom. Loyalty in the hard years was rewarded with trust in the good years.
  • Jashobeam, "chief of all the commanders" (vv2–3). He was the first of the three greatest warriors, who killed 300 men at one time (11:11). He was from Perez, the leading clan of Judah, David's own clan (2:4–5; Ruth 4:18–22).
  • "Dodai the Ahohite" (v4). The Hebrew seems to have lost a few words. In 11:12 the second of the three is "Eleazar the son of Dodo the Ahohite," and most scholars think Eleazar commanded this division.
  • Benaiah, "son of Jehoiada the chief priest" (vv5–6). This is the man who killed a lion in a pit on a snowy day (11:22–25). He commanded David's bodyguard, and under Solomon he replaced Joab as commander of the army (1 Kings 2:35). His father, a priest, had led a large company of Aaronites to make David king (12:27). "Chief priest" probably means a leading priest, not the high priest.
  • "Asahel the brother of Joab… and his son Zebadiah after him" (v7). Asahel was killed by Abner early in David's reign, before David ruled all Israel (2 Sam 2:18–23). So either this arrangement goes back to David's first years in Hebron, or the division kept his name in his honor while his son led it. The text does not say which.
  • The rest of the list (vv8–15). Shamhuth (Shammoth in 11:27), Ira of Tekoa (11:28), Helez (11:27), Sibbecai, who killed a Philistine giant (20:4), Abiezer of Anathoth (11:28), Maharai (11:30), Benaiah of Pirathon (11:31) and Heldai, from the clan of Othniel, Israel's first judge (Judg 3:9–11). Most are from Judah and Benjamin, but Ephraim appears twice (vv10, 14), a sign the army was truly national.
  • Commentators: Henry and Guzik both admire the system's wisdom. Henry notes that "none were compelled to be in service… above one month in the year," so the burden was light, and that it kept most men trained for an emergency. Seeing the heroes put in command, he says, "It is well with a kingdom when honour thus attends merit." JFB reads the first place given to Jashobeam as "the dignity of precedence, not of authority," and agrees that v4 needs "Eleazar the son of Dodo" supplied from 2 Samuel 23:9. JFB and Guzik both note Asahel's earlier death, and JFB takes the division to have been named in his memory.

Leaders of Tribes (vv16–24)

  • The tribal chiefs (vv16–22). The tribes still had their own leaders under the king, as they had under Moses (Num 1:5–16). The order roughly follows Numbers 1. Levi is listed and Aaron's priestly family is given its own chief, Zadok (v17), the high priest. Manasseh is split into its western half and the half "in Gilead" east of the Jordan.
  • Gad and Asher are missing. No one knows why. With Levi and Aaron added and Manasseh divided, the list still comes to twelve tribes plus Aaron. Some think the Chronicler wanted twelve; others think the names were lost. JFB simply says, "for what reason is unknown," which is the honest answer.
  • "Elihu, one of David's brothers" (v18). No brother of David by this name appears in Samuel. He may be Eliab, the eldest (1 Sam 16:6), under a variant name, or another brother. Either way, the leader of Judah was family.
  • "Jaasiel the son of Abner" (v21). Abner had been Saul's general and David's opponent before he was murdered by Joab (2 Sam 3:27). David put Abner's son over Benjamin, Saul's own tribe. It was a wise and gracious act that helped heal an old wound.
  • "David did not count those below twenty years of age" (v23). This looks back to the census of chapter 21. Israel's earlier censuses also counted men "from twenty years old and upward" who could go to war (Num 1:3). The reason given is faith: "the LORD had promised to make Israel as many as the stars of heaven" (Gen 15:5; 22:17). A people God has promised to multiply cannot be fully counted.
  • "Joab… began to count, but did not finish" (v24). Joab hated the order and left out Levi and Benjamin (21:6). "Yet wrath came upon Israel for this." The plague that killed 70,000 (21:14) is remembered here in one line. The census was not wrong because counting is wrong. It was wrong because David sought security in numbers rather than in God (21:1–3).
  • "The number was not entered in the chronicles of King David" (v24). The royal records left the total out. There is a quiet irony: our book, called "Chronicles," does give the figures from Joab's report (21:5), but the official tally was never completed or filed. A count begun in pride ended in judgment and was not honored with a place in the record.
  • Commentators: Henry thinks the age limit was a weak excuse, since the promise "chiefly respected the effective men," and says the census was "never perfected" and "David was ashamed of it." JFB thinks the age limit "had probably quieted David's conscience." Guzik's point is that trust belongs in God's promise rather than in counting. Henry draws a lesson from Abner's son being honored: David returned good for evil.

Other Officers (vv25–34)

  • The king's treasuries (v25). Azmaveth kept the treasuries in Jerusalem, and Jonathan the stores "in the country, in the cities, in the villages, and in the towers." These were the king's own goods, separate from the temple treasuries of 26:20–28.
  • Twelve stewards over David's estate (vv25–31). Fields, vineyards, wine cellars, olive and sycamore groves, oil stores, cattle, camels, donkeys and flocks, each with its manager. The Shephelah (v28) is the low hill country between the Judean hills and the coastal plain, good olive and sycamore-fig country (1 Kings 10:27; Amos 7:14). Sharon (v29) is the fertile coastal plain to the north. Kings in the ancient world held large estates, and these supported the court without taxing the people heavily for it.
  • "Obil the Ishmaelite… Jaziz the Hagrite" (vv30–31). Two foreigners held posts of trust. The Ishmaelites were desert traders famous for their camels (Gen 37:25), and the Hagrites were a desert people Israel had defeated east of the Jordan (5:10, 19–22). David put people in charge because they knew the work, not because of their birth. It is a small glimpse of outsiders brought into the kingdom.
  • "Stewards of King David's property" (v31). A steward manages what belongs to another. The word fits David himself, who will soon pray, "All things come from you, and of your own have we given you" (29:14).
  • "Jonathan, David's uncle, was a counselor" (v32). The word dod usually means "uncle," but it can mean a close relative more broadly. Some identify him with Jonathan the son of Shimea, David's nephew (20:7), but that is uncertain. He was "a man of understanding and a scribe," a learned man. He and Jehiel "attended the king's sons," probably as tutors.
  • "Ahithophel was the king's counselor" (v33). His advice was treated "as if one consulted the word of God" (2 Sam 16:23). But he joined Absalom's rebellion, and when his advice was ignored he went home and hanged himself (2 Sam 15:12; 17:23). Many think he turned against David because he was Bathsheba's grandfather (2 Sam 11:3; 23:34), though the text never says so. Psalm 41:9, "even my close friend in whom I trusted… has lifted his heel against me," is often linked to him, and Jesus applied that verse to Judas (John 13:18).
  • "Hushai the Archite was the king's friend" (v33). "The king's friend" was an official title for a trusted confidant (1 Kings 4:5). Hushai lived up to it. When Absalom rebelled, David sent him back to Jerusalem to defeat Ahithophel's advice, and he did (2 Sam 15:32–37; 17:5–14). Two advisers stand side by side in this verse: one who betrayed and one who stayed true.
  • "Jehoiada the son of Benaiah, and Abiathar… Joab" (v34). Abiathar the priest had escaped Saul's massacre and stayed with David through everything (1 Sam 22:20–23). Yet Abiathar and Joab both later backed Adonijah's bid for the throne against Solomon (1 Kings 1:7). The list closes with men who would soon have to choose.
  • Commentators: Henry calls Ahithophel "a very cunning man" and Hushai "an honest man," and he adds that, with all his counselors, David "preferred his Bible before them all," citing Psalm 119:24: "your testimonies are my delight; they are my counselors." Henry admires David's estate run "not for pleasure, but for profit," and says leaders who want industrious people "must themselves be examples of industry." Guzik, drawing on others, stresses that everything these men managed belonged to the king and was only entrusted to them, and that each should be faithful in his own area without envying another's. JFB notes that the camels were probably kept east of the Jordan, which explains why desert men were put over them. JFB has no notes on vv32–34.

Pointing to Christ

This chapter is an ordered kingdom: loyal heroes in command, tribes united, enemies' sons welcomed, foreigners trusted, every field and flock in a faithful steward's hand. And it is still a broken kingdom, marked by a census that brought wrath and an adviser who betrayed. The Chronicler's readers had none of it left, but they had God's promise of a son of David whose kingdom would last forever (17:11–14). That King has come. He calls his followers not merely servants but "friends" (John 15:15), he was betrayed by a trusted companion as David was, and he entrusts his servants with what is his until he returns (Matt 25:14–30). His people cannot be counted, as many as the stars, just as God promised (Rev 7:9).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik notes that the rotation system of 24,000 men per month was both militarily effective and economically wise — citizens served without being permanently removed from productive life. He highlights the theological significance of v.23-24, where David's faith in God's promise restrained the impulse to count comprehensively, and observes that the Chronicler's mention of the failed census serves as a humility check within an otherwise impressive organizational chapter.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Preparation Under God's Sovereignty (vv.1-15) — David organizes twelve monthly divisions of 24,000 men each, an impressive standing militia built through careful planning. Do you prepare diligently for what's ahead while still trusting God rather than your own strength or systems?
  • Trusting Promises Over Statistics (vv.23-24) — David refuses to count those under twenty because God had already promised to multiply Israel like the stars, while Joab's earlier census brought wrath. Where might you be leaning on numbers or metrics instead of resting in God's stated promises?
  • Comprehensive Stewardship (vv.25-31) — David appoints specific leaders over every category of his property, from vineyards to herds to storehouses, leaving nothing unmanaged. What areas of your own life — finances, time, relationships, health — currently have no real plan or attention behind them?
  • Wise and Loyal Counsel (vv.32-34) — David surrounds himself with advisors who combine wisdom with faithfulness, and also has Jehiel mentoring the next generation of leaders. Who speaks into your life, and are you also investing in those who will lead after you?
