Summary

Hiram, king of Tyre, who had loved David, sends envoys to Solomon on hearing he has become king (v. 1). Solomon explains that David could not build a temple because of constant warfare, but now that God has given him rest on every side, he intends to build a house for the Lord's name as God promised through David, and he asks Hiram to supply cedar cut by skilled Sidonian woodcutters (vv. 2-6). Hiram rejoices, blesses the Lord for giving David such a wise son, and agrees to provide cedar and cypress timber, floated by sea to wherever Solomon directs, in exchange for wheat and oil for his household (vv. 7-11). The Lord gives Solomon wisdom as promised, and Solomon and Hiram make a peace treaty between them (v. 12). Solomon conscripts a labor force of thirty thousand Israelites, sending them to Lebanon in monthly shifts of ten thousand under Adoniram, along with seventy thousand burden-bearers and eighty thousand stonecutters in the hills (vv. 13-16). Great, costly stones are quarried and prepared, and Solomon's and Hiram's workers cut timber and stone to build the temple (vv. 17-18).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Jeremiah or a prophetic school. Originally one book with 2 Kings in the Hebrew Bible. Covers approximately 120 years (970-850 BC) — from David's death through Ahaziah's reign.
Historical Context: Chapter 5 begins the Temple narrative — the central achievement of Solomon's reign and the theological heart of 1 Kings. David had desired to build God's house but was forbidden because he was "a man of war" (1 Chronicles 28:3). Solomon, whose name means "peace" (from "shalom"), is the designated builder. The chapter records the diplomatic and logistical preparations: the alliance with Hiram of Tyre for materials, and the conscription of labor forces. Tyre was the dominant Phoenician city-state, renowned for its cedar forests, skilled craftsmen, and maritime trade. Hiram had been David's ally (2 Samuel 5:11) and now extends that relationship to Solomon. The cedars of Lebanon were the finest building material in the ancient Near East — fragrant, durable, and resistant to insects. Their use for God's house signified that only the best was acceptable for divine worship. The massive labor force (30,000 conscripted Israelites plus 150,000 burden-bearers and stonecutters) reveals both the scale of the project and the cost to the nation. This chapter shows that building God's house requires both divine calling and human effort — supernatural purpose accomplished through natural means.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: A Phoenician alliance initiated by Hiram before Solomon even asks for it, rest named as the precondition for building rather than an incidental blessing, a workforce of over one hundred eighty thousand men mobilized for a single national project, Adoniram named as the overseer who will be stoned when the same labor becomes a grievance, and cedar and stone quarried and cut before ever reaching the site so the temple rose without hammer or axe being heard there
The Alliance with Hiram (vv.1-12): Hiram initiates contact — sending servants to congratulate Solomon. Solomon's response is theological: David could not build because of wars; now God has given "rest on every side" (v.4). The Hebrew "menucha" (rest) is the same word used for the Promised Land's rest (Deuteronomy 12:10) and the Temple's purpose (Psalm 132:8, 14). Peace is the precondition for building God's dwelling. Solomon's request is specific: cedar timber and skilled woodcutters ("there is not among us any that can skill to hew timber like unto the Sidonians," v.6). Hiram's response is enthusiastic — he praises Yahweh (v.7), suggesting either genuine respect for Israel's God or diplomatic courtesy. The agreement is mutually beneficial: Tyre provides timber; Israel provides food (20,000 cors of wheat and 20,000 cors of oil annually). The chapter emphasizes that "the LORD gave Solomon wisdom" (v.12) — even this diplomatic success is attributed to God's gift.
The Labor Force (vv.13-18): Solomon conscripts 30,000 Israelites for Lebanon duty — rotating 10,000 per month (one month working, two months home). Additionally, 70,000 burden-bearers and 80,000 stonecutters work in the hill country, supervised by 3,300 officers. Total workforce: approximately 183,300 men. This is an enormous national mobilization. The rotation system shows some consideration for the workers, but the scale of forced labor will become a grievance. Adoniram (the same official from 4:6) oversees the conscription — and will be stoned to death when the people revolt in chapter 12. The foundation stones are "great stones, costly stones, and hewed stones" (v.17) — precision-cut blocks for the Temple's foundation. Everything about this project communicates: God deserves the best, and building for God requires sacrifice.

Map & Geography

  • Jerusalem is central — site of Solomon's Temple and royal palace; capital of the united kingdom, then of Judah (southern kingdom) after the division.
  • The kingdom divides after Solomon: Israel (north, capital eventually at Samaria) and Judah (south, capital Jerusalem).
  • Key locations: Gibeon (Solomon's dream, ch.3), Tyre (Hiram's city in Phoenicia, chs.5-7), Mount Carmel (Elijah vs. Baal prophets, ch.18), Zarephath (Sidonian widow, ch.17), Mount Horeb/Sinai (Elijah flees, ch.19), Ramoth-gilead (Ahab's death, ch.22).

Deep Dive

This Deep Dive was put together with AI help, drawing on the commentators named below. I’m a learner, not a scholar, so test everything against Scripture. Spot a mistake or have an idea? Let me know. How this site is made

Setting: Early in Solomon's reign, around 967 BC. Chapter 4 showed his kingdom organized, at peace and prospering. Now he turns to the great work his father longed to do: building a house for the LORD. David had wanted to build it, but God said through Nathan that David's son would build it instead (2 Sam 7:12–13). Chronicles adds that David was told he could not build because he had "shed much blood" in war (1 Chr 22:8; 28:3), and that he gathered huge stores of materials and handed Solomon the plans (1 Chr 22:2–5, 14–16; 28:11–19). Chapter 5 covers the preparations: a treaty with the Phoenician king of Tyre for timber and craftsmen, and a great labor force to cut and haul stone. Second Chronicles 2 tells the same story with extra details. In Hebrew Bibles this chapter is numbered 5:15–32, since the end of ch 4 is counted there as 5:1–14.

Preparations for Building the Temple (vv1–18)

  • "Hiram king of Tyre sent his servants to Solomon… for Hiram always loved David" (v1). Tyre was a rich Phoenician port city on an island just off the coast of what is now Lebanon, a center of sea trade across the Mediterranean. Hiram had already sent cedar and craftsmen to build David's palace (2 Sam 5:11). "Loved" here is treaty language: in ancient diplomatic letters, allied kings described each other as "loving" one another. A new king meant the treaty had to be renewed, so Hiram sends envoys. JFB: relations were "renewed with his son and successor."
  • "David my father could not build a house for the name of the LORD his God because of the warfare" (v3). Kings gives the practical reason, constant war. Chronicles gives the deeper one, that David was a man of war and bloodshed (1 Chr 22:8). Both are true. "Under the soles of his feet" pictures a conqueror with his foot on his enemies, as ancient carvings show kings standing on defeated foes (Josh 10:24; Ps 110:1).
  • "The LORD my God has given me rest on every side. There is neither adversary nor misfortune" (v4). "Rest" was what God had promised when Israel entered the land: once he gave them rest from their enemies, they were to worship at the place he would choose (Deut 12:10–11). Solomon sees that the time has come. "Adversary" is satan, literally an opponent or accuser, the word that later became the name of the devil. Guzik points this out. Here it means any human enemy. Sadly, God will later "raise up an adversary" (satan) against Solomon when his heart turns (11:14, 23).
  • "I intend to build a house for the name of the LORD my God" (v5). Not a house for the LORD to live in, but for his name. Pagan temples were thought to be the homes of gods, who needed food and care. Israel's God fills heaven and earth. Solomon himself will say, "heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you; how much less this house that I have built!" (8:27). The "name" stands for God's revealed character and his chosen presence among his people (Deut 12:5, 11). Guzik makes this point.
  • "Command that cedars of Lebanon be cut for me" (v6). The cedars of Lebanon were the finest building timber of the ancient world: huge, straight, fragrant and resistant to rot and insects. Egyptian and Mesopotamian kings boasted of bringing them home for their temples and palaces. Only a few small groves survive today. The Tyndale notes say "Sidonians" was a general name for Phoenicians, since Sidon was the older city. They were the master woodcutters and sailors of the age.
  • "Blessed be the LORD this day, who has given to David a wise son" (v7). Hiram praises Israel's God. That does not mean he converted. Ancient kings often spoke respectfully of an ally's god. JFB: it is "no decisive evidence that Hiram was a worshipper of the true God." But the wisdom God gave Solomon is now being seen by the nations, as 4:34 promised.
  • "I will make it into rafts to go by sea to the place you direct" (v9). The logs were bound together and floated down the coast. Chronicles names the landing place as Joppa (2 Chr 2:16), modern Jaffa near Tel Aviv, and from there they were hauled about 35 miles uphill to Jerusalem. "Cypress" (berosh) may be juniper or fir. Its exact species is uncertain.
  • "You shall meet my wishes by providing food for my household" (v9). Tyre was a city of merchants on a rocky island with little farmland, so it needed grain and oil. Israel had plenty of both. JFB: Israel, "being agricultural, furnished corn and oil."
  • "20,000 cors of wheat… and 20,000 cors of beaten oil" (v11). A cor was about six bushels (220 liters, the ESV footnote says), so this was a vast amount each year. "Beaten oil" was the finest olive oil, crushed by hand rather than pressed (Exod 27:20). The Hebrew text actually reads "twenty cors" of oil, and the ESV follows the Greek translation's 20,000. The figures in 2 Chronicles 2:10 are different again, because they describe supplies for the woodcutters rather than for Hiram's court.
  • "The LORD gave Solomon wisdom, as he promised him. And there was peace between Hiram and Solomon, and the two of them made a treaty" (v12). The writer ties even this business deal to God's gift in 3:12. "Peace" is again shalom, a play on Solomon's name. A formal treaty (berith, covenant) now binds the two kingdoms. The partnership will later extend to a joint fleet trading on the Red Sea (9:26–28; 10:22).
  • "King Solomon drafted forced labor out of all Israel, and the draft numbered 30,000 men" (v13). This is the first mention of Israelites being drafted. Chapter 9:20–22 says the permanent slave labor was drawn from the leftover Canaanite peoples, and Israelites were not made slaves. The difference may be between permanent slavery (Canaanites) and temporary national service (Israelites). Either way, Samuel had warned that a king would "take your sons… to plow his ground and to reap his harvest" (1 Sam 8:11–17).
  • "10,000 a month in shifts. They would be a month in Lebanon and two months at home" (v14). One month away, two months home, so men could still work their own farms. JFB calls the levy "a stretch of arbitrary power" but thinks the rotation "reconciled the people to this forced labor." Adoniram ran it, the same man the northern tribes would later stone (12:18).
  • "70,000 burden-bearers and 80,000 stonecutters in the hill country" (v15). Chronicles says these were foreigners living in Israel, mainly Canaanites (2 Chr 2:17–18). The quarries were in the hills near Jerusalem. The limestone bedrock under Jerusalem has been quarried since ancient times.
  • "Besides Solomon's 3,300 chief officers who were over the work" (v16). Chronicles gives 3,600 (2 Chr 2:18). Adding the 550 chief officers of 9:23 gives 3,850, and Chronicles' 3,600 plus 250 (2 Chr 8:10) also gives 3,850. The two books seem to divide the same total of supervisors differently, as the Tyndale notes suggest.
  • "Great, costly stones in order to lay the foundation of the house with dressed stones" (v17). "Costly" means precious, of high quality, and Guzik notes the word means "quality stones." They were laid in the foundation, where no one would see them. "Dressed" means cut and squared, shaped at the quarry so that no hammer would be heard at the temple site (6:7). JFB describes the stones as "bevelled," some very long.
  • "The men of Gebal" (v18). Gebal is the Phoenician city the Greeks called Byblos, north of Beirut, one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world. It exported papyrus to Greece, and from its name came the Greek word biblos, "book," and so our word "Bible." Its craftsmen were famous shipbuilders and stonemasons (Ezek 27:9).
  • Commentators: Henry introduces the chapter with Zechariah's words of the coming Branch, "he shall build the temple of the LORD" (Zech 6:12), already reading the temple-builder as a picture of Christ. Guzik credits David's wise alliances for Hiram's friendship, and draws a lesson from the hidden costly foundation stones: the unseen work in a believer's life, and in a church, matters as much as what is seen. He also mentions a Jewish legend that no worker died or fell ill during the building, which he presents as legend only. JFB says the heavy labor "was assigned to the remnant of the Canaanites" and prisoners of war. The Tyndale notes say that Hiram is a short form of Ahiram, a name found in Phoenician inscriptions, and that Egyptian texts mention Byblos as a timber port.

Pointing to Christ

Solomon, "the man of rest," builds a house for God's name with cedar from the nations and stones cut far away, fitted together without the sound of a hammer. The temple was the place where God put his name and met his people. Jesus said, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up," speaking of his body (John 2:19–21). He is the place where God's name and presence live among us: "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). And he is building a living temple now, out of people from Israel and the nations together: "you yourselves like living stones are being built up as a spiritual house" (1 Pet 2:5), "Christ Jesus himself being the cornerstone" (Eph 2:20). Hiram's Phoenicians worked on Solomon's house, and Gentiles from every nation are being shaped into Christ's.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes that Solomon's alliance with Hiram shows wisdom in action — using available resources and expertise rather than insisting on doing everything independently. He also notes the tension between the glory of the Temple project and the burden of forced labor. Building for God should not oppress God's people. The chapter reveals that even righteous projects can be accomplished through unjust means.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Building in God's Season, Not Ours (v.4) — David couldn't build the Temple because his reign was consumed by war, but Solomon could because God had finally given rest on every side. Where are you trying to force a project or plan before God has actually opened the door for it?
  • Admitting What You Can't Do Alone (v.6) — Solomon openly told Hiram that Israel had no one skilled enough to cut timber like the Sidonians, and asked for help instead of pretending otherwise. What area of your life needs you to admit a limitation and seek help rather than struggle on in pride?
  • Crediting God Even for the Ordinary Wins (v.12) — The narrator attributes Solomon's successful diplomacy with Hiram directly to the wisdom God had given him. Do you recognize God's hand in your everyday decisions, or only in the obviously "spiritual" moments?
  • The Hidden Cost Behind a Great Achievement (vv.13-18) — The massive labor force that built the Temple's foundation, overseen by Adoniram, planted a burden that would later help tear the kingdom apart. What is a worthwhile goal in your life costing the people around you, and are you paying attention to that cost?
