1“Do not let your hearts be troubled. You believe in God; believe in Me as well.

10Do you not believe that I am in the Father and the Father is in Me? The words I say to you, I do not speak on My own. Instead, it is the Father dwelling in Me, performing His works.

11Believe Me that I am in the Father and the Father is in Me—or at least believe on account of the works themselves.

12Truly, truly, I tell you, whoever believes in Me will also do the works that I am doing. He will do even greater things than these, because I am going to the Father.

13And I will do whatever you ask in My name, so that the Father may be glorified in the Son.

14If you ask Me for anything in My name, I will do it.

15If you love Me, you will keep My commandments.

16And I will ask the Father, and He will give you another Advocate to be with you forever—

18I will not leave you as orphans; I will come to you.

20On that day you will know that I am in My Father, and you are in Me, and I am in you.

Summary

Jesus comforts the disciples ahead of His departure, telling them not to be troubled and promising that in His Father's house are many rooms, where He is going to prepare a place for them and will return to take them there (vv. 1-3). When Thomas asks how to know the way, Jesus declares that He himself is the way, the truth, and the life, and that no one comes to the Father except through Him (vv. 5-6). He answers Philip that to have seen Him is to have seen the Father (vv. 8-9), then promises that after He leaves, the Father will send another Counselor, the Spirit of truth, to remain with believers forever (vv. 16-17). Responding to Judas (not Iscariot), Jesus explains that those who love Him and keep His word will be indwelt by Him and the Father, and that the coming Spirit will teach them all things (vv. 22-26). He leaves them His peace, unlike the world's peace, telling them not to be troubled or afraid (v. 27), then closes by affirming His love-driven obedience to the Father before urging them to rise and go (vv. 30-31).

Authorship & Background

Author: The Apostle John (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Classification: Gospel — Narrative and Discourse
Key Themes: Troubled hearts addressed directly, a house with many rooms, an exclusive claim about the way, another Comforter of the same kind, and a peace distinguished from the world's
Historical Context: Chapter 14 is part of the Upper Room Discourse (chapters 13-17), spoken on the night of Jesus' arrest. The disciples were troubled — Jesus had just announced His departure, predicted Peter's denial, and identified a betrayer. Chapter 14 is Jesus' direct response to their troubled hearts. This chapter contains some of the most comforting and theologically rich words in all of Scripture: the promise of heaven (vv.1-3), the exclusive claim of Christ as the only way to the Father (v.6), the promise of the Holy Spirit as Comforter (vv.16-17,26), and the gift of Christ's peace (v.27).
In My Father's House (vv.1-6): This is the Upper Room Discourse, spoken the night of the arrest after Jesus has announced his departure, predicted Peter's denial and identified a betrayer. The disciples are troubled, and the chapter is the direct answer to that. Let not your heart be troubled, and the reason offered is a place prepared. Thomas's admission that they do not know the way produces v.6, the most exclusive sentence in the Gospel: I am the way, the truth, and the life.
He That Hath Seen Me (vv.7-11): Philip asks to be shown the Father, which after thirteen chapters is a poignant request. The reply is gentle and complete: he that hath seen me hath seen the Father, how sayest thou then, shew us the Father? The mutual indwelling of Father and Son is offered as the thing to believe, with the works given as the fallback for anyone who cannot.
Whatsoever Ye Shall Ask (vv.12-14): Greater works than these shall the believer do, because he goes to the Father — a statement about scope rather than power, since the mission widens after Pentecost. The promise about asking is twice qualified by “in my name”, which the chapter treats as a limit as much as a license.
Another Comforter (vv.15-24): The Spirit is introduced as the Paraclete, one called alongside to help, and the word “another” in v.16 means another of the same kind — so the Spirit is personal in the way Jesus is, not a force. He will abide for ever, teach, and bring things to remembrance. Love and obedience are tied together three times in this section, and Judas not Iscariot asks the only other question in the chapter.
My Peace I Give unto You (vv.25-31): Peace is given and immediately distinguished: not as the world giveth. The instruction to rejoice at his going is the hardest thing in the chapter for people who do not want him to go. Verse 30 notes that the prince of this world comes and has nothing in him, and the chapter ends with arise, let us go hence — so the remaining discourse is spoken on the way out.

Setting: The same Thursday night, in the upstairs room in Jerusalem, about AD 30 or 33. Jesus has washed the disciples' feet, exposed the traitor and sent Judas out into the night, then told the eleven that he is leaving and that "where I am going you cannot come" (13:33). He has just warned Peter that he will deny him three times before morning. The disciples are shaken. Their teacher is going away, one of them is a traitor, and their boldest member is about to fall. Chapter 14 is Jesus' answer to their fear, and it opens with the words "Let not your hearts be troubled." Through the chapter four disciples interrupt with questions, Peter (13:36), Thomas, Philip and the other Judas, and each question opens a new line of teaching.

I Am the Way, and the Truth, and the Life (vv1–14)

  • "Let not your hearts be troubled" (v1). The verb is tarassō, the word John has used three times of Jesus himself, at Lazarus's tomb (11:33), facing his hour (12:27) and announcing his betrayer (13:21). The one who has felt that turmoil now speaks peace into theirs. "Hearts" is plural, but "heart" in Greek is singular: the group shares one troubled heart.
  • "Believe in God; believe also in me" (v1). In Greek both verbs can be read either as commands or as statements. The ESV takes both as commands. The KJV takes the first as a statement: "ye believe in God, believe also in me." Either way, Jesus places trust in himself right beside trust in God, which no mere prophet could do. JFB calls this "the concentration of our trust in the Unseen and Impalpable One upon His Own Incarnate Son."
  • "In my Father's house are many rooms" (v2). "My Father's house" was Jesus' phrase for the temple (2:16). Now it means the Father's own dwelling, of which the temple was only a copy. "Rooms" is monai, from menō, "remain, abide." The KJV's "mansions" came through the Latin mansiones, which meant dwelling places or stopping places, not grand houses. The point is not size but permanence and welcome: there is room, and it lasts.
  • "I go to prepare a place for you" (v2). Many readers see a picture of a Jewish betrothal, in which the groom went away to build rooms onto his father's house and came back for his bride. The text itself does not mention a wedding, but Jesus does use bridegroom language elsewhere (3:29; Matt 25:1–13). The main point is that his going is for their sake. The preparation is his death, resurrection and ascension, which open the way.
  • "I will come again and will take you to myself" (v3). Christians read this "coming" in several ways: his return from the grave on Easter (v18), his coming by the Spirit (v23), his coming for each believer at death, or his second coming at the end. The words "take you to myself, that where I am you may be also" fit the final return best, though John often lets a promise carry more than one fulfillment. The heart of the promise is not a place but a person: "where I am."
  • "Lord, we do not know where you are going" (v5). Thomas is the realist of the group. He was ready to die with Jesus (11:16) and later will not believe without evidence (20:25). His honest confusion draws out one of the great sentences of the Bible.
  • "I am the way, and the truth, and the life" (v6). This is the sixth of the seven "I am" sayings with a picture attached in John. Jesus does not say he shows the way, teaches the truth or gives life, though he does all three. He says he is them. In the Old Testament "the way" is the path of life God sets before his people (Ps 16:11; 86:11). Truth is what God is (Ps 31:5), and life comes from him alone (Deut 30:20). Jesus claims all three. Many read "the way" as the main point, with "truth" and "life" explaining why he is the way.
  • "No one comes to the Father except through me" (v6). This is the claim that sets Christian faith apart from the idea that all religions lead to God. Peter preaches the same in Acts 4:12. Jesus does not say it with arrogance. He says it to frightened friends who need to know the road is real and that they are on it.
  • "From now on you do know him and have seen him" (v7). Knowing Jesus is knowing the Father. "From now on" probably looks to the hour of the cross and resurrection now underway, in which the Father's love is fully revealed.
  • "Lord, show us the Father, and it is enough for us" (v8). Philip wants what Moses asked for: "Please show me your glory" (Exod 33:18). God answered Moses that no one could see his face and live. Philip is asking for a vision, a theophany.
  • "Whoever has seen me has seen the Father" (v9). There is sadness in "Have I been with you so long?" Philip had walked with Jesus since the first chapter (1:43). The vision he asks for has been beside him for three years. This is the claim of the prologue: "No one has ever seen God; the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known" (1:18). Jesus is not the Father, but he perfectly shows the Father (Col 1:15; Heb 1:3).
  • "I am in the Father and the Father is in me" (vv10–11). Theologians later called this mutual indwelling (in Greek perichōrēsis). The Son's words and works are the Father's words and works. Jesus offers two grounds for believing it: his word, "or else… the works themselves." Faith resting on evidence is still faith, and Jesus honors it.
  • "Greater works than these will he do" (v12). This does not mean bigger miracles. Nobody does more than raise Lazarus. The reason given is "because I am going to the Father," which means after the Spirit comes. On Pentecost Peter preached once and three thousand believed (Acts 2:41), a harvest larger than any single response recorded in Jesus' own ministry. The works are greater in reach, spreading across the world, and in kind, bringing people into the full life of the Spirit after the cross. JFB puts it: "not in degree but in kind."
  • "Whatever you ask in my name, this I will do" (vv13–14). "In my name" means more than adding Jesus' name at the end of a prayer. A name stood for the person and his authority and character. To pray in his name is to pray as his representatives, for what he wants, "that the Father may be glorified in the Son." Verse 14 says "ask me," and many manuscripts include "me," showing that prayer may be addressed to Jesus himself as well as to the Father.
  • Commentators: Guzik notes that monē comes from the verb "to stay," so "dwelling places" is closer to the Greek, though he defends "mansions" as fitting God's generosity, and says heaven matters most because Jesus is there. He argues that if Jesus was not the only way, he was not an honest teacher. Guzik and JFB agree that the greater works depend on the Spirit, and Guzik warns that "in my name" is not a formula for getting what we want. Henry writes, "Heaven is a house, not a tent or tabernacle," and calls Christ "the true and living way." On v12 Henry says that winning so much of the world to Christ, against every outward disadvantage, "was the miracle of all."

Jesus Promises the Holy Spirit (vv15–31)

  • "If you love me, you will keep my commandments" (v15). This is not a threat but a description of what love does. The theme runs through the chapter (vv21, 23, 24) and frames the promise of the Spirit. Jesus himself shows it at the end: "I do as the Father has commanded me, so that the world may know that I love the Father" (v31).
  • "Another Helper" (v16). The word is paraklētos, literally "one called alongside," used in Greek for a legal advocate, a friend who speaks for someone in court. English translations differ because no one word covers it: Comforter (KJV), Counselor, Advocate, Helper. 1 John 2:1 uses the same word for Jesus as our "advocate with the Father." "Another" (allos) means another of the same kind. Jesus has been their Paraclete, and the Spirit will be one just like him.
  • "To be with you forever" (v16). In the Old Testament the Spirit came upon particular people for particular tasks and could depart (1 Sam 16:14; Ps 51:11). Jesus promises a Spirit who stays.
  • "The Spirit of truth, whom the world cannot receive" (v17). The Spirit belongs with the Truth (v6). "The world" in John means humanity organized against God. It cannot receive the Spirit because it does not see or know him. "He dwells with you and will be in you" marks a change coming at Pentecost: the Spirit with them in Jesus, then in them. Some important manuscripts read "is in you." The ESV follows "will be," which fits the promise of a coming change.
  • "I will not leave you as orphans; I will come to you" (v18). Disciples of a rabbi were sometimes called his children, and a teacher's death left them "orphans." Jesus has just called them "little children" (13:33). He promises he will not abandon them. The coming here is most naturally his return after the resurrection, when "you will see me" (v19), and also his ongoing presence by the Spirit.
  • "Because I live, you also will live" (v19). The Easter promise in five words. The life of Jesus is the source and guarantee of theirs (11:25–26; Col 3:3–4).
  • "In that day you will know that I am in my Father, and you in me, and I in you" (v20). "That day" is the day of resurrection and the age it opens. The union of Father and Son (v10) is opened to the disciples. This is the "abide in me" of the next chapter (15:4).
  • "I will love him and manifest myself to him" (v21). "Manifest" (emphanizō) means to make visible or clear. Jesus will show himself to those who love him.
  • "Judas (not Iscariot)" (v22). John carefully separates this man from the traitor. He is the apostle called "Judas the son of James" in Luke 6:16 and Acts 1:13, probably the same as Thaddaeus in Matthew and Mark. His question is natural: why not show yourself to everyone? Jesus' brothers had asked much the same (7:4). The disciples still expected a public Messiah.
  • "We will come to him and make our home with him" (v23). "Home" is monē, the same word as the "rooms" of v2. Jesus is going to prepare rooms for them in the Father's house. Meanwhile the Father and Son will make their room in the disciple. The temple was where God made his home among his people. Now his people become his home (1 Cor 3:16; Eph 2:22).
  • "The word that you hear is not mine but the Father's" (v24). Rejecting Jesus' words is rejecting the Father's. This repeats 12:49.
  • "He will teach you all things and bring to your remembrance all that I have said" (v26). This is one of the plainest statements of the Trinity in the Gospels: "the Helper, the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name." It is also a promise about the New Testament. The apostles would remember and understand Jesus' teaching by the Spirit's help, which is why John can say "his disciples remembered" (2:22; 12:16). Christians rightly apply it in a secondary way to the Spirit's help in reading Scripture, but the first promise was to the apostles who heard him.
  • "Peace I leave with you; my peace I give to you" (v27). Shalom was the ordinary Hebrew greeting and farewell, but it meant wholeness and well-being, not just calm. Jesus leaves it like a man leaving a bequest in his will. JFB: "Christ is the Executor of His own Testament." The world's peace depends on circumstances. His holds in the middle of trouble (16:33). The prophets promised a Prince of Peace (Isa 9:6) and peace to those far and near (Isa 57:19). After the resurrection his first word to them is "Peace be with you" (20:19).
  • "Let not your hearts be troubled, neither let them be afraid" (v27). The chapter comes full circle to v1, adding "afraid" (deiliaō, to be cowardly or fearful).
  • "If you loved me, you would have rejoiced" (v28). Jesus asks them to love him enough to be glad for him. His departure is a homecoming.
  • "The Father is greater than I" (v28). In the fourth century Arius used this verse to argue that the Son was a created being, lower than God. The church answered at Nicaea (AD 325) and since. The same Gospel says "the Word was God" (1:1) and "I and the Father are one" (10:30). Most interpreters take "greater" to refer to the Son's role, sent by the Father and obedient to him, especially in his humble earthly life, not to a lesser divine nature. A son is not less human than his father, though the father has a greater position. Here "greater" explains why the disciples should rejoice: Jesus is returning to the glory of the Father's presence.
  • "I have told you before it takes place" (v29). As with Judas's betrayal (13:19), Jesus predicts so that the events will strengthen faith, not destroy it.
  • "The ruler of this world is coming. He has no claim on me" (v30). Satan is coming in the form of Judas and the arresting soldiers. "He has no claim on me" is literally "in me he has nothing." There is no sin in Jesus that gives Satan a foothold, no debt that the accuser can call in. Jesus goes to death freely (10:18), not as Satan's prisoner.
  • "So that the world may know that I love the Father" (v31). The cross is many things, but here Jesus names one: the world's clearest view of the Son's love for the Father, shown in obedience.
  • "Rise, let us go from here" (v31). This line puzzles readers, since Jesus keeps talking for three more chapters and only "went out" in 18:1. Some think chs 15–17 were spoken as they walked through the city toward the Kidron Valley. Some think they stood up and Jesus kept talking before they left. Some scholars think John gathered more of the night's teaching after this point. The text does not say, and none of these views changes the meaning.
  • Commentators: Guzik and JFB both explain paraklētos as one called alongside to help, and Guzik notes that allos means "another of the same kind": as Jesus showed the Father, the Spirit shows Jesus. On v28, Guzik takes "greater" as a statement about the Son's position in the incarnation, not his being. JFB reads "I will come to you" (v18) as Christ's coming by the Spirit and says Satan found in Jesus "nothing of His own—nothing to fasten on." JFB gives no separate comment on vv24 and 29. Henry writes that "the world's peace begins in ignorance, consists with sin, and ends in endless troubles," and on v30 that "the foresight of a temptation gives us great advantage in our resistance of it."

Pointing to Christ

On the night before he dies, Jesus spends his words comforting the people who are about to abandon him. He does not offer them a technique for calm. He offers himself: the way to the Father, the truth about the Father, the life of the Father in a human face. Whoever has seen him has seen the Father. He goes to prepare a place, and while he is gone the Father and the Son make their home in those who love him through the Spirit who stays forever. The ruler of this world is coming, but finds nothing in Jesus to hold. He walks to the cross by choice, "so that the world may know that I love the Father," and because he lives, those who belong to him will live also.

Reflection

  • A Command to Trust (v.1) — Jesus doesn't ask the disciples not to feel troubled — He commands them not to let their hearts stay troubled, calling for a choice to trust. What is troubling your heart right now, and what would it look like to actively choose trust over anxiety?
  • The Way, the Truth, and the Life (v.6) — In a moment of comfort to grieving friends, Jesus makes an exclusive claim: no one comes to the Father except through Him. How do you hold that conviction with both confidence and compassion?
  • Love Shown Through Obedience (v.15) — Jesus ties love for Him directly to keeping His commands, not just feeling affection toward Him. What commandment of Christ are you currently struggling to keep, and what does that reveal about where your love actually is?
  • The Spirit Who Remains (v.26) — Jesus promises the Holy Spirit will teach and remind believers of everything He said, staying with them for good. How present is the Spirit's ongoing teaching in your daily life, not just at conversion?
  • A Different Kind of Peace (v.27) — Christ's peace doesn't depend on good circumstances the way the world's peace does. Are you experiencing His peace right now, or are you waiting for your circumstances to be right before you feel at peace?
