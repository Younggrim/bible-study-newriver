9“All this I will give You,” he said, “if You will fall down and worship me.”

Summary

The Spirit leads Jesus into the wilderness, where he fasts forty days and is tempted three times by the devil — to turn stones to bread, to throw himself from the temple, and to worship Satan in exchange for the kingdoms of the world — answering each temptation with Scripture until the devil leaves him (vv. 1-11). Hearing that John the Baptist has been arrested, Jesus withdraws to Capernaum in Galilee, fulfilling Isaiah's prophecy that a great light would dawn on that region (vv. 12-16). He begins preaching that people should repent because the kingdom of heaven has come near, then calls the fishermen Peter, Andrew, James, and John to leave their nets and follow him as fishers of men (vv. 17-22). Jesus travels through Galilee teaching in synagogues, proclaiming the good news of the kingdom, and healing every disease, drawing large crowds from Galilee, the Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea, and beyond the Jordan (vv. 23-25).

Authorship & Background

Author: Matthew (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 4 records the temptation of Jesus in the wilderness, the beginning of His Galilean ministry, and the calling of the first four disciples. The temptation follows immediately after the baptism — the Spirit leads Jesus into the wilderness specifically to be tested (v.1). This is not an accident but a divine appointment.
The Temptation: Jesus fasted 40 days — echoing Israel's 40 years in the wilderness. Where Israel failed every test, Jesus passed every one. He is the true Israel, the obedient Son who does what Israel could not. Every temptation was answered with Scripture from Deuteronomy — the book Moses wrote to prepare Israel for the Promised Land.
Classification: Gospel — Narrative
Key Themes: temptation following baptism not by accident but by the Spirit's own leading, forty days of fasting deliberately echoing Israel's forty years in the wilderness, three temptations answered every time from the very book of Deuteronomy that once described Israel failing the same tests, two pairs of brothers called mid-workday with four words and no recorded discussion, straightway left their nets, and a costly detail left almost unremarked, Zebedee left sitting alone in the boat
The Temptation in the Wilderness (vv.1-11): He is led up of the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted, so the testing is commissioned rather than accidental. Forty days and forty nights of fasting sets the parallel with Israel's forty years and Moses' forty days. Three temptations, and all three are answered from Deuteronomy, from the same part of the law that describes the wilderness generation failing the same tests. Bread, answered with man shall not live by bread alone. The temple pinnacle, where scripture is quoted at him and he answers thou shalt not tempt the Lord thy God. Then all the kingdoms of the world for one act of worship, refused with get thee hence, Satan. Then angels came and ministered unto him.
He Dwelt in Capernaum (vv.12-14,16-17): The move is prompted by John's arrest, and Matthew notes the geography carefully because he wants Isaiah's prophecy to land on it: he leaves Nazareth and settles in Capernaum, on the border of Zebulun and Naphtali. Then the quotation about a great light shining on people that sat in darkness. And the ministry opens with exactly the words John was using, from that time Jesus began to preach, and to say, Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand. The message does not change when the messenger does.
Galilee of the Gentiles (v.15): Jesus based His ministry in Capernaum, in the region of Galilee — a mixed Jewish-Gentile area looked down upon by Jerusalem elites. This fulfilled Isaiah 9:1-2 and signaled the Gospel's reach to all people.
Fishers of Men (vv.18-22): Two pairs of brothers, called in the middle of a working day. Simon and Andrew are casting a net, James and John are mending theirs with their father in the boat. The call is four words and a promise, follow me, and I will make you fishers of men, and Matthew records no discussion either time. What he records is speed, they straightway left their nets, and immediately they left the ship and their father. The second detail is the costly one: Zebedee is left sitting in the boat.
All Galilee, and the Great Multitudes (vv.23-25): Three verses summarizing a whole campaign, and the summary has three verbs, teaching in their synagogues, preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing all manner of sickness. The list of who came is geographic and reaches outside Israel, from Galilee, Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea, and from beyond Jordan. The crowd assembled in these verses is the audience for the sermon that begins in the next, which is why Matthew puts the summary here rather than after it.

Map & Geography

  • Judean Wilderness: The barren, desolate region where Jesus was tempted for 40 days. Traditionally located west of Jericho.
  • Pinnacle of the Temple: The southeastern corner of the Temple complex in Jerusalem, overlooking the Kidron Valley — a dramatic drop.
  • Capernaum: A fishing town on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee. Jesus made this His ministry headquarters ("His own city," Matt. 9:1).
  • Galilee, Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea, beyond the Jordan (v.25): The regions from which crowds gathered, showing the breadth of Jesus' early fame.

Setting: About AD 28–29. Jesus has just been baptized in the Jordan, the Spirit has rested on him, and the Father has declared from heaven, "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased" (3:17). The very next word is "Then." Before he preaches a sermon or calls a disciple, Jesus is tested. Matthew has been tracing Israel's story in Jesus' life: God's son called out of Egypt (2:15), brought through the water (3:16), and now led into the wilderness, where Israel spent forty years and failed. After the temptation the chapter moves north to Galilee, where Jesus makes his home in Capernaum, begins to preach, calls his first four disciples and heals great crowds.

The Temptation of Jesus (vv1–11)

  • "Then Jesus was led up by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil" (v1). The same Spirit who came down on him at the Jordan now leads him into the desert. The testing is not an accident or a sign that something has gone wrong. It is part of God's plan. Mark says the Spirit "drove him out" (Mark 1:12), a strong word. "Led up" is literal: from the Jordan valley, far below sea level, up into the barren hills.
  • "Tempted." The Greek peirazō means both "test" and "tempt." God tests to prove and strengthen. The devil tempts to make someone fall. Both are happening here. God led Israel into the wilderness "to humble you, testing you to know what was in your heart" (Deut 8:2). James says God himself "tempts no one" to sin (Jas 1:13). God allows the tempter, and uses the test.
  • "The devil" (v1). Diabolos means "slanderer" or "accuser." He is also called "the tempter" (v3) and, in v10, "Satan," a Hebrew word meaning "adversary" (Job 1:6; Zech 3:1). He is a real personal being in the Gospels, not a figure of speech.
  • "After fasting forty days and forty nights, he was hungry" (v2). Forty recalls Israel's forty years in the wilderness (Deut 8:2), Moses' forty days on Sinai without food (Exod 34:28), and Elijah's forty-day journey to Horeb (1 Kings 19:8). "He was hungry" is Matthew's understatement. Jesus is truly human, and after six weeks without food he is at the edge of what a body can bear. The tempter comes at the moment of greatest weakness.
  • "If you are the Son of God" (v3). The Father has just called him "my beloved Son." The devil goes straight for that identity. The Greek form of "if" (ei with the indicative) assumes the statement for argument's sake: "If, as you are, the Son of God…" The devil is not mainly questioning whether Jesus is the Son. He is suggesting what kind of Son he should be: one who uses his power for himself.
  • "Command these stones to become loaves of bread" (v3). The flat limestone rocks of the Judean desert look a little like the round loaves of the time. Making bread is not wrong in itself. Jesus will later multiply bread for thousands (14:13–21). The temptation is to act on his own, apart from the Father, to meet his own need. Israel in the wilderness grumbled for bread and doubted God (Exod 16:2–3).
  • "Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God" (v4). Jesus quotes Deuteronomy 8:3. In context, Moses tells Israel that God let them hunger and then fed them with manna "that he might make you know that man does not live by bread alone." Israel had to learn to depend on God's word day by day. Jesus, the true Israel, chooses to trust his Father's word over his stomach. All three of his answers come from Deuteronomy 6–8, Moses' sermons to Israel at the end of their forty years.
  • "The devil took him to the holy city and set him on the pinnacle of the temple" (v5). "The holy city" is Jerusalem. "Pinnacle" (pterygion) means "little wing," probably a high corner of the temple courts. Josephus describes the southeast corner over the Kidron Valley as a dizzying drop. Whether this was a physical journey or a vision, the text does not say.
  • "If you are the Son of God, throw yourself down, for it is written" (v6). The devil has noticed that Jesus answers with Scripture, so now he quotes Scripture too. The Bible can be twisted, and the devil knows it well.
  • "He will command his angels concerning you" (v6). This is Psalm 91:11–12, a psalm of trust in God's protection. The devil leaves out a key phrase, "to guard you in all your ways." The promise is for those walking in God's ways, not for those who throw themselves into danger to force God's hand. The temptation is to prove God's care with a public spectacle, perhaps also to win the crowds in the temple courts with a miracle.
  • "Again it is written, 'You shall not put the Lord your God to the test'" (v7). This is Deuteronomy 6:16, which goes on: "as you tested him at Massah." At Massah Israel demanded water and asked, "Is the LORD among us or not?" (Exod 17:7). Testing God means demanding that he prove himself on our terms. Jesus will not do it. "Again it is written" is the right way to read Scripture: one passage alongside another, not one verse torn from its context.
  • "A very high mountain… all the kingdoms of the world and their glory" (v8). No mountain is high enough to see the whole world, so this was likely a vision. Luke says it was "in a moment of time" (Luke 4:5). Moses saw the promised land from Mount Nebo just before he died (Deut 34:1–4). Jesus is shown the whole world.
  • "All these I will give you, if you will fall down and worship me" (v9). Was this offer real? Jesus calls Satan "the ruler of this world" (John 12:31), and John says "the whole world lies in the power of the evil one" (1 John 5:19). But Satan's power is borrowed and temporary. The Father has already promised the nations to his Son: "Ask of me, and I will make the nations your heritage" (Ps 2:8). The offer is a shortcut to the crown without the cross. Jesus will receive "all authority in heaven and on earth" (28:18), but only after he suffers. Peter will repeat this temptation later, urging Jesus to avoid the cross, and Jesus answers him with the same words: "Get behind me, Satan!" (16:23).
  • "Be gone, Satan! For it is written, 'You shall worship the Lord your God and him only shall you serve'" (v10). This is Deuteronomy 6:13, from the passage right after the Shema ("Hear, O Israel," Deut 6:4). Israel bowed to the golden calf and later to the gods of Canaan. Jesus will bow only to his Father. Now, for the first time, he calls the tempter by name and orders him off.
  • The order of temptations. Luke puts the temple temptation last (Luke 4:1–13). Matthew's order ends with the mountain and Jesus' command "Be gone," and the mountain theme continues in Matthew (5:1; 17:1; 28:16). Luke's ends at the temple in Jerusalem, where his Gospel begins and ends. Each writer arranges the material to fit his story. The events are the same.
  • "Then the devil left him, and behold, angels came and were ministering to him" (v11). Luke adds that the devil left him "until an opportune time" (Luke 4:13). The battle would return, above all in Gethsemane and at the cross. The angels the devil said would catch him (v6) come anyway, in God's time and God's way. "Ministering" (diakoneō) is the word for serving at table. They probably brought him food, as an angel fed Elijah in the wilderness (1 Kings 19:5–8).
  • The second Adam and the true Israel. Adam was tested in a garden with every tree but one and fell. Jesus was tested in a desert with nothing and stood. Israel was tested forty years in the wilderness and failed. Jesus was tested forty days and did not. Hebrews says that because he "has been tempted as we are, yet without sin," he can help those who are tempted (Heb 2:18; 4:15). His victory was not a display of divine power. He fought as a man filled with the Spirit and armed with the word of God, and he won.
  • Commentators: Guzik notes that Satan omitted "in all your ways" from Psalm 91, and that the offer of the kingdoms was an offer to bypass the cross. He says the "if" is better understood as "since," a challenge to prove his Sonship rather than a denial of it. The grammar supports reading it as an assumption for argument's sake, though not necessarily as strong as "since." JFB says Jesus "parts with Satan on the rock of Scripture," calls the demand for worship "the sole but monstrous condition," and argues that Jesus naming Satan at the end confirms Matthew's order is the actual one. JFB takes the angels' ministry as bringing food. Henry warns that "great privileges, and special tokens of Divine favour, will not secure any from being tempted," and calls worldly glory "the most charming temptation to the unthinking and unwary."

Jesus Begins His Ministry (vv12–17)

  • "When he heard that John had been arrested, he withdrew into Galilee" (v12). Herod Antipas had arrested John for condemning his marriage to his brother's wife (14:3–4). John's Gospel shows that Jesus had already spent some time in Judea before this (John 1–3). Matthew begins Jesus' main public ministry here, in Galilee. "Withdrew" is the word used when Joseph took the family away from danger (2:14, 22). Jesus does not rush to confront Herod. His hour has not yet come.
  • "Leaving Nazareth he went and lived in Capernaum by the sea" (v13). Luke tells how Nazareth rejected him (Luke 4:16–30). Capernaum was a fishing town on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee, on a trade road, with a customs post (9:9) and a Roman garrison (8:5). Peter's house was there (8:14), and archaeologists have found what may be its remains, under a fifth-century church, a short walk from a later synagogue built on an earlier one's foundations. Capernaum becomes "his own city" (9:1).
  • "In the territory of Zebulun and Naphtali" (v13). These were the tribal lands around the western and northern shores of the lake. They were the first part of Israel conquered by Assyria, in 732 BC, when Tiglath-pileser III took the region and deported its people (2 Kings 15:29). They had been in darkness longest.
  • "The people dwelling in darkness have seen a great light" (vv15–16). This is Isaiah 9:1–2. Isaiah promised that God would "make glorious the way of the sea… Galilee of the nations," the very land first humbled. The next verses are some of the most famous in the Old Testament: "For to us a child is born… and his name shall be called Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God" (Isa 9:6–7). Matthew is saying that child has come, and the light has dawned exactly where Isaiah said it would.
  • "Galilee of the Gentiles" (v15). Galilee had a mixed population, with many Gentiles and Greek-speaking cities nearby. Judeans looked down on Galileans as unsophisticated and lax (John 7:52; Matt 26:73). It is fitting that the Messiah's light shines first in a place with so many outsiders. It anticipates the mission to "all nations" (28:19).
  • "From that time Jesus began to preach, saying, 'Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand'" (v17). "From that time" marks a new stage in Matthew (repeated in 16:21). Jesus' first message is word for word John's (3:2). The difference is that John announced the King was coming. Jesus is the King. "Preach" (kēryssō) means to announce publicly, as a herald proclaims a king's decree.
  • Commentators: JFB lists reasons for choosing Capernaum: its central position, mixed population and access to the lake, and calls Galilee "the frontier between the Holy Land and the external world." He argues that Isaiah's prophecy found no real fulfilment in Israel's history until Christ came. Henry observes that "Christ will not stay long where he is not welcome," pointing to his leaving Nazareth. Guzik notes the herald's sense of kēryssō.

Jesus Calls the First Disciples (vv18–22)

  • "While walking by the Sea of Galilee" (v18). The Sea of Galilee is a freshwater lake about 13 miles long and 7 miles wide, some 700 feet below sea level. Fishing was a major industry, and the salted fish of Magdala were exported widely.
  • "Simon (who is called Peter) and Andrew his brother, casting a net into the sea" (v18). The word (amphiblēstron) is a circular casting net, thrown by hand from the shore or shallow water. John's Gospel shows these brothers had already met Jesus through John the Baptist (John 1:35–42). This is not their first encounter but the call to leave everything.
  • "Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men" (v19). Rabbis did not usually call disciples. Students chose their teacher. Jesus calls them. "Follow me" means to come after a teacher as his disciple, learning his life as well as his words, as Elisha followed Elijah (1 Kings 19:19–21). "Fishers of men" may echo Jeremiah 16:16, where God says "I am sending for many fishers," though there the fishing is for judgment. Jesus turns their trade into a picture of gathering people into the kingdom. Notice "I will make you." He takes responsibility for training them.
  • "Immediately they left their nets and followed him" (v20). Matthew stresses the immediate response. They left their livelihood. Peter later says, "See, we have left everything and followed you" (19:27).
  • "James the son of Zebedee and John his brother, in the boat with Zebedee their father, mending their nets" (v21). "Mending" is katartizō, to repair or restore, the same word Paul uses for restoring a fallen believer (Gal 6:1) and, in a related form, for equipping the saints (Eph 4:12). Zebedee had a boat and hired servants (Mark 1:20), so the family was not poor. James was the first apostle martyred (Acts 12:2). John, tradition holds, lived longest and wrote the Gospel of John.
  • "They left the boat and their father and followed him" (v22). This is costlier than leaving nets. They leave family. Jesus will later say that loyalty to him must come before father and mother (10:37). He does not call everyone to leave home, but he calls everyone to put him first.
  • Luke's account. Luke 5:1–11 tells of a miraculous catch of fish before the call. Some think it is the same event told with more detail. JFB and others think Luke describes a separate, later moment, when the men who had already begun to follow left their boats for good. Either way, the call came to men at work.
  • Commentators: JFB calls it "raising them from a lower to a higher fishing," and argues this is a different event from John 1 and Luke 5, since here "the four are called separately, in pairs." He also notes that Zebedee's household was "in easy circumstances." Henry says "Christ chooses the foolish things of the world to confound the wise," and that his followers "must be ready to part with" whatever they have when he calls. Guzik holds up their immediate response as the model for every disciple.

Jesus Ministers to Great Crowds (vv23–25)

  • "Teaching in their synagogues and proclaiming the gospel of the kingdom and healing every disease" (v23). Three verbs sum up Jesus' ministry: teaching, preaching, healing. The synagogue was the local meeting place for prayer and Scripture reading on the Sabbath. A visiting teacher could be invited to read and speak (Luke 4:16–21; Acts 13:15). Matthew repeats this summary almost word for word in 9:35, framing chapters 5–9: first Jesus' words (the Sermon on the Mount, chs 5–7), then his works (the miracles, chs 8–9).
  • "The gospel of the kingdom" (v23). The good news is that God's reign has arrived in Jesus. The healings are signs of it. Isaiah had pictured the kingdom's coming this way: "the eyes of the blind shall be opened… the lame man shall leap like a deer" (Isa 35:5–6). Jesus points John the Baptist to exactly these signs (11:4–5).
  • "His fame spread throughout all Syria" (v24). Syria was the Roman province to the north, which officially included Judea and Galilee at this time. Matthew means the news spread far beyond Jewish territory.
  • "Those oppressed by demons, those having seizures, and paralytics" (v24). Matthew distinguishes between disease and demonic oppression. Not every illness is blamed on demons. "Having seizures" is literally "moonstruck" (selēniazomai), since people linked epilepsy with the phases of the moon. The English word "lunatic" comes from the same idea. Jesus "healed them," all of them.
  • "Great crowds followed him from Galilee and the Decapolis, and from Jerusalem and Judea, and from beyond the Jordan" (v25). The Decapolis ("ten cities") was a league of Greek cities, mostly east of the Jordan, with largely Gentile populations. "Beyond the Jordan" is Perea. Crowds come from every direction: Jewish heartland and Gentile borderlands alike. They are the audience for the Sermon on the Mount, which begins in the next verse (5:1).
  • Commentators: JFB calls the healings "at once His credentials and illustrations" of the gospel he preached. Henry draws a spiritual lesson: "Sin is the sickness, disease, and torment of the soul: Christ came to take away sin, and so to heal the soul." Guzik notes that Jesus made preaching the priority, with healing serving it, and surveys the reasons demonic activity seems so concentrated in the Gospels.

Pointing to Christ

Where Adam fell in a garden and Israel failed in the wilderness, Jesus stood firm, hungry and alone, armed only with the Spirit and the word of God. He refused bread on his own terms, refused to test his Father, and refused a crown without a cross. Then he walked into the darkest corner of Israel and the light dawned. He called ordinary fishermen to follow him and healed every disease. The Son who would not bow to Satan is the King who will one day receive every kingdom, rightly, from his Father's hand.

Reflection

  • Led Into the Test (v.1) — The Spirit deliberately leads Jesus into the wilderness to be tempted, not to harm Him but to prove Him. Can you identify a wilderness season God used to deepen your character?
  • Answering Temptation With the Word (v.4) — Though genuinely hungry and able to turn stones to bread, Jesus refuses to bypass trust in God and answers with Scripture instead. What specific verses are you memorizing to fight the specific temptations you face?
  • Scripture Misused (v.6) — Satan himself quotes Scripture to tempt Jesus, showing that the Bible can be twisted to justify almost anything. How do you guard against using Scripture to rationalize what you want rather than to discover what God wants?
  • A Shortcut Without the Cross (vv.9-10) — Satan offers Jesus the kingdoms of the world without suffering, a shortcut to something already His by right. Where in your life are you being offered a shortcut that bypasses God's path?
  • Immediate, Costly Obedience (vv.19-22) — Peter, Andrew, James, and John leave their nets and their father the moment Jesus calls them, with no negotiation or delay. What nets are you still holding onto that keep you from following more fully?
  • Fame Through Compassion (vv.23-24) — Jesus' reputation spreads through the region because of His healing ministry, before He has even preached the Sermon on the Mount. How does meeting people's practical needs open doors for the Gospel in your own life?
