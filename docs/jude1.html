1Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and a brother of James, To those who are called, loved by God the Father, and kept in Jesus Christ:

2Mercy, peace, and love be multiplied to you.

3Beloved, although I made every effort to write to you about the salvation we share, I felt it necessary to write and urge you to contend earnestly for the faith entrusted once for all to the saints.

4For certain men have crept in among you unnoticed—ungodly ones who were designated long ago for condemnation. They turn the grace of our God into a license for immorality, and they deny our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ.

5Although you are fully aware of this, I want to remind you that after Jesus had delivered His people out of the land of Egypt, He destroyed those who did not believe.

6And the angels who did not stay within their own domain but abandoned their proper dwelling—these He has kept in eternal chains under darkness, bound for judgment on that great day.

7In like manner, Sodom and Gomorrah and the cities around them, who indulged in sexual immorality and pursued strange flesh, are on display as an example of those who sustain the punishment of eternal fire.

8Yet in the same way these dreamers defile their bodies, reject authority, and slander glorious beings.

9But even the archangel Michael, when he disputed with the devil over the body of Moses, did not presume to bring a slanderous charge against him, but said, “The Lord rebuke you!”

10These men, however, slander what they do not understand, and like irrational animals, they will be destroyed by the things they do instinctively.

11Woe to them! They have traveled the path of Cain; they have rushed for profit into the error of Balaam; they have perished in Korah’s rebellion.

12These men are hidden reefs in your love feasts, shamelessly feasting with you but shepherding only themselves. They are clouds without water, carried along by the wind; fruitless trees in autumn, twice dead after being uprooted.

13They are wild waves of the sea, foaming up their own shame; wandering stars, for whom blackest darkness has been reserved forever.

14Enoch, the seventh from Adam, also prophesied about them: “Behold, the Lord is coming with myriads of His holy ones

15to execute judgment on everyone, and to convict all the ungodly of every ungodly act of wickedness and every harsh word spoken against Him by ungodly sinners.”

16These men are discontented grumblers, following after their own lusts; their mouths spew arrogance; they flatter others for their own advantage.

17But you, beloved, remember what was foretold by the apostles of our Lord Jesus Christ

18when they said to you, “In the last times there will be scoffers who will follow after their own ungodly desires.”

19These are the ones who cause divisions, who are worldly and devoid of the Spirit.

20But you, beloved, by building yourselves up in your most holy faith and praying in the Holy Spirit,

21keep yourselves in the love of God as you await the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ to bring you eternal life.

22And indeed, have mercy on those who doubt;

23save others by snatching them from the fire; and to still others show mercy tempered with fear, hating even the clothing stained by the flesh.

24Now to Him who is able to keep you from stumbling and to present you unblemished in His glorious presence, with great joy—

25to the only God our Savior be glory, majesty, dominion, and authority through Jesus Christ our Lord before all time, and now, and for all eternity. Amen.

Summary

Jude, writing as a brother of James, greets his readers and explains he originally meant to write about their shared salvation but instead felt compelled to urge them to contend earnestly for the faith, since ungodly people have secretly slipped in among them (vv. 1-4). He recalls past examples of God's judgment on unbelief and rebellion — Israel in the exodus, the angels who abandoned their proper place, and Sodom and Gomorrah — as warnings, then likens the false teachers to Cain, Balaam, and Korah, describing them as waterless clouds and fruitless trees (vv. 5-13). He cites Enoch's prophecy of the Lord's coming judgment on the ungodly and reminds readers that the apostles predicted such scoffers would arise (vv. 14-19). Believers are called to build themselves up in the faith, pray in the Spirit, and show mercy to those who waver while showing care for others (vv. 20-23). The letter closes with a doxology praising God, who alone is able to keep believers from stumbling and present them blameless before His glory (vv. 24-25).

Authorship & Background

Author: Jude (v.1) — "the servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James." This identifies him as Jude (Judas), the half-brother of Jesus (Matt 13:55, Mark 6:3) and brother of James (the author of James and leader of the Jerusalem church). Like James, Jude humbly identifies himself as Christ's servant rather than His brother.
Written approximately AD 65-80. One of the shortest books in the NT (25 verses).
Recipient: "Them that are sanctified by God the Father, and preserved in Jesus Christ, and called" (v.1) — believers generally.
Purpose: Jude intended to write about "the common salvation" but was compelled to write urgently about false teachers who had infiltrated the church (v.3). He exhorts believers to "earnestly contend for the faith" (v.3). The letter closely parallels 2 Peter 2.
Theme: Contending for the faith against false teachers. Warning, examples of judgment, and encouragement to persevere.
Classification: Epistle — General
Key Themes: Contending for the faith once delivered, false teachers who infiltrate rather than announce themselves, judgment illustrated from history, and believers kept by God while keeping themselves
Greeting and the Change of Subject (vv.1-4): Jude introduces himself as the servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James, which identifies him as a half-brother of Jesus who declines to say so. He intended to write about “the common salvation” and changed course because circumstances demanded it: certain men have crept in unnoticed, turning grace into license and denying the Lord. Hence the letter's charge to “earnestly contend for the faith which was once delivered unto the saints” — a faith with fixed content, handed over rather than developed.
Three Examples of Judgment (vv.5-7): Jude reaches for three cases his readers already knew: Israel saved out of Egypt and then destroyed in the wilderness for unbelief, angels who abandoned their proper place, and Sodom and Gomorrah. The first is the sharpest — being delivered is not the same as arriving. Together they establish that privileged position does not exempt anyone from judgment, which is precisely what the false teachers were claiming.
The Character of the Intruders (vv.8-16): The description is unsparing: they despise authority, speak of what they do not understand, and follow instinct rather than the Spirit. Jude sets Michael's restraint in disputing over the body of Moses against their recklessness, then piles up images — clouds without water, trees without fruit, wandering stars. Cain, Balaam and Korah supply the pattern of envy, greed and rebellion. Verses 14-15 quote a prophecy attributed to Enoch, which Jude uses as a recognizable witness to coming judgment without thereby settling anything about that book's status.
Building Yourselves Up (vv.17-23): The tone turns from warning to instruction, and it is practical: remember what the apostles said, build yourselves up in the faith, pray in the Holy Spirit, keep yourselves in the love of God, wait for mercy. Then the harder part — how to treat those already affected. Jude distinguishes cases: have compassion on some, and save others “with fear, pulling them out of the fire”, hating the garment spotted by the flesh. Rescue is expected, and so is care about being pulled in while attempting it.
Doxology: Able to Keep You (vv.24-25): The letter that began by describing believers as “preserved in Jesus Christ” ends by ascribing that preservation to God: able to keep you from falling and to present you faultless. Set beside the command in v.21 to keep yourselves in the love of God, the two are held together rather than played off — the same tension the whole letter maintains. It is one of the fullest doxologies in the New Testament, and after twenty-three verses of warning it lands on security rather than fear.

Deep Dive

Setting: Jude is one of the shortest books in the Bible. The author is "Jude… brother of James," which most likely makes him a younger half-brother of Jesus, the Judas named in Mark 6:3 and Matthew 13:55. Like James, he did not believe in Jesus during his ministry (John 7:5). He became a believer after the resurrection, when the brothers were praying with the disciples (Acts 1:14). The date is uncertain, probably AD 65–80. The readers are likely Jewish Christians, since Jude draws so heavily on Old Testament history and Jewish tradition. Jude shares a great deal of material with 2 Peter 2, and most think one used the other. The letter is an urgent alarm about false teachers already inside the church. It ends with one of the most beloved blessings in Scripture.

Greeting (vv1–2)

  • "Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James" (v1). Jude could have called himself "brother of the Lord." He chooses "servant" (doulos, bondservant) instead. The brother who once doubted now counts it his highest title to belong to Jesus.
  • "Called, beloved in God the Father and kept for Jesus Christ" (v1). These are three marks of every believer: God's call, God's love, and God's keeping. "Kept" (tēreō) is the letter's key word. It returns in v6 (angels "kept" for judgment), v13 (darkness "reserved"), v21 ("keep yourselves") and v24 ("able to keep you"). The letter opens and closes with God keeping his people.
  • "May mercy, peace, and love be multiplied to you" (v2). Jude adds "love" to the usual greeting. Mercy, peace and love are exactly what the false teachers lacked.
  • Commentators: Guzik reads Jude's choice of "servant" over "brother" as humility, and notes that Jude, like James, believed only after the resurrection. Henry distinguishes this Judas from Judas Iscariot. He notes that "the same names may be common to the best and worst persons." He adds that "God takes great care of the good name of his sincere and useful servants." Clement of Alexandria, cited by JFB, said Jude through "reverential awe" didn't call himself the Lord's brother.

Contend for the Faith (vv3–4)

  • "I was very eager to write to you about our common salvation" (v3). Jude had planned a warm letter about the salvation all believers share. Circumstances forced a different one.
  • "Contend for the faith that was once for all delivered to the saints" (v3). "Contend" (epagōnizomai) is an athletic or wrestling word: strain, fight hard. "The faith" here is not personal trust. It is the body of truth the apostles taught, the gospel itself. "Once for all" (hapax) means it was delivered completely and finally. It is not up for revision or addition. "Delivered" (paradidōmi) is the word for handing down a tradition (1 Cor 15:3).
  • "Certain people have crept in unnoticed" (v4). The verb (pareisdyō) means to slip in sideways, through a side door. The threat isn't outside persecution. It's people inside the church, sitting at the table.
  • "Who long ago were designated for this condemnation" (v4). This means their judgment was written about long ago in Scripture and prophecy. Jude is about to show where.
  • "Who pervert the grace of our God into sensuality" (v4). "Sensuality" (aselgeia) means shameless indulgence. They taught that grace means freedom to sin, the very thing Paul rejected: "Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound? By no means!" (Rom 6:1–2). Grace that leaves people unchanged isn't grace (Titus 2:11–12).
  • "And deny our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ" (v4). They may not have denied him in words, but they did in practice. "Master" (despotēs) is a strong word for absolute owner. Refusing his rule is denying him.
  • Commentators: Guzik defines "the faith" as the core gospel truths shared by all true Christians, a revelation complete and not to be replaced. He sees two dangers in the intruders: moral corruption and doctrinal denial of Christ. JFB agrees: "No other faith or revelation is to supersede it." Henry insists that what we defend must be the real Christian faith, "not the discriminating badges of this or the other party." He calls the gospel a "common salvation," offered freely to all: "God's decrees are dark, his covenants are plain."

Judgment on False Teachers (vv5–16)

  • "Jesus, who saved a people out of the land of Egypt, afterward destroyed those who did not believe" (v5). This is the first of three examples of judgment. Most early manuscripts read "Jesus" here. Others read "the Lord," and the ESV follows "Jesus." If original, it is a striking statement that the pre-existent Son was active in the Exodus (compare 1 Cor 10:4, 9). The warning is that being rescued from Egypt didn't guarantee entering the promised land. Belonging to God's people outwardly doesn't save anyone who refuses to trust him (Num 14; Heb 3:16–19).
  • "The angels who did not stay within their own position of authority… kept in eternal chains" (v6). This is the second example. Two main readings: - Many, following ancient Jewish tradition and 1 Enoch, link this to Genesis 6:1–4, where "sons of God" took human wives. The phrase "likewise" in v7 points that way. - Others take it as the original fall of Satan and his angels, out of pride. - Either way, angels who abandoned their God-given place are held for judgment. Rank and privilege don't exempt anyone.
  • "Sodom and Gomorrah… indulged in sexual immorality and pursued unnatural desire" (v7). This is the third example (Gen 19). "Unnatural desire" is literally "other flesh" (sarkos heteras). Most read this as homosexual acts; some read it as desire for angelic beings, since the visitors were angels. Their ruined cities were "an example" anyone could see.
  • "Relying on their dreams, defile the flesh, reject authority, and blaspheme the glorious ones" (v8). They justify themselves by claimed visions. "The glorious ones" (doxai) are probably angelic beings, perhaps even fallen ones. They speak lightly and mockingly about spiritual powers they don't understand.
  • "The archangel Michael… disputing about the body of Moses… said, 'The Lord rebuke you'" (v9). Michael is the leading angel of Daniel 10:13, 21 and 12:1, and of Revelation 12:7. The dispute over Moses' burial (Deut 34:5–6) isn't recorded in the Old Testament. Early church writers say it came from a Jewish work, the Assumption (or Testament) of Moses, which survives only in fragments. Michael's words echo Zechariah 3:2. The point is about restraint. Even the mightiest angel didn't bring a railing accusation against the devil. He left judgment to God. If he didn't presume, how much less should these men?
  • "Like unreasoning animals" (v10). They mock the spiritual world they can't see. What they do understand, their bodily instincts, is exactly what destroys them.
  • "The way of Cain… Balaam's error… Korah's rebellion" (v11). Three more Old Testament figures: - Cain (Gen 4) represents self-made religion and hatred of the righteous (1 John 3:12). - Balaam (Num 22–25; 31:16) represents ministry for profit. He was paid to curse Israel and then taught Moab to seduce them. - Korah (Num 16) represents rebellion against God's appointed leaders. The earth swallowed him. - The verbs move from walking to running headlong to perishing.
  • "Hidden reefs at your love feasts" (v12). The "love feasts" (agapai) were fellowship meals, often with the Lord's Supper (1 Cor 11:20–22). "Hidden reefs" (spilades) are rocks just under the surface that wreck ships. The KJV's "spots" follows a similar word (spiloi) used in 2 Peter 2:13. Both senses fit: they are a stain and a danger. "Shepherds feeding themselves" echoes Ezekiel 34:2.
  • Four pictures from nature (vv12–13): - Waterless clouds promise rain and give nothing. - Fruitless trees in late autumn, twice dead, uprooted. No fruit at harvest time, dead in root and branch, pulled up entirely. - Wild waves… casting up the foam of their own shame (Isa 57:20): restless, leaving filth on the shore. - Wandering stars (planētai, the root of "planets"): heavenly bodies that seemed to drift off course, useless for navigation.
  • "Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied" (vv14–15). Jude quotes almost word for word from 1 Enoch 1:9, a Jewish book written in the centuries before Christ and widely read in Jesus' day. Fragments were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls. Quoting it doesn't make 1 Enoch Scripture, any more than Paul's quoting Greek poets endorses their works (Acts 17:28; Titus 1:12). It means this particular statement is true. Notice the fourfold "ungodly" in v15. It's the key word of the whole letter. "Ten thousands of his holy ones" echoes Deuteronomy 33:2 and Zechariah 14:5.
  • "Grumblers, malcontents… loud-mouthed boasters, showing favoritism to gain advantage" (v16). Grumbling was the sin of the wilderness generation (v5; 1 Cor 10:10). They flatter powerful people for what they can get.
  • Commentators: Guzik favors the Genesis 6 reading of v6, linking it with the "strange flesh" of v7. He cautions that speculating on the details is unhelpful. On v9 he admits Jude's source is uncertain. Early teachers linked it to the Assumption of Moses, and it may have been a special revelation. His main point is Michael's restraint: believers shouldn't mock spiritual enemies. On v14 he holds that quoting Enoch affirms only the words Jude cites, not the whole book. JFB notes the oldest manuscripts read "Jesus" in v5. It reads the angels' "chains" as a figure for their restricted state and certain doom, and traces their sin to ambition. It suggests a tradition like the Assumption of Moses lies behind v9, but stresses that only the inspired written Word can be relied on. It reads the word in v12 as "rocks," pictures of shipwreck. Henry admits interpreters are "at a loss" over the body of Moses. He lists two views of the Enoch prophecy: either it came down by tradition, or Jude was directly inspired to know it.

A Call to Persevere (vv17–23)

  • "Remember, beloved, the predictions of the apostles" (vv17–18). The apostles warned this would happen (Acts 20:29–30; 1 Tim 4:1; 2 Tim 3:1–5; 2 Pet 3:3). The quote is very close to 2 Peter 3:3. False teachers in the church aren't a sign that God has lost control. They were foretold.
  • "It is these who cause divisions, worldly people, devoid of the Spirit" (v19). "Worldly" is psychikos, merely natural, unspiritual (1 Cor 2:14). They probably claimed to be the truly spiritual ones. Jude says they don't have the Spirit at all.
  • "Building yourselves up… praying in the Holy Spirit… keep yourselves in the love of God… waiting for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ" (vv20–21). There is one command, "keep yourselves," surrounded by three practices: - building on the apostolic faith; - praying in the Spirit, in dependence and in line with his will (Rom 8:26–27; Eph 6:18); - waiting eagerly for Christ's return. - The Trinity is all here: Holy Spirit, God the Father's love, Jesus' mercy. God keeps us (vv1, 24), and we keep ourselves in his love (John 15:9–10). Those two truths sit side by side without contradiction (Phil 2:12–13).
  • "Have mercy on those who doubt; save others by snatching them out of the fire; to others show mercy with fear" (vv22–23). The manuscripts differ here. Some have three groups and some two, but the pastoral sense is clear. Waverers need patient mercy. Those close to ruin need urgent rescue, "a brand plucked from the fire" (Zech 3:2; Amos 4:11). Others still need mercy, but with caution, "hating even the garment stained by the flesh" (chitōn, an undergarment). Love the person, but don't get careless about the sin that clings to them. Contending for the faith (v3) never means abandoning people.
  • Commentators: Guzik explains keeping ourselves in God's love as staying in harmony with a love God already has for us, not earning it. On vv22–23 he urges balance: gentle compassion for some, firmer confrontation for others, always with fear and humility rather than pride. JFB sees three groups with three responses, and reads the stained garment as "avoiding the most remote contact with sin." Henry says "sensualists are the worst separatists." He calls a living hope of eternal life an armor against sin: "a lively faith of the blessed hope will help us to mortify our cursed lusts."

Doxology (vv24–25)

  • "Now to him who is able to keep you from stumbling" (v24). After all the warnings, Jude ends with assurance. The God who keeps fallen angels for judgment keeps his own people from falling. "Stumbling" (aptaistos) pictures a sure-footed horse that doesn't trip on rough ground.
  • "And to present you blameless before the presence of his glory with great joy" (v24). "Blameless" (amōmos) is sacrificial language for a lamb without defect. The joy may be ours, or God's, or both (Zeph 3:17; Heb 12:2).
  • "To the only God, our Savior, through Jesus Christ our Lord, be glory, majesty, dominion, and authority, before all time and now and forever" (v25). These four praises span all of time: before the ages, now, and forever. "Through Jesus Christ" is the only way anyone gives God true glory. The doxology is still used to close worship services today.
  • Commentators: Guzik sees a partnership: God keeps believers, and believers work out their salvation. He notes that we declare God's glory rather than add to it. JFB reads "keep you from falling" as guarding believers from stumbling, and "the presence of his glory" as Christ's coming revelation. It notes that Christ shares the Father's sovereignty. The Tyndale notes say the doxology fit the readers exactly. Shaken by false teachers, they needed to remember a God strong enough to hold them.

Pointing to Christ

Jude moves between two kinds of keeping. God keeps the ungodly for judgment, and he keeps his beloved for joy. The difference is Jesus Christ, "our only Master and Lord," whom the false teachers denied and whose mercy believers wait for. Contending for the faith is not a cold fight about ideas. It means defending the gospel of the One who alone can present sinners "blameless before the presence of his glory with great joy." A brother who once doubted Jesus ends his letter by giving him all glory forever.

Reflection

  • Contending for the Faith (v.3) — Jude set aside his plan to write about their shared salvation and urged believers instead to earnestly contend for the faith entrusted to them. Are you actively engaging and defending what you believe, or passively assuming it will take care of itself?
  • Grace Turned Into a License (v.4) — False teachers had crept in who twisted God's grace into an excuse for sin and denied Christ as Lord. Have you ever treated grace as cover for compromise instead of a reason for holiness?
  • Restraint in the Face of Evil (v.9) — Even the archangel Michael, disputing with the devil himself, didn't presume to condemn him but appealed to the Lord's authority instead. Where do you need to trade your own outrage for humble reliance on God to deal with what's beyond you?
  • Building Yourselves Up (vv.20-21) — Jude gives a fourfold rhythm for spiritual survival: build on the faith, pray in the Spirit, keep yourselves in God's love, and wait expectantly for His mercy. Which of these four is weakest in your life right now?
  • Compassion Mixed With Caution (vv.22-23) — Jude calls believers to show mercy to those who doubt and to rescue others from danger, while being careful not to be pulled into their sin themselves. How do you stay engaged with struggling people without being compromised by it?
  • Kept From Falling (vv.24-25) — The letter closes by praising the One who is able to keep you from stumbling and present you faultless with great joy. Does your confidence rest in your own grip on faith, or in God's power to hold onto you?
