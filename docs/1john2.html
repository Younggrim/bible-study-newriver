1My little children, I am writing these things to you so that you will not sin. But if anyone does sin, we have an advocate before the Father—Jesus Christ, the Righteous One.

2He Himself is the atoning sacrifice for our sins, and not only for ours but also for the sins of the whole world.

3By this we can be sure that we have come to know Him: if we keep His commandments.

4If anyone says, “I know Him,” but does not keep His commandments, he is a liar, and the truth is not in him.

5But if anyone keeps His word, the love of God has been truly perfected in him. By this we know that we are in Him:

6Whoever claims to abide in Him must walk as Jesus walked.

7Beloved, I am not writing to you a new commandment, but an old one, which you have had from the beginning. This commandment is the message you have heard.

8Then again, I am also writing to you a new commandment, which is true in Him and also in you. For the darkness is fading and the true light is already shining.

9If anyone claims to be in the light but hates his brother, he is still in the darkness.

10Whoever loves his brother remains in the light, and there is no cause of stumbling in him.

11But whoever hates his brother is in the darkness and walks in the darkness. He does not know where he is going, because the darkness has blinded his eyes.

12I am writing to you, little children, because your sins have been forgiven through His name.

13I am writing to you, fathers, because you know Him who is from the beginning. I am writing to you, young men, because you have overcome the evil one. I have written to you, children, because you know the Father.

14I have written to you, fathers, because you know Him who is from the beginning. I have written to you, young men, because you are strong, and the word of God abides in you, and you have overcome the evil one.

15Do not love the world or anything in the world. If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him.

16For all that is in the world—the desires of the flesh, the desires of the eyes, and the pride of life—is not from the Father but from the world.

17The world is passing away, along with its desires; but whoever does the will of God remains forever.

18Children, it is the last hour; and just as you have heard that the antichrist is coming, so now many antichrists have appeared. This is how we know it is the last hour.

19They went out from us, but they did not belong to us. For if they had belonged to us, they would have remained with us. But their departure made it clear that none of them belonged to us.

20You, however, have an anointing from the Holy One, and all of you know the truth.

21I have not written to you because you lack knowledge of the truth, but because you have it, and because no lie comes from the truth.

22Who is the liar, if it is not the one who denies that Jesus is the Christ? This is the antichrist, who denies the Father and the Son.

23Whoever denies the Son does not have the Father, but whoever confesses the Son has the Father as well.

24As for you, let what you have heard from the beginning remain in you. If it does, you will also remain in the Son and in the Father.

25And this is the promise that He Himself made to us: eternal life.

26I have written these things to you about those who are trying to deceive you.

27And as for you, the anointing you received from Him remains in you, and you do not need anyone to teach you. But just as His true and genuine anointing teaches you about all things, so remain in Him as you have been taught.

28And now, little children, remain in Christ, so that when He appears, we may be confident and unashamed before Him at His coming.

29If you know that He is righteous, you also know that everyone who practices righteousness has been born of Him.

Summary

John explains that when believers sin, Jesus Christ the righteous serves as advocate before the Father and as the atoning sacrifice for the sins of the whole world (vv. 1-2). Genuine knowledge of God is proven by keeping his commands and walking as Christ walked (vv. 3-6), and this is expressed above all in an old-yet-new commandment to love one another, since hating a brother leaves a person still in darkness (vv. 7-11). John addresses children, fathers, and young men in turn, reminding each of what they possess in Christ (vv. 12-14), then warns against loving the world — the desires of the flesh and the eyes, and pride in possessions — since the world is passing away while obedience to God lasts forever (vv. 15-17). Calling this the last hour, he warns that many antichrists have appeared, people who left the fellowship because they never truly belonged, and that denying the Father and the Son marks someone as antichrist (vv. 18-23). He urges his readers to hold onto what they heard from the beginning, trusting the Spirit's anointing to teach them, and to abide in Christ so they have confidence at his coming, since everyone who practices righteousness has been born of him (vv. 24-29).

Authorship & Background

Author: The Apostle John (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Historical Context: Chapter 2 addresses Christ as our advocate (vv.1-2), the test of obedience (vv.3-6), the command to love (vv.7-11), assurance for different maturity levels (vv.12-14), the warning against worldliness (vv.15- 17), and the threat of antichrists (vv.18-29). John continues providing "tests of life" — how to know you truly know God.
Classification: Epistle — General
Key Themes: An advocate supplied for the sin John has just written to prevent, obedience treated as proof of knowledge rather than a separate requirement, an old commandment restated as new because it is now true in Christ and in the reader, three groups addressed by what is already true of them before anything is asked of them, and antichrists whose departure is offered as evidence they never belonged
Christ Our Advocate (vv.1-2): John writes so they won't sin — but when they do, Christ is their advocate (defense attorney) with the Father (v.1). He is the propitiation (satisfaction of God's wrath) for our sins and the whole world's (v.2).
The Test of Obedience (vv.3-6): "We know that we know him, if we keep his commandments" (v.3). Claiming to know God while disobeying is lying (v.4). Walk as Christ walked (v.6).
The Command to Love (vv.7-11): The old/new commandment: love one another (vv.7-8). Claiming to be in the light while hating a brother = still in darkness (v.9). Loving = abiding in light (v.10). Hating = blinded by darkness (v.11).
Little Children, Fathers, Young Men (vv.12-14): Three groups addressed twice over, and each address gives a reason rather than a command. The little children because their sins are forgiven and because they know the Father, the fathers because they have known him that is from the beginning, the young men because they are strong and have overcome the wicked one. Nothing is asked in these verses. John is telling his readers what is already true of them before he tells them what to avoid.
Warning Against Worldliness (vv.15-17): "Love not the world" (v.15). Three categories of worldliness: lust of the flesh, lust of the eyes, pride of life (v.16). The world passes away; doing God's will abides forever (v.17).
Antichrists (vv.18-29): Many antichrists have come (v.18). They left the church — proving they were never truly part of it (v.19). The antichrist denies that Jesus is the Christ (v.22). Believers have the "anointing" (the Spirit) who teaches truth (vv.20, 27). Abide in Him (v.28).

Setting: The apostle John, writing late in the first century, probably from Ephesus, to churches in Asia that had just been split. A group had walked out (v19) and was still trying to draw others after them (v26). They claimed to know God and live in the light while ignoring his commands, treating sin lightly and denying that Jesus is the Christ come in the flesh. Chapter 1 answered two of their slogans ("we have fellowship with him," "we have no sin") and promised forgiveness to those who confess. Chapter 2 keeps going: it names Christ as advocate for believers who sin, then gives tests of real faith (obedience, love, and holding to the truth about Jesus), with a warm word to every stage of the Christian life in the middle.

Christ Our Advocate (vv1–6)

  • "My little children" (v1). Teknia, a tender diminutive, "dear children." John uses it seven times in this letter (2:1, 12, 28; 3:7, 18; 4:4; 5:21) and in the Gospels only Jesus uses it, in the upper room (John 13:33). An old pastor writes like a father.
  • "So that you may not sin. But if anyone does sin" (v1). John holds two things together that people tend to pull apart. Chapter 1 could be twisted into "everyone sins, so it doesn't matter." John says the aim is not to sin at all. But he does not leave the one who fails without hope. The verb "does sin" is a single act, not a lifestyle.
  • "We have an advocate with the Father" (v1). "Advocate" is paraklētos, someone called alongside to help, often a legal helper who speaks for a person in court. In the Gospel, Jesus calls the Holy Spirit "another Helper" (paraklētos, John 14:16), which implies Jesus himself was the first. The Spirit is our advocate on earth. Here Jesus is our advocate "with the Father," pleading in heaven (Rom 8:34; Heb 7:25). Notice the "we": John includes himself.
  • "Jesus Christ the righteous" (v1). The advocate's case does not rest on the defendant's innocence but on his own. He is righteous, and his sacrifice is the ground of his plea.
  • "He is the propitiation for our sins" (v2). Hilasmos appears only here and in 4:10. The lexicon gives "a means of appeasing, propitiation," and in the Greek Old Testament it is linked with the Day of Atonement and the sin offering (Lev 25:9; Ezek 44:27). Propitiation means turning away God's just anger against sin by a sacrifice that satisfies it. Some translations prefer "expiation" (wiping out sin) or "atoning sacrifice," and scholars have debated the word. But John says Jesus is the propitiation, not just that he made one. The judge's own Son is both the advocate and the sacrifice, and in 4:10 God himself sends him out of love. This is not an angry God being bought off by a kind Son.
  • "Not for ours only but also for the sins of the whole world" (v2). Christians read this phrase in different ways. Many Arminian and Lutheran readers take it to mean Christ's death was offered for every person, though only believers receive its benefit. Many Reformed readers take "the whole world" to mean people of every nation, not just Jewish believers or John's own circle (compare John 11:51–52; Rev 5:9). All agree on what John is excluding: there is no other sacrifice, and no one is beyond the reach of this one.
  • "By this we know that we have come to know him" (v3). The false teachers claimed special knowledge. John gives a plain test: "if we keep his commandments." Knowing God in the Bible is never only information. It is relationship that shapes conduct (Jer 22:16; John 17:3).
  • "Whoever says 'I know him'… is a liar" (v4). This is the fourth "says" claim since 1:6. The pattern is the same each time: what someone says against how they live.
  • "In him truly the love of God is perfected" (v5). "Perfected" (teleioō) means brought to its goal, made complete, not flawless. "The love of God" could mean God's love for us, our love for God, or God's kind of love. John often leaves such phrases open, and here all three overlap: God's love reaches its purpose when it produces obedience.
  • "Walk in the same way in which he walked" (v6). "He" in the Greek is ekeinos, "that one," John's usual way of pointing to Jesus. Abiding in Christ is not a feeling but a way of walking, the way Jesus actually lived.
  • Commentators: Henry explains the courtroom picture: "The proper office and business of an advocate is with the judge; with him he pleads the client's cause." Guzik adds that this advocate does not deny the client's guilt but pleads his own sacrifice. JFB notes that "paraclete" is "the same term as is applied to the Holy Ghost." On propitiation, JFB calls it a sacrifice "provided by the Father's love, removing the estrangement, and appeasing the righteous wrath," and Guzik likewise stresses that God himself supplies what turns his wrath away. They differ on "the whole world." Guzik argues that the sacrifice is sufficient for all people, though not everyone is forgiven. Henry calls Christ "the universal atonement and propitiation for all that are saved." Guzik adds that walking as Jesus walked means sharing his daily fellowship with the Father, not copying his miracles.

The New Commandment (vv7–14)

  • "No new commandment, but an old commandment" (v7). The command is to love (vv9–10). It is "old" because the readers heard it "from the beginning," at their conversion, and because it goes back to Leviticus 19:18. The false teachers were offering novelties. John points back to what they already had.
  • "At the same time, it is a new commandment" (v8). Jesus called it "a new commandment" in the upper room: "just as I have loved you, you also are to love one another" (John 13:34). What made it new was the standard and the power: Jesus' own self-giving love, now living "in him and in you." The word for new is kainos, new in kind and freshness, not merely recent.
  • "The darkness is passing away and the true light is already shining" (v8). John sees history turning. A new age has dawned with Christ, "the true light" (John 1:9), and the old darkness is on its way out, though not gone yet. Believers live at dawn.
  • "Whoever says he is in the light and hates his brother" (v9). A fifth claim. "Brother" (adelphos) here means a fellow believer, male or female. In John's world there is no neutral ground between love and hate: to withhold love from a brother is, in his terms, to hate him (compare 3:15–17).
  • "In him there is no cause for stumbling" (v10). "Cause for stumbling" is skandalon, a trap or an obstacle in the path. The ESV footnote allows "in it," meaning in the light. Either way, the one who loves walks a clear road and does not trip others up.
  • "The darkness has blinded his eyes" (v11). Hatred does not just hide the way. Over time it takes away the ability to see. "Does not know where he is going" echoes Jesus in John 12:35.
  • A poem to the whole church (vv12–14). The ESV sets these verses as poetry. There are two sets of three: "little children," "fathers," "young men," and then "children," "fathers," "young men" again. Readers have taken this two ways. Some think the three names are stages of spiritual growth: new believers, mature believers, and those in the strength of the fight. Others, noting that "little children" is what John calls everyone (v1), think "children" means the whole church, and "fathers" and "young men" then divide it into older and younger. Either way, each group is told something already true of them, not something to achieve.
  • "Because your sins are forgiven for his name's sake" (v12). The first word to the church is forgiveness, given "for his name's sake," on account of who Jesus is, not who we are.
  • "You know him who is from the beginning" (v13). Mature believers are marked not by novelty but by deep knowledge of the eternal Christ (1:1).
  • "You have overcome the evil one… you are strong, and the word of God abides in you" (vv13–14). The young are fighters, and their strength is the word living in them. That is how Jesus overcame the devil in the wilderness (Matt 4:1–11). "Overcome" (nikaō) is a key word for John (5:4–5; Rev 2–3).
  • "I am writing… I write" (vv12–14). The tense shifts from "I am writing" (present) to "I write" (in Greek an aorist, "I wrote"). Some think John is referring back to an earlier letter or to the Gospel, but most take it as a stylistic shift in which the writer looks at his own letter from the readers' side. It is repetition for emphasis.
  • Commentators: JFB notes that the oldest manuscripts begin v7 with "Beloved," not "Brethren" as in the KJV, which suits a passage about love. Guzik calls love the third leg of a stool, beside obedience and sound doctrine. He also reads the two words for children differently: teknia (v12) stresses belonging and dependence, and paidia (v13) stresses immaturity and the need for teaching. Henry takes the groups as stages of growth: "The youngest sincere disciple is pardoned," and "young men are wont to glory in their strength," which John redirects to strength in the word. Guzik pictures the young men as front-line fighters with Scripture living in them.

Do Not Love the World (vv15–17)

  • "Do not love the world" (v15). This seems to clash with John 3:16, where "God so loved the world." John uses "world" (kosmos) in two ways. Sometimes it means the people God made and loves. Here it means human life organized without God and against him, under "the evil one" (5:19). God loves the world of people. Believers are not to love the world as a system of values.
  • Three desires (v16). "The desires of the flesh" are the cravings of fallen human nature, appetite and lust. "The desires of the eyes" are wanting what we see, the pull of appearance and possession. "The pride of life" uses bios, which means life in the sense of livelihood and goods (the ESV footnote offers "pride in possessions"): the swagger that comes from what we own and achieve. Readers have long noticed the parallel with Eve, who saw the fruit was "good for food… a delight to the eyes… desired to make one wise" (Gen 3:6), and with Jesus' three temptations (Luke 4:1–13). The tempter has not needed new tactics.
  • "The world is passing away… but whoever does the will of God abides forever" (v17). "Passing away" is the same verb as the darkness in v8. Loving the world is not only wrong but foolish. It is investing everything in something already on its way out.
  • Commentators: Guzik defines "the world" as the human system in rebellion against God, not the planet or its people. He warns that running off to a monastery does not solve the problem, ties the three desires to Genesis 3:6, and points to Lot as a man who drifted toward Sodom. Henry describes the lust of the flesh as "the humour and appetite of indulging fleshly pleasures."

Warning Concerning Antichrists (vv18–27)

  • "It is the last hour" (v18). The New Testament treats the whole stretch between Christ's first and second comings as "the last days" (Acts 2:17; Heb 1:2). John adds urgency: it is the final hour of this age. It was true then and is truer now.
  • "Antichrist is coming… many antichrists have come" (v18). John is the only New Testament writer who uses the word antichristos (2:18, 22; 4:3; 2 John 7). The prefix anti can mean "against" or "in place of," and an antichrist is both: a rival who opposes Christ by offering a substitute. The readers had been taught that a final opponent would come (compare 2 Thess 2:3–4; Rev 13). John does not deny that. He says the spirit of that figure is already at work in the false teachers. Christians differ on whether a single end-time antichrist should be expected. Most historic interpreters have expected one, while also seeing many forerunners in every age.
  • "They went out from us, but they were not of us" (v19). This is the clearest window into what had happened. The false teachers had been members of the church. Their leaving showed they had never truly belonged. John is not saying every person who changes churches is lost. He is speaking of people who left the apostles' teaching about Christ.
  • "You have been anointed by the Holy One" (v20). "Anointing" is chrisma, from the same root as Christos, "the Anointed One," and it stands in pointed contrast to anti-christos. The anointing is almost certainly the Holy Spirit, given to all believers (2 Cor 1:21–22; Acts 10:38). "The Holy One" may be the Father or Jesus (John 6:69).
  • "You all have knowledge" (v20). The ESV footnote notes that some manuscripts read "you know everything." The better reading, "you all know," makes John's point sharper. The false teachers claimed knowledge belonged to an elite. John says every believer has the Spirit and knows the truth.
  • "Who is the liar but he who denies that Jesus is the Christ?" (v22). The issue was not whether a figure called "the Christ" existed but whether the man Jesus is the Christ. Some, like Cerinthus (a teacher whom early church writers place in Ephesus in John's day), taught that a divine "Christ" came on the human Jesus at his baptism and left before the cross. John calls this denying both the Father and the Son, because the Father is known only through the Son (John 14:6–9).
  • "Whoever confesses the Son has the Father also" (v23). Older translations like the KJV printed this second half in italics, because the Textus Receptus they followed lacked it. The great early manuscripts include it, and modern translations print it as part of the text.
  • "Let what you heard from the beginning abide in you" (v24). Again "from the beginning." The answer to novelty is faithfulness to the original message. If the word abides in them, they abide in the Son and the Father. "Abide" (menō, to remain, stay) runs through this chapter and the whole letter.
  • "You have no need that anyone should teach you" (v27). This does not reject human teachers. John is teaching them as he writes. It means they do not need the new teachers' "deeper" secrets. The Spirit who anointed them confirms the truth they were taught at the start (John 14:26; 16:13).
  • Commentators: Guzik expects a single future Antichrist and reads the name as "instead of" Jesus more than simply "against" him. He agrees with Stott that those who left had never truly belonged. JFB lists both meanings, a false Christ and an adversary of Christ. On v20 JFB explains "unction" as "anointing unguent, namely, the Holy Spirit." On v27 he says believers received it "once for all" and it "now still abides." On v23 he says the second half "ought not to be in italics… for the oldest Greek manuscripts have them." Guzik stresses that every believer has the anointing, and that teachers still have their place.

Children of God (vv28–29)

  • "Abide in him, so that when he appears we may have confidence" (v28). "Confidence" (parrēsia) is freedom of speech, the boldness to stand before someone without shame. "Coming" is parousia, the word for a king's official visit, used of Christ's return. John includes himself: "we." The alternative is to "shrink from him in shame," like someone caught.
  • "Everyone who practices righteousness has been born of him" (v29). This is the first mention of new birth in the letter, and it opens the section that runs into chapter 3. Righteous living does not cause the new birth. It shows it. Children resemble their Father.
  • Commentators: Guzik sees confidence at Christ's coming as the fruit of abiding, and practicing righteousness as a family likeness that shows who one's Father is. Henry's heading for these verses is simply the call to abide in Christ so as not to be ashamed before him at his coming.

Pointing to Christ

The chapter is full of Jesus in his different roles. He is the advocate pleading for his people and the propitiation that makes the plea just. He is the example whose walk believers follow and the true light already shining. He is the Anointed One whose Spirit anoints every believer, the Christ whom the antichrists deny, and the King whose coming his people can face with confidence. Every test John gives (obedience, love, truth) comes back to him. Those who fail can still come to him, because "we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous."

Reflection

  • Christ Our Advocate After We Sin (v.1) — John reassures believers that when they sin, Christ acts as their advocate before the Father rather than their accuser. When you fail, is your first instinct to run to him or to hide from him?
  • Walking as Jesus Walked (v.6) — Claiming to remain in Christ carries the obligation to actually live the way he lived. Does your daily pattern of life resemble Jesus's, or is the claim bigger than the reality?
  • The Command Not to Love the World (v.15) — John names fleshly cravings, covetous desire, and prideful self-sufficiency as the world's grip on the heart. Which of those three pulls at you the hardest right now?
  • The World Passing Away (v.17) — Everything the world offers is temporary, but doing God's will lasts forever. Are you investing your time and energy in what's fading or what's permanent?
  • Genuine Faith Revealed by Endurance (v.19) — John explains that those who left the church proved by leaving that they were never truly part of it. How does that both warn you and reassure you about your own perseverance?
