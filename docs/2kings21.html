1Manasseh was twelve years old when he became king, and he reigned in Jerusalem fifty-five years. His mother’s name was Hephzibah.

2And he did evil in the sight of the LORD by following the abominations of the nations that the LORD had driven out before the Israelites.

3For he rebuilt the high places that his father Hezekiah had destroyed, and he raised up altars for Baal. He made an Asherah pole, as King Ahab of Israel had done, and he worshiped and served all the host of heaven.

4Manasseh also built altars in the house of the LORD, of which the LORD had said, “In Jerusalem I will put My Name.”

5In both courtyards of the house of the LORD, he built altars to all the host of heaven.

6He sacrificed his own son in the fire, practiced sorcery and divination, and consulted mediums and spiritists. He did great evil in the sight of the LORD, provoking Him to anger.

7Manasseh even took the carved Asherah pole he had made and set it up in the temple, of which the LORD had said to David and his son Solomon, “In this temple and in Jerusalem, which I have chosen out of all the tribes of Israel, I will establish My Name forever.

8I will never again cause the feet of the Israelites to wander from the land that I gave to their fathers, if only they are careful to do all I have commanded them—the whole Law that My servant Moses commanded them.”

9But the people did not listen and Manasseh led them astray, so that they did greater evil than the nations that the LORD had destroyed before the Israelites.

10And the LORD spoke through His servants the prophets, saying,

11“Since Manasseh king of Judah has committed all these abominations, acting more wickedly than the Amorites who preceded him, and with his idols has caused Judah to sin,

12this is what the LORD, the God of Israel, says: ‘Behold, I am bringing such calamity upon Jerusalem and Judah that the news will reverberate in the ears of all who hear it.

13I will stretch out over Jerusalem the measuring line used against Samaria and the plumb line used against the house of Ahab, and I will wipe out Jerusalem as one wipes out a bowl—wiping it and turning it upside down.

14So I will forsake the remnant of My inheritance and deliver them into the hands of their enemies. And they will become plunder and spoil to all their enemies,

15because they have done evil in My sight and have provoked Me to anger from the day their fathers came out of Egypt until this day.’”

16Moreover, Manasseh shed so much innocent blood that he filled Jerusalem from end to end, in addition to the sin that he had caused Judah to commit, doing evil in the sight of the LORD.

17As for the rest of the acts of Manasseh, along with all his accomplishments and the sin that he committed, are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Judah?

18And Manasseh rested with his fathers and was buried in his palace garden, the garden of Uzza. And his son Amon reigned in his place.

19Amon was twenty-two years old when he became king, and he reigned in Jerusalem two years. His mother’s name was Meshullemeth daughter of Haruz; she was from Jotbah.

20And he did evil in the sight of the LORD, as his father Manasseh had done.

21He walked in all the ways of his father, and he served and worshiped the idols his father had served.

22He abandoned the LORD, the God of his fathers, and did not walk in the way of the LORD.

23Then the servants of Amon conspired against him and killed the king in his palace.

24But the people of the land killed all those who had conspired against King Amon, and they made his son Josiah king in his place.

25As for the rest of the acts of Amon, along with his accomplishments, are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Judah?

26And he was buried in his tomb in the garden of Uzza, and his son Josiah reigned in his place.

Summary

Manasseh becomes king of Judah at twelve and reigns fifty-five years, rebuilding the high places his father Hezekiah had destroyed, erecting altars to Baal and an Asherah pole inside the temple itself, worshiping the starry host, sacrificing his own son in the fire, and practicing sorcery and divination (vv. 1-9). Through his prophets the LORD declares that such disaster will fall on Jerusalem that the ears of all who hear of it will tingle, and that Judah will be measured by the same standard of judgment used against Samaria and the house of Ahab (vv. 10-15). Manasseh also fills Jerusalem with innocent blood, making his reign, along with his son and successor Amon's, the low point of Judah's kings; Amon reigns only two years before his own officials assassinate him, and the people of the land install his young son Josiah as king (vv. 16-26).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Jeremiah or a prophetic school. Originally one book with 1 Kings. Covers approximately 250 years (850-586 BC) from Elijah's translation through the Babylonian exile.
Historical Context: Chapter 21 records the reign of Manasseh (c. 697- 642 BC), who reigned longer than any other king of Judah — 55 years — and was, by the biblical author's assessment, the most wicked king Judah ever produced. His reign represents the theological point of no return: after Manasseh, God's judgment on Judah becomes irreversible, even Josiah's later reforms cannot avert it. Manasseh systematically undid every reform his father Hezekiah had accomplished, rebuilding the high places, erecting Baal altars, placing an Asherah pole inside the Temple itself, practicing child sacrifice, and filling Jerusalem with innocent blood. He is the anti-Hezekiah, the anti-David — the king who made the exile inevitable.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: A fifty-five year reign, the longest of any king of Judah, judged the most wicked precisely because of what it undid, an Asherah image placed inside the very temple built to house the name of the LORD, judgment pronounced as irreversible, the line of Samaria and the plummet of Ahab's house applied to Jerusalem itself, a dish wiped and turned upside down as the image for total destruction, and a son who inherits the idolatry of Manasseh's reign without any hint of his father's late repentance
Manasseh's Abominations (vv.1-9): Manasseh began at age twelve — presumably under regents who reversed Hezekiah's reforms. The catalogue of his sins is comprehensive and horrifying. He rebuilt the high places his father destroyed (v.3), erected altars for Baal and made an Asherah — "as did Ahab king of Israel" — the worst comparison possible for a king of Judah. He worshiped the host of heaven (astral deities), built pagan altars within the Temple courts (vv.4-5), burned his son as an offering, and practiced every form of divination and necromancy (v.6). Most shockingly, he placed the carved Asherah image inside the Temple itself (v.7) — the very house where God placed His name. Verse 9 delivers the devastating summary: Manasseh "seduced them to do more evil than did the nations whom the LORD destroyed before the children of Israel." The people of God became worse than the Canaanites — the very nations expelled for their wickedness.
God's Irreversible Judgment (vv.10-16): God speaks through unnamed prophets, and the verdict is final. Jerusalem will receive the same judgment as Samaria — "the line of Samaria and the plummet of the house of Ahab" (v.13). The measuring tools of destruction applied to the northern kingdom will now measure Judah. God will "wipe Jerusalem as a man wipeth a dish, wiping it, and turning it upside down" — a vivid image of total destruction followed by complete inversion. The remnant of God's inheritance will be abandoned to their enemies (v.14). Verse 16 adds that Manasseh "shed innocent blood very much, till he had filled Jerusalem from one end to another." Jewish tradition identifies this with the martyrdom of Isaiah (sawn in two, cf. Hebrews 11:37). The chapter concludes with Amon's brief, evil reign and his assassination by his own servants (vv.19-26), followed by the people installing eight-year-old Josiah as king — the last hope of the Davidic line.
Amon's Two Years (vv.17-26): Manasseh is buried in the garden of his own house rather than with the kings, which the writer records without comment. Amon reigns two years and gets six verses, and the verdict is that he did as his father Manasseh did, walked in all the way that his father walked in, and served the idols that his father served. What is conspicuously absent is Manasseh's late repentance, which Chronicles records and this book does not. Amon inherited the idolatry and not the change of mind. His own servants conspire against him and kill him in his house, and the people of the land make Josiah king, a boy of eight.

Deep Dive

This Deep Dive was put together with AI help, drawing on the commentators named below. I’m a learner, not a scholar, so test everything against Scripture. Spot a mistake or have an idea? Let me know. How this site is made

Setting: Judah, about 697–640 BC. Hezekiah, one of Judah's best kings, has died after the fifteen extra years God gave him (20:6), and his son Manasseh takes the throne. Assyria is at the height of its power under Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal, who even conquered Egypt, and Judah is a small vassal paying tribute. Assyrian records list "Manasseh king of Judah" among the western kings who sent building materials to Nineveh. Into this world of imperial religion and astrology, Manasseh undoes everything his father did and goes much further. Kings gives his 55-year reign, the longest of any king of Judah or Israel, only eighteen verses, and almost all of them are about his sin and its consequences. Later in Kings, the writer names Manasseh as the reason Judah fell (23:26; 24:3–4), and Jeremiah says the same (Jer 15:4). 2 Chronicles 33 adds a surprising twist: late in life Manasseh repented.

Manasseh Reigns in Judah (vv1–9)

  • "Manasseh was twelve years old when he began to reign, and he reigned fifty-five years" (v1). He was born about three years into Hezekiah's extra fifteen years, as JFB and Guzik note. He probably ruled alongside his father at first, from about 697 BC, then alone from 686 to 642 BC. The Tyndale notes observe that his long reign was not a reward for faithfulness but the fruit of God's patience and his promise to David. Long life and power are not proof of God's approval (Eccl 8:12–13).
  • "His mother's name was Hephzibah" (v1). The name means "my delight is in her," the very name God promises to give restored Zion in Isaiah 62:4. Whatever her own faith, her son brought Jerusalem as far from that name as it ever went.
  • "According to the despicable practices of the nations whom the LORD drove out" (v2). "Despicable practices" (toʿebot, "abominations") is the word Deuteronomy uses for the Canaanite religion that Israel was told never to copy (Deut 18:9–12). The Canaanites lost the land for these things (Lev 18:24–28). Now the king of Judah brings them back.
  • "He rebuilt the high places that Hezekiah his father had destroyed" (v3). Hezekiah had torn them down at great cost (18:4, 22). Manasseh puts them back. Guzik observes that reform in one generation is no guarantee for the next.
  • "Erected altars for Baal and made an Asherah, as Ahab king of Israel had done" (v3). This is the first time a king of Judah is compared to Ahab, the worst king of the north (1 Kings 16:30–33). Baal was the Canaanite storm and fertility god; Asherah was the mother-goddess, worshiped through a wooden pole or carved image. Guzik notes that Asherah worship involved fertility rites.
  • "And worshiped all the host of heaven and served them" (v3). The sun, moon and stars. Assyria and Babylon practiced elaborate astral religion and astrology, and vassal kings often took up the overlord's worship. Moses warned against exactly this (Deut 4:19; 17:3). The northern kingdom fell for it (17:16). Archaeologists have found many small figurines and seals with astral symbols in Judah from this period. Later, Josiah removed horses and chariots dedicated to the sun from the temple entrance (23:11).
  • "He built altars in the house of the LORD, of which the LORD had said, 'In Jerusalem will I put my name'" (v4). The writer keeps quoting God's promise about the temple (vv4, 7), so that the outrage is plain. The place God chose for his name was filled with other names. "In the two courts of the house of the LORD" (v5). The inner court of the priests and the outer court of the people, as JFB explains. Every worshiper coming to the temple had to walk past star altars.
  • "He burned his son as an offering" (v6). Child sacrifice, usually linked to the god Molech, in the Valley of Hinnom south of Jerusalem (Lev 18:21; Jer 7:31). His grandfather Ahaz had done it (16:3). Chronicles says Manasseh burned "his sons" there (2 Chr 33:6). This is the practice that made that valley's name, Gehenna, a word for hell.
  • "Used fortune-telling and omens and dealt with mediums and with necromancers" (v6). A whole catalog of the occult. "Fortune-telling" may be reading clouds or signs (JFB: "from an observation of the clouds"); "omens" means interpreting signs such as the flight of birds or the patterns in oil on water; "mediums" and "necromancers" claimed to call up the dead. Deuteronomy 18:10–12 bans every one by name. King Saul consulting the medium at Endor is the classic warning (1 Sam 28). Assyrian kings employed armies of diviners, and Manasseh imitated them.
  • "The carved image of Asherah that he had made he set in the house" (v7). JFB calls this "the most aggravated outrage of the royal idolater." God's house, where the ark stood under the cherubim, now held an image of a Canaanite goddess. Ezekiel later saw in a vision "the image of jealousy" at the temple's gate (Ezek 8:3–5), probably something similar.
  • "I will not cause the feet of Israel to wander anymore… if only they will be careful to do according to all that I have commanded" (v8). God's promise of the land always came with this condition (1 Kings 9:6–9). The writer quotes it here because Judah is about to break it.
  • "But they did not listen, and Manasseh led them astray to do more evil than the nations had done" (v9). The people are not innocent bystanders. They followed willingly. Chronicles: "The LORD spoke to Manasseh and to his people, but they paid no attention" (2 Chr 33:10). Guzik stresses the people's share in the guilt. The shocking verdict is that God's own people became worse than the Canaanites God had driven out for their wickedness.
  • Commentators: Henry sums up Manasseh as one who "devoted himself to sin, to all manner of wickedness, idolatry, and murder." JFB explains the occult terms and the two temple courts. Guzik notes that Assyrian records name Manasseh as a tribute payer, that long reigns are not signs of approval, and that reform does not carry itself into the next generation.

Manasseh's Idolatry Denounced (vv10–18)

  • "The LORD said by his servants the prophets" (v10). The prophets are not named. JFB suggests Hosea, Joel, Nahum, Habakkuk and Isaiah, but the dates of several are uncertain and most do not fit this reign. Chronicles says the words of "the seers who spoke to him" were recorded in a lost book (2 Chr 33:18–19). God did not leave Manasseh without warning.
  • "Things more evil than all that the Amorites did" (v11). "Amorites" here means the peoples of Canaan in general (Gen 15:16: "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete"). God waited four hundred years before judging them. The Tyndale notes recall that Ahab too was condemned for acting like the Amorites (1 Kings 21:26).
  • "Such disaster that the ears of everyone who hears of it will tingle" (v12). The same words God spoke to the boy Samuel about the fall of Eli's house and the loss of the ark (1 Sam 3:11), and to Jeremiah about Jerusalem (Jer 19:3). JFB: "a strong metaphorical form of announcing an extraordinary and appalling event."
  • "I will stretch over Jerusalem the measuring line of Samaria, and the plumb line of the house of Ahab" (v13). A builder uses a measuring line and a plumb line to check whether a wall is straight. God will measure Jerusalem by the same standard he used for Samaria, which fell in 722 BC, and for Ahab's dynasty, which was wiped out (9–10). Amos saw God standing with a plumb line beside a crooked wall (Amos 7:7–9), and Isaiah used the same image (Isa 28:17). The Tyndale notes say Manasseh should have expected the same justice. Guzik: similar sins bring similar judgment.
  • "I will wipe Jerusalem as one wipes a dish, wiping it and turning it upside down" (v13). A homely, chilling picture. After a meal the bowl is wiped clean and turned over to dry, with nothing left in it. Jerusalem will be emptied of its people. JFB calls it "a figure unmistakably significant."
  • "I will forsake the remnant of my heritage" (v14). After the northern kingdom's fall, Judah was the "remnant" of God's inheritance, his own people (Deut 32:9). "Forsake" (natash) means to abandon or leave. Guzik suggests the word was used of skinning an animal, but the lexicon gives that sense only for a related Aramaic word; in Hebrew it simply means "leave, abandon." "They shall become a prey and a spoil." Fulfilled when Babylon carried Judah away (25:8–21), as the Tyndale notes say.
  • "Since the day their fathers came out of Egypt, even to this day" (v15). Manasseh is the last straw, not the only straw. The whole history from the golden calf on is in view (Ps 106; Ezek 20).
  • "Manasseh shed very much innocent blood, till he had filled Jerusalem from one end to another" (v16). Beyond idolatry, mass murder, probably of those who resisted his religion. JFB calls him "a cruel persecutor of all who did not conform." The phrase "from one end to another" means from mouth to mouth, as a vessel is filled to the brim. Later the writer says this innocent blood was something "the LORD would not pardon" (24:4).
  • The tradition about Isaiah. Jewish tradition, recorded in the Talmud and in an early Jewish-Christian writing called the Ascension of Isaiah, says Manasseh had Isaiah sawn in two inside a hollow tree. Many think Hebrews 11:37, "they were sawn in two," alludes to it. The Bible itself does not say how Isaiah died, and the story cannot be confirmed. JFB, Guzik and the Tyndale notes all mention it as tradition.
  • "The rest of the acts of Manasseh… and the sin that he committed" (v17). Kings tells nothing more, but 2 Chronicles 33:11–17 does. The Assyrians took Manasseh "with hooks and bound him with chains of bronze and brought him to Babylon." There, "he entreated the favor of the LORD his God and humbled himself greatly," and God heard him and brought him back to Jerusalem. "Then Manasseh knew that the LORD was God." He removed the foreign gods from the temple and restored true worship. The Tyndale notes date the capture to around 650–648 BC, when Ashurbanipal crushed a revolt centered on Babylon. A later prayer, the Prayer of Manasseh, was written to imagine his words of repentance and is included in some Bibles' Apocrypha; it is not part of Scripture for Protestants or Jews.
  • Why does Kings leave the repentance out? Kings is explaining why Judah fell, and Manasseh's late reform did not undo what he had done. The people kept sacrificing at the high places (2 Chr 33:17), and his son went straight back to idolatry. Chronicles, written later for the returned exiles, shows that even the worst sinner can be forgiven. Both are true. Guzik notes the repentance came too late to change the nation, though not too late for Manasseh himself.
  • "Buried in the garden of his house, in the garden of Uzza" (v18). Not in the royal tombs where Hezekiah was honored (2 Chr 32:33), as the Tyndale notes observe. Uzza may be a short form of Uzziah, another king buried apart because of leprosy (2 Chr 26:23), but that is a guess.
  • Commentators: Henry: "God devoted him, and Jerusalem for his sake, to ruin," and the city "hastened apace towards its destruction, which slumbered not." JFB explains the measuring line and plummet as marking out a building for destruction. Guzik traces how tolerated idolatry grows into persecution of true worshipers, and points to Manasseh's repentance in Chronicles as hope for those raised well who wander far.

Amon Reigns in Judah (vv19–26)

  • "Amon was twenty-two years old when he began to reign, and he reigned two years" (v19). About 642–640 BC. He was born when Manasseh was 45, perhaps before his father's repentance. His name is unusual. Some think it honors the Egyptian god Amun, but it may simply be a Hebrew word meaning "faithful" or "craftsman." "Meshullemeth the daughter of Haruz of Jotbah." Jotbah may be in Galilee or in the far south near the Gulf of Aqaba; scholars disagree.
  • "He walked in all the way in which his father walked and served the idols that his father served" (v21). He copied his father's sins, not his father's repentance. Chronicles: "he did not humble himself before the LORD, as Manasseh his father had humbled himself, but this Amon incurred guilt more and more" (2 Chr 33:23). Guzik and the Tyndale notes make the same point. "He abandoned the LORD, the God of his fathers" (v22).
  • "The servants of Amon conspired against him and put the king to death in his house" (v23). "Servants" means royal officials. Their motive is not given. Some think there was a power struggle between a pro-Egyptian party and those loyal to Assyria. Palace murders were common in the northern kingdom (15:10, 14, 25, 30) but rare in Judah. JFB: he "was massacred by some of his own domestics."
  • "But the people of the land struck down all those who had conspired against King Amon" (v24). "The people of the land" (ʿam haʾaretz) were the landowning citizens of Judah, a group that stood up for David's line at key moments, as when they helped crown the boy-king Joash (11:14–20). They would not let the dynasty be broken. "And the people of the land made Josiah his son king in his place." Josiah was eight (22:1). Guzik sees the people's action as a sign that, after decades of wicked kings, the nation was ready for something better.
  • "And he was buried in his tomb in the garden of Uzza" (v26). Like his father, outside the royal tombs. "And Josiah his son reigned in his place." From the worst king's line comes the best. Amon's one good legacy was his son.
  • Commentators: Henry notes that Amon "died quickly by the sword, and left good Josiah his successor." JFB says Amon "continued the idolatrous policy of his father" and that the people "slew the regicide conspirators." Guzik observes that Amon imitated the north's habit of assassination, and finds hope in the people's choice of Josiah.

Pointing to Christ

Manasseh did more evil than anyone in Judah's history, filled Jerusalem with innocent blood, and still, when he humbled himself in chains, God heard him and brought him home (2 Chr 33:12–13). That is the gospel before the gospel: "where sin increased, grace abounded all the more" (Rom 5:20). Paul called himself the foremost of sinners for the same reason, so that Christ might display "his perfect patience as an example to those who were to believe in him" (1 Tim 1:15–16). Yet the chapter also warns that forgiveness does not erase every consequence. The city still fell. Only one king could carry the full weight of a nation's sin and rise free of it: the Son of David, whose own innocent blood was shed in Jerusalem, and "speaks a better word than the blood of Abel" (Heb 12:24).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes the staggering contrast between Hezekiah and Manasseh — father and son, reformer and destroyer. He notes that Manasseh's 55-year reign means he had more time to corrupt Judah than any other king, and that the depth of his syncretism (mixing pagan worship directly into Temple worship) made the corruption systemic rather than merely tolerated. Guzik also highlights that 2 Kings deliberately omits Manasseh's later repentance (recorded in 2 Chronicles 33) to emphasize the national consequences.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Privilege Doesn't Guarantee Faithfulness (vv.1-3) — Manasseh had a godly father, a reformed nation, and a restored temple, yet chose evil beyond even the pagan nations God had driven out. Does a good upbringing or church background ever lull you into assuming your own faith is secure?
  • One Life's Influence Can Corrupt Many (v.9) — Manasseh actively led the nation into sins worse than the Canaanites had committed. Whose faith are you shaping right now — for better or worse — through the example you set?
  • A Point Where Consequences Can't Be Undone (vv.10-15) — Even though Manasseh's own later repentance is recorded elsewhere, God's judgment on the nation still stood. Are you assuming there will always be time to turn back, or does the possibility of a point of no return create real urgency for you?
  • Filling the Land With Innocent Blood (v.16) — Manasseh's cruelty was so extensive that it becomes the specific charge named against him. What unaddressed harm to others, or through neglect, might be quietly building against you?
  • Unchecked Sin Compounds in the Next Generation (vv.19-22) — Amon followed his father's evil without even Manasseh's eventual turning. What patterns are you tolerating today that could become full rebellion in the next generation of your family?
