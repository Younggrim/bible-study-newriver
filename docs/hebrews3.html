1Therefore, holy brothers, who share in the heavenly calling, set your focus on Jesus, the apostle and high priest whom we confess.

2He was faithful to the One who appointed Him, just as Moses was faithful in all God’s house.

3For Jesus has been counted worthy of greater glory than Moses, just as the builder of a house has greater honor than the house itself.

4And every house is built by someone, but God is the builder of everything.

5Now Moses was faithful as a servant in all God’s house, testifying to what would be spoken later.

6But Christ is faithful as the Son over God’s house. And we are His house, if we hold firmly to our confidence and the hope of which we boast.

7Therefore, as the Holy Spirit says: “Today, if you hear His voice,

8do not harden your hearts, as you did in the rebellion, in the day of testing in the wilderness,

9where your fathers tested and tried Me, and for forty years saw My works.

10Therefore I was angry with that generation, and I said, ‘Their hearts are always going astray, and they have not known My ways.’

11So I swore on oath in My anger, ‘They shall never enter My rest.’”

12See to it, brothers, that none of you has a wicked heart of unbelief that turns away from the living God.

13But exhort one another daily, as long as it is called today, so that none of you may be hardened by sin’s deceitfulness.

14We have come to share in Christ if we hold firmly to the end the assurance we had at first.

15As it has been said: “Today, if you hear His voice, do not harden your hearts, as you did in the rebellion.”

16For who were the ones who heard and rebelled? Were they not all those Moses led out of Egypt?

17And with whom was God angry for forty years? Was it not with those who sinned, whose bodies fell in the wilderness?

18And to whom did He swear that they would never enter His rest? Was it not to those who disobeyed?

19So we see that it was because of their unbelief that they were unable to enter.

Summary

The author calls believers, who share in a heavenly calling, to consider Jesus as the apostle and high priest of their confession, faithful to God just as Moses was faithful in God's house — though Jesus, as the house's builder, deserves greater honor than Moses, who was only a servant within it (vv. 1-6). Quoting Psalm 95, he warns readers not to harden their hearts as Israel did in the wilderness, when that generation tested God for forty years despite seeing his works, provoking him to swear they would never enter his rest (vv. 7-11). He urges believers to watch that no one develops an unbelieving heart that turns away from the living God, but to encourage one another daily so that none are hardened by sin's deceitfulness (vv. 12-13). Sharing in Christ depends on holding firm to the end, and the chapter closes by tracing Israel's failure to enter God's rest back to unbelief (vv. 14-19).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown (see Chapter 1 notes for full discussion).
Historical Context: Chapter 3 demonstrates Christ's superiority over Moses (vv.1-6), then issues the second major warning: don't harden your hearts like Israel in the wilderness (vv.7-19). Moses was the greatest figure in Judaism — lawgiver, deliverer, mediator. If Christ is greater than Moses, then His message demands even greater faithfulness. The wilderness generation serves as a warning: they heard God's voice but hardened their hearts and never entered the Promised Land.
Classification: Epistle — Sermon or Treatise
Key Themes: A house compared to its builder to argue for a Son greater than a servant, Moses honored fully before being surpassed, a second warning drawn from Israel's forty years rather than from a new offense, hardening traced to unbelief rather than to circumstance, and daily exhortation urged as the remedy for a heart no one notices going wrong
Christ Greater Than Moses (vv.1-6): Both were faithful (v.2). But Moses was faithful as a servant in God's house; Christ is faithful as a Son over God's house (vv.5-6). The builder is greater than the building (v.3). "Whose house are we" — believers are God's house (v.6).
Warning #2: Don't Harden Your Hearts (vv.7-19): Quoting Psalm 95:7-11, the author warns against repeating Israel's wilderness failure. They heard God's voice but hardened their hearts (v.8). They tested God for 40 years (v.9). God swore they would not enter His rest (v.11). Application: "Take heed... lest there be in any of you an evil heart of unbelief" (v.12). "Exhort one another daily" (v.13). The wilderness generation failed because of unbelief (v.19).

Deep Dive

Setting: The unnamed preacher of Hebrews is writing his "word of exhortation" (13:22) to weary Jewish Christians, most likely in the 60s, who are tempted to drift back from Christ to the safer ground of temple and synagogue. Chapter 1 showed the Son above the angels: the final word of God, the radiance of his glory, seated on the throne. Chapter 2 gave the first warning ("we must pay much closer attention," 2:1) and then showed the same Son made "for a little while lower than the angels," sharing flesh and blood with his "brothers" so that he might become "a merciful and faithful high priest" (2:17). Chapter 3 picks up those two words. It takes "faithful" first and sets Jesus beside the most honored man in Israel's history, Moses. Then it turns to Israel's wilderness generation, who followed Moses out of Egypt and never reached the land, and makes them the sermon's second warning. "High priest" waits for chapters 4–10.

Jesus Greater Than Moses (vv1–6)

  • "Therefore, holy brothers" (v1). "Therefore" ties the chapter to what came before: because Jesus is the Son who became our brother and high priest (2:11, 17), here is what to do. The readers are "holy," set apart by his sacrifice (2:11), not by their own achievement. As the ESV footnote says, "brothers" (adelphoi) means brothers and sisters, the whole church family.
  • "You who share in a heavenly calling" (v1). "Share" is metochoi, "partners" or "sharers." It is the word used in 1:9 for the Son's "companions," and it comes back in v14: "we have come to share in Christ." The calling is "heavenly" because it comes from heaven and leads there (11:16; 12:22). Israel was called to an earthly land, and these believers to something greater.
  • "Consider Jesus" (v1). The verb katanoeō means to take careful note of, to fix the mind on. Jesus uses it in "consider the ravens" (Luke 12:24). It is not a passing glance but a long, steady look. The sermon's cure for spiritual drift is to look hard at Jesus (12:2).
  • "The apostle and high priest of our confession" (v1). This is the only place in the New Testament where Jesus is called an "apostle." The lexicon gives apostolos as "one sent on a mission," a messenger or envoy. Jesus is the one sent from God to us, as in chapter 1, where God "has spoken to us by his Son." He is also the high priest who goes from us to God, as in chapter 2. Moses and Aaron split those roles; Jesus holds both. "Confession" (homologia) is what the church says together about Jesus (4:14; 10:23), the faith it publicly owns.
  • "Faithful to him who appointed him, just as Moses also was faithful" (v2). The writer quotes Numbers 12:7, where God defends Moses against Miriam and Aaron: "he is faithful in all my house." God added that he spoke with Moses "mouth to mouth" (Num 12:8). The Tyndale notes add that many first-century Jews saw Moses as the greatest man in history, ranked even above the angels. So the writer begins by honoring Moses, not attacking him. Jesus and Moses share the same virtue. The comparison is between two faithful men, and only then does the writer show which is greater. "Appointed" is literally "made" (poieō). It refers to his appointment to office, as in 1 Samuel 12:6, where the LORD "appointed" Moses and Aaron, not to his creation.
  • "As much more glory as the builder of a house has more honor than the house itself" (v3). The "house" in Numbers 12 is not a building but a household, God's people. Moses was part of that household, its most faithful member. Jesus is the one who built it. However great a house is, the builder deserves the credit.
  • "The builder of all things is God" (v4). The parenthesis seems to move from Jesus to God, but in chapter 1 the Son is the one "through whom also he created the world" (1:2) and the Lord who "laid the foundation of the earth" (1:10). If Jesus built the house and God built all things, the writer is quietly placing Jesus on the Creator's side of the line, with Moses on the creature's side.
  • "Moses was faithful in all God's house as a servant" (v5). "Servant" here is therapōn, found only here in the New Testament, taken straight from the Greek of Numbers 12:7. The lexicon gives "attendant" or "servant," and it often translates the Hebrew ʿeved. It is an honorable title: Moses is "the servant of the LORD" at his death (Deut 34:5). But a servant, however trusted, is not the son of the house.
  • "To testify to the things that were to be spoken later" (v5). Moses' whole ministry pointed beyond itself. Moses himself promised that God would raise up "a prophet like me" (Deut 18:15), and Jesus said, "he wrote of me" (John 5:46). Hebrews will argue at length (chs 8–10) that the tabernacle was "a copy and shadow" of the real thing (8:5).
  • "But Christ is faithful over God's house as a son" (v6). Moses is "in" the house, and Christ is "over" it. Behind this stands God's promise to David about his royal son: "I will confirm him in my house and in my kingdom forever" (1 Chr 17:14; compare 2 Sam 7:13–14, already quoted in 1:5). The son is the heir. He has authority over the household, and it belongs to him.
  • "And we are his house" (v6). While the temple still stood in Jerusalem, the writer tells these believers that God's true house is not made of stone. It is a people (1 Pet 2:5; Eph 2:19–22). Leaving the church for the temple would mean leaving God's house to go back to its shadow.
  • "If indeed we hold fast our confidence and our boasting in our hope" (v6). "Confidence" is parrēsia, which first meant free, open speech and then the boldness that goes with it. These believers had once stood openly for Christ and lost property for it, and the writer later pleads, "do not throw away your confidence" (10:35, the same word). "Boasting" (kauchēma) is the thing one glories in, here the hope itself. What is held fast is not private feeling but public, unashamed hope.
  • "Firm to the end" (v6). The ESV footnotes that some manuscripts add these words here. They are missing from several of the earliest copies, including the oldest papyrus of Hebrews and Codex Vaticanus. Most scholars think a scribe brought them in from v14, where the same Greek phrase certainly belongs. Nothing in the teaching changes, since v14 says the same thing.
  • Commentators: Henry puts the contrast in a line: "Christ was a maker of the house, Moses but a member in it." He also notes that "no less power was requisite to make the church than to make the world," which ties v4 back to chapter 1. JFB takes "consider" as "consider attentively," sees "Apostle" and "High Priest" as the two sides of Christ's one work as Mediator, and calls Moses' role "a ministering attendant" rather than a slave. The lexicon supports "attendant," but the contrast in the verse is between servant and son, not servant and slave. Guzik stresses that the writer never runs Moses down. He only lifts Jesus higher. Guzik reads the "if" of v6 as showing that real faith proves itself over the long run, and he joins it with Philippians 1:6, where God finishes what he starts.

A Rest for the People of God (vv7–19)

  • "Therefore, as the Holy Spirit says" (v7). Psalm 95 was written centuries earlier, and 4:7 credits it to David. But the writer says the Spirit says, present tense, not said. Scripture is God speaking now to the church that hears it. Christians have long sung this psalm at morning prayer as the Venite.
  • "Today, if you hear his voice" (v7). The psalm opens with joyful worship ("Oh come, let us sing to the LORD," Ps 95:1) and then turns sharply to warning. "Today" is the key word of the whole passage, and the writer will lean on it again in v13, v15 and 4:7. In David's day "today" was still open, long after Moses. The writer's point is that it is still open for his readers, but it will not stay open forever.
  • "Do not harden your hearts as in the rebellion, on the day of testing" (v8). In Hebrew, Psalm 95:8 names two places: "as at Meribah, as on the day at Massah." Those names mean "quarreling" and "testing." At Rephidim the people demanded water and asked, "Is the LORD among us or not?" (Exod 17:1–7). There was a second Meribah incident near the end of the journey (Num 20:2–13). The Greek Old Testament, which Hebrews quotes, translated the place names into ordinary words, "the rebellion" (parapikrasmos, "provocation") and "testing" (peirasmos). So the reader hears not only about two places but about the pattern of the whole forty years. The verb "harden" (sklērynō) is the word the Greek Old Testament uses for the hardening of Pharaoh's heart (Exod 7:3). Rescued from Pharaoh, Israel became like him.
  • "Saw my works for forty years" (v9). They saw the plagues, the sea split open, manna every morning, water from the rock, the pillar of cloud and fire. Seeing did not produce trust. In the psalm the forty years belong with God's anger ("for forty years I loathed that generation," Ps 95:10). Hebrews moves the phrase to "saw my works" and adds "therefore" before "I was provoked." Then in v17 it uses the forty years with God's anger, as the psalm does. Both are true. For forty years they saw his works, and for forty years they provoked him.
  • "They always go astray in their heart; they have not known my ways" (v10). The problem was not a single bad day but a heart that kept wandering. To "know his ways" in the Old Testament means to know God by walking with him (Exod 33:13).
  • "As I swore in my wrath, 'They shall not enter my rest'" (v11). The oath is recorded in Numbers 14:21–23, 28–30, after the people refused to enter Canaan at Kadesh (Num 13–14). Literally the Greek reads "if they shall enter my rest," a Hebrew oath formula that leaves the curse unspoken: "if they enter, may I be…" "Rest" (katapausis, Hebrew menuchah) first meant the land itself, Israel's settled home after the wandering (Deut 12:9; Josh 21:44). The Tyndale notes stress that this wrath is God's just response to sin, not a fit of temper. In 4:1–11 the writer argues that the rest was never only Canaan, since David was still offering it long after Joshua. It is God's own Sabbath rest, still open to his people.
  • "Take care, brothers, lest there be in any of you an evil, unbelieving heart" (v12). Now the writer applies the psalm, and the Tyndale notes point out his method. He takes the psalm's key words, "heart," "today," "hear," "enter," "rest," and "unbelief," and works through them one by one. This way of explaining Scripture was common among Jewish teachers. "Any of you" makes it personal: no one is to be left behind.
  • "Leading you to fall away from the living God" (v12). "Fall away" is aphistēmi, to stand off or withdraw from. The related noun gives English the word "apostasy." The lexicon lists this verse under "fall away, apostatize." The irony is sharp. These Jewish believers might think that going back to the temple was going back to the God of their fathers. The writer says that turning from Jesus means turning from "the living God" himself.
  • "But exhort one another every day, as long as it is called 'today'" (v13). "Exhort" is parakaleō, which the lexicon gives as both "admonish, exhort" and "encourage, comfort." It is a word for coming alongside someone. The cure for a hardening heart is not only private resolve but daily contact with other believers who will speak truth in love. Later the writer warns against "neglecting to meet together" (10:24–25). Paul gives the same charge: "encourage one another and build one another up" (1 Thess 5:11).
  • "Hardened by the deceitfulness of sin" (v13). "Deceitfulness" (apatē) is the word Jesus used for "the deceitfulness of riches" choking the word in the parable of the soils (Matt 13:22). Sin hardens not by force but by lies. Israel's own lie was that Egypt had been better (Num 14:3–4).
  • "For we have come to share in Christ" (v14). "Have come to share" is a perfect tense in Greek, "we have become sharers," a settled fact. The "if" clause that follows does not make it come true. It shows that it already is true. Holding firm to the end is the evidence that a person really did come to share in Christ.
  • "If indeed we hold our original confidence firm to the end" (v14). "Confidence" here is hypostasis, the same word translated "nature" in 1:3 and "assurance" in 11:1. The lexicon traces it from "foundation" or "support" to "steadiness, firmness," and so to assurance or confidence. The faith they began with is solid ground, and the danger is walking off it.
  • What do the warnings mean? Faithful traditions read the "ifs" of vv6 and 14 differently. Reformed readers hold that those truly joined to Christ will persevere, and that God uses warnings like these to keep them. Those who finally fall away show they never truly shared in Christ (1 John 2:19). Wesleyan and Arminian readers take the warnings as describing a real danger: a genuine believer can, by persistent unbelief, turn away and be lost. A smaller group reads the "rest" as a fuller life or reward rather than salvation itself. All agree the warning is meant to be taken seriously.
  • "Today, if you hear his voice" (v15). The psalm is quoted again to sum up vv12–14, the line the preacher most wants remembered.
  • Three questions (vv16–18). Each question takes up one line of the psalm: who rebelled, with whom was God angry, and to whom did he swear. Each answer comes from the wilderness story in Numbers. The shock is in the first answer: "all those who left Egypt led by Moses." The people who rebelled were not outsiders. They had been redeemed, had walked through the sea and had eaten the manna (1 Cor 10:1–5). Only Joshua and Caleb of that adult generation entered the land (Num 14:30).
  • "Some... howbeit not all" (v16). The KJV reads v16 as a statement: "some, when they had heard, did provoke: howbeit not all." The Greek word tines can be read either as "some" or, with a different accent, as the question "who?" Early manuscripts had no accents. Nearly all modern translations take it as a question, which fits the run of questions in vv16–18 and gives the stronger point: not some, but almost all.
  • "Whose bodies fell in the wilderness" (v17). "Bodies" is kōla, literally "limbs." The Greek Old Testament uses it in God's sentence at Kadesh: "your dead bodies shall fall in this wilderness" (Num 14:29).
  • "Those who sinned... those who were disobedient... because of unbelief" (vv17–19). The writer moves from sin, to disobedience, to unbelief, and stops there. Unbelief is the root. Disobedience is what it looks like on the outside. Numbers itself makes the link: "How long will they not believe in me, in spite of all the signs?" (Num 14:11).
  • "So we see that they were unable to enter" (v19). The spies blamed the walls and giants of Canaan (Num 13:31), but unbelief was the real obstacle. The chapter ends on the warning, and the promise is still open: "while the promise of entering his rest still stands" (4:1).
  • Commentators: Henry on "today": "this very day, for to-morrow it may be too late." He calls hardening "the spring of all our other sins," says plainly that "a heart of unbelief is an evil heart," and holds that "perseverance in faith is the best evidence of the sincerity of our faith," close to the Reformed reading of v14. On "harden," Guzik notes that people usually blame others or their circumstances, when the verse puts the hardening in their own hands. He passes on Spurgeon's point that claiming to be unable to believe is usually a refusal to believe, and he notes how many people think one can be a good Christian without church, which v13 contradicts. JFB reads v16 "rather interrogatively," as the ESV does. On v17 it notes that the Greek is literally "limbs," and on v19 it adds that the people could not enter "though desiring it" (Num 14:39–45). Both Guzik and JFB connect the "forty years" to the forty years between Jesus' ministry and the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70. The timing is striking, but it is a suggestion of theirs, not a claim the text makes.

Pointing to Christ

The chapter sets two pictures side by side. In one, Moses leads a redeemed people out of slavery, and nearly all of them fall in the desert because they will not trust the God who saved them. In the other, Jesus is not only a better leader but the builder and heir of the house, the Son who was faithful where Israel was not. In the wilderness Jesus was tested for forty days and answered with Deuteronomy, the book of those forty years: "You shall not put the Lord your God to the test" (Matt 4:7, quoting Deut 6:16, "as you tested him at Massah"). Where the fathers hardened their hearts, the Son trusted. The rest Israel lost is now held open in him. So the call is not to try harder than the wilderness generation but to "consider Jesus," keep listening to his voice "today," and keep one another listening until the end.

Reflection

  • Fixing Your Mind on Christ (v.1) — Believers are called to consider Jesus carefully, as the apostle and high priest of their confession. Is Christ getting your careful, sustained attention, or has He become an afterthought in your daily thinking?
  • Responding to God Today (vv.7-8) — The Holy Spirit's warning is urgent: hear His voice today, and don't harden your hearts as Israel did in the wilderness. Is there something God is speaking to you about right now that you keep putting off?
  • Guarding Against an Unbelieving Heart (v.12) — Believers are told to watch out for an evil, unbelieving heart that turns away from the living God. Where in your life are you struggling to actually believe what God has promised?
  • The Need for Daily Encouragement (v.13) — The remedy for a hardening heart is other believers exhorting one another every day, before sin's deception can take hold. Do you have people in your life who speak truth into you daily, or are you facing that battle alone?
  • Unbelief Kept Them Out (v.19) — Despite every advantage — God's presence, miracles, Moses's leadership — the wilderness generation failed to enter His rest because of unbelief. What promise of God is your own unbelief currently keeping you from entering into?
