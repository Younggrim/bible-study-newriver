8The LORD is the strength of His people, a stronghold of salvation for His anointed.

Summary

A lament that turns into praise. David cries out to God as his rock, fearing that silence from God would leave him no different than the dead, and lifts his hands toward the sanctuary (vv. 1-2). He asks not to be dragged away with the wicked, whose friendly words mask malice, and asks God to repay them according to their deeds since they disregard his works (vv. 3-5). The tone shifts abruptly as David blesses the LORD for hearing his plea for mercy, calling him his strength and shield in whom his heart trusts, so that he is helped and his heart leaps for joy (vv. 6-7). The psalm closes asking God to save his people and shepherd them as their inheritance forever (vv. 8-9).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Individual Lament / Thanksgiving
Attributed Author: David — "A Psalm of David"
Key Themes: Fear of God's silence, separation from the wicked's fate, God as rock and shield, answered prayer producing praise, shepherd-king theology
Historical Context: David fears that if God remains silent, he will die ("become like them that go down into the pit"). He prays toward the "holy oracle" (debir = the inner sanctuary, Holy of Holies) — the symbolic location of God's presence and speech. The psalm's structure shows the pivot from desperate plea (vv.1-5) to joyful thanksgiving (vv.6-9). The key wicked characteristic: they "speak peace to their neighbours, but mischief is in their hearts" (v.3) — peaceful words concealing violent intentions. V.9's prayer to "feed them" and "lift them up forever" echoes Psalm 23's shepherd imagery — David as king asks God to shepherd His people.
Be Not Silent to Me (vv.1-2): Unto thee will I cry, O LORD my rock, be not silent to me. The fear is not of judgment but of no answer at all, lest, if thou be silent to me, I become like them that go down into the pit. Then the posture, when I lift up my hands toward thy holy oracle, which is the standing gesture of prayer in the psalter and is directed at a place.
Draw Me Not Away with the Wicked (vv.3-5): Draw me not away with the wicked, and with the workers of iniquity, which speak peace to their neighbours, but mischief is in their hearts. The offense named is the gap between what is said and what is meant, and the petition against them asks for accuracy rather than severity, give them according to their deeds, and according to the wickedness of their endeavours. And the reason is a failure of attention, because they regard not the works of the LORD.
The LORD Hath Heard the Voice of My Supplications (vv.6-7): Blessed be the LORD, because he hath heard the voice of my supplications. The turn is sudden and unexplained, as in Psalms 6 and 13, and the tense changes to the perfect. Then a stack of titles, the LORD is my strength and my shield, my heart trusted in him, and I am helped, and the response is physical, therefore my heart greatly rejoiceth, and with my song will I praise him.
Save Thy People (vv.8-9): The LORD is their strength, and he is the saving strength of his anointed. The psalm widens from the individual to the nation in its last two verses, which is the pattern of Psalms 25 and 51 as well. And the closing petition uses the shepherd language of Psalm 23 in the plural, feed them also, and lift them up for ever.

Deep Dive

Setting: "Of David." Like Psalm 27, it moves from urgent prayer to confident praise, but here the turn is sharper. Verses 1–5 are a cry for help, and then at v6, with no warning, "Blessed be the LORD! For he has heard." Henry admits it is hard to tell whether David wrote in the middle of trouble, praising God in faith before the answer came, or after the rescue, remembering the prayers he had prayed. No occasion is named. JFB notices that the closing verses pray for the king ("his anointed") and the people together, and suggests Absalom's rebellion, when David's own people were divided against him (2 Sam 15–18). That is a guess, but it fits a psalm about enemies who "speak peace" while plotting evil. The previous psalms keep echoing. "Do not drag me off with the wicked" (v3) repeats 26:9, and the fear of God's silence picks up 27:9, "Hide not your face from me."

To You, O LORD, I Call (vv1–2)

  • "To you, O LORD, I call; my rock" (v1). "Rock" is one of David's favorite names for God (18:2, 31, 46; 19:14). A rock was a place of safety in the wilderness, high, solid and immovable. Morgan, whom Guzik cites, observes that in the Old Testament "rock" is used as a picture of God and never of a man. David begins with who God is before he says what he needs.
  • "Be not deaf to me, lest, if you be silent to me" (v1). The Hebrew verb charash can mean both "be deaf" and "be silent," and David uses it in both senses here, then adds a second word for silence. A God who doesn't answer looks to David like a God who hasn't heard. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, puts it simply: God seems deaf when prayer goes unanswered and silent when he does not act to rescue.
  • "I become like those who go down to the pit" (v1). "The pit" (bor) is a cistern or a grave, and in the Psalms it stands for death and the world of the dead (30:3; 88:4). David is not exaggerating for effect. Without God's answer, he is as good as dead. Henry: "Nothing can be so cutting, so killing, to a gracious soul, as the want of God's favour."
  • "Hear the voice of my pleas for mercy" (v2). "Pleas for mercy" (tachanun) comes from the root for grace or favor. David is not demanding his rights. He is asking for grace.
  • "When I lift up my hands" (v2). Raised open hands were the ordinary posture of prayer in Israel (63:4; 134:2; 141:2; 1 Tim 2:8). Trapp, cited by Guzik, likens them to a beggar's outstretched hands waiting to be filled.
  • "Toward your most holy sanctuary" (v2). The word is debir, the innermost room of God's house, the Most Holy Place where the ark sat between the cherubim (1 Kings 6:16–23). The older English "oracle" (KJV) came from an ancient theory that debir was related to dabar, "to speak," as the place where God spoke. JFB follows that theory ("place of speaking"), and it would make a nice point: David lifts his hands to the place from which God answers. But the lexicon (BDB) calls that derivation incorrect. Debir probably means the "back room," the hindmost chamber. Israelites away from the sanctuary prayed facing it (1 Kings 8:29–30; Dan 6:10).
  • Is the word too late for David? The word appears mostly in descriptions of Solomon's temple, and some scholars take that to mean the psalm was written after David. Kidner, cited by Guzik, answers that the word was already standard by Solomon's time, so it was probably in use before him. The tent in David's day had an inner room for the ark too.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Be not silent to me, as one angry at my prayers." He reads the prayer as asking God not only to grant the request but to "speak to me joy and gladness." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that people going through the motions may be content without an answer, but genuine people praying cannot rest until heaven replies. JFB reads "go down into the pit" as "share their fate," the fate of the dead.

Do Not Drag Me Off with the Wicked (vv3–5)

  • "Do not drag me off with the wicked" (v3). The verb pictures being pulled away, like a prisoner hauled off with a gang of criminals, or sheep dragged to slaughter. David asks God not to sweep him up in the judgment the wicked deserve (26:9). Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes that this goes further than 27:12. There David asked not to be handed over to his enemies. Here he asks not to share their fate.
  • "Who speak peace with their neighbors while evil is in their hearts" (v3). "Peace" is shalom, the normal greeting. These are people who smile, greet and wish you well while planning your ruin. It is the sin of Joab, who greeted Amasa, "Is it well with you, my brother?" and stabbed him while holding his beard as if to kiss him (2 Sam 20:9–10). It is also the sin of Judas, who betrayed the Son of Man with a kiss (Luke 22:48). JFB: "Hypocrisy is the special wickedness mentioned."
  • "Give to them according to their work" (v4). Four times in one verse David asks for justice: according to their work, according to their evil deeds, according to the work of their hands, their due reward. The piling up is deliberate. He is not asking for cruelty beyond what they deserve, only that what they have done would come back on them. This is the principle Paul states plainly: God "will render to each one according to his works" (Rom 2:6; also 2 Tim 4:14; Rev 22:12).
  • Can a Christian pray this way? This is one of the milder "imprecatory" psalms, prayers asking God to judge enemies. Jesus taught "love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matt 5:44), and Christians have rightly asked how these prayers fit. A few things help. David does not take revenge himself. He hands the matter to God (Rom 12:19). He asks for justice, not for more than justice. And he prays against people who are fighting God, not people who merely annoy him. Kidner, cited by Guzik, says these verses voice the protest of any healthy conscience at wrong, and the conviction that a day of judgment must come. Henry likewise calls it "not the language of passion or revenge," and reads it as a prophecy: unless sin is undone by repentance, "there will certainly come a reckoning day."
  • "Because they do not regard the works of the LORD or the work of his hands" (v5). There is a play on words. The wicked are judged for their "work" and "the work of their hands" (v4), and the root of it is that they ignore God's "works" and "the work of his hands" (v5). They pay no attention to what God has made and done, in creation, in history and in his care for his people. Isaiah condemns the same blindness: "they do not regard the deeds of the LORD, or see the work of his hands" (Isa 5:12). Paul says those who ignore what can be seen of God in creation are "without excuse" (Rom 1:20–21).
  • "He will tear them down and build them up no more" (v5). Building and tearing down is the language of houses and dynasties. Jeremiah uses the pair for God's judgment and restoration (Jer 1:10; 24:6). Notice that David now speaks about God in the third person. He has stopped addressing God and is stating what God will do. The prayer is turning into confidence.
  • Commentators: Henry reads "do not drag me off" three ways: save me from their traps, from copying their sins, and from sharing their doom. "Good men dread the way of sinners; the best are sensible of the danger they are in of being drawn aside into it." JFB calls v4 an "imprecation" justified by v5, and says the stacking of terms describing their sin "greatly" strengthens it. He explains "destroy… and not build them up" as a positive statement strengthened by its negative. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's image of soft words "oily with pretended love" as the devil's net.

Blessed Be the LORD (vv6–7)

  • "Blessed be the LORD! For he has heard the voice of my pleas for mercy" (v6). The words of v2 return exactly. "Hear the voice of my pleas for mercy" has become "he has heard the voice of my pleas for mercy." Something has changed between v5 and v6. Either the answer has come, or David has received an inner assurance from God that it will. Laments in the Psalms often turn this way (6:8–9; 22:21–22). Henry: "How soon are the saints' sorrows turned into songs and their prayers into praises!" He adds a line worth keeping: "What we win by prayer we must wear by praise."
  • "The LORD is my strength and my shield" (v7). Strength to carry on and a shield against attack. David had called God "a shield about me" (3:3) and "my shield" (18:2). It is the same promise God gave Abraham: "Fear not, Abram, I am your shield" (Gen 15:1).
  • "In him my heart trusts, and I am helped" (v7). Trust comes first, then help. Henry points out a double gift: God gave the help in due time, and also "my very trusting in him has helped me, in the mean time, and kept me from fainting." The previous psalm said the same: faith kept David from losing heart (27:13).
  • "My heart exults, and with my song I give thanks to him" (v7). "Heart" comes twice: the heart that trusts is the heart that rejoices. JFB: the repetition "denotes his sincerity." "Exults" is a strong word for open, triumphant joy. The man who feared going down to the pit is singing.
  • Commentators: Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that real praise rests on reasons, not on mood, and here the reason is plain: "he has heard." He also notes that many know God as "a" strength and "a" shield, but David says "my." Henry lays out the pattern of the verse: dependence, then the experience of help, then joy, then praise. "Let us first bless God and then bless ourselves."

The Strength of His People (vv8–9)

  • "The LORD is the strength of his people" (v8). David moves from "my strength" (v7) to "the strength of his people." What God is for one believer he is for all. Henry: there is no less sunlight for any of us because others share it, and the same is true of God's favor.
  • "He is the saving refuge of his anointed" (v8). "Saving refuge" is literally "a stronghold of salvations" (ma'oz yeshu'ot), using the same "stronghold" word as 27:1. "His anointed" (mashiach) is the king. David is speaking of himself in the third person, as the one God anointed through Samuel (1 Sam 16:13). The king's safety and the people's safety are bound together. When God protects his anointed, he protects the nation.
  • "Oh, save your people and bless your heritage!" (v9). "Heritage" (nachalah) means an inheritance, a family's land handed down and never to be sold. Israel is God's own possession (Deut 9:29; 32:9). Henry notices that David does not say "my people." He says "your people." He does not own them. God does.
  • "Be their shepherd and carry them forever" (v9). David, the shepherd boy turned king, asks God to do for Israel what he himself once did for sheep. "Be their shepherd" (ra'ah) means both to feed and to lead. Clarke, cited by Guzik, notes the word covers feeding a flock and ruling it. "Carry them" (nasa') is to lift and bear. Isaiah uses the picture of the shepherd who will "gather the lambs in his arms" and "carry them in his bosom" (Isa 40:11). Moses spoke of God carrying Israel through the wilderness "as a man carries his son" (Deut 1:31). "Forever" means not only through this crisis.
  • From "me" to "us." The psalm began with one man afraid of going down to the pit. It ends with that man praying for the whole people of God. Meyer, cited by Guzik, observes that when we are nearest the Lord, we start caring about what he cares about, and above all about his flock.
  • Commentators: Henry reads "his anointed" two ways: David "in the type," whose strength was the strength of the whole kingdom, and Christ "in the anti-type," God's Messiah, who "diffuses strength to all the members." He notes the KJV margin reads "rule them" for "feed them." JFB takes the distinction between the people and the anointed as a hint of Absalom's rebellion and reads "feed them" as "as a shepherd (Ps 23:1)." Guzik notes that "anointed" is the word that gives us "Messiah."

Pointing to Christ

David prayed that he would not be left in God's silence and go down to the pit. Jesus prayed in Gethsemane with loud cries and tears "to him who was able to save him from death, and he was heard" (Heb 5:7). Yet the answer came through the pit, not around it. He was betrayed by one who "spoke peace" with a kiss, and he was dragged off with the wicked, "numbered with the transgressors" (Isa 53:12). He took the judgment that this psalm asks God to give the wicked, so that the wicked who turn to him would not have to receive "according to their work." God was the "saving refuge of his anointed" when he raised him from the dead. And the anointed King now does what v9 asks: he is the good shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep (John 10:11), and he carries his people forever.

Reflection

  • God's Silence Feels Like Death (v.1) — David fears that if God does not answer, he will become like those who go down to the grave. When God feels silent, do you keep crying out, or do you go quiet too?
  • Beware Peaceful Words With Harmful Hearts (v.3) — David warns of people who speak kindly to their neighbors while planning mischief. Are you discerning enough to notice when someone's words do not match their intentions?
  • From Trust to Joy to Praise (v.7) — David describes a sequence that begins with trust and ends in praise once God proves to be his strength. Does your trust in God consistently lead you toward joy and praise, or does it stall out along the way?
  • Praying Beyond Yourself (v.9) — David moves from his personal crisis to interceding for God's people as a whole. Does your prayer life expand outward to others once your own need has been met?
