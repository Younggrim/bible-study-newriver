1Simon Peter, a servant and apostle of Jesus Christ, To those who through the righteousness of our God and Savior Jesus Christ have received a faith as precious as ours:

2Grace and peace be multiplied to you through the knowledge of God and of Jesus our Lord.

3His divine power has given us everything we need for life and godliness through the knowledge of Him who called us by His own glory and excellence.

4Through these He has given us His precious and magnificent promises, so that through them you may become partakers of the divine nature, now that you have escaped the corruption in the world caused by evil desires.

5For this very reason, make every effort to add to your faith virtue; and to virtue, knowledge;

6and to knowledge, self-control; and to self-control, perseverance; and to perseverance, godliness;

7and to godliness, brotherly kindness; and to brotherly kindness, love.

8For if you possess these qualities and continue to grow in them, they will keep you from being ineffective and unproductive in your knowledge of our Lord Jesus Christ.

9But whoever lacks these traits is nearsighted to the point of blindness, having forgotten that he has been cleansed from his past sins.

10Therefore, brothers, strive to make your calling and election sure. For if you practice these things you will never stumble,

11and you will receive a lavish reception into the eternal kingdom of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ.

12Therefore I will always remind you of these things, even though you know them and are established in the truth you now have.

13I think it is right to refresh your memory as long as I live in the tent of my body,

14because I know that this tent will soon be laid aside, as our Lord Jesus Christ has made clear to me.

15And I will make every effort to ensure that after my departure, you will be able to recall these things at all times.

16For we did not follow cleverly devised fables when we made known to you the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, but we were eyewitnesses of His majesty.

17For He received honor and glory from God the Father when the voice came to Him from the Majestic Glory, saying, “This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.”

18And we ourselves heard this voice from heaven when we were with Him on the holy mountain.

19We also have the word of the prophets as confirmed beyond doubt. And you will do well to pay attention to it, as to a lamp shining in a dark place, until the day dawns and the morning star rises in your hearts.

20Above all, you must understand that no prophecy of Scripture comes from one’s own interpretation.

21For no such prophecy was ever brought forth by the will of man, but men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit.

Summary

Peter greets his readers and reminds them that God's divine power has granted them everything needed for life and godliness through the knowledge of Christ, along with precious promises that let them share in the divine nature and escape the world's corruption (vv. 1-4). He urges them to build on their faith a chain of qualities — virtue, knowledge, self-control, steadfastness, godliness, brotherly affection, and love — since possessing these in increasing measure keeps a believer fruitful, while lacking them leaves a person blind and forgetful of having been cleansed from past sins (vv. 5-9). He calls them to confirm their calling and election through such diligence, promising a rich entrance into Christ's eternal kingdom (vv. 10-11). Knowing his death is near, Peter says he intends to keep reminding them of these truths so they remember after he is gone (vv. 12-15). He insists that the apostles' message rests not on cleverly invented stories but on their own eyewitness experience of Christ's majesty, including the voice they heard from heaven on the mountain of transfiguration (vv. 16-18). This confirms the prophetic word, to which readers do well to pay attention, since no prophecy of Scripture came from human will but from men who spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit (vv. 19-21).

Authorship & Background

Author: Simon Peter (v.1). Written approximately AD 65-68, shortly before Peter's martyrdom (v.14; cf. John 21:18-19). This is Peter's final letter — his "last will and testament" to the church.
Recipient: The same audience as 1 Peter (3:1) — believers scattered throughout Asia Minor.
Purpose: To warn against false teachers (ch.2) and scoffers who deny Christ's return (ch.3), while encouraging believers to grow in knowledge and godliness (ch.1). Peter knows his death is near and wants to leave a lasting reminder.
Theme: True knowledge of God vs. false teaching. "Knowledge" (gnōsis/ epignōsis) appears 16 times in 3 chapters. True knowledge produces godliness; false knowledge produces immorality.
Classification: Epistle — General
Key Themes: Everything needed for life and godliness already given, virtues added in sequence, a calling made sure, an eyewitness of the transfiguration, and prophecy that did not originate with its speakers
Greeting: Like Precious Faith (vv.1-2): Peter writes as “a servant and an apostle” and addresses those who have obtained “like precious faith” — faith of equal standing with his own. For a letter that will spend chapter 2 attacking false teachers who claimed superior knowledge, beginning by leveling himself with his readers is a considered move. Grace and peace are wished “through the knowledge of God”, and knowledge is the word the whole letter contests.
All Things Given, Great Promises (vv.3-4): The claim is that divine power has already given everything pertaining to life and godliness. Nothing further is awaited and nothing secret is missing, which cuts directly against teachers offering hidden advancement. “Partakers of the divine nature” is strong language and is bounded by the same verse: it is the means of escaping corruption, not absorption into deity. The promises are called “exceeding great and precious”, and they are the mechanism, not a reward held in reserve.
Add to Your Faith: The Chain of Virtues (vv.5-9): Seven qualities are added in sequence — virtue, knowledge, temperance, patience, godliness, brotherly kindness, charity — each supplied on top of the last, with love at the end. The construction is deliberate: this is growth with an order rather than a checklist. Verse 8 makes the promise practical, that these things keep a believer from being barren or unfruitful, and v.9 gives the alternative bluntly — whoever lacks them is blind and has forgotten he was cleansed.
Make Your Calling Sure (vv.10-15): “Give diligence to make your calling and election sure.” The certainty in view is the believer's own assurance, evidenced by the growth just described, and the promise attached is that such a person will never fall. Peter then says plainly that he intends to keep reminding them as long as he is in “this tabernacle”, and that he knows his death is near — which reads as a man deliberately leaving something behind. The letter is being written as a legacy.
We Were Eyewitnesses (vv.16-18): Against the charge of following “cunningly devised fables” Peter offers testimony: he was on the holy mount and heard the voice. The transfiguration is cited not as a mystical credential but as evidence in a dispute about reliability. He is a witness rather than a theorist, and the distinction is the point — the majesty he speaks of was seen with eyes.
Prophecy Not of Private Interpretation (vv.19-21): The written word is called “a light that shineth in a dark place” and in one sense preferred to his own experience, since it is available to every reader while the mount was not. Verse 20's “private interpretation” is most naturally about origin rather than reading method, as v.21 explains: prophecy came not by the will of man but as men spoke moved by the Holy Ghost. The argument sets a text with a divine source against teachers with only themselves to appeal to.

Setting: This is Peter's farewell letter. He knows his death is near (v14), so he is probably writing from Rome in the mid-60s AD, shortly before he was executed under Nero, as early church tradition holds. He calls it his "second letter" (3:1), so the readers are probably the same churches in Asia Minor who received 1 Peter. The danger has changed. 1 Peter dealt with pressure from outside, slander and persecution. 2 Peter deals with a threat from inside: false teachers in the churches who scoff at the promise of Christ's return and use grace as an excuse for immoral living (2:1–3; 3:3–4). No New Testament book's authorship has been more debated. Many modern scholars think a later follower wrote it in Peter's name, pointing to its different Greek style and its close overlap with Jude. Others, including most evangelical scholars, accept it as Peter's, noting that the letter itself claims his name, his eyewitness experience on the mountain (vv16–18) and his coming death, and that a different secretary or purpose could explain the style. Chapter 1 lays the foundation: what God has given, how believers should grow, and why the apostles' message about Christ is trustworthy.

Greeting (vv1–2)

  • "Simeon Peter" (v1). "Simeon" is the old Hebrew form of his name, used of him only here and in Acts 15:14, where James calls him that at the Jerusalem council. "Peter" (Petros, "rock") is the name Jesus gave him (Matt 16:18). The two names together sum up his story: the fisherman from Galilee and the apostle Christ made him.
  • "A servant and apostle of Jesus Christ" (v1). "Servant" (doulos, slave) comes first. In the Old Testament, "servant of the LORD" was an honored title for Moses, Joshua and David. Peter is under Christ's authority before he has any of his own.
  • "Those who have obtained a faith of equal standing with ours" (v1). "Equal standing" (isotimos) means "equally precious" or "of the same honor." It was used of foreigners who received the same civic rights as citizens. Gentile believers who never saw Jesus have exactly the same faith as the apostles who did. "Obtained" (lanchanō) means to receive by lot, by gift: they did not earn it.
  • "By the righteousness of our God and Savior Jesus Christ" (v1). In Greek one article governs both nouns, "the God and Savior of us, Jesus Christ." By the normal rules of Greek grammar this names one person, so Jesus is called "God" (compare Titus 2:13; John 20:28). Peter uses exactly the same construction in v11, "our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ," where no one doubts that one person is meant.
  • "Grace and peace be multiplied to you in the knowledge of God and of Jesus our Lord" (v2). The greeting is the same as 1 Peter 1:2, with one addition: "in the knowledge." "Knowledge" (epignōsis, full or personal knowledge) is a key word of this letter (vv2, 3, 8; 2:20). The false teachers will claim special knowledge. Peter insists that true knowledge is knowing God and Jesus personally, and that it shows itself in a changed life.

Confirm Your Calling and Election (vv3–15)

  • "His divine power has granted to us all things that pertain to life and godliness" (v3). The grammar likely points back to Jesus (v2). "All things" means nothing essential is missing. Believers do not need the false teachers' extra secrets. "Godliness" (eusebeia) is reverent, God-honoring living, and it appears four times in this letter.
  • "Him who called us to his own glory and excellence" (v3). "Excellence" (aretē) was a prized word in Greek moral philosophy, the virtue or quality that makes something what it should be. Peter applies it first to God and then asks believers to add it to their faith (v5).
  • "Precious and very great promises" (v4). In this letter the central promise is Christ's return and the new heavens and new earth (3:4, 9, 13). The promises are the means God uses to transform his people.
  • "That through them you may become partakers of the divine nature" (v4). This is one of the most striking phrases in the New Testament. It does not mean believers become God or are absorbed into him. The Tyndale notes, JFB and most interpreters read it as sharing in God's moral character, his holiness and love, by the Spirit's work in us (1 Pet 1:15–16; Eph 4:24). Eastern Orthodox theology builds its doctrine of theosis (union with God) partly on this verse, while still insisting that the creature never becomes the Creator. Peter used "partaker" (koinōnos) of sharing in the coming glory (1 Pet 5:1).
  • "Having escaped from the corruption that is in the world because of sinful desire" (v4). Here is the contrast with chapter 2: the false teachers promise freedom while they themselves are "slaves of corruption" (2:19). The root of the world's decay is "sinful desire" (epithymia), wanting what God forbids.
  • "Make every effort to supplement your faith with virtue" (v5). God has given everything (v3), and so believers must "make every effort." Grace does not cancel effort; it fuels it (Phil 2:12–13). "Supplement" is epichorēgeō, to supply or provide. It is built on chorēgos, the wealthy citizen in Greek cities who paid for the chorus in a public drama and often outdid himself in generosity. The picture is lavish provision, not grudging addition. The same word comes back in v11: God will "richly provide" an entrance.
  • The ladder of qualities (vv5–7). Peter lists eight qualities in a chain, each step "with" the next. This form, called a sorites, was common in ancient moral writing (compare Rom 5:3–5). The point is not a strict order but that each quality grows into and needs the others, beginning with faith and ending with love. Faith: the foundation, trust in Christ. Virtue (aretē): moral excellence, the courage to do what is good. Knowledge (gnōsis): practical understanding of God's will, discernment. Self-control (enkrateia): mastery over desires and appetites, the opposite of the false teachers' indulgence (2:10, 14). Steadfastness (hypomonē): endurance, holding up under pressure, the virtue that waits for the Lord's return. Godliness (eusebeia): a life lived in reverence toward God. Brotherly affection (philadelphia): warm family love among believers. Love (agapē): self-giving love for all, the crown of the list (1 Cor 13:13).
  • "They keep you from being ineffective or unfruitful" (v8). "Ineffective" (argos) means idle, lazy, not working. A faith that knows Christ but never grows is a contradiction. Jesus told the parable of the fig tree that took up ground without fruit (Luke 13:6–9).
  • "So nearsighted that he is blind" (v9). "Nearsighted" is myōpazō, which gives English "myopia." The lexicon allows two senses: to be short-sighted, or to blink and close the eyes. Either way, this person sees only what is close at hand and has lost sight of the eternal. Worse, he has "forgotten that he was cleansed from his former sins." Forgetting the gospel is the root of a fruitless life.
  • "Be all the more diligent to confirm your calling and election" (v10). God calls and chooses, and nothing can change his purpose. But believers can be sure of it in their own hearts only as they see his fruit in their lives. Peter does not make salvation depend on effort. He makes assurance grow with obedience. Calvinist and Arminian readers both use this verse. The first see good works as evidence of an unshakable election, the second as a warning that perseverance matters. Both agree that a fruitless "faith" should not feel secure.
  • "If you practice these qualities you will never fall" (v10). "Fall" here means to stumble so badly as to come to ruin, not to make any mistake at all (James 3:2). The contrast is with the false teachers and their followers, who do fall away (2:20–22).
  • "There will be richly provided for you an entrance into the eternal kingdom" (v11). "Richly provided" uses the same chorus-funding verb as v5. The believer supplies virtue; God supplies a lavish welcome home. Peter's heaven is not a narrow escape but a grand arrival.
  • "I intend always to remind you" (vv12–13). Peter writes to people who already know the truth. Reminding is not insulting. Most Christian growth is remembering what has been forgotten (v9). "Stir you up" (diegeirō) means to wake someone from sleep, the word used when the disciples woke Jesus in the storm (Mark 4:38).
  • "As long as I am in this body" (v13). The word is skēnōma, a tent or tabernacle. Paul uses the same picture: "the tent that is our earthly home" (2 Cor 5:1). A tent is temporary and easily taken down, as Peter knows his will be.
  • "The putting off of my body will be soon, as our Lord Jesus Christ made clear to me" (v14). This is most likely Jesus' words by the Sea of Galilee: "when you are old, you will stretch out your hands, and another will dress you and carry you where you do not want to go," which John explains was about "by what kind of death he was to glorify God" (John 21:18–19). Early church tradition says Peter was crucified in Rome, and later tradition adds that he asked to be crucified upside down.
  • "After my departure" (v15). "Departure" is exodos, literally "a way out," used of the Israelites leaving Egypt and as a polite word for death. It is the same word Luke uses at the transfiguration, where Moses and Elijah spoke with Jesus "of his departure, which he was about to accomplish at Jerusalem" (Luke 9:31). Peter has just used "tent" (v13), the word he blurted out on the mountain ("let us make three tents," Luke 9:33), and he goes straight on to the transfiguration in v16. The memory of that day seems to shape his words.
  • "So that… you may be able at any time to recall these things" (v15). Peter wants his teaching to outlast him. Some think this points to this letter itself. An early tradition from Papias says Mark wrote down Peter's preaching in his Gospel, and some see that as part of what Peter means.
  • Commentators: JFB and Guzik both read v1 as calling Jesus "our God and Saviour," with JFB explaining that one article governs both nouns. All three sources say "partakers of the divine nature" means sharing God's holiness, not his essence. JFB puts it plainly: "not God's essence, but His holiness." On v5 JFB and Guzik both explain the chorus background of "supplement," which the lexicon's word history supports. Guzik borrows Adam Clarke's picture of dancers joined hand in hand, while JFB notes the list adds seven qualities to faith, "the perfect number." Henry stresses that "Faith unites the weak believer to Christ, as really as it does the strong one." On v10 JFB says election is made sure "in respect to our part," not God's, matching the Tyndale notes. On v11 Guzik contrasts a waterlogged ship limping into harbor with one coming in under full sail, and JFB writes, "The reward of grace hereafter shall correspond to the work of grace here." On v15 JFB points out that exodos is "the very word… used in the Transfiguration" and treats the shared tent language as evidence that Peter wrote the letter.

Christ's Glory and the Prophetic Word (vv16–21)

  • "We did not follow cleverly devised myths" (v16). "Myths" (mythoi) were the stories of the Greek gods, tales everyone knew were made up. The false teachers apparently said that the apostles' teaching about Christ's return was such a tale (3:3–4). Peter answers with eyewitness testimony.
  • "The power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ" (v16). "Coming" is parousia, the word for a king's official arrival or visit, and the standard New Testament term for Christ's return (Matt 24:27; 1 Thess 4:15). The dispute in this letter is about the future, and Peter grounds the future in something he saw.
  • "We were eyewitnesses of his majesty" (v16). "Eyewitnesses" (epoptai) was used in the Greek mystery religions for those initiated into the highest level, who had "seen" the secret rites. Peter may be turning the word: the apostles are not initiates into secret myths but witnesses of something that happened in public history.
  • "When he received honor and glory from God the Father" (v17). This is the transfiguration (Matt 17:1–8; Mark 9:2–8; Luke 9:28–36), where Jesus' face shone like the sun and his clothes became white as light. Peter, James and John saw a preview of the glory Jesus will have when he returns. All three Gospels place the transfiguration right after Jesus' promise that some standing there would see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom (Matt 16:28).
  • "The voice was borne to him by the Majestic Glory" (v17). "Majestic Glory" is a reverent way of saying God, much like "the Majesty on high" (Heb 1:3). The words are "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased," echoing Psalm 2:7 and Isaiah 42:1. In the Gospels the voice adds, "listen to him," spoken just after Peter's rash proposal to build three tents (Mark 9:5–7). Peter remembers the voice that corrected him.
  • "We were with him on the holy mountain" (v18). The Gospels do not name the mountain. Tradition from the fourth century points to Mount Tabor in Galilee. Many scholars prefer Mount Hermon, near Caesarea Philippi where the previous scene took place. Peter calls it "holy" because God's presence made it so, as at Sinai (Exod 3:5).
  • "We have the prophetic word more fully confirmed" (v19). There are two main readings. (1) The Old Testament prophecies are now made more certain because the transfiguration confirmed them. (2) The prophetic Scriptures are an even surer witness than Peter's experience on the mountain. JFB rejects the second, saying the transfiguration "gives us a pledge" that strengthens the prophetic word. Guzik leans toward the second. Most modern translations, including the ESV, follow the first.
  • "As to a lamp shining in a dark place" (v19). "Dark" (auchmēros) means dry, dirty, squalid, like a gloomy, neglected room. Scripture is the lamp that lets believers find their way through such a world (Ps 119:105).
  • "Until the day dawns and the morning star rises in your hearts" (v19). The "day" is the day of the Lord, when Christ returns. "Morning star" is phōsphoros, "light-bearer," the name for Venus, which shines just before sunrise. Jesus calls himself "the bright morning star" (Rev 22:16), and Numbers 24:17 foretold that "a star shall come out of Jacob." "In your hearts" probably means that at his coming believers will know him fully, and the lamp of prophecy will no longer be needed.
  • "No prophecy of Scripture comes from someone's own interpretation" (v20). "Interpretation" is epilysis, a loosening, a solving or explaining, used only here. There are two readings. Many think Peter is speaking about where prophecy came from: the prophets did not make up their own explanations of what they saw. Others think he is speaking about how prophecy is read: no individual reader is free to twist it to suit himself, as the false teachers do (3:16). Verse 21 suggests Peter has the first mainly in mind, but both are true.
  • "Men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit" (v21). "Carried along" (pherō) is the word Luke uses of Paul's ship "driven along" by the storm (Acts 27:15, 17). The prophets were fully human: they "spoke," each in his own style and setting. But the Spirit carried them, so what they said came "from God." Paul says the same thing in different words (2 Tim 3:16). Peter has used the same verb for the voice "borne" from heaven (vv17–18): God's voice was carried down on the mountain, and God's word was carried through the prophets.
  • Commentators: Guzik and Henry both see the transfiguration voice as personal for Peter. Guzik notes that the same voice that once rebuked him had become a treasured memory. JFB calls v16 "a precautionary protest of the Spirit against the rationalistic theory" that the gospel is myth. JFB and Guzik differ over v19, as above, and JFB identifies the morning star as "the Lord Jesus." On v20 JFB says prophecies "were not mere words of the individuals," taking the verse to be about origin. Guzik applies it as a warning that modern claims to prophecy must be tested by Scripture. On v21 JFB and Guzik both explain the sailing image. JFB makes the prophets "passive, rather than active instruments," which goes further than Guzik, who has the prophets raising their sails and cooperating as God carries them. Peter's own wording, "men spoke," suggests real human involvement.

Pointing to Christ

The chapter is full of Jesus: the "God and Savior" whose righteousness gives faith (v1), whose divine power supplies everything for life (v3), whose kingdom will open wide (v11), whose words shaped Peter's death (v14), whose glory Peter saw on the mountain (v17) and who will rise like the morning star (v19). Peter's case against the scoffers is not a clever argument but a person: the beloved Son on whom the Father's voice rested. The same Jesus who revealed his glory for a moment on the mountain will reveal it to the whole world when he comes.

Reflection

  • Given Everything for Life and Godliness (v.3) — Peter says God's power has already granted believers everything needed for life and godliness through knowing Christ. Are you drawing on what's already yours, or still searching for something more?
  • Building the Ladder of Christian Character (vv.5-7) — Peter lists qualities believers should deliberately add to their faith, one building on the next, from self-control to brotherly love. Which quality on that list needs the most attention in your life right now?
  • Making Your Calling and Election Sure (v.10) — Believers are urged to confirm their calling through diligent, visible growth rather than passive assumption. Does your life produce real evidence of a growing faith?
  • Eyewitnesses of Christ's Majesty (vv.16-18) — Peter insists his message isn't invented myth but firsthand testimony of seeing Christ's glory on the mountain. Does your confidence in the gospel rest on that kind of solid ground?
  • Scripture Carried Along by the Holy Spirit (v.21) — Peter explains that Scripture didn't originate in human imagination but came as men were carried along by the Holy Spirit. Do you treat the Bible as God's word, or quietly as just another human opinion?
