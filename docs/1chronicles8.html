Summary

The chapter details Benjamin's genealogy beginning with Bela and his brothers (vv. 1-5), then traces family heads who settled in Geba, Moab, and Jerusalem, including the builders of Ono and Lod (vv. 6-28). It turns to the family of Gibeon, whose descendants include Ner, Kish, and ultimately King Saul and his son Jonathan (vv. 29-33). The line continues through Jonathan's son Merib-baal down through many generations to Azel's sons and the skilled archers of Ulam (vv. 34-40), preparing the reader for Saul's death narrated in chapter 10.

Authorship & Background

Author: Traditionally attributed to Ezra the scribe. Originally one book with 2 Chronicles. Written post-exile (approximately 450-400 BC) for the returned remnant. The Chronicler retells Israel's history from a priestly/worship perspective, emphasizing the Davidic covenant, Temple worship, and God's faithfulness. Key themes: genealogical continuity (God preserved His people), proper worship (the Temple and its services), the Davidic line (pointing to Messiah), and hope for restoration.
Historical Context: Chapter 8 provides an extensive genealogy of Benjamin — the second most important tribe for the post-exilic community (after Judah). Benjamin's significance is threefold: (1) Jerusalem straddled the border between Judah and Benjamin, so both tribes had claims on the holy city; (2) Benjamin was the tribe of Saul, Israel's first king; and (3) the post-exilic community was primarily composed of Judah and Benjamin (Ezra 1:5; 4:1).
Classification: Historical Narrative — Genealogy
Key Themes: A second and far longer treatment of Benjamin than the tribe received in the previous chapter, families tracked by where they resettled and remarried after near destruction in Judges twenty, a list narrowing at last to one household, Kish and Saul and his four sons, Jonathan's own line followed ten more generations through the man 2 Samuel calls Mephibosheth, and Saul given a family here with no reign, no failure and no death, since the narrative saves all three for chapter ten
The Sons of Benjamin (vv.1-28): Benjamin gets a second and much longer treatment than in chapter 7, and the reason arrives in the last section: this chapter exists to reach Saul. Twenty-eight verses of families, with settlements at Geba, Moab, Ono, Lod and Aijalon, and a note that some drove away the inhabitants of Gath. The Chronicler is unusually interested in where these families moved and remarried, which for a tribe nearly destroyed in Judges 20 is a record of survival.
The House of Saul (vv.29-40): The list narrows to Gibeon and then to one household: Kish, and Saul, and Saul's four sons, Jonathan, Malchi-shua, Abinadab and Esh-baal. Jonathan's line is then followed for ten more generations through Merib-baal, the Mephibosheth of 2 Samuel. Saul is given no reign, no failure and no death here, only a family. The Chronicler will kill him in chapter 10 and this is the only place he tells us who he was.

Map & Geography

  • Largely parallels 1-2 Samuel geographically. Jerusalem (Zion/City of David) is the theological and political center.
  • Key sites: Hebron (David's first capital, 7 years), the threshing floor of Ornan/Araunah (future Temple site, ch.21).
  • Chapters 1-9 (genealogies) contain tribal allotments but minimal narrative geography.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik notes that Benjamin receives extended treatment because of the tribe's dual significance: the tribe of the first king (Saul) and the tribe that remained loyal to Judah after the division of the kingdom. He emphasizes that the Chronicler's use of "Baal" names (Esh-baal, Merib-baal) reflects historical accuracy rather than theological endorsement.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Restored from Near Extinction (vv.1-28) — Benjamin was once reduced to a mere 600 men after the civil war of Judges 19-21, yet here the tribe fills an entire chapter with clans, warriors, and city-builders. What part of your own story feels too far gone for God to rebuild?
  • God Sees Past the Label (v.33) — Saul's sons carry names built on "Baal," yet they were faithful members of Israel, not pagan worshipers. Are you quick to judge someone by a name, background, or label instead of looking at who they actually are?
  • Faithfulness That Outlasts a Lifetime (v.34) — David's covenant kindness to Merib-baal produced a line of descendants recorded for generations afterward. What promise or act of kindness could you extend today that might still be bearing fruit long after you're gone?
  • A Small Tribe, A Disproportionate Impact (v.40) — Benjamin was the smallest of the tribes, yet it produced mighty archers and a large, capable family. Where might God want to use your small size or limited resources for outsized impact?
