Summary

Ezra records a census of the exiles who return from Babylon to Judah and Jerusalem under Zerubbabel's leadership, listing them by family, town of origin, and role (vv. 1-35). The priests, Levites, singers, gatekeepers, and temple servants are counted separately, along with the descendants of Solomon's servants (vv. 36-58). Some families cannot prove their genealogical descent from Israel and are barred from serving as priests until a priest can consult the Urim and Thummim to settle the question (vv. 59-63). The whole assembly totals about 42,360 people, plus servants, singers, and livestock, and many of the leaders give generously toward rebuilding the temple once they arrive in Jerusalem (vv. 64-70).

Authorship & Background

Author: Ezra the priest and scribe (likely author/compiler). Written approximately 450-400 BC. Ezra covers two returns from Babylonian exile: (1) Zerubbabel's return and Temple rebuilding (538-516 BC, chapters 1-6); (2) Ezra's return and spiritual reform (458 BC, chapters 7-10).
Classification: Historical Narrative — Post-Exilic
Key Themes: A register kept by name and family, organization by clan and then by town, a shortage of Levites that will recur, those unable to prove their descent, and giving offered before anything is built
Historical Context: Chapter 2 is the census of the returning exiles — a detailed register of 42,360 Israelites (plus 7,337 servants) who made the long journey from Babylon to Jerusalem under Zerubbabel's leadership. This list is paralleled almost identically in Nehemiah 7:6-73, confirming its official documentary status. The list is organized by family/clan (vv.3-20), by town of origin (vv.21-35), by priestly families (vv.36-39), by Levites, singers, and gatekeepers (vv.40-42), by Temple servants/Nethinim (vv.43-58), and finally by those who could not prove their genealogy (vv.59-63). The meticulous record-keeping serves a vital theological purpose: God knows His people by name and family. Every individual who responded to the call is recorded. The relatively small number of Levites (74) compared to priests (4,289) foreshadows the recurring problem of Levitical shortage in the post-exilic community (see Ezra 8:15-20). The chapter concludes with the returnees settling in their ancestral towns and giving generously for the Temple rebuilding — a community reconstituting itself on the land God had promised their fathers.
The Register of Those Who Returned (vv.1-2): The chapter is a census, and the list is reproduced almost identically in Nehemiah 7:6-73, which indicates an official document rather than a summary. Eleven leaders are named with Zerubbabel and Jeshua at the head. What follows is not narrative and is not meant to be read as one — it is the community's roll, and the point of keeping it is that these particular people answered.
Counted by Family (vv.3-20): The first grouping is by clan, family by family with numbers attached. The figures run from thousands down to dozens, so the register does not round or tidy. Reading a list of household names is the least dramatic passage in the book and the most concrete evidence of what the return actually consisted of: not a movement, but these families.
Counted by Town (vv.21-35): The organizing principle changes halfway through, from descent to place — the men of Bethlehem, of Netophah, of Anathoth, of Kiriath-arim. The shift matters because these people are going back to towns their grandparents left, and identity is being recorded both by blood and by ground. Both were needed to reclaim an inheritance.
Priests, Levites, Singers and Servants (vv.36-58): The temple personnel are counted separately: 4,289 priests, and 74 Levites. That gap is the most consequential number in the chapter. Priests returned in strength and Levites did not, and the shortage recurs at 8:15-20 when Ezra finds no Levites in his own company and has to recruit them. Singers, gatekeepers and the Nethinim follow, all listed by household.
Those Who Could Not Prove Their Genealogy (vv.59-70): Some could not produce their descent, and the priests among them are set aside from holy things until a priest should arise with Urim and Thummim — a suspension rather than a rejection, and one with no stated end. The totals close the register at 42,360 with 7,337 servants. The last verses record giving toward the work and the people settling in their own cities, so the chapter ends with a community placed on the ground before anything is built.

Setting: Ezra 1 ended with Cyrus's decree (538 BC) and the temple vessels handed over for the journey home. Ezra 2 lists who went. It is a register of families and towns, priests and Levites, singers and gatekeepers, temple servants and the household staff of the returnees, with a count of their animals and their gifts for the temple. Lists like this can feel tedious to modern readers, but this one mattered deeply. After the exile, the returning community had to answer basic questions: Who is a true Israelite? Who may serve as a priest? Which families belong to which towns? The list answered them, and it showed that God's people had survived judgment as real families with real names. The same list, with small differences in names and numbers, appears again in Nehemiah 7:6–73, where Nehemiah finds "the book of the genealogy of those who came up at the first" (Neh 7:5) nearly a century later. A third version appears in the apocryphal book 1 Esdras (5:4–46). The Tyndale notes suggest the list may have been drawn up a little later than the first journey, recording people as they settled in their towns, rather than as a single headcount on the day they left. That would help explain why the versions differ. It is not certain whether everyone listed traveled in one caravan in 538 or in several groups in the years that followed. The ESV gives the whole chapter one heading, "The Exiles Return." The subheadings below follow the list's own divisions. As a list, it gets a shorter treatment than a narrative chapter.

The Leaders of the Return (vv1–2)

  • "The people of the province" (v1). Judah was no longer a kingdom. It was a small province of the Persian Empire, part of the vast satrapy (governing district) called "Beyond the River," meaning west of the Euphrates. JFB describes it as reduced to "an obscure, servile, tributary province." The returnees were still Persian subjects, and would remain so.
  • "Each to his own town" (v1). They went back, as far as possible, to the towns their families had come from. Land in Israel was tied to families and tribes (Num 26:52–56; Lev 25:23), so going home meant reclaiming an inheritance.
  • "They came with Zerubbabel, Jeshua, Nehemiah, Seraiah…" (v2). Eleven names are given here. Nehemiah 7:7 has twelve, adding Nahamani, which may have dropped out of Ezra's copy. Twelve leaders would match the twelve tribes of Israel, a quiet claim that the returnees stood for the whole people, not just Judah. Ezra 6:17 makes the same point when the dedication offering includes "twelve male goats, according to the number of the tribes of Israel."
  • Zerubbabel and Jeshua. These two led the return. Zerubbabel was a grandson of King Jehoiachin (1 Chr 3:17–19), the heir of David's royal line, though he served as a Persian-appointed governor rather than a king (Hag 1:1). Jeshua (a form of Joshua) was the high priest, grandson of the last high priest before the exile (1 Chr 6:15; Ezra 3:2). Together they stand for the two great offices of Israel, king and priest. The prophet Zechariah saw them as "the two anointed ones who stand by the Lord of the whole earth" (Zech 4:14), and Zerubbabel appears in both genealogies of Jesus (Matt 1:12; Luke 3:27). The Tyndale notes point out that the Nehemiah and Mordecai of v2 are not the famous men of those names. Nehemiah the wall-builder came almost a century later, and Esther's Mordecai lived in Susa.
  • Commentators: JFB thinks the leaders' names were probably taken from Persian archives and singles out "Jeshua, the high priest, and Nehemiah." Henry draws a sober lesson from how small the returning people were compared with Israel in its glory: "See how sin lowers a nation, which righteousness would exalt!" (compare Prov 14:34). Guzik follows those who identify Zerubbabel with the Sheshbazzar of 1:8.

The Families and the Towns (vv3–35)

  • "The number of the men of the people of Israel" (v2). The list counts men, probably heads of households and adult males, not women and children. "Sons of" (v3) means descendants or members of a clan, not literal sons. JFB notes the same.
  • Listed by family (vv3–20). Most of the first groups are named after a clan ancestor. Some names are unusual. "Parosh" (v3) means "flea," and "Pahath-moab" (v6) means "governor of Moab," perhaps a title from an ancestor's service. Guzik passes on Yamauchi's note that many exiles carried Babylonian and Persian names, as Zerubbabel himself did. "Adonikam" (v13) means "my Lord has arisen." His family numbers 666, a figure with no special meaning here, despite the attention the number gets from Revelation 13:18.
  • Listed by town (vv21–35). From v21 the list changes to people identified by hometown: "the men of Netophah," "the men of Anathoth." Not every family could trace a famous ancestor, but they knew where they came from. The towns are almost all in the old territory of Judah and Benjamin, within about 20 miles of Jerusalem. The returnees were settling a small area.
  • "The sons of Bethlehem, 123" (v21). David's hometown, and the town where Micah said a ruler would one day come from (Mic 5:2).
  • "The men of Anathoth, 128" (v23). This was Jeremiah's hometown, a priestly town in Benjamin (Jer 1:1). The people of Anathoth had tried to kill Jeremiah (Jer 11:21–23). Yet during the siege of Jerusalem, God told him to buy a field there, as a sign that "houses and fields and vineyards shall again be bought in this land" (Jer 32:6–15). Now 128 men of Anathoth come home. JFB notes the pleasure of seeing them return.
  • "The men of Bethel and Ai, 223" (v28). Bethel was where Jacob saw the ladder to heaven (Gen 28:19), and later the site of Jeroboam's golden calf (1 Kings 12:29). Ai was the town Israel failed to take because of Achan's sin, then conquered (Josh 7–8). Old names, with long histories of faith and failure, are being resettled.
  • "The sons of Jericho, 345" (v34). The first city Israel conquered under Joshua (Josh 6), rebuilt under a curse (1 Kings 16:34).
  • "The sons of Senaah, 3,630" (v35). The largest group in the list. Senaah's location is unknown. Some have suggested the name, which may be related to a word meaning "hated," refers to people of low social standing rather than a town. That is a guess.
  • Ezra and Nehemiah differ. About twenty numbers in this section differ between Ezra 2 and Nehemiah 7. For example, the sons of Arah are 775 here (v5) and 652 in Nehemiah 7:10. Several explanations have been offered. JFB and Poole, through Guzik, suggest some who registered to go died or changed their minds, so one list records those who enrolled and the other those who arrived. Others think the lists come from different dates. Copying errors are also likely, since Hebrew numbers were easy to miscopy.
  • Commentators: Henry notes simply that "an account was kept of the families that came up out of captivity." God knows his people by name, family and place. Guzik passes on Clarke's view that the three versions of the list, compared carefully, help correct one another.

Priests, Levites, Singers and Gatekeepers (vv36–42)

  • "The priests… 973… 1,052… 1,247… 1,017" (vv36–39). Four priestly families returned, a total of 4,289, about one in ten of all who came. David had organized the priests into twenty-four divisions that took turns serving at the temple (1 Chr 24:1–19). JFB notes that "only four of the courses of the priests returned," and that these were later divided again into twenty-four groups using the old names. By the time of Jesus, Zechariah, the father of John the Baptist, served in "the division of Abijah" (Luke 1:5), one of those restored names.
  • "The Levites… 74" (v40). A tiny number. The Levites assisted the priests and cared for the temple. For every Levite who came, there were about fifty-eight priests, the reverse of what would be expected. The Tyndale notes say it was a very low number. Later, when Ezra himself led a second group, he found "none of the sons of Levi" and had to send for them specially (Ezra 8:15–20). Why so few? The text does not say. Guzik suggests the Levites, who had lost many of their old roles, may have had less reason to leave settled lives in Babylon. Morgan, as Guzik reports, also remarks on the imbalance.
  • "The singers: the sons of Asaph, 128" (v41). Asaph was one of the music leaders David appointed for worship (1 Chr 16:5, 7; 25:1). Twelve psalms carry his name (Ps 50; 73–83). His descendants kept the family calling through the exile, and at the laying of the temple foundation they would lead the praise "with cymbals" (Ezra 3:10). The people who had hung up their lyres in Babylon because they could not "sing the LORD's song in a foreign land" (Ps 137:4) are taking them home.
  • "The sons of the gatekeepers… 139" (v42). Gatekeepers guarded the temple gates and storerooms and kept order (1 Chr 9:17–27; 26:1–19).

Temple Servants and Solomon's Servants (vv43–58)

  • "The temple servants" (v43). The Hebrew is Nethinim, "the given ones," from the verb natan, "to give." They were people given to the temple to help the Levites. Ezra 8:20 says David and his officials had appointed them. The Tyndale notes, like many interpreters, think they were descendants of the Gibeonites. Joshua made them "cutters of wood and drawers of water for the congregation and for the altar of the LORD" (Josh 9:27). That is a reasonable guess, not stated in the text.
  • Foreign names. Several names in vv43–54 sound foreign. "Meunim" (v50) recalls the Meunites, an Arabian people Uzziah defeated (2 Chr 26:7). "Nephisim" (v50) may connect to Naphish, a son of Ishmael (Gen 25:15). "Sisera" (v53) is the name of the Canaanite general killed by Jael (Judg 4:21). Kidner, as Guzik reports, suggests some were descended from captives taken in David's wars. Whatever their origins, these families of non-Israelite descent had served the God of Israel for generations, and chose to return with his people. The story of Rahab and Ruth continues.
  • "The sons of Solomon's servants" (v55). Solomon had drafted the remaining Canaanite peoples of the land as forced laborers for his building projects (1 Kings 9:20–21). JFB suggests they were either such conscripted foreigners or royal servants whose position was an honor. The Tyndale notes suggest some names describe jobs. "Hassophereth" (v55) means "the scribe," and "Pochereth-hazzebaim" (v57) may mean "binder of gazelles," perhaps a hunter.
  • "All the temple servants and the sons of Solomon's servants were 392" (v58). More than five times the number of Levites. The humblest temple workers were more willing to go back than many of those who held higher office.

Those Who Could Not Prove Their Descent (vv59–63)

  • "Tel-melah, Tel-harsha, Cherub, Addan, and Immer" (v59). Babylonian place names where Jewish exiles had lived. A tel is a mound, often formed by the ruins of an older town. Tel-melah means "mound of salt." Ezekiel lived at Tel-abib, another such settlement (Ezek 3:15).
  • "They could not prove their fathers' houses or their descent, whether they belonged to Israel" (v59). Six hundred and fifty-two people (v60) believed they were Israelites but had lost the family records that would prove it. The Tyndale notes explain why that mattered: belonging affected marriage, inheritance and worship. They were not turned away. They came with the others. But their status was uncertain. The genealogies of 1 Chronicles 1–9 show how carefully families kept such records.
  • "Of the sons of the priests… the sons of Barzillai" (v61). Barzillai the Gileadite was the wealthy old man who fed David and his men when they fled from Absalom (2 Sam 17:27–29; 19:31–39). A priest had married into his family and taken the family name, perhaps for its prestige. JFB is critical: they preferred "worldly ambition" to their priestly name, and lost the proof of their priestly line. Kidner, as Guzik reports, also sees the borrowed name as the source of the complication.
  • "They were excluded from the priesthood as unclean" (v62). "Excluded… as unclean" (vay'go'alu) means they were treated as defiled, barred from priestly duty. The law was strict about who could serve at the altar (Num 3:10; 16:40). After the disaster of the exile, the community wanted to get worship right.
  • "The governor told them that they were not to partake of the most holy food" (v63). "Governor" here is the Persian title Tirshatha, a word for an official of high rank. JFB connects it to a Persian word meaning "severe," though its origin is uncertain. The "most holy food" was the portion of sacrifices reserved for priests (Lev 2:3; 6:17–18).
  • "Until there should be a priest to consult Urim and Thummim" (v63). The Urim and Thummim were objects kept in the high priest's breastpiece and used to ask God for a decision (Exod 28:30; Num 27:21; 1 Sam 14:41). How they worked is not known. The Tyndale notes compare them to casting lots, with the outcome understood as God's decision. They are never mentioned in use after the early monarchy, and Jewish tradition holds that they were lost with the first temple. Adeney, as Guzik reports, notes that rabbinic tradition counts them among the things never recovered. So the decision was postponed, not denied. Guzik stresses that it was a pause pending clarity, not a final rejection. There is no record that the question was ever resolved.
  • Commentators: Henry draws a spiritual application: "Those who have no evidence that they are, by the new birth, spiritual priests unto God" have no claim to the privileges of believers. He also warns against despising a relationship with God in hard times, since those who do so cannot later claim it when it brings honor.

The Whole Assembly (vv64–67)

  • "The whole assembly together was 42,360" (v64). Nehemiah 7:66 and 1 Esdras give the same total. But the numbers listed in Ezra 2 add up to only 29,818. Nehemiah's list adds up to 31,089, and 1 Esdras to 30,143, as Kidner notes through Guzik. So all three versions agree on the total and disagree on the parts. The Tyndale notes suggest the total may include groups not itemized, perhaps women or children, or members of other tribes. JFB attributes the difference to omissions. The total agrees in all three, which suggests it was a fixed figure in the records. JFB estimates the whole company with servants at about fifty thousand.
  • "Besides their male and female servants, of whom there were 7,337" (v65). About one servant for every six free people. The Tyndale notes say the number of servants and animals shows that some returnees were wealthy. Exile had not left everyone poor.
  • "And they had 200 male and female singers" (v65). These are different from the temple singers of v41. They were probably singers for weddings, funerals and festivals (2 Sam 19:35; 2 Chr 35:25). Nehemiah 7:67 says 245.
  • "Their horses were 736, their mules were 245, their camels were 435, and their donkeys were 6,720" (vv66–67). Donkeys carried most of the goods and many of the people. Horses and mules were for the wealthier. The long journey of about 900 miles needed thousands of pack animals. JFB counts about 8,000 animals.
  • A small remnant. Guzik notes that most Jews stayed in Babylon, where many prospered, and that Jewish life continued there for centuries. Yamauchi's estimates, as Guzik reports, of the total Jewish population in Babylonia vary widely. Fifty thousand people returning was a small fraction of the nation that had once filled the land, a fulfillment of the prophets' word that "only a remnant of them will return" (Isa 10:22).

Gifts for the House of God (vv68–70)

  • "When they came to the house of the LORD that is in Jerusalem" (v68). The temple was still a ruin, and they called the place "the house of the LORD." JFB says that on reaching the site, the family heads gave for the temple before seeing to their own houses, in a surge of "piety and patriotism." Haggai would later rebuke the people for doing the opposite, living in "paneled houses, while this house lies in ruins" (Hag 1:4).
  • "Made freewill offerings… to erect it on its site" (v68). "On its site" means on its old foundation. They did not choose a new location. Worship was to be rebuilt on the place God had chosen (Deut 12:5; 2 Chr 3:1).
  • "According to their ability they gave" (v69). No one was told how much to give. The Tyndale notes connect this to the voluntary gifts for the tabernacle (Exod 35:4–9) and the temple repairs under Joash (2 Kings 12). Paul teaches the same principle: give "as he may prosper" (1 Cor 16:2) and "according to their means" (2 Cor 8:3). Guzik makes this connection too.
  • "61,000 darics of gold, 5,000 minas of silver, and 100 priests' garments" (v69). A daric was a Persian gold coin, weighing about a quarter ounce (8.5 grams), named after King Darius. JFB says "darics" is a better rendering than the KJV's "drams." Since darics were first minted under Darius I (522–486 BC), after this return, the writer is probably stating the value in the currency of his own day. A mina weighed about a pound and a quarter, so 5,000 minas is roughly three tons of silver. JFB calls the gift of priests' garments "very appropriate." Nehemiah 7:70–72 gives a fuller breakdown of these gifts by giver, with somewhat different figures.
  • "All the rest of Israel in their towns" (v70). The chapter ends with the people settled. "All Israel" is a deliberate phrase: this small company is the people of God, the whole nation in miniature. Jerusalem itself remained thinly populated for decades. Nehemiah later had to cast lots to move one family in ten into the city (Neh 11:1).
  • Commentators: Henry notes that the people's gifts were small beside those of David's princes, yet God accepted them because they gave according to their ability. He urges readers to "Seek first the kingdom of God, his favour and his glory," and not to grudge the cost of worship. Guzik links the phrase about giving by ability to Paul's teaching on giving.

Pointing to Christ

Ezra 2 is a list of names, and that is its point: God's people are not a faceless crowd. He knows them by family and by town, priests and gatekeepers, singers and temple servants, even those whose records were lost. That list has its fulfillment in "the book of life" (Phil 4:3; Rev 21:27), where every name of God's people is written. The heir of David, Zerubbabel, led them home, and from his line would come "Jesus, who is called Christ" (Matt 1:12–16). The priests who could not prove their descent waited for a priest who could hear from God. Jesus is that priest, "a priest forever, after the order of Melchizedek" (Heb 7:17), who does not need a genealogy to serve and who makes all who trust him "a royal priesthood" (1 Pet 2:9). Foreigners like the Nethinim and Solomon's servants went home with Israel, a picture of the Gentiles who, through Christ, "are no longer strangers and aliens, but… fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God" (Eph 2:19).

Reflection

  • God Keeps a Record of the Faithful (vv.1-2) — Every family who made the costly decision to leave Babylon is named and numbered, family by family. Does it change how you view your own quiet faithfulness to know God is keeping account of it?
  • Every Family Had a Role to Fill (vv.36-58) — Priests, Levites, singers, gatekeepers, and temple servants are each listed by their function within the rebuilt community. Have you identified the role God has given you to fill, or are you still standing on the sidelines?
  • Waiting for Verification, Not Rushing Ahead (vv.61-63) — Some who claimed priestly lineage but couldn't prove it were set aside until a priest with Urim and Thummim could confirm their status, rather than being waved through or permanently rejected. Where do you need patience to let God confirm a calling instead of assuming it for yourself?
  • A Small Remnant Was Enough (v.64) — Of the vast number carried into exile, only about 42,360 made the journey home, yet that remnant was sufficient for God's purposes. If you're part of a small, faithful minority somewhere, how does this encourage you that numbers aren't what God measures?
  • Giving According to Ability, Not Equality (vv.68-69) — Family leaders gave toward the Temple's rebuilding in proportion to what they had, not in equal amounts. Is your giving shaped by genuine sacrifice relative to what you've been given, or by comparison to others?
