1Zedekiah was twenty-one years old when he became king, and he reigned in Jerusalem eleven years. His mother’s name was Hamutal daughter of Jeremiah; she was from Libnah.

2And Zedekiah did evil in the sight of the LORD, just as Jehoiakim had done.

3For because of the anger of the LORD, all this happened in Jerusalem and Judah, until He finally banished them from His presence. And Zedekiah also rebelled against the king of Babylon.

4So in the ninth year of Zedekiah’s reign, on the tenth day of the tenth month, Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon marched against Jerusalem with his entire army. They encamped outside the city and built a siege wall all around it.

5And the city was kept under siege until King Zedekiah’s eleventh year.

6By the ninth day of the fourth month, the famine in the city was so severe that the people of the land had no food.

7Then the city was breached; and though the Chaldeans had surrounded the city, all the men of war fled the city by night by way of the gate between the two walls near the king’s garden. They headed toward the Arabah,

8but the army of the Chaldeans pursued the king and overtook Zedekiah in the plains of Jericho, and his whole army deserted him.

9The Chaldeans seized the king and brought him up to the king of Babylon at Riblah in the land of Hamath, where he pronounced judgment on Zedekiah.

10There at Riblah the king of Babylon slaughtered the sons of Zedekiah before his eyes, and he also killed all the officials of Judah.

11Then he put out Zedekiah’s eyes, bound him with bronze shackles, and took him to Babylon, where he kept him in custody until his dying day.

12On the tenth day of the fifth month, in the nineteenth year of Nebuchadnezzar’s reign over Babylon, Nebuzaradan captain of the guard, a servant of the king of Babylon, entered Jerusalem.

13He burned down the house of the LORD, the royal palace, and all the houses of Jerusalem—every significant building.

14And the whole army of the Chaldeans under the captain of the guard broke down all the walls around Jerusalem.

15Then Nebuzaradan captain of the guard carried into exile some of the poorest people and those who remained in the city, along with the deserters who had defected to the king of Babylon and the rest of the craftsmen.

16But Nebuzaradan captain of the guard left behind some of the poorest of the land to tend the vineyards and fields.

17Moreover, the Chaldeans broke up the bronze pillars and stands and the bronze Sea in the house of the LORD, and they carried all the bronze to Babylon.

18They also took away the pots, shovels, wick trimmers, sprinkling bowls, dishes, and all the articles of bronze used in the temple service.

19The captain of the guard also took away the basins, censers, sprinkling bowls, pots, lampstands, pans, and drink offering bowls—anything made of pure gold or fine silver.

20As for the two pillars, the Sea, the twelve bronze bulls under it, and the movable stands that King Solomon had made for the house of the LORD, the weight of the bronze from all these articles was beyond measure.

21Each pillar was eighteen cubits tall and twelve cubits in circumference; each was hollow, four fingers thick.

22The bronze capital atop one pillar was five cubits high, with a network of bronze pomegranates all around. The second pillar, with its pomegranates, was similar.

23Each capital had ninety-six pomegranates on the sides, and a total of a hundred pomegranates were above the surrounding network.

24The captain of the guard also took away Seraiah the chief priest, Zephaniah the priest of second rank, and the three doorkeepers.

25Of those still in the city, he took a court official who had been appointed over the men of war, as well as seven trusted royal advisers. He also took the scribe of the captain of the army, who had enlisted the people of the land, and sixty men who were found in the city.

26Nebuzaradan captain of the guard took them and brought them to the king of Babylon at Riblah.

27There at Riblah in the land of Hamath, the king of Babylon struck them down and put them to death. So Judah was taken into exile, away from its own land.

28These are the people Nebuchadnezzar carried away: in the seventh year, 3,023 Jews;

29in Nebuchadnezzar’s eighteenth year, 832 people from Jerusalem;

30in Nebuchadnezzar’s twenty-third year, Nebuzaradan captain of the guard carried away 745 Jews. So in all, 4,600 people were taken away.

31On the twenty-fifth day of the twelfth month of the thirty-seventh year of the exile of Jehoiachin king of Judah, in the first year of the reign of Evil-merodach king of Babylon, he pardoned Jehoiachin king of Judah and released him from prison.

32And he spoke kindly to Jehoiachin and set his throne above the thrones of the other kings who were with him in Babylon.

33So Jehoiachin changed out of his prison clothes, and he dined regularly at the king’s table for the rest of his life.

34And the king of Babylon provided Jehoiachin a daily portion for the rest of his life, until the day of his death.

1Zedekiah was one and twenty years old when he began to reign, and he reigned eleven years in Jerusalem. And his mother’s name was Hamutal the daughter of Jeremiah of Libnah.

2And he did that which was evil in the eyes of the LORD, according to all that Jehoiakim had done.

3For through the anger of the LORD it came to pass in Jerusalem and Judah, till he had cast them out from his presence, that Zedekiah rebelled against the king of Babylon.

4And it came to pass in the ninth year of his reign, in the tenth month, in the tenth day of the month, that Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon came, he and all his army, against Jerusalem, and pitched against it, and built forts against it round about.

5So the city was besieged unto the eleventh year of king Zedekiah.

6And in the fourth month, in the ninth day of the month, the famine was sore in the city, so that there was no bread for the people of the land.

7Then the city was broken up, and all the men of war fled, and went forth out of the city by night by the way of the gate between the two walls, which was by the king’s garden; (now the Chaldeans were by the city round about:) and they went by the way of the plain.

8But the army of the Chaldeans pursued after the king, and overtook Zedekiah in the plains of Jericho; and all his army was scattered from him.

9Then they took the king, and carried him up unto the king of Babylon to Riblah in the land of Hamath; where he gave judgment upon him.

10And the king of Babylon slew the sons of Zedekiah before his eyes: he slew also all the princes of Judah in Riblah.

11Then he put out the eyes of Zedekiah; and the king of Babylon bound him in chains, and carried him to Babylon, and put him in prison till the day of his death.

12Now in the fifth month, in the tenth day of the month, which was the nineteenth year of Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon, came Nebuzar-adan, captain of the guard, which served the king of Babylon, into Jerusalem,

13And burned the house of the LORD, and the king’s house; and all the houses of Jerusalem, and all the houses of the great men, burned he with fire:

14And all the army of the Chaldeans, that were with the captain of the guard, brake down all the walls of Jerusalem round about.

15Then Nebuzar-adan the captain of the guard carried away captive certain of the poor of the people, and the residue of the people that remained in the city, and those that fell away, that fell to the king of Babylon, and the rest of the multitude.

16But Nebuzar-adan the captain of the guard left certain of the poor of the land for vinedressers and for husbandmen.

17Also the pillars of brass that were in the house of the LORD, and the bases, and the brasen sea that was in the house of the LORD, the Chaldeans brake, and carried all the brass of them to Babylon.

18The caldrons also, and the shovels, and the snuffers, and the bowls, and the spoons, and all the vessels of brass wherewith they ministered, took they away.

19And the basons, and the firepans, and the bowls, and the caldrons, and the candlesticks, and the spoons, and the cups; that which was of gold in gold, and that which was of silver in silver, took the captain of the guard away.

20The two pillars, one sea, and twelve brasen bulls that were under the bases, which king Solomon had made in the house of the LORD: the brass of all these vessels was without weight.

21And concerning the pillars, the height of one pillar was eighteen cubits; and a fillet of twelve cubits did compass it; and the thickness thereof was four fingers: it was hollow.

22And a chapiter of brass was upon it; and the height of one chapiter was five cubits, with network and pomegranates upon the chapiters round about, all of brass. The second pillar also and the pomegranates were like unto these.

23And there were ninety and six pomegranates on a side; and all the pomegranates upon the network were an hundred round about.

24And the captain of the guard took Seraiah the chief priest, and Zephaniah the second priest, and the three keepers of the door:

25He took also out of the city an eunuch, which had the charge of the men of war; and seven men of them that were near the king’s person, which were found in the city; and the principal scribe of the host, who mustered the people of the land; and threescore men of the people of the land, that were found in the midst of the city.

26So Nebuzar-adan the captain of the guard took them, and brought them to the king of Babylon to Riblah.

27And the king of Babylon smote them, and put them to death in Riblah in the land of Hamath. Thus Judah was carried away captive out of his own land.

28This is the people whom Nebuchadrezzar carried away captive: in the seventh year three thousand Jews and three and twenty:

29In the eighteenth year of Nebuchadrezzar he carried away captive from Jerusalem eight hundred thirty and two persons:

30In the three and twentieth year of Nebuchadrezzar Nebuzar-adan the captain of the guard carried away captive of the Jews seven hundred forty and five persons: all the persons were four thousand and six hundred.

31And it came to pass in the seven and thirtieth year of the captivity of Jehoiachin king of Judah, in the twelfth month, in the five and twentieth day of the month, that Evil-merodach king of Babylon in the first year of his reign lifted up the head of Jehoiachin king of Judah, and brought him forth out of prison,

32And spake kindly unto him, and set his throne above the throne of the kings that were with him in Babylon,

33And changed his prison garments: and he did continually eat bread before him all the days of his life.

4King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon came against Jerusalem with his whole army and set up camp outside it. They built siege ramps all around it. He arrived on the tenth day of the tenth month in the ninth year that Zedekiah ruled over Judah.

5The city remained under siege until Zedekiah’s eleventh year.

6By the ninth day of the fourth month the famine in the city was so severe the residents had no food.

7They broke through the city walls, and all the soldiers tried to escape. They left the city during the night. They went through the gate between the two walls that is near the king’s garden. (The Babylonians had the city surrounded.) Then they headed for the rift valley.

8But the Babylonian army chased after the king. They caught up with Zedekiah in the plains of Jericho, and his entire army deserted him.

9They captured him and brought him up to the king of Babylon at Riblah in the territory of Hamath, and he passed sentence on him there.

10The king of Babylon had Zedekiah’s sons put to death while Zedekiah was forced to watch. He also had all the nobles of Judah put to death there at Riblah.

11He had Zedekiah’s eyes put out and had him bound in chains. Then the king of Babylon had him led off to Babylon, and he was imprisoned there until the day he died.

12On the tenth day of the fifth month, in the nineteenth year of King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon, Nebuzaradan, the captain of the royal guard who served the king of Babylon, arrived in Jerusalem.

13He burned down the Lord’s temple, the royal palace, and all the houses in Jerusalem, including every large house.

14The whole Babylonian army that came with the captain of the royal guard tore down the walls that surrounded Jerusalem.

16But he left behind some of the poor and gave them fields and vineyards.

Summary

This closing chapter is a historical appendix, largely paralleling the account in 2 Kings, recounting Zedekiah's rebellion against Babylon and the resulting siege and fall of Jerusalem (vv. 1-11). It describes the burning of the temple and city, the demolition of the walls, and the deportation of the population, along with a list of the numbers taken into exile over several successive years (vv. 12-30). The temple's bronze pillars, sea, and other furnishings are catalogued in detail before being carried off to Babylon (vv. 17-23). The chapter ends on an unexpected note of hope: years later, the Babylonian king Evil-Merodach releases the exiled King Jehoiachin from prison, restores him to a position of honor, and provides for him at the royal table for the rest of his life (vv. 31-34).

Authorship & Background

Author: Likely a later editor/compiler (not Jeremiah himself), as 44:30 appears to be his last prophecy. This chapter closely parallels 2 Kings 24:18-25:30.
Classification: Historical Appendix — Jerusalem's Fall and Jehoiachin's Release
Key Themes: Prophecy fulfilled (everything Jeremiah predicted came true), the temple destroyed, the Davidic monarchy ended (seemingly), yet Jehoiachin's release — a spark of hope for David's line
Historical Context: This chapter was likely added by an editor to provide the fulfillment record: everything Jeremiah prophesied actually happened. The detailed inventory of temple furnishings carried to Babylon (vv.17-23) emphasizes the magnitude of loss. The deportation numbers (vv.28-30: 4,600 total over three deportations) may count only adult males. The chapter's final note (vv.31-34) is the release of King Jehoiachin from prison by Evil-Merodach (Amel-Marduk) of Babylon in 562 BC — 37 years after his exile. This tiny note of royal favor preserves hope: David's line survives. The book ends not with destruction but with a king at a royal table — a whisper of messianic hope.
Zedekiah's Reign (vv.1-3): Zedekiah was one and twenty years old when he began to reign, and he reigned eleven years in Jerusalem, and he did that which was evil in the eyes of the LORD. This chapter is an appendix. It comes from the same source as 2 Kings 24 and 25 and is largely identical to it, and it is here because the words of Jeremiah ended at 51:64 and somebody thought the reader should still be told how it came out.
The Siege (vv.4-5): The ninth year, tenth month, tenth day, Nebuchadrezzar came, and all his army, and pitched against it, and built forts against it round about. So the city was besieged unto the eleventh year of king Zedekiah. Eighteen months in two verses, which is the proportion a chronicle keeps and the reverse of the proportion the rest of this book keeps.
The Breach, the Flight and the Capture (vv.6-11): In the fourth month, the ninth day of the month, the famine was sore in the city, so that there was no bread for the people of the land. Then the flight by night through the gate between the two walls, the capture in the plains of Jericho, and the sentence at Riblah, they slew the sons of Zedekiah before his eyes, then he put out the eyes of Zedekiah, and bound him in chains, and carried him to Babylon, and put him in prison till the day of his death.
The Temple Burned (vv.12-14): In the fifth month, in the tenth day of the month, came Nebuzaradan, and burned the house of the LORD, and the king's house, and all the houses of Jerusalem, and all the walls of Jerusalem round about were broken down. That date is the one the Jewish calendar still keeps as the ninth of Av, the fast for the destruction of both temples, and it is fixed by this verse and its parallel in Kings.
The Deportation (vv.15-16): The poor of the people, and the residue, and the fugitives, and the rest of the multitude, carried away captive. And then the same clause that starts chapter 40's whole story, but Nebuzaradan left certain of the poor of the land for vinedressers and for husbandmen. The people at the bottom stay, and are given the vineyards.
The Vessels Catalogued (vv.17-23): The longest passage in the chapter is an inventory, and the disproportion is worth asking about. The pillars, the bases, the brasen sea, the pots, the shovels, the snuffers, the bowls and the spoons, and then the two pillars measured to the cubit with the pomegranates counted, ninety and six on a side and a hundred on the network. A chronicle that gave eighteen months of siege two verses gives the temple furniture seven. The reason is at the other end of the exile: Ezra 1 records these objects being brought back and counted again. Somebody kept the list.
Executed at Riblah (vv.24-27): Seraiah the chief priest, Zephaniah the second priest, three door keepers, the officer set over the men of war, and threescore men of the people of the land, taken to Riblah and killed there. This is the event Ezekiel 11:10 had described in advance in one clause, I will judge you in the border of Israel, and Riblah is on the northern border.
The Deportation Numbers (vv.28-30): Three deportations counted separately, three thousand and twenty and three in the seventh year, eight hundred thirty and two in the eighteenth, seven hundred forty and five in the twenty-third, all the persons were four thousand and six hundred. The figures are much lower than the totals in 2 Kings and are generally taken to count adult men only. A book of this length ends its history with an audit total.
Jehoiachin at the King's Table (vv.31-34): The book ends on a meal. In the seven and thirtieth year of the captivity, Evil-merodach king of Babylon brought Jehoiachin out of prison, and spake kindly to him, and set his throne above the throne of the kings that were with him, and changed his prison garments, and he did continually eat bread before him all the days of his life. Babylonian administrative tablets excavated at the city record rations issued to Yaukin, king of the land of Yahud, which is this man under his own name, so the last scene in the book has independent documentation. It is not a restoration. Nothing is given back and nobody goes home. What it establishes is that the line Jeremiah had said would lose the throne was still alive, out of prison, and eating at the emperor's table, which for a book that ends in exile is as much of an ending as it is prepared to give.

Deep Dive

Setting: Chapter 51 ended with an editor's note: "Thus far are the words of Jeremiah." Chapter 52 is a historical appendix, added by a later hand, telling once more how Jerusalem fell in 586 BC. Almost all of it is copied from 2 Kings 24:18–25:30, with a few changes and some details found only here, and parts of it also appear in Jeremiah 39. Why end a prophet's book with a chapter of history? Because it proves the prophet right. For forty years Jeremiah had warned that the city would be burned, the temple looted, the king taken and the people exiled, and he had been beaten, jailed and called a traitor for saying so. Here, in flat, unemotional prose, every one of those warnings comes true. The chapter also closes with a quiet note of hope, which 2 Kings ends with too: the captive king of David's line is let out of prison. The final events take place around 561 BC, in Babylon, about twenty-five years after the fall of the city.

The Fall of Jerusalem Recounted (vv1–11)

  • "Zedekiah was twenty-one years old when he became king, and he reigned eleven years" (v1). He reigned 597–586 BC. Nebuchadnezzar put him on the throne after deporting his nephew Jehoiachin, and changed his name from Mattaniah to Zedekiah, "the LORD is my righteousness" (2 Kgs 24:17). Jeremiah had promised a future king called "The LORD is our righteousness" (23:6). Zedekiah carried the name without the faith. His mother Hamutal was also the mother of King Jehoahaz (2 Kgs 23:31), so Zedekiah was a full brother of that short-lived king and a son of good King Josiah. Her father, "Jeremiah of Libnah," is a different man from the prophet.
  • "He did what was evil in the sight of the LORD, according to all that Jehoiakim had done" (v2). Zedekiah seems to have been weak rather than wicked by nature. He consulted Jeremiah in secret, protected him at times, and feared his own officials (37:17; 38:5, 19). But he never obeyed. The book of Jeremiah shows that indecision before God is itself a choice.
  • "Because of the anger of the LORD… he cast them out from his presence" (v3). The underlying cause of the fall is given first. Babylon's armies were the means. The LORD's judgment on long, unrepented sin was the reason (2 Kgs 24:3–4; Jer 15:4).
  • "And Zedekiah rebelled against the king of Babylon" (v3). He had sworn loyalty in God's name (2 Chr 36:13; Ezek 17:13–19). Egypt's new Pharaoh Hophra promised help (37:5), and the pro-Egypt party in Jerusalem won the argument. Jeremiah had urged surrender as the only way to save the city (38:17–18).
  • "In the ninth year of his reign, in the tenth month, on the tenth day" (v4). January 588 BC. Ezekiel, far away in Babylon, was told to write down that very day (Ezek 24:1–2). "They built siegeworks all around it": ramps and towers to attack the walls and starve the city.
  • "The famine was so severe in the city that there was no food" (v6). Lamentations describes what this meant in terrible detail: children begging for bread, mothers cooking their own children (Lam 2:12, 20; 4:4–10).
  • "A breach was made in the city" (v7). July 586 BC, after about eighteen months of siege. "By the way of a gate between the two walls, by the king's garden": a gate near the southeast corner of the city, near the Pool of Siloam, leading down into the Kidron Valley. "In the direction of the Arabah": down toward the Jordan Valley, perhaps hoping to cross to Ammon.
  • "Overtook Zedekiah in the plains of Jericho" (v8). There is a painful irony in the place. Jericho was where Israel's conquest of the land began (Josh 6). Here the kingdom ends. "All his army was scattered from him."
  • "Brought him up to the king of Babylon at Riblah in the land of Hamath" (v9). Riblah, on the Orontes River in modern Syria, about 200 miles north of Jerusalem, was Nebuchadnezzar's field headquarters. Pharaoh Neco had used the same base to depose King Jehoahaz in 609 BC (2 Kgs 23:33).
  • "Slaughtered the sons of Zedekiah before his eyes… put out the eyes of Zedekiah" (vv10–11). The last thing Zedekiah saw was the end of his line. Blinding was a known Babylonian and Assyrian punishment for rebel vassals. Two prophecies that seemed to contradict each other were both fulfilled: Jeremiah said Zedekiah would see the king of Babylon "eye to eye" (32:4; 34:3), and Ezekiel said he would be brought to Babylon "yet he shall not see it" (Ezek 12:13). He saw Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah, and never saw Babylon. "Put him in prison till the day of his death" is a detail not found in 2 Kings. The Greek version says "the house of the mill," where prisoners were set to grinding grain, as Samson was (Judg 16:21).
  • Commentators: Guzik notes how the eighteen months of siege are compressed into a few verses and draws out the irony of the king's capture at Jericho, the place of Israel's first victory. He pairs the blinding with Ezekiel 12:13. JFB explains the "siegeworks" as wooden towers for watching and attacking the city, and reads the prison as a place of forced labor, following the Greek. Matthew Henry draws the moral of vv1–3: "None are cast out of God's presence but those who by sin have first thrown themselves out," and of Zedekiah's flight, "there is no escaping the judgments of God."

The Temple Burned (vv12–23)

  • "In the fifth month, on the tenth day… the nineteenth year of King Nebuchadnezzar" (v12). August 586 BC, about a month after the walls were breached. Second Kings 25:8 says "the seventh day." The difference is small and the reason is not known for certain. One old explanation is that Nebuzaradan arrived on the seventh and set the fires on the tenth. Jewish tradition remembers the day as Tisha b'Av, the ninth of the month Av, when both this temple and the second temple (AD 70) were destroyed, and Lamentations is still read in synagogues on that day.
  • "Nebuzaradan the captain of the bodyguard" (v12). His title (rab ṭabbaḥim) literally means "chief of the cooks" or "chief of the slaughterers." It had become the title of a senior officer. He is the same man who later freed Jeremiah and gave him the choice of where to go (40:1–5).
  • "He burned the house of the LORD, and the king's house and all the houses of Jerusalem" (v13). Solomon's temple had stood for almost four hundred years. Its stone walls were lined with cedar and its floors were cypress (1 Kgs 6:15–18), so it burned fiercely. Jeremiah had warned in the temple gate itself that God would do to this house what he did to Shiloh (7:12–14; 26:6). Those who trusted "the temple of the LORD" as a lucky charm (7:4) now saw it in flames.
  • "Broke down all the walls around Jerusalem" (v14). A city without walls could not defend itself or be a capital. The walls lay broken until Nehemiah rebuilt them, about 140 years later (Neh 6:15).
  • "Carried away captive some of the poorest of the people… and the deserters" (v15). The deserters included those who had followed Jeremiah's advice to surrender (21:9; 38:2). "Left some of the poorest of the land to be vinedressers and plowmen" (v16). The land itself was not abandoned. Gedaliah would govern these people at Mizpah (ch 40).
  • "The pillars of bronze… the stands and the bronze sea… the Chaldeans broke in pieces" (v17). These were the great bronze works Solomon commissioned from Hiram of Tyre (1 Kgs 7:13–47): two free-standing pillars at the temple entrance named Jachin and Boaz, ten wheeled stands for basins, and the "sea," a huge bronze basin for priests' washing, set on twelve bronze bulls. Too big to carry, they were smashed for the metal. Jeremiah had foretold this very thing, naming the pillars, the sea and the stands, when false prophets were promising that the temple vessels would come back soon (27:19–22).
  • "The pots and the shovels and the snuffers and the basins" (vv18–19). The ordinary tools of daily worship, used for ashes, lamps, sprinkling blood and pouring drink offerings. "What was of gold… as gold, and what was of silver, as silver": the plunder was sorted by metal, its sacred purpose ignored. Some of these vessels were later used at Belshazzar's feast (Dan 5:2–3) and returned under Cyrus (Ezra 1:7–11).
  • "The bronze of all these things was beyond weight" (v20). The same was said when Solomon made them (1 Kgs 7:47). What took years of skill and devotion to create was hauled off as scrap.
  • "The height of the one pillar was eighteen cubits… it was hollow" (v21). About 27 feet high and 18 feet around, with bronze about three inches thick. "A capital of bronze. The height of the one capital was five cubits" (v22). Second Kings 25:17 says three cubits, perhaps counting only the decorated part, and 2 Chronicles 3:15 gives a combined height for the pillars. The figures come from different ways of measuring.
  • "There were ninety-six pomegranates on the sides; all the pomegranates were a hundred" (v23). Ninety-six facing outward and four more at the corners, or ninety-six fully visible and four partly hidden, are the usual explanations. Pomegranates, full of seed, were a picture of fruitfulness and blessing, and they also decorated the high priest's robe (Exod 28:33–34). The writer lingers over the beauty that was lost.
  • Commentators: JFB explains the breaking of the bronze as making it "more portable" and points to the fulfilment of 27:19. He takes the different dates in v12 and 2 Kings as arrival and burning, and says such small differences "confirm the genuineness of Scripture," since copyists who were inventing would have smoothed them out. Guzik notes the measurements do not line up perfectly with 1 Kings 7, and stresses that the point is the immense quantity and beauty of what was lost. Matthew Henry: "The remembrance of their beauty and value shows us the more the evil of sin."

The People Exiled to Babylon (vv24–30)

  • "Seraiah the chief priest, and Zephaniah the second priest" (v24). This Seraiah is a different man from Baruch's brother in 51:59. He was the grandson of Hilkiah, the high priest who found the Book of the Law in Josiah's reign (1 Chr 6:13–14; 2 Kgs 22:8). His son Jehozadak went into exile (1 Chr 6:15), and his grandson Joshua returned as high priest with Zerubbabel (Hag 1:1). The descendants of the high priest Seraiah also included Ezra (Ezra 7:1). Zephaniah the second priest appears earlier as a go-between for Zedekiah and Jeremiah (21:1; 37:3). "The three keepers of the threshold" were senior priests in charge of the temple gates and the money collected there (2 Kgs 12:9).
  • "Seven men of the king's council" (v25). Literally "seven who saw the king's face," his closest advisers. Second Kings 25:19 says five. Small differences like this are common between copies of the same list. "The secretary of the commander of the army, who mustered the people of the land": the official who kept the draft rolls.
  • "The king of Babylon struck them down and put them to death at Riblah" (v27). These were the leaders held responsible for the rebellion. "So Judah was taken into exile out of its land." The sentence is short and final, the same words used in 2 Kings 25:21. The promise to Abraham of this land had seemed permanent. But Moses had warned that persistent rebellion would bring exile (Lev 26:33; Deut 28:64).
  • "This is the number of the people whom Nebuchadnezzar carried away captive" (vv28–30). This list is found only here, not in 2 Kings. "In the seventh year, 3,023 Judeans" is the deportation of 597 BC with Jehoiachin. Second Kings 24:8–16 counts it as the eighth year and gives 10,000, or 7,000 soldiers plus 1,000 craftsmen. The year difference is a matter of counting: Babylon counted a king's first partial year as his "accession year" and the next as year one, while Judah often counted the partial year as year one. The numbers are harder. Most scholars think Jeremiah's figures count only adult men of certain categories, perhaps those of Judah specifically, while Kings gives a rounded total. "In the eighteenth year… 832 persons" is the deportation from Jerusalem itself in 586 BC (again a year off from Kings for the same reason). "In the twenty-third year… 745 persons" is 582/581 BC, a third deportation mentioned nowhere else, probably Babylon's reprisal after Gedaliah was murdered (ch 41). "All the persons were 4,600."
  • Why so few? Whatever they counted, these figures show that the exile did not empty the land of every person. It removed the leaders, the skilled workers and the people of Jerusalem, the ones who shaped the nation's life. Those people, and their children, became the community through which God carried his promises forward: Daniel, Ezekiel, and later Zerubbabel, Ezra and Nehemiah.
  • Commentators: JFB notes the high priest Seraiah is a different man from the Seraiah of 51:59. On the numbers, JFB suggests the 3,023 were "men of Judah" only, with others counted separately, and that the 4,600 excludes women and children. Guzik reports several attempts to reconcile the totals, including that the figures count only adult males, and draws from the plain, passionless tone of these verses the contrast with the grief poured out in Lamentations. He also observes that Israel had held the land for something like eight centuries and lost it through idolatry and sin.

Jehoiachin Released from Prison (vv31–34)

  • "In the thirty-seventh year of the exile of Jehoiachin" (v31). Jehoiachin had been king of Judah for only three months, at eighteen, before he surrendered in 597 BC (2 Kgs 24:8–12). He was now about fifty-five. Jeremiah had said none of his offspring would sit on David's throne and rule in Judah (22:24–30), and Jehoiachin himself spent most of his life in custody.
  • "Evil-merodach king of Babylon, in the year that he began to reign" (v31). The name is the Hebrew form of Amel-Marduk, "man of Marduk." The "evil" in English is just how the Hebrew spelling happens to come out and has nothing to do with the English word. He was Nebuchadnezzar's son and reigned only about two years (562–560 BC) before he was assassinated. New kings often released prisoners at their accession as a show of grace. The date is March 561 BC, by the usual reckoning. Second Kings 25:27 gives the twenty-seventh day instead of the twenty-fifth.
  • "Graciously freed Jehoiachin" (v31). The Hebrew is literally "lifted up the head of," the same phrase used when Pharaoh restored his cupbearer (Gen 40:13, 20). After thirty-seven years, the head of David's house is lifted.
  • "Gave him a seat above the seats of the kings who were with him in Babylon" (v32). Other deposed kings were also held at the court. Jehoiachin is given first place among them.
  • "Jehoiachin put off his prison garments. And every day of his life he dined regularly at the king's table" (v33). In the 1930s archaeologists published clay tablets found in the storerooms of Nebuchadnezzar's palace, near the Ishtar Gate. They list oil rations for foreign captives, including "Ya'ukin, king of the land of Yahud," and his five sons. The tablets date from around 592 BC, well before this release, so they show he was kept at the court and fed even before his promotion. They also show the royal line was not wiped out. He had sons, and one of his grandsons was Zerubbabel (1 Chr 3:17–19), who led the first return and laid the foundation of the second temple.
  • "A regular allowance was given him by the king… until the day of his death" (v34). The book of Jeremiah ends with these words. It does not end in triumph. Jehoiachin is still in Babylon, still a dependent, and he dies there. But David's line is alive, the king of Babylon is feeding it, and the God who said "I will not make a full end" (5:18; 30:11; 46:28) has kept his word.
  • Why end here? The closing verses quietly answer the question hanging over the whole book: was God's covenant with David finished? Jeremiah had said the line would not reign for a time (22:30), but also that God would raise up "a righteous Branch" for David (23:5; 33:15–17). A released prisoner eating at a foreign king's table is a small sign, but it is a sign that the line God promised to keep was being kept.
  • Commentators: Guzik calls this a small ray of hope, a sign that God was not finished with his people, and mentions the ration tablets as outside confirmation. JFB passes on a Jewish tradition that Evil-merodach had himself been imprisoned by his father and befriended Jehoiachin there. That is only a legend, but it shows how readers have wondered at the kindness. JFB also sees the higher seat as "a mark of respect" and the new garments as clothes "suitable to a king." Matthew Henry draws the encouragement: "Those under oppression will find it is not in vain for them to hope and quietly to wait for the salvation of the Lord," for "our times are in God's hand."

Pointing to Christ

The book ends with a son of David, stripped of his throne, brought out of prison, given new clothes and a place at the king's table. It is only a shadow, but it points ahead. Jehoiachin's line ran through Zerubbabel to Joseph and to Jesus (Matt 1:11–12, 16), and the curse of 22:30 did not stop God's promise. The true Son of David was born, rejected and put to death outside the walls of the rebuilt Jerusalem, and then raised in triumph to the right hand of the Father. Where Jehoiachin received a seat "above the seats of the kings," Christ is seated "far above all rule and authority" (Eph 1:20–21). And he does for his people what Evil-merodach did for one captive king: he brings them out of prison (Isa 61:1; Luke 4:18), takes away the filthy garments (Zech 3:3–5), and gives them a place at his table for the rest of their days (Luke 22:29–30).

Reflection

  • Rebellion That Finally Runs Out of Road (vv.1-3) — Zedekiah rebels against Babylon despite years of clear warning, and this time the consequences finally land. Is there a warning you've been ignoring for so long that you've started to assume it will never actually catch up with you?
  • Nothing Built by Human Hands Is Permanent (v.13) — Even Solomon's magnificent temple, the center of Israel's worship, is burned to the ground. Where might you be placing your ultimate security in a structure, institution, or achievement rather than in God Himself?
  • Hope Arriving After Decades of Darkness (v.31) — Jehoiachin is released from prison after thirty-seven years and given a seat at the king's table. Is there a long-held hope you've nearly given up on that you need to keep trusting God with, even on His timeline rather than yours?
  • The Book's Final Image Is Provision, Not Destruction (v.34) — After fifty-two chapters heavy with judgment, Jeremiah closes with a released king given a regular allowance of food for the rest of his life. What does it tell you about God's character that even the darkest book in Scripture ends with an image of sustaining care?
