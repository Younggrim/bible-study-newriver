1Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ, To the elect who are exiles of the Dispersion throughout Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia, chosen

2according to the foreknowledge of God the Father and sanctified by the Spirit for obedience to Jesus Christ and sprinkling by His blood: Grace and peace be yours in abundance.

3Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ! By His great mercy He has given us new birth into a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead,

4and into an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, reserved in heaven for you,

5who through faith are shielded by God’s power for the salvation that is ready to be revealed in the last time.

6In this you greatly rejoice, though now for a little while you may have had to suffer grief in various trials

7so that the proven character of your faith—more precious than gold, which perishes even though refined by fire—may result in praise, glory, and honor at the revelation of Jesus Christ.

8Though you have not seen Him, you love Him; and though you do not see Him now, you believe in Him and rejoice with an inexpressible and glorious joy,

9now that you are receiving the goal of your faith, the salvation of your souls.

10Concerning this salvation, the prophets who foretold the grace to come to you searched and investigated carefully,

11trying to determine the time and setting to which the Spirit of Christ in them was pointing when He predicted the sufferings of Christ and the glories to follow.

12It was revealed to them that they were not serving themselves, but you, when they foretold the things now announced by those who preached the gospel to you by the Holy Spirit sent from heaven. Even angels long to look into these things.

13Therefore prepare your minds for action. Be sober-minded. Set your hope fully on the grace to be given you at the revelation of Jesus Christ.

14As obedient children, do not conform to the passions of your former ignorance.

15But just as He who called you is holy, so be holy in all you do,

16for it is written: “Be holy, because I am holy.”

17Since you call on a Father who judges each one’s work impartially, conduct yourselves in reverent fear during your stay as foreigners.

18For you know that it was not with perishable things such as silver or gold that you were redeemed from the empty way of life you inherited from your forefathers,

19but with the precious blood of Christ, a lamb without blemish or spot.

20He was known before the foundation of the world, but was revealed in the last times for your sake.

21Through Him you believe in God, who raised Him from the dead and glorified Him; and so your faith and hope are in God.

22Since you have purified your souls by obedience to the truth so that you have a genuine love for your brothers, love one another deeply, from a pure heart.

23For you have been born again, not of perishable seed, but of imperishable, through the living and enduring word of God.

24For, “All flesh is like grass, and all its glory like the flowers of the field; the grass withers and the flowers fall,

25but the word of the Lord stands forever.” And this is the word that was proclaimed to you.

Summary

Peter writes to elect exiles scattered across Asia Minor, blessing God for the living hope believers have through Jesus' resurrection and for an imperishable inheritance kept in heaven for them (vv. 1-5). He explains that present trials refine genuine faith the way fire refines gold, producing praise and honor when Jesus is revealed (vv. 6-9). The prophets who foretold this salvation searched carefully to understand what the Spirit of Christ was indicating, even though the message was ultimately for a later generation, and angels themselves long to look into it (vv. 10-12). Peter calls his readers to holy conduct and reverent living, reminding them they were ransomed not with perishable things like silver or gold but with the precious blood of Christ (vv. 13-21), and closes by urging sincere love for one another since they have been born again through the living and enduring word of God (vv. 22-25).

Authorship & Background

Author: Peter the Apostle (v.1), with Silvanus (Silas) as secretary (5:12). Written from "Babylon" (5:13) — likely a code name for Rome. Approximately AD 62-64, during or just before Nero's persecution.
Recipient: "Strangers scattered" throughout five Roman provinces in Asia Minor (modern Turkey): Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia (v.1). A mix of Jewish and Gentile Christians facing increasing persecution.
Purpose: To encourage believers suffering for their faith. Peter's message: suffering is temporary, glory is coming, and your identity in Christ gives you hope that transcends circumstances. The letter is saturated with hope — the word appears repeatedly.
Theme: Hope and holiness in the midst of suffering. Living as "strangers and pilgrims" (2:11) in a hostile world while awaiting an eternal inheritance.
Classification: Epistle — General
Key Themes: A living hope through the resurrection, faith tested like gold, the salvation prophets searched into, holiness grounded in God's character, redemption by precious blood, and new birth through an enduring word
Elect Strangers Scattered Abroad (vv.1-2): Peter writes to believers across five Roman provinces in Asia Minor, calling them “strangers” — resident aliens, people living somewhere they do not belong. That word governs the whole letter, which is written to Christians under social pressure and teaches them to understand their displacement rather than escape it. The greeting names Father, Spirit and Christ in one breath, tying election, sanctification and obedience together from the first sentence.
A Living Hope, an Inheritance Reserved (vv.3-5): The blessing that follows is one long sentence of confidence: begotten again to a living hope by the resurrection, to an inheritance incorruptible, undefiled and unfading, reserved in heaven. Each adjective denies a way earthly inheritance fails — it can decay, be polluted, or simply lose its shine. “Kept by the power of God” in v.5 is a military term for a garrison, and it is the believers who are guarded, not only the inheritance.
Tested Faith, Inexpressible Joy (vv.6-9): The trials are conceded as real and grievous, and framed as temporary and purposeful: faith tried by fire as gold is, and gold perishes while faith does not. Verse 8 is remarkable for a letter written within living memory of Jesus — “whom having not seen, ye love”. Peter had seen Him; his readers had not, and he treats their unseeing love as no lesser thing. The joy is called unspeakable and full of glory, language of something exceeding what can be reported.
What Prophets and Angels Longed to See (vv.10-12): The prophets who spoke of this grace searched their own writings to learn when and how it would come, testifying beforehand of “the sufferings of Christ, and the glory that should follow”. That order — suffering then glory — is the pattern Peter will apply to his readers' own situation. The closing note that angels desire to look into these things puts the readers, ordinary people under local hostility, in possession of something the prophets awaited.
Be Holy, For He Is Holy (vv.13-16): The first imperative of the letter arrives only now, once the ground has been laid: gird up the loins of your mind, be sober, hope to the end. Holiness is commanded on the basis of God's character rather than on threat, quoting Leviticus — be holy, for I am holy. “As obedient children” frames it as family resemblance. The former ignorance and its lusts are named as a life already left, not a temptation still being negotiated.
Redeemed by Precious Blood (vv.17-21): Redemption is described commercially and then the metaphor is broken: not with silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ, a lamb without blemish foreordained before the foundation of the world. The vanity of the fathers' tradition is named plainly, which for readers with deep inherited practice is a costly claim. Faith and hope are placed in God rather than in circumstance, because it is God who raised Him.
Born Again by the Enduring Word (vv.22-25): The chapter closes on love “with a pure heart fervently”, grounded in new birth from incorruptible seed. Isaiah 40 returns — all flesh is grass, the flower falls, but the word of the Lord endures for ever. In a letter to people whose social standing was collapsing, the argument is that they have been born of the one thing in the passage that does not wither.

Deep Dive

Setting: The letter comes from Simon Peter, the fisherman Jesus renamed "Rock" and the leading apostle of the Gospels. He writes from "Babylon" (5:13), which most scholars and early tradition take as a code name for Rome, the great city that, like ancient Babylon, held God's people in exile. Silvanus (Silas, Paul's old travel partner) helped write or carry it (5:12), which may explain its polished Greek. The usual date is the early 60s AD, shortly before Emperor Nero turned on the Christians of Rome in AD 64, and tradition holds that Peter himself was crucified there soon after. Some scholars think a disciple wrote in Peter's name later, but the early church accepted the letter without question, and the case for Peter remains strong. The readers live across what is now northern and central Turkey. They are mostly Gentile converts, since Peter speaks of their "former ignorance" and the "futile ways inherited from your forefathers" (vv14, 18; compare 4:3–4). They are not yet facing state persecution, but they are being slandered, mocked and pushed to the edges of society for their new faith. Peter's message runs through the whole letter: you are God's chosen people, strangers here, and suffering now is the road to glory, as it was for Christ.

Greeting (vv1–2)

  • "Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ" (v1). "Peter" is the Greek form of the name Jesus gave him, Kēphas in Aramaic, "rock" (John 1:42). He does not defend his authority as Paul often had to. Everyone knew who he was. The man who once denied Jesus three times now writes to strengthen others, just as Jesus told him to (Luke 22:32; John 21:15–17).
  • "Elect exiles" (v1). Two words that belong together. "Elect" means chosen by God. "Exiles" (parepidēmos) means a temporary resident living away from home. The Greek Old Testament uses it of Abraham, who called himself "a sojourner and foreigner" among the Hittites when he bought a grave for Sarah (Gen 23:4), and Hebrews uses it of all the patriarchs (Heb 11:13). Chosen by God means being a stranger in the world. Peter's readers felt the second; he wants them to know the first.
  • "Of the Dispersion" (v1). "Dispersion" (diaspora) was the word for Jews living outside the land of Israel, scattered since the exiles to Assyria and Babylon (James 1:1). Peter applies it to a mainly Gentile church. They have become the new people of God, scattered among the nations with a homeland in heaven. An early Christian writing, the Epistle to Diognetus, later put it this way: Christians live in their own countries, "but only as sojourners… every foreign land is to them as their native country, and every land of their birth as a land of strangers."
  • "Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia" (v1). Five Roman provinces covering most of Asia Minor north of the Taurus Mountains. The order traces a loop a messenger could ride, landing at a Black Sea port in Pontus and finishing in Bithynia. People from Pontus, Cappadocia and Asia heard Peter preach on the Day of Pentecost (Acts 2:9), so some of these churches may have started then. The Spirit once stopped Paul from entering Bithynia (Acts 16:7). Someone else brought the gospel there.
  • "According to the foreknowledge of God the Father, in the sanctification of the Spirit, for obedience to Jesus Christ and for sprinkling with his blood" (v2). Father, Spirit and Son each have a part, centuries before the church wrote out a doctrine of the Trinity. "Foreknowledge" in Scripture is more than knowing facts ahead of time. It means God setting his love on people beforehand (Rom 8:29; Amos 3:2). Christians have long discussed how this relates to human choice. Calvinist readers see God's choice as the ground of faith. Arminian readers see God choosing those he knew would believe. Both agree Peter's point is comfort: these mocked and marginal believers were chosen by God before anyone else rejected them.
  • "Sprinkling with his blood" (v2). The background is Exodus 24:3–8. When Israel promised at Sinai, "All that the LORD has spoken we will do, and we will be obedient," Moses sprinkled them with blood and said, "Behold the blood of the covenant." Peter pairs "obedience" and "sprinkling" in the same way: God's people are brought into a new covenant through Jesus' blood (Heb 12:24).
  • Commentators: Guzik notes that blood was sprinkled at three Old Testament moments: to make the covenant (Exod 24), to ordain priests (Exod 29) and to cleanse a healed leper (Lev 14). Christ's blood does all three: covenant, priesthood and cleansing. He also observes that Peter, unlike Paul, never needs to defend his apostleship. JFB reads "foreknowledge" as God's "foreordaining love," not mere foresight, and takes the sprinkling as the continual cleansing of those already justified. Henry sees salvation coming from "the Father's electing love, the Son's redemption, and the Spirit's sanctifying work." JFB thinks the readers were mainly Jewish Christians. Most modern scholars and the Tyndale notes, reading vv14 and 18, think they were mainly Gentiles.

Born Again to a Living Hope (vv3–12)

  • "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ!" (v3). Jewish prayers often began "Blessed are you, O Lord." Peter keeps the form but names God freshly: the Father of Jesus. Paul opens Ephesians and 2 Corinthians with exactly the same words (Eph 1:3; 2 Cor 1:3). In Greek vv3–12 is one long sentence of praise.
  • "He has caused us to be born again" (v3). The verb anagennaō, "beget again," appears in the New Testament only here and in v23, framing the chapter. It recalls Jesus' words to Nicodemus (John 3:3–7). New birth is God's work, "according to his great mercy," not our achievement.
  • "To a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead" (v3). "Hope" in the Bible is not wishful thinking but confident expectation. It is "living" because it rests on a living person. Peter knew what a dead hope felt like. He had watched Jesus die and gone back to fishing (John 21:3). The empty tomb brought both Jesus and Peter's hope back to life.
  • "An inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading" (v4). Israel's inheritance was the land of Canaan (Num 26:52–56), but land can be invaded, polluted by sin (Jer 2:7) and lost to exile. Peter describes the new inheritance with three Greek words that each begin with the same "un-" sound: aphthartos (can't decay), amiantos (can't be stained), amarantos (can't fade, used of flowers that do not wilt). It is "kept in heaven for you," like money in a vault no thief can reach (Matt 6:20).
  • "By God's power are being guarded through faith" (v5). The inheritance is kept for them, and they are kept for it. "Guarded" (phroureō) is a military word for a garrison guarding a city. Paul uses it of the peace of God that will "guard your hearts" (Phil 4:7). Notice the two sides: God's power does the guarding, and faith is the means by which it works. For believers under pressure, the point is security.
  • "A salvation ready to be revealed in the last time" (v5). Salvation here is mainly future: the full rescue at Christ's return. The New Testament speaks of salvation in three tenses: we have been saved (Eph 2:8), are being saved (1 Cor 1:18) and will be saved (Rom 13:11). Peter is looking forward.
  • "Though now for a little while, if necessary, you have been grieved by various trials" (v6). Three qualifiers soften the blow. The trials are short ("a little while") compared with eternity. They come only when needed ("if necessary"), within God's purpose. And they are real: "grieved" admits the pain. "Various" is poikilos, "many-colored," the word the Greek Old Testament uses for Joseph's coat (Gen 37:3). Trials come in every shade. Peter uses the same word for God's "varied grace" in 4:10: there is grace in every color too.
  • "The tested genuineness of your faith—more precious than gold" (v7). "Tested genuineness" is dokimion, the proven quality left after testing, as when metal is put through the furnace. The same word appears in James 1:3. The Greek Old Testament uses it for the crucible that tests silver and gold (Prov 27:21). Gold refined in fire is still only gold, and one day it will perish. Faith that comes through fire lasts. Whether the "praise and glory and honor" go to the believer or to God is debated, and both are probably in view (Matt 25:21; Eph 1:12).
  • "Though you have not seen him, you love him" (v8). Peter had seen Jesus: walked with him, eaten with him, touched him after he rose. His readers had not. Yet they love him just as truly. This echoes Jesus' word to Thomas: "Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed" (John 20:29).
  • "Joy that is inexpressible and filled with glory" (v8). Literally "unspeakable," joy beyond words, and "glorified," already touched by the glory to come. This joy is not the absence of grief (v6). It lives alongside it.
  • "The outcome of your faith, the salvation of your souls" (v9). "Soul" (psychē) here means the whole person, not just the inner part. Peter is not describing an escape of the spirit from the body, but the rescue of the whole self, finally completed at the resurrection.
  • "The prophets… searched and inquired carefully" (v10). Isaiah, Jeremiah, Daniel and the rest spoke more than they fully understood. Daniel asked, "O my lord, what shall be the outcome of these things?" and was told the words were "shut up and sealed until the time of the end" (Dan 12:8–9).
  • "What person or time" (v11). The Greek can be read "what time or what kind of time." The ESV takes the first word as "person," and the Tyndale notes allow both. Either way, the prophets knew the Messiah would come and suffer and then be glorified (Isa 52:13–53:12; Ps 22), but not who he would be or when.
  • "The Spirit of Christ in them" (v11). A striking phrase. The Spirit who spoke through the Old Testament prophets is called the Spirit of Christ. Christ was not absent before Bethlehem. His Spirit was already speaking of him. Jesus taught the same on the road to Emmaus, "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets" (Luke 24:25–27).
  • "The sufferings of Christ and the subsequent glories" (v11). This is the pattern of the whole letter: suffering, then glory (4:13; 5:1, 10). It is the pattern Peter once refused to accept, when he rebuked Jesus for speaking of the cross (Mark 8:31–33). Now it is the heart of his message.
  • "They were serving not themselves but you" (v12). The prophets were like servants preparing a feast they would never sit down to. Hebrews says they "did not receive what was promised, since God had provided something better for us" (Heb 11:39–40).
  • "Things into which angels long to look" (v12). "Look" is parakyptō, to stoop down and peer in. It is the exact word used of Peter himself on Easter morning, when he "stooped and looked in" at the empty tomb and saw the linen cloths (Luke 24:12; John 20:5). Angels, who serve in God's presence, bend down in wonder at the gospel that these ordinary believers have heard.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Jesus Christ was the main subject of the prophets' studies," and "the doctrine of the prophets, and that of the apostles, exactly agree, as coming from the same Spirit of God." He also says, "God not only gives his people grace, but preserves them unto glory." Guzik says the inheritance is ultimately God himself, so it begins already in this life. He insists that the "if necessary" of v6 covers not only the trial but even the grief, and he rejects the idea that a Christian should feel nothing. He also notes that "various" means "many-colored," which the lexicon supports. JFB treats the resurrection in v3 as both the cause and the pattern of believers' future rising, and links the prophets' search to Christ's sufferings and the glories after.

Called to Be Holy (vv13–25)

  • "Therefore, preparing your minds for action" (v13). Literally "girding up the loins of your mind." Men wore long robes. To work or run, they tucked the hem into the belt. Israel ate the first Passover this way, "your belt fastened," ready to leave Egypt (Exod 12:11). Jesus said, "Stay dressed for action" (Luke 12:35). Peter applies the picture to the mind: no loose, trailing thoughts. Today we would say "roll up your sleeves."
  • "Being sober-minded" (v13). Nēphō literally means not drunk. Peter uses it three times (4:7; 5:8). It means clear-headed and alert. The man who fell asleep in Gethsemane when told to watch (Mark 14:37–38) learned its value.
  • "Set your hope fully on the grace that will be brought to you at the revelation of Jesus Christ" (v13). Grace is not only behind us, at conversion. It is also ahead, arriving with Jesus. This is the only direct command in vv3–13. Everything else in the letter flows from where hope is set.
  • "As obedient children, do not be conformed to the passions of your former ignorance" (v14). Literally "children of obedience," a Hebrew way of saying "people marked by obedience." "Conformed" is the word Paul uses in Romans 12:2, to be squeezed into a mold. "Former ignorance" is how a Jew described Gentile life before knowing God (Acts 17:30; Eph 4:17–18), one of the clearest hints that the readers were Gentiles.
  • "As he who called you is holy, you also be holy in all your conduct" (v15). "Holy" (hagios) means set apart, belonging to God and therefore different. It is first something God is, then something he makes his people. "All your conduct" leaves nothing out: work, home, speech, money. "Conduct" (anastrophē, way of life) is a favorite word of Peter's, occurring six times in this letter.
  • "You shall be holy, for I am holy" (v16). This refrain appears several times in Leviticus (11:44–45; 19:2; 20:7). In 11:44 it comes in the food laws that kept Israel distinct from the nations. Peter applies it to the church, now distinct not by diet but by character.
  • "If you call on him as Father who judges impartially" (v17). Two truths held together. God is "Father," close and loving. He is also Judge, and he "shows no partiality" (Acts 10:34, Peter's own words to Cornelius). Children of the Judge should not presume on the relationship.
  • "Conduct yourselves with fear throughout the time of your exile" (v17). "Fear" here is reverence, not terror (Prov 1:7). "Exile" is paroikia, a stay in a foreign land as a resident alien. Paul uses it for Israel's stay in Egypt (Acts 13:17). Believers are passing through.
  • "You were ransomed from the futile ways inherited from your forefathers" (v18). "Ransomed" (lytroō) was the word for buying a slave's freedom or paying to release a prisoner of war. Israel was "redeemed" from Egypt (Deut 7:8). "Futile ways inherited from your forefathers" describes the empty traditions of the pagan world, ancestral gods and customs that went nowhere. Jesus used the same idea: "the Son of Man came… to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45).
  • "Not with perishable things such as silver or gold" (v18). In the ancient world a slave's ransom was paid in silver. Peter heard the temple tax debate (Matt 17:24–27), and later told a lame beggar, "I have no silver and gold" (Acts 3:6). He knew money could not buy what was needed.
  • "But with the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb without blemish or spot" (v19). The Passover lamb had to be "without blemish" (Exod 12:5), and its blood marked the houses God passed over on the night he freed Israel. Isaiah pictured the Servant "like a lamb that is led to the slaughter" (Isa 53:7). John the Baptist pointed at Jesus: "Behold, the Lamb of God" (John 1:29). Peter was there to hear it, and his brother Andrew was one of the two disciples who followed Jesus after that word (John 1:35–40).
  • "He was foreknown before the foundation of the world but was made manifest in the last times" (v20). The cross was not God's backup plan. Christ was "foreknown," the same root as the "foreknowledge" of v2. The believers were chosen in God's plan; so was the Lamb (Rev 13:8). "The last times" began with his coming.
  • "So that your faith and hope are in God" (v21). God raised Jesus and gave him glory, which is the pattern of suffering and glory again (v11). Because God did that for Jesus, believers can trust him with their own suffering.
  • "Having purified your souls by your obedience to the truth" (v22). "Purified" (hagnizō) was the word for ritual cleansing before worship (Acts 21:24, 26). For these believers it happened when they obeyed the gospel. The purpose is "a sincere brotherly love." "Brotherly love" is philadelphia, family affection, now extended to people from every background who share a new birth.
  • "Love one another earnestly from a pure heart" (v22). "Earnestly" (ektenōs) means stretched out, at full strength. Luke uses the related word for the church praying "earnestly" for Peter in prison (Acts 12:5), and of Jesus praying in agony in Gethsemane (Luke 22:44). Peter repeats the command in 4:8: "keep loving one another earnestly."
  • "Born again, not of perishable seed but of imperishable" (v23). The second use of anagennaō. Human birth comes from perishable seed and leads to a perishable life. New birth comes from God's word, which cannot die, and so produces a life that cannot die. "Imperishable" is the same word used of the inheritance (v4). James says the same: "he brought us forth by the word of truth" (James 1:18).
  • "All flesh is like grass… but the word of the Lord remains forever" (vv24–25). A quotation from Isaiah 40:6–8 in its Greek form. Isaiah 40 opens with "Comfort, comfort my people" to exiles in Babylon, promising that God would bring them home. Peter, writing from "Babylon" to exiles, picks the same chapter. Empires and reputations wither like spring grass in the Middle Eastern summer. God's word outlasts them all.
  • "And this word is the good news that was preached to you" (v25). The enduring word is not abstract. It is the gospel these believers heard and believed. The same message that brought them to life will carry them home.
  • Commentators: Henry: "God makes those holy whom he saves," and "the price paid for man's redemption was the precious blood of Christ." He compares worldly possessions to "the flowers and plants of the field." Guzik defines holiness first as being set apart, not merely moral purity, and urges that a life bought with Christ's blood cannot be treated as worthless. He gives a historical note on Isaiah 40: Emperor Diocletian ordered the Scriptures burned in AD 303, and about a quarter century later Constantine ordered fifty new copies. JFB says God is holy by nature, while creatures become holy through being sanctified. He reads the fear of v17 as "reverential," not "slavish," and stresses substitution and ransom in vv18–19. JFB links v13 to Jesus' "let your loins be girded about" (Luke 12:35), as the Tyndale notes do with the image of tucking up a robe.

Pointing to Christ

Everything in this chapter is anchored in Jesus. He is the risen one whose resurrection makes hope alive (v3), the unseen Lord whom believers love (v8), the one the prophets' Spirit spoke of (v11), and the Lamb without blemish whose blood ransoms and sprinkles his people (vv2, 19). His life traced the path of "sufferings… and subsequent glories" that Peter's readers now walk, and God raised him and "gave him glory" (v21). Peter, who once tried to talk Jesus out of the cross, now sees it as the price of his own freedom, more precious than any silver or gold, and the guarantee of an inheritance no one can take away.

Reflection

  • Living Hope Through the Resurrection (v.3) — Peter says believers have been born again to a hope that's alive, grounded in Christ's resurrection rather than wishful thinking. Is your hope alive and active, or has it gone stale and merely theoretical?
  • An Imperishable Inheritance (v.4) — Believers are promised an inheritance in heaven that can never perish, spoil, or fade. How would remembering that inheritance change the way you handle today's losses or disappointments?
  • Faith Refined Like Gold (vv.6-7) — Trials test and purify faith the way fire refines gold, producing something more valuable than what existed before. Can you see God using your current hardship to refine rather than ruin your faith?
  • Loving the Unseen Christ (v.8) — Peter commends believers who love and rejoice in Jesus even though they have never seen him. Is your love for Christ growing, or has it cooled into routine?
  • Called to Holiness (vv.15-16) — Because God who called you is holy, you're called to reflect that holiness in every area of life. Where is there the widest gap between your daily conduct and God's character?
  • Redeemed by Precious Blood (vv.18-19) — Peter reminds readers they were ransomed not with money but with the precious blood of a spotless Christ. Does the cost of your redemption shape how seriously you take your new life?
