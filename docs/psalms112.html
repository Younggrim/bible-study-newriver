Summary

This acrostic wisdom psalm serves as a companion to Psalm 111, describing the blessed life of the person who fears the LORD and delights in his commands (v. 1). Such a person's descendants are called mighty and blessed (v. 2), his household prospers, and his righteousness endures forever (v. 3). Light breaks into darkness for the upright, who are gracious, compassionate, and just (v. 4), generous in lending and wise in managing their affairs (v. 5). Because their heart is fixed and trusts in the LORD, they are unshaken and unafraid even of bad news, secure until they see their enemies overcome (vv. 6-8). Their generosity to the poor is lasting, and they are honored and exalted (v. 9), while the wicked who witness this prosperity are consumed with envy and frustration, their own desires coming to nothing (v. 10).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Acrostic Wisdom Psalm / Beatitude Psalm
Attributed Author: Anonymous
Key Themes: The blessed life of the God-fearing person, generosity and righteousness, fearlessness in the face of evil, the contrast between the righteous and the wicked, the legacy of the upright
Historical Context: Psalm 112 is the companion to Psalm 111 — both are acrostic psalms with matching structure. Psalm 111 describes God's character; Psalm 112 describes the person who fears God — and remarkably, the attributes are parallel. The God-fearer becomes like GOD: gracious, compassionate, righteous (v.4 echoes 111:4). This is the biblical principle of transformation through worship — you become like what you revere. The psalm is essentially a portrait of what happens when someone truly fears God and delights in His commands: generational blessing, stability, generosity, courage, and lasting impact.
Blessed Is the Man That Feareth the LORD (v.1): Praise ye the LORD. Blessed is the man that feareth the LORD, that delighteth greatly in his commandments. Psalm 111 ended on the fear of the LORD and this psalm begins there, taking up the same acrostic form for twenty-two lines and turning it on the man instead of on God. Delight rather than compliance is what is said of his relation to the commandments.
His Righteousness Endureth for Ever (vv.2-3): His seed shall be mighty upon earth: the generation of the upright shall be blessed. Wealth and riches shall be in his house: and his righteousness endureth for ever. That last clause was said of God in Psalm 111:3, word for word, and here it is said of the man, which is the point of pairing the two psalms. The promise of wealth is the kind of statement Job's friends made and Job disproved, and the psalter itself questions it at length in Psalm 37 and Psalm 73; this psalm gives the rule and the others give the exceptions.
Light in the Darkness (v.4): Unto the upright there ariseth light in the darkness: he is gracious, and full of compassion, and righteous. The three adjectives were used of God in Psalm 111:4, and here it is ambiguous whether they describe God or the upright man; the Hebrew allows either and the pairing of the psalms suggests the ambiguity is intended. Darkness is assumed, not denied, and what is promised is light inside it.
A Good Man Sheweth Favour, and Lendeth (v.5): A good man sheweth favour, and lendeth: he will guide his affairs with discretion. Lending in Israel meant lending without interest to a fellow Israelite, so the verse describes a transfer that is not an investment. Discretion keeps it from becoming sentimental: the generosity is managed rather than impulsive.
His Heart Is Fixed, Trusting in the LORD (vv.6-8): Surely he shall not be moved for ever: the righteous shall be in everlasting remembrance. Against the grass and the forgotten place of Psalm 103:16, this promises that a life is remembered. He shall not be afraid of evil tidings: his heart is fixed, trusting in the LORD. Fixed is the same word Psalm 108 uses of the heart set on praise, and the fearlessness is located in what the heart is attached to rather than in temperament. The last clause is harder to like, until he see his desire upon his enemies, and the psalm leaves it standing.
He Hath Dispersed, He Hath Given to the Poor (v.9): He hath dispersed, he hath given to the poor; his righteousness endureth for ever; his horn shall be exalted with honour. Paul quotes this verse in 2 Corinthians 9:9 while raising money for the Jerusalem collection, and he quotes it as the description of the giver he wants his readers to be. Dispersed is a scattering word, used of sowing, which fits the argument he builds around it.
The Desire of the Wicked Shall Perish (v.10): The wicked shall see it, and be grieved; he shall gnash with his teeth, and melt away: the desire of the wicked shall perish. The acrostic ends on the alternative, and what the wicked man loses is not his goods but his wanting, which is the sharpest thing in the psalm. Seeing it is part of the grief; the blessing of the upright is visible to the one who does not share it.

Deep Dive

Setting: Psalm 112 is the twin of Psalm 111. It has no title or named author, it opens with the same "Praise the LORD!" (Hallelu-Yah), and it is the same kind of acrostic: twenty-two lines, each beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, laid out in exactly the same pattern. Psalm 111 ended, "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" (111:10). Psalm 112 picks up there: "Blessed is the man who fears the LORD." Psalm 111 described God, and Psalm 112 describes the person who fears him, and it does so with many of the same words. God's "righteousness endures forever" (111:3), and so does the godly man's (112:3, 9). God is "gracious and merciful" (111:4), and so is he (112:4). Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, compared the pair to the sun and the moon: the second shines with light reflected from the first. The psalm is a wisdom psalm, like Psalm 1, and it ends the same way, with the wicked perishing. It describes how life generally goes for the godly, not a guarantee against every hardship. In fact it expects "bad news" (v7) and "adversaries" (v8). Paul quotes v9 when he urges the Corinthians to give generously (2 Cor 9:9). The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

Blessed Is the Man Who Fears the LORD (vv1–3)

  • "Praise the LORD!" (v1). This psalm is about a person, but it opens with praise to God. Whatever is good in the godly man is God's gift, so God gets the credit.
  • "Blessed is the man who fears the LORD" (v1). "Blessed" ('ashre) means happy, flourishing, well off. It is the first word of Psalm 1 and the word behind Jesus' Beatitudes (Matt 5:3–12). To "fear the LORD" is to live in reverent awe and trust toward God, taking him seriously as God. JFB puts it simply: "True fear produces obedience and this happiness."
  • "Who greatly delights in his commandments!" (v1). Fear is joined to delight. This is not grudging obedience. Psalm 111:2 spoke of those who delight in God's works. Here the godly delight in his words. Psalm 1:2 says the same: "his delight is in the law of the LORD."
  • "His offspring will be mighty in the land" (v2). "Mighty" (gibbor) usually means a strong warrior, but here it suggests people of standing and influence. A godly life tends to bless the next generation (Prov 20:7). It is a general principle, not a promise that every child of believers will flourish, as the stories of Eli's and Samuel's sons show (1 Sam 2:12; 8:3).
  • "The generation of the upright will be blessed" (v2). "Generation" here means the whole company of upright people, not only one family.
  • "Wealth and riches are in his house" (v3). The Old Testament often presents prosperity as one of God's blessings (Prov 3:9–10; 10:22). The rest of the Bible keeps this in balance. Many godly people are poor (Ps 37:16; Jas 2:5), and Jesus warned that wealth can choke faith (Mark 4:19). Here the wealth is clearly tied to generosity (vv5, 9). It is not hoarded but shared, and in that sense it is good.
  • "And his righteousness endures forever" (v3). This is the line said of God in Psalm 111:3. The godly person's righteousness lasts because it is a reflection of God's own. Some interpreters, Clarke among them, think "righteousness" here leans toward generous giving, since in later Hebrew tsedaqah came to mean almsgiving, and v9 repeats the line right after mentioning gifts to the poor.
  • Commentators: Henry begins with "the character of the righteous" and then lists their blessings, starting with the "blessing entailed upon their posterity." JFB notes that God gives temporal blessings to those who serve him but may withhold them when they would get in the way of better spiritual blessings. Guzik, citing Barnes through Spurgeon, calls the promise of wealth a general principle, not an absolute guarantee, and passes on Kidner's point that the prosperity described has a moral dimension and becomes a means of doing good.

Light Dawns in the Darkness for the Upright (vv4–6)

  • "Light dawns in the darkness for the upright" (v4). The upright are not spared darkness. They have dark times like anyone else. But light rises for them in the middle of it. "Dawns" (zarach) is the word for the sun rising. Isaiah promises that the generous will see their "light rise in the darkness" (Isa 58:10), and Micah says, "when I sit in darkness, the LORD will be a light to me" (Mic 7:8).
  • "He is gracious, merciful, and righteous" (v4). Psalm 111:4 said "the LORD is gracious and merciful." Now the same words describe the godly man. The Hebrew does not name the subject, so some translations read this line as describing God, the light himself. The KJV's "he is gracious" leaves it open. The ESV takes it as the upright man, which fits the pattern of the paired psalms: he has become like the God he fears. Jesus said, "Be merciful, even as your Father is merciful" (Luke 6:36).
  • "It is well with the man who deals generously and lends" (v5). "Deals generously" (chonen) comes from the same root as "gracious" in v4. His grace shows in money. The law commanded Israelites to lend freely to the poor without interest (Exod 22:25; Deut 15:7–11). Psalm 37:26 says of the righteous, "He is ever lending generously." Jesus went further: "lend, expecting nothing in return" (Luke 6:35).
  • "Who conducts his affairs with justice" (v5). "Justice" (mishpat) here means sound judgment, good management. Generosity is not carelessness. The godly man is open-handed and wise at the same time.
  • "For the righteous will never be moved" (v6). "Moved" means shaken, toppled. Storms will come, but his life is founded on God and will not finally fall (Ps 15:5; Matt 7:24–25).
  • "He will be remembered forever" (v6). "The memory of the righteous is a blessing, but the name of the wicked will rot" (Prov 10:7). God himself remembers (Mal 3:16). Jesus said of the woman who anointed him, "what she has done will also be told in memory of her" (Mark 14:9).
  • Commentators: Henry lists "comfort," "wisdom" and "stability" among the blessings of the righteous in these verses. JFB says light in darkness means relief in trouble (Ps 27:1; 97:11) and that "the upright—are like God," citing Luke 6:36 and Psalm 111:4. Guzik, citing Poole, notes that "discretion" in the older translations means judgment: the godly man neither gains wealth unjustly nor squanders it. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, says the generous man is neither a borrower nor a hoarder.

He Is Not Afraid of Bad News (vv7–9)

  • "He is not afraid of bad news" (v7). This is one of the most quoted lines of the psalm. "Bad news" (shemu'ah ra'ah) is literally "an evil report," the kind of message that makes the heart stop: a messenger at the door, a doctor's call, word of war. The godly man is not exempt from such news. He is free from the dread of it.
  • "His heart is firm, trusting in the LORD" (v7). "Firm" (nakon) is the word in Psalm 108:1 and 57:7: "My heart is steadfast, O God." His heart is fixed, and the reason is given: "trusting in the LORD." His security is not in his wealth (v3) but in God. Isaiah says, "You keep him in perfect peace whose mind is stayed on you, because he trusts in you" (Isa 26:3).
  • "His heart is steady; he will not be afraid" (v8). "Steady" (samukh) means upheld, supported, propped up. His heart does not hold itself up. God holds it. JFB offers "firm in right principles" as a fair sense.
  • "Until he looks in triumph on his adversaries" (v8). The godly have enemies. The psalm is realistic. But they will see God's verdict in the end (Ps 54:7; 118:7). The point is not gloating but vindication.
  • "He has distributed freely; he has given to the poor" (v9). "Distributed freely" (pizzar) means scattered, as a farmer scatters seed. His giving is generous and wide-flung, not grudging or narrow. Proverbs 11:24 says, "One gives freely, yet grows all the richer."
  • Paul's use of v9 (2 Cor 9:9). Paul quotes this verse, from the Greek translation, in his appeal for the collection for the poor believers in Jerusalem: "As it is written, 'He has distributed freely, he has given to the poor; his righteousness endures forever.'" Paul's point is that God supplies the giver: "he who supplies seed to the sower… will supply and multiply your seed for sowing" (2 Cor 9:10). The scattering picture of the Hebrew fits his seed image exactly.
  • "His righteousness endures forever; his horn is exalted in honor" (v9). The line from v3 returns. A "horn" (qeren) was the symbol of an animal's strength and dignity, so to have one's horn lifted up is to be given strength and honor (1 Sam 2:1; Ps 92:10). The man who gave himself away is raised up.
  • Commentators: Henry lists "stability" and honor among the blessings of the righteous. JFB says that generosity, good judgment and trust in God together guard the righteous from fear and bring success over enemies. Guzik notes Paul's use of v9 in 2 Corinthians 9, and says the generosity described is wise and planned rather than random. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, says the godly man may change his plans but not the purpose of his soul.

The Desire of the Wicked Will Perish (v10)

  • "The wicked man sees it and is angry" (v10). The blessing of the righteous is visible, and the wicked hate seeing it. "Angry" (ka'as) means vexed, provoked. Envy cannot bear another's good.
  • "He gnashes his teeth and melts away" (v10). Gnashing teeth is a sign of rage and frustration (Ps 35:16; 37:12; Acts 7:54, where the council "ground their teeth" at Stephen). Jesus used the same picture for those shut out of the kingdom: "there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth" (Luke 13:28). "Melts away" means wastes away, dissolves.
  • "The desire of the wicked will perish!" (v10). The psalm ends like Psalm 1: "the way of the wicked will perish" (1:6), using the same Hebrew verb ('avad). The upright man's righteousness "endures forever" (vv3, 9). The wicked man's desire, the thing he lived for, comes to nothing. The acrostic ends on the last letter of the alphabet with a final contrast.
  • Commentators: Henry ends with "the misery of the wicked." JFB says the wicked are disappointed in their malicious wishes and undone by their own evil passions. Guzik connects the gnashing of teeth to Jesus' words in Luke 13:28, and passes on Trapp's description of the envy of the covetous.

Pointing to Christ

Psalm 112 describes a man who fears God, delights in his commands, is gracious and merciful, gives freely to the poor, stands firm in the face of bad news, and is finally lifted up in honor. Only one person has fully been that man. Jesus delighted to do his Father's will (John 4:34; Ps 40:8). He was gracious and merciful as his Father is. He did not merely scatter his wealth to the poor. He "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Cor 8:9), the verse that leads straight into Paul's use of this psalm. He heard the worst news of all, that he must suffer and die, and set his face toward Jerusalem with a heart fixed on his Father (Luke 9:51). And God exalted him and gave him the name above every name (Phil 2:9). Those who are joined to him begin to reflect the same likeness, the moon catching the light of the sun.

Reflection

  • Delighting in God's Commands (v.1) — The blessed person doesn't merely obey the LORD's commandments but delights greatly in them. Is your relationship with God's Word marked by duty or by genuine delight?
  • Becoming Like the God You Fear (v.4) — Light rises in the darkness for the upright, who are gracious, compassionate, and righteous — the same words used of God Himself. Do the people around you see God's character reflected in yours?
  • Unshaken by Bad News (v.7) — The one who trusts the LORD is not afraid of evil tidings, because his heart is fixed and steadfast. When bad news comes, does your heart stay anchored in God, or does it spiral into anxiety?
  • Generosity That Endures (v.9) — The righteous person scatters gifts freely to the poor, and their righteousness endures forever. Are you holding onto your resources out of fear, or releasing them in trust that God will sustain you?
