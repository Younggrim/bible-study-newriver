Summary

The author contrasts God's past revelation through the prophets, given at many times and in various ways, with his final revelation through his Son (vv. 1-2), who reflects God's own glory and bears the very stamp of his nature, upholding the universe by his word and now seated at God's right hand after purifying people from sin (v. 3). A series of Old Testament quotations then establishes the Son's superiority to angels: he alone is called God's Son and is worshiped by angels (vv. 4-6), his throne and reign are eternal while creation itself will wear out and be discarded like an old garment (vv. 8-12), and he sits enthroned while angels remain merely serving spirits sent to assist those who will inherit salvation (vv. 13-14).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown. Hebrews is the only NT book without a named author. Suggested authors include Paul, Barnabas, Apollos, Luke, Priscilla, and others. Origen (3rd century) famously said, "Who wrote Hebrews, God alone knows." The style is different from Paul's letters, but the theology is consistent with Pauline thought. Written before AD 70 (the temple is still standing, cf. 8:4, 10:11).
Recipient: Jewish Christians (hence "Hebrews") who were tempted to return to Judaism under persecution. They were considering abandoning Christ for the familiar comfort of the old covenant system — temple, priesthood, sacrifices.
Purpose: To demonstrate that Christ is better than everything in the old covenant — better than angels (chs.1-2), Moses (ch.3), Joshua (ch.4), Aaron (chs.5-7), the old covenant (chs.8-10). Therefore: don't go back. Hold fast.
Theme: The supremacy and sufficiency of Christ. "Better" appears 13 times. Christ is the final, complete, superior revelation of God.
Chapter 1: Christ is superior to angels — proven by seven OT quotations.
Classification: Epistle — Sermon or Treatise
Key Themes: The Son as God's final word, seven Old Testament citations establishing His superiority to angels, creation and inheritance held by the same person, and a throne that does not pass
God Has Spoken by His Son (vv.1-2a): The letter opens without greeting, sender or addressee — unusual enough that many read it as a sermon later circulated as a letter. It begins instead with a contrast of revelation: God spoke “at sundry times and in divers manners” by the prophets, and has now spoken “by his Son”. The older revelation is not disparaged; it is described as partial and progressive, arriving in fragments. What follows is presented as the same God finishing a sentence He had been speaking for centuries.
Seven Things True of the Son (vv.2b-3): In two verses the writer stacks seven claims: heir of all things, agent of creation, the brightness of God's glory, the express image of His person, sustainer of all things by His word, the one who purged sins, and the one seated at the right hand of the Majesty on high. “Express image” translates “charaktēr”, the impression a die leaves on a coin — not a likeness but an exact stamp. The seated posture matters and will be pressed later in the letter: priests under the old covenant stood because their work was never finished.
Better Than the Angels (vv.4-9): The comparison with angels is not arbitrary. In Second Temple Judaism angels were held to have mediated the law at Sinai, so a claim that the Son surpasses them is a claim about the covenant He brings. The argument is made entirely from Scripture: Psalm 2:7, 2 Samuel 7:14, Psalm 97:7, Psalm 104:4 and Psalm 45:6-7 are cited in turn. Verse 6 has angels worshipping Him, and vv.8-9 address Him directly as God with an everlasting throne — the letter's boldest Christological move, and it is made by quotation rather than assertion.
The Unchanging Creator (vv.10-12): Psalm 102, addressed in its original setting to the LORD, is applied to the Son. The heavens are “the work of thy hands” and will wear out like a garment to be folded away, while He remains the same. The point is permanence against transience, and it prepares the letter's recurring pastoral appeal: hold fast to what does not change, since everything the readers can see will.
The Question Left Hanging (vv.13-14): The chapter ends with Psalm 110:1 — sit at my right hand until I make thine enemies thy footstool — and a question no angel could be the answer to. Angels are then given their actual role, ministering spirits sent to serve those who will inherit salvation. The rhetorical effect is to leave the readers holding a comparison they cannot escape, which is exactly the pressure chapter 2 opens by applying.

Deep Dive

Setting: Hebrews does not name its author, and nobody knows for certain who wrote it. Early Eastern churches thought of Paul, but the writer says he heard the gospel from others (2:3), and his polished Greek is the finest in the New Testament. Barnabas, Luke, Apollos and Priscilla have all been suggested. The church father Origen said that only God knows who wrote it. It reads more like a sermon than a letter, and the writer calls it "my word of exhortation" (13:22). The audience seems to be Jewish Christians who had already suffered for their faith (10:32–34), had grown tired and "dull of hearing" (5:11), and were tempted to drift back to the old ways of temple and synagogue, where life was safer (2:1; 10:35–39). The greeting from "those who come from Italy" (13:24) suggests a link with Rome, and the earliest writer to use Hebrews, Clement of Rome around AD 96, wrote from there. The sacrifices are described as still going on, and the temple's fall in AD 70 is never mentioned, so most date it in the 60s. The whole sermon makes one argument: Jesus is better, better than the prophets, the angels, Moses, the priests and the sacrifices, so do not turn back. Chapter 1 begins at the top.

The Supremacy of God's Son (vv1–4)

  • One sentence. Verses 1–4 are a single, carefully built sentence in Greek, the grand opening of the sermon. In English it is easy to rush through. It rewards reading slowly, phrase by phrase.
  • "Long ago, at many times and in many ways, God spoke" (v1). The first fact is that God speaks. Two rare Greek words begin the book, polymerōs kai polytropōs, "in many portions and in many ways," with a run of "p" sounds that shows the writer's skill. God spoke in pieces, a little to each prophet across many centuries, and in many forms: promises, laws, visions, dreams, history, poetry, a burning bush, a still small voice. This does not mean the Old Testament was faulty. It means it was partial and still unfolding.
  • "But in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son" (v2). The sentence is built on contrasts: long ago and these last days; our fathers and us; the prophets and a Son. In Greek it is literally "in a Son," without "his," stressing what kind of messenger this is. The prophets brought God's word. The Son is God's word (John 1:1, 14). "The last days" in the New Testament means the whole age that began with Christ (Acts 2:17), not only the very end.
  • Seven things about the Son (vv2–3). The writer piles up seven statements, tracing the Son from before creation to his throne: he is (1) heir of all things, (2) the one through whom the world was made, (3) the radiance of God's glory, (4) the exact imprint of his nature, (5) the one who upholds the universe, (6) the one who made purification for sins, and (7) the one who sat down at God's right hand. Colossians 1:15–20, John 1:1–18 and Philippians 2:6–11 sketch the same sweep.
  • "Whom he appointed the heir of all things, through whom also he created the world" (v2). "Heir" echoes Psalm 2:8, where God promises his anointed king, "I will make the nations your heritage." The Son who will inherit everything is the one who made everything (John 1:3; 1 Cor 8:6; Col 1:16). "The world" is literally "the ages," all of time and everything in it.
  • "He is the radiance of the glory of God" (v3). "Radiance" is apaugasma, found only here in the New Testament. The lexicon's main meaning is brightness shining out of a source, like light from the sun, rather than light bouncing off something else, like the moon. The Son is not a second light reflecting God. He is God's own glory shining out. Jewish writers before this used the word of divine Wisdom, "a reflection of eternal light" (Wisdom of Solomon 7:26), but Wisdom never became flesh or died for sins. In the fourth century, defenders of the Son's full deity, especially Athanasius, used this verse to argue that, just as the sun has never existed without its light, there was never a time when the Father existed without the Son. The Nicene Creed's "Light from Light, true God from true God" grows from it.
  • "And the exact imprint of his nature" (v3). "Exact imprint" is charaktēr, the mark a seal leaves in wax or a die stamps on a coin. Every line of the die appears in the coin. The English word "character" comes from it, but the meaning here is not moral character. It means exact likeness: "Whoever has seen me has seen the Father" (John 14:9; Col 1:15). "Nature" is hypostasis, God's essential being, his very reality. (The KJV's "person" reflects how the word was used in later debates about the Trinity.)
  • "He upholds the universe by the word of his power" (v3). "Upholds" is pherō, to bear or carry, in a tense that means continuous action. The Son is not like the Greek giant Atlas, straining to hold up a still world. He carries everything along, keeping it in being and moving it toward its goal, and he does it by speaking, as God created by speaking (Gen 1:3; Ps 33:6).
  • "After making purification for sins" (v3). "Purification" (katharismos) is a priestly word, used in the Greek Old Testament for ritual cleansing. It introduces the great theme of Hebrews 7–10: the Son is also the priest, and he offered himself. Many later manuscripts add "by himself" (as in the KJV), which the oldest leave out. Either way the work is his alone, and it is finished.
  • "He sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high" (v3). This is the first of many uses of Psalm 110:1 in Hebrews (1:13; 8:1; 10:12; 12:2). The right hand is the place of highest honor and authority (1 Kings 2:19). "Sat down" matters: Old Testament priests never sat down on duty, because their work was never done (10:11–12). The Son sat down because nothing was left to do. "The Majesty" is a reverent Jewish way of saying "God" without saying the name.
  • "Having become as much superior to angels as the name he has inherited is more excellent than theirs" (v4). "Having become" does not mean the Son was once less than angels by nature. Verses 2–3 have just said he made the world. It describes his exaltation as the Son who became human, died and rose (2:9). In the fourth-century debates the Arians, who taught that the Son was created, used this verse, and orthodox writers answered in just this way. The "name" is probably "Son," which v5 goes on to explain. Some think it is the divine name itself (compare Phil 2:9–11).
  • Why angels? Jewish thought in this period had a great deal to say about angels: named archangels, ranks of heavenly beings, angels in worship. Above all, tradition held that the law at Sinai was given through angels (Deut 33:2 in the Greek Old Testament; Acts 7:53; Gal 3:19). Hebrews 2:2 calls the law "the message declared by angels." So to show the Son above the angels is to show that the message spoken through the Son is greater than Sinai. There may also have been people who thought of Jesus as a kind of exalted angel, or who gave angels too much honor (Col 2:18).
  • Commentators: Henry reads vv1–3 as "the excellency of the gospel above that of the law," calls the Son "the most excellent messenger that was ever sent into the world," and says "the gospel revelation is the last we are to expect from God." JFB on v1: "All was not revealed to each one prophet," and on v3 he gives apaugasma as "the effulgence of His glory," light shining out, as Guzik also does. Guzik pictures the Son as a prism that gathers the scattered colors of earlier revelation into one beam, and says the Son is not only the messenger but the message. The Tyndale notes leave the "name" in v4 open between "Son" and the divine name. JFB notes that "by himself" in v3 is omitted in the oldest manuscripts.

The Son Superior to Angels (vv5–14)

  • A chain of seven quotations. The writer now strings together seven Old Testament passages, a method Jewish teachers used to make a case by gathering texts. The section opens and closes with the same question, "to which of the angels…?" (vv5, 13), which marks it off as a unit. The quotations are mostly from the Greek Old Testament, the Bible his readers used.
  • "You are my Son, today I have begotten you" (v5). Psalm 2:7. In its first setting this is a coronation psalm: on the day the king of David's line is enthroned, God declares him his "son." No angel was ever addressed that way. Angels as a group are sometimes called "sons of God" (Job 1:6), but none is ever singled out and named "my Son." The New Testament applies this "today" to Jesus' resurrection (Acts 13:33; Rom 1:4), when he was declared Son with power. That declaration did not make him the Son, since he already created the world (v2). It publicly enthroned him. Some commentators, like the church fathers who spoke of the Son's "eternal generation," hear also the Son's timeless relationship to the Father.
  • "I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son" (v5). From God's promise to David about his royal descendant (2 Sam 7:14; 1 Chr 17:13). The promise was first about Solomon but looked beyond him to a king whose throne would last forever. Jesus is that "Son of David."
  • "When he brings the firstborn into the world" (v6). "Firstborn" (prōtotokos) is a title of rank, not a claim that the Son was created first. God says of David's line, "I will make him the firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth" (Ps 89:27). The firstborn received the inheritance and the father's authority. The Greek word order suggests "when he again brings the firstborn into the world," which many take to mean his return in glory. Others think of his coming at the incarnation or his entry into heaven at the ascension.
  • "Let all God's angels worship him" (v6). This line is not in most English versions of the Old Testament, because it is missing from the traditional Hebrew text of Deuteronomy 32:43. But it is in the Greek Old Testament, and a Hebrew manuscript of Deuteronomy found among the Dead Sea Scrolls has a similar line ("bow down to him, all gods"). Hebrews is quoting a genuine ancient form of the text, not inventing one. Psalm 97:7 has a similar call. In Deuteronomy the "him" is the LORD. Hebrews applies it to the Son. Angels worship him, and no one may worship angels (Rev 22:8–9).
  • "He makes his angels winds, and his ministers a flame of fire" (v7). Psalm 104:4. The Hebrew can be read "he makes winds his messengers," meaning that nature serves God. The Greek Old Testament reads it as Hebrews does, with angels as the object: God makes his angels into wind and fire as he chooses. The Hebrew word ruach and the Greek pneuma both mean "wind" and "spirit." The point is that angels are powerful but changeable servants, made and used by God. "Ministers" is leitourgos, a word for someone in public or priestly service.
  • "Your throne, O God, is forever and ever" (v8). Psalm 45, a royal wedding song, addresses the king in startling words, and Hebrews says God speaks them "of the Son." This is one of the places where the New Testament calls Jesus "God" (with John 1:1; 20:28; Rom 9:5; Titus 2:13). The Greek can grammatically be read "God is your throne," and the ESV footnote mentions it. A few early manuscripts read "the scepter of his kingdom," which would fit that reading. But "O God" is the more natural reading in both the psalm and Hebrews, and most translations and commentators take it that way. The angels are servants; the Son has a throne.
  • "Therefore God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness beyond your companions" (v9). Kings were installed by anointing with oil (1 Sam 10:1; 16:13). The Son is anointed because he "loved righteousness and hated wickedness." Here the Son is called God (v8), yet the Father is "your God." Hebrews holds both together without trying to explain it, and later Christian teaching on the Trinity grew from texts like this. "Anointed" is the root of "Messiah" and "Christ."
  • "You, Lord, laid the foundation of the earth in the beginning" (vv10–12). Psalm 102:25–27, a prayer to the LORD as Creator, which Hebrews applies to the Son. The Greek Old Testament adds "Lord" in v25, and its wording invites hearing God answer someone he calls Lord. So a passage about the LORD's eternity is said of Jesus. Creation will "wear out like a garment" and be rolled up "like a robe," changed for a new creation (Isa 34:4; 2 Pet 3:13; Rev 21:1). "But you are the same, and your years will have no end." Later the sermon says it plainly: "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever" (13:8). The added words "like a garment" in v12 are found in the oldest manuscripts of Hebrews but not in the Greek psalm.
  • "Sit at my right hand until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet" (v13). Psalm 110:1, the Old Testament verse most often quoted in the New. Jesus himself used it to show that the Messiah is more than David's son (Mark 12:35–37). Ancient kings literally put their feet on the necks of defeated enemies (Josh 10:24). The Son sits, and his victory is certain.
  • "Ministering spirits sent out to serve for the sake of those who are to inherit salvation" (v14). The final contrast. The Son sits on the throne; angels are sent out on errands. "Ministering" (leitourgikos) is a word the Greek Old Testament uses for things set apart for temple service. Their service is not only to God but on behalf of his people: an angel freed Peter from prison (Acts 12:7–11), and "the angel of the LORD encamps around those who fear him" (Ps 34:7). The angels, glorious as they are, serve the heirs of salvation, ordinary believers. And "inherit salvation" sets up the warning that opens chapter 2: if the message through angels was binding, how will anyone escape who ignores "such a great salvation" (2:1–3)?
  • Commentators: On v5's "today," JFB says it was "fulfilled at the resurrection of Jesus," and notes that Alford instead referred it "to the eternal generation of the Son." Guzik leans the same way as Alford and reads "begotten" as pointing to shared nature, which is a theological reading rather than what the word itself says; the lexicon puts the psalm's "begotten" under figurative, coronation language. On v6 JFB argues from the Greek word order for "when he again brings in," the second coming. On v7 Henry writes, "It is the glory of God that he has such servants; it is yet more so that he does not need them." On vv8–9 all three take "O God" as addressed to the Son, and Guzik notes all three persons of the Trinity in v9: the Father, the anointed Son and the anointing Spirit. Henry: Christ's throne is unlike "all earthly thrones, which are tottering." On v13 JFB explains the footstool from "the custom of conquerors putting the feet on the necks of the conquered." Guzik draws out the contrast of sitting and standing: the Son sits as sovereign while angels stand ready as servants. On v14 Henry adds that angels still serve believers "in opposing the malice and power of evil spirits."

Pointing to Christ

Hebrews 1 does not point to Christ. It begins with him and stays there. Every prophet spoke in part, and the Son is the final word; every angel serves, and the Son sits on the throne; the heavens wear out, and he remains the same. And the one who is the radiance of God's glory is also the one who "made purification for sins." The sermon's whole aim is to keep weary believers from drifting, and its first answer is not a rule but a person: look at who Jesus is, and there will be nothing to go back to.

Reflection

  • God's Final Word (vv.1-2) — After speaking through prophets in many ways over many centuries, God has now spoken fully and finally through His Son. Do you treat Christ as God's definitive word to you, or just one voice among many you listen to?
  • The Exact Imprint of God (v.3) — Christ is described as the radiance of God's glory and the exact representation of His being, sustaining all things by His own powerful word. When you want to know what God is really like, do you look to Jesus first?
  • Sat Down in Victory (v.3) — After purging sins, Christ sat down at God's right hand — a posture of completed work, not ongoing labor. Are you resting in the fact that His sacrifice is finished, or still striving as if there's more for you to add?
  • An Eternal, Unchanging Throne (vv.8-12) — The Father addresses the Son as God, declares His throne eternal, and describes Him as the Creator who remains the same while everything else wears out and changes. In a life full of shifting circumstances, does Christ's unchanging nature give you real stability?
  • Angels in Your Service (v.14) — Angels are described as ministering spirits sent out to serve those who will inherit salvation. Does it change how you see your struggles to know you're served by angels — not because of your own worth, but because of your inheritance in Christ?
