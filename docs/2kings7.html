1Then Elisha said, “Hear the word of the LORD! This is what the LORD says: ‘About this time tomorrow at the gate of Samaria, a seah of fine flour will sell for a shekel, and two seahs of barley will sell for a shekel.’”

2But the officer on whose arm the king leaned answered the man of God, “Look, even if the LORD were to make windows in heaven, could this really happen?” “You will see it with your own eyes,” replied Elisha, “but you will not eat any of it.”

3Now there were four men with leprosy at the entrance of the city gate, and they said to one another, “Why just sit here until we die?

4If we say, ‘Let us go into the city,’ we will die there from the famine in the city; but if we sit here, we will also die. So come now, let us go over to the camp of the Arameans. If they let us live, we will live; if they kill us, we will die.”

5So they arose at twilight and went to the camp of the Arameans. But when they came to the outskirts of the camp, there was not a man to be found.

6For the Lord had caused the Arameans to hear the sound of chariots, horses, and a great army, so that they said to one another, “Look, the king of Israel must have hired the kings of the Hittites and Egyptians to attack us.”

7Thus the Arameans had arisen and fled at twilight, abandoning their tents and horses and donkeys. The camp was intact, and they had run for their lives.

8When the lepers reached the edge of the camp, they went into a tent to eat and drink. Then they carried off the silver, gold, and clothing, and went and hid them. On returning, they entered another tent, carried off some items from there, and hid them.

9Finally, they said to one another, “We are not doing what is right. Today is a day of good news. If we are silent and wait until morning light, our sin will overtake us. Now, therefore, let us go and tell the king’s household.”

10So they went and called out to the gatekeepers of the city, saying, “We went to the Aramean camp and no one was there—not a trace—only tethered horses and donkeys, and the tents were intact.”

11The gatekeepers shouted the news, and it was reported to the king’s household.

12So the king got up in the night and said to his servants, “Let me tell you what the Arameans have done to us. They know we are starving, so they have left the camp to hide in the field, thinking, ‘When they come out of the city, we will take them alive and enter the city.’”

13But one of his servants replied, “Please, have scouts take five of the horses that remain in the city. Their plight will be no worse than all the Israelites who are left here. You can see that all the Israelites here are doomed. So let us send them and find out.”

14Then the scouts took two chariots with horses, and the king sent them after the Aramean army, saying, “Go and see.”

15And they tracked them as far as the Jordan, and indeed, the whole way was littered with the clothing and equipment the Arameans had thrown off in haste. So the scouts returned and told the king.

16Then the people went out and plundered the camp of the Arameans. It was then that a seah of fine flour sold for a shekel, and two seahs of barley sold for a shekel, according to the word of the LORD.

17Now the king had appointed the officer on whose arm he leaned to be in charge of the gate, but the people trampled him in the gateway, and he died, just as the man of God had foretold when the king had come to him.

18It happened just as the man of God had told the king: “About this time tomorrow at the gate of Samaria, two seahs of barley will sell for a shekel, and a seah of fine flour will sell for a shekel.”

19And the officer had answered the man of God, “Look, even if the LORD were to make windows in heaven, could this really happen?” So Elisha had replied, “You will see it with your own eyes, but you will not eat any of it!”

20And that is just what happened to him. The people trampled him in the gateway, and he died.

Summary

Amid Samaria's famine, Elisha prophesies that by the next day fine flour and barley will sell at ordinary prices at the city gate; the king's officer scoffs that this couldn't happen even if the Lord opened windows in the sky, and Elisha tells him he will see it happen but not eat any of it (vv. 1-2). Four leprous men at the gate, facing death either way, decide to surrender to the besieging Syrian camp, only to find it deserted — the Lord had made the Syrians hear the sound of a great army and they fled, abandoning everything (vv. 3-7). The lepers loot the camp before reporting the news to the city, and after scouts confirm the Syrians are gone, the people rush out to plunder the camp, causing food prices to collapse exactly as Elisha foretold (vv. 8-16). The doubting officer, put in charge of the gate, is trampled to death by the surging crowd, fulfilling Elisha's word against him (vv. 17-20).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Jeremiah or a prophetic school. Originally one book with 1 Kings. Covers approximately 250 years (850-586 BC) from Elijah's translation through the Babylonian exile.
Historical Context: Chapter 7 is the dramatic resolution to the siege of Samaria that dominated chapter 6. The city is starving — cannibalism has occurred, the king has threatened Elisha's life, and all seems lost. Into this darkness, Elisha speaks an audacious prophecy: within twenty-four hours, food will be so abundant that prices will collapse to normal levels. The fulfillment comes through the most unlikely agents — four lepers who have nothing to lose. God routs the Syrian army not with swords but with sound: the noise of a phantom army sends them fleeing in panic. The chapter demonstrates God's ability to reverse the most desperate situations overnight, while also solemnly illustrating the consequences of unbelief: the officer who doubted sees the abundance but does not live to enjoy it.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: A prophecy of overnight abundance answered with an officer's cynicism even if the LORD made windows in heaven, four lepers reasoning their way into the enemy camp because staying anywhere else guarantees death, an empty Syrian camp abandoned because God made an army of noise no one actually needed to fight, the exact prices Elisha named fulfilled to the shekel within a day, and the doubting officer trampled to death at the very gate where the abundance he refused to believe pours through
Elisha's Audacious Prophecy (vv.1-2): In the midst of famine so severe that a donkey's head costs eighty shekels, Elisha declares: "To morrow about this time shall a measure of fine flour be sold for a shekel" — a collapse in prices representing sudden, overwhelming abundance. The officer's response is pure skepticism: "if the LORD would make windows in heaven, might this thing be?" — even if God opened the sky itself, this seems impossible. His unbelief is not ignorance but chosen cynicism in the face of God's word. Elisha's judgment is precise: "thou shalt see it with thine eyes, but shalt not eat thereof." The officer will witness God's faithfulness but not participate in it. Unbelief does not prevent God from acting — it prevents the unbeliever from benefiting.
The Four Lepers' Decision (vv.3-8): Four lepers sit at the city gate — excluded from society, excluded from the city, dying of the same famine that kills everyone else. Their logic is flawless: "Why sit we here until we die?" If they enter the city, they die. If they stay, they die. If they go to the Syrians, they might die — or might live. Desperation becomes the doorway to discovery. They choose the only option with any possibility of survival — and find the camp abandoned. God had caused the Syrians to hear the sound of a massive army — chariots, horses, a great host — and they fled in terror, leaving everything behind. The lepers feast, take treasure, hide it — then their conscience speaks: "We do not well: this day is a day of good tidings, and we hold our peace" (v.9). Good news that is hoarded becomes judgment on those who hoard it.
The News Reaches the City (vv.9-16): The lepers report to the gatekeepers; the news reaches the king. His response is suspicion: he assumes a Syrian trap. One servant suggests sending scouts — "they are as all the multitude of Israel that are consumed" anyway (the horses are as good as dead regardless). Two chariots confirm the report: the road to the Jordan is littered with abandoned equipment. The people rush out and plunder the camp. Elisha's prophecy is fulfilled exactly: "a measure of fine flour was sold for a shekel, and two measures of barley for a shekel" — within twenty-four hours, famine became feast. God's word is precise in its timing and its terms.
The Officer's Death (vv.17-20): The doubting officer is placed in charge of the city gate — the very place where the prophecy is fulfilled. As the starving population rushes out, they trample him to death. He sees the abundance — exactly as Elisha said — but does not eat of it. The repetition of the prophecy (vv.18-19) and its fulfillment (v.20) drives the point home with deliberate emphasis. The narrator wants no ambiguity: God's word was spoken, doubted, and fulfilled to the letter — including the judgment on the doubter. Unbelief does not make God's word fail; it makes the unbeliever fail to participate in God's faithfulness.

Deep Dive

Setting: About 850–842 BC, still under King Joram of Israel. The chapter picks up in the middle of a scene. Ben-hadad of Syria has Samaria, Israel's capital, under siege. Food prices are absurd, a donkey's head sells for eighty shekels, and two mothers have eaten one of their sons (6:24–29). The king, wearing hidden sackcloth, has sworn to behead Elisha and has come down to the prophet's house in a rage, saying, "This trouble is from the LORD! Why should I wait for the LORD any longer?" (6:33). The chapter break falls badly. Chapter 7 is God's answer to that despairing question, given to the king's face.

Elisha Promises Food (vv1–2)

  • "Hear the word of the LORD" (v1). The king came to take Elisha's head. Elisha answers with a promise, not a defense. The man who gave up waiting for the LORD is told to wait one more day.
  • "Tomorrow about this time a seah of fine flour shall be sold for a shekel, and two seahs of barley for a shekel" (v1). A seah was a dry measure of about seven liters, roughly seven quarts. A shekel was not cheap by normal standards, but compared with "a donkey's head for eighty shekels" (6:25), this is a collapse in prices overnight. The promise is oddly specific, down to the grain, the price, the hour and the place.
  • "At the gate of Samaria" (v1). The city gate was the town's marketplace and its court. Farmers still sell produce at town gates in parts of the Middle East. The promise is that the market will reopen at the very place where hunger has been most visible.
  • "The captain on whose hand the king leaned" (v2). This is the king's personal aide, a senior officer whose arm the king took when walking (compare 5:18, where Naaman's king leans on him). The Hebrew word, shalish, was originally the "third man" in a chariot. He speaks for the court.
  • "If the LORD himself should make windows in heaven, could this thing be?" (v2). "Windows" ('arubbot) is the word for the floodgates of heaven opened at the flood (Gen 7:11) and the windows God promises to open in Malachi 3:10. The captain is sneering: even if God poured flour from the sky like rain, it could not happen by tomorrow. His mistake is to think the only ways God can act are the ones he can picture.
  • "You shall see it with your own eyes, but you shall not eat of it" (v2). The prophet does not argue. He states two facts, and both will come true.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Man's extremity is God's opportunity of magnifying his own power." He calls the captain's response a serious sin: "Unbelief is a sin by which men greatly dishonour and displease God." JFB calls the remark "scoffing infidelity." Guzik quotes Wiseman that the gate was both market and court, and passes on G. Campbell Morgan's point that unbelief often knows God exists but can only imagine one way for him to work.

The Syrians Flee (vv3–20)

  • "Four men who were lepers at the entrance to the gate" (v3). The law required those with serious skin disease to live outside the camp, calling out "Unclean!" (Lev 13:45–46). So they live at the gate, outside the walls, between the city and the enemy, depending on scraps. With the city starving, there are no scraps. A Jewish tradition makes them Gehazi and his three sons (5:27), but the text gives no names, and the tradition has no support. God's deliverance of the capital is about to come through the four least important people in the kingdom.
  • "Why are we sitting here until we die?" (vv3–4). Their logic is simple. The city means death by famine. Sitting here means death. The Syrian camp might mean death, but it might mean life. "If they kill us we shall but die." This is not a heroic faith, but it is clear-headed, and it moves them.
  • "They arose at twilight" (v5). Evening twilight, as darkness falls. They come to "the edge of the camp," probably the part nearest the city, and find it empty.
  • "The Lord had made the army of the Syrians hear the sound of chariots and of horses" (v6). Here the narrator stops and explains what the lepers could not know. The same twilight hour, the Syrians heard an army that was not there. Whether God put the sound in the air or in their minds, the text does not say. The same God who opened the servant's eyes to an army of chariots (6:17) now opens the Syrians' ears to one. The Syrians who once came to seize a prophet with "horses and chariots" (6:14) now run from the sound of horses and chariots.
  • "The kings of the Hittites and the kings of Egypt" (v6). The great Hittite empire had collapsed centuries before, but smaller "Neo-Hittite" kingdoms still ruled north of Syria, in what is now southern Turkey and northern Syria, and often fought the Syrians. Assyrian records of this period still call that region "the land of Hatti." Egypt lay far to the south. The Syrians imagine Israel has paid armies from both sides to trap them in a pincer. Panic does the rest.
  • "They fled away in the twilight and abandoned their tents, their horses, and their donkeys" (v7). They leave even the animals they could have ridden. Proverbs 28:1: "The wicked flee when no one pursues." God promised Israel this kind of victory if they were faithful (Lev 26:8, 36; Deut 28:7), and he gives it here to an unfaithful king out of sheer mercy.
  • "They went into a tent and ate and drank, and they carried off silver and gold and clothing and went and hid them" (v8). Starving men eat first, which is natural. Then they start hiding treasure, twice. They have stumbled on rescue for a whole city and they are filling their own pockets.
  • "We are not doing right. This day is a day of good news" (v9). "Good news" is besorah, the Hebrew word for welcome tidings, the word behind "gospel" (compare Isa 52:7, the feet of him "who brings good news"). They realize silence is wrong while people inside are dying, and that "punishment will overtake us" if they wait for morning.
  • "They came and called to the gatekeepers" (v10). As lepers, they cannot go in. They shout their message to the men at the gate. Their report is plain: no one there, horses tied, donkeys tied, tents standing.
  • "I will tell you what the Syrians have done to us" (v12). The king is woken in the night and assumes a trap. Pretending to retreat and then ambushing those who come out was an old trick (Josh 8:3–23 at Ai). His suspicion is not foolish in itself, but it is the instinct of a man who has not believed the word given him that very day.
  • "Let some men take five of the remaining horses" (v13). A servant offers a sensible test. His reasoning is grim: the horses will die anyway, like everyone else, so risk them. In the end "two horsemen," literally two chariots with their horses, are sent (v14).
  • "All the way was littered with garments and equipment that the Syrians had thrown away in their haste" (v15). The scouts follow the trail east to the Jordan, about 25 miles. The road is strewn with cloaks and gear dropped by men running for their lives. They are heading home toward Damascus, beyond the river.
  • "So a seah of fine flour was sold for a shekel… according to the word of the LORD" (v16). The prices land exactly on the figures Elisha gave. The narrator underlines it: the word of the LORD came true to the letter and to the hour.
  • "The people trampled him in the gate, so that he died" (v17). The king puts his aide in charge of the gate to control the crowd. Starving people rush the gate toward the camp and the market, and he is crushed. He saw the food with his own eyes and never ate it.
  • The prophecy told twice (vv18–20). The narrator repeats the whole exchange from vv1–2, almost word for word. Hebrew storytelling uses repetition for emphasis. The point is not that a man died in a crowd but that God's word does not fail, in mercy or in judgment.
  • Commentators: Henry draws the moral of the lepers' change of heart: "Selfish narrow-spirited people cannot expect to prosper." On the captain he writes, "If believing shall not be seeing, seeing shall not be enjoying." JFB calls the Syrians' panic "an illusion of the sense of hearing," a miracle "which God wrought directly," and notes that "similar stratagems" to the one the king feared were common in Eastern warfare. JFB does not comment on the Hittites and Egyptians or on the five horses. Guzik leans on the lepers for application: quoting Spurgeon, he says Jesus did not save us so that we would live for ourselves, and adds that those who are enjoying good news are the ones best placed to share it. He also notes that the lepers told the only people they could reach, the gatekeepers, and that was enough.

Pointing to Christ

God rescued a city that had given up on him, through outcasts who could not even come inside the gate, and the rescue came as a gift, with not one Israelite soldier lifting a sword. The gospel works the same way. It is "good news" for people who can do nothing but die where they sit (Eph 2:1–5), carried by people who were outsiders themselves, and it is wrong to keep it quiet. Jesus told his own story of a feast set for the poor, the crippled and the outcasts while the invited guests made excuses (Luke 14:15–24). And the captain stands as a warning: it is possible to be close to the king, to see God's provision with your own eyes, and still miss it through unbelief (Heb 3:19; 4:1–2).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik highlights the irony of God's chosen instruments: four lepers — the most marginalized, despised people in society — become the first to discover God's provision and the messengers of deliverance to an entire city. God consistently uses the unlikely and the broken to accomplish His purposes. Guzik also notes that the officer's unbelief was not punished by exclusion from the blessing but by his own position: he was trampled precisely because he was at the gate where abundance arrived.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Skepticism in the Face of a Promise (v.2) — The king's officer scoffs that even open windows in heaven couldn't produce the abundance Elisha predicts. When God's promise sounds too good to be true, is your first reaction doubt or hope?
  • Desperation Leading to Discovery (vv.3-4) — Four lepers reason that staying means certain death, so moving toward the enemy camp at least offers a chance at life. What door are you avoiding simply because it feels risky, even though staying where you are guarantees nothing changes?
  • Good News Too Good to Keep (v.9) — After feasting and hiding treasure, the lepers' consciences convict them for staying silent while the city still starves. Who around you needs to hear the good news you have already discovered?
  • God's Word Fulfilled to the Letter (v.16) — The famine ends exactly as Elisha said, with prices collapsing to normal within a single day. Where has God already proven His word trustworthy in ways you have been slow to remember?
  • Seeing the Blessing Without Receiving It (v.20) — The doubting officer watches the prophecy fulfilled before his eyes but is trampled at the very gate where it happens. What promise of God have you watched come true for others while still refusing to fully trust it yourself?
