The Foundation

The Backbone of Scripture's Story

A covenant — Hebrew berith — is a solemn, binding agreement between two parties, far weightier than a casual promise. In the ancient Near East, covenants between kings and vassals, or between individuals, were ratified with ceremony and often with blood sacrifice: the parties would walk between the halves of slaughtered animals, invoking on themselves the fate of those animals should they break faith (see Genesis 15:9-17 and Jeremiah 34:18). That ancient background is what makes Genesis 15 so striking — God alone, appearing as a smoking firepot and flaming torch, passes between the pieces while Abram sleeps. God binds himself unilaterally to a promise, staking his own faithfulness on it.

The covenants of the Bible are not six unrelated contracts scattered across the Old Testament. They are progressive and cumulative — each one builds on what came before, adding detail to a single unfolding plan rather than replacing it. The covenant with Adam establishes humanity's calling and the tragedy of its loss. The covenant with Noah secures the stage on which redemption will play out. The covenant with Abraham names the family through whom blessing will come. The covenant with Moses forms that family into a nation and exposes humanity's inability to keep God's law by its own effort. The covenant with David promises an eternal king from that nation. And the new covenant, promised through Jeremiah and inaugurated by Christ, fulfills and surpasses every covenant before it. Read together, they form the connective tissue of the whole biblical storyline — which is why Paul can simply call Israel's forefathers the people "of the covenants" (Romans 9:4) and why Hebrews can spend chapter after chapter showing how Christ is the substance every earlier covenant only sketched in shadow (Hebrews 8:6-13).

A crucial distinction runs through all six: some covenants are conditional, resting on human obedience, while others are unconditional, resting entirely on God's own word. The covenant at Sinai was explicitly conditional — "If you will obey my voice and keep my covenant" (Exodus 19:5) — and Israel's long history of blessing and exile is the record of how that condition played out. The covenants with Noah, Abraham, David, and the new covenant in Christ are unconditional: God binds himself to fulfill them regardless of human performance, which is exactly why they could survive humanity's repeated unfaithfulness. This is not an accident of the text — it is the point. Israel's failure under the conditional Mosaic covenant was never going to be the last word, because three unconditional, grace-grounded covenants surrounded it, each one pointing forward to a day when God's law would no longer be written on stone tablets that could be broken but on hearts that could be transformed (Jeremiah 31:33; 2 Corinthians 3:3).

Seeing the covenants as one connected story changes how the whole Bible reads. Instead of a collection of separate episodes, Genesis through Revelation becomes a single account of a covenant-keeping God pursuing a covenant people through covenant partners who kept failing him — until the last Adam, the true seed of Abraham, the perfect law-keeper, and the eternal Son of David arrived to succeed where every human covenant partner before him had failed. That is the force of Paul's claim in 2 Corinthians 1:20: "All the promises of God find their Yes in him." Every rainbow, every covenant sign, every "I will be your God and you will be my people" throughout the Old Testament was already reaching toward Christ, and in him none of it falls to the ground.

The Adamic Covenant

The Covenant

God's first covenant is made with Adam, representing all humanity, in the garden of Eden. Its terms are laid out in two connected passages: in Genesis 1:26-28, God blesses the man and woman and commissions them to "be fruitful and multiply, and fill the earth and subdue it," exercising dominion over every living creature as God's image-bearers; in Genesis 2:15-17, God places Adam in the garden "to work it and keep it," granting him freedom to eat from every tree except one — the tree of the knowledge of good and evil — on pain of death. The promises on offer were extraordinary: ongoing life, unbroken fellowship with God, delegated rule over creation, and full provision for every need. The Tree of Life stood in the garden as a kind of living sign of that fellowship — continual access to God's presence and to life itself, available as long as Adam remained faithful to the one restriction God had set.

This covenant is conditional in the sharpest sense: a single command, with life or death as the stakes. Adam did not keep it. Genesis 3 records the fall, and Hosea 6:7 looks back on it bluntly — "like Adam, they transgressed the covenant." Every covenant that follows in Scripture is, in one sense, God's response to this first, catastrophic covenant failure: a long work of rescuing what was lost in the garden and restoring humanity to the calling it was given at the beginning.

Fulfillment in Christ

Paul names Jesus directly as the solution to Adam's failure: he is the "last Adam," a "life-giving spirit" where the first Adam became a source of death (1 Corinthians 15:45). Where Adam was given a garden and one restriction and failed it, Jesus was driven into the wilderness and tested far more severely, and succeeded (Matthew 4:1-11). Romans 5:12-21 draws the comparison out at length: "as by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous" (Romans 5:19). Everything Adam forfeited — fellowship with God, standing before him, dominion rightly exercised — is restored in Christ, the one who succeeds as humanity's representative where the first man fell. And the first hint of that rescue comes even before Adam and Eve leave the garden, in God's word to the serpent in Genesis 3:15, the first promise of a coming deliverer.

The Noahic Covenant

The Covenant

After the flood, God establishes a covenant with Noah that is deliberately universal — made not only with Noah and his descendants but with "every living creature" and "all future generations" (Genesis 9:9-12), and so, in effect, with the whole created order. Unlike every covenant that follows it, this one asks nothing of its human partner at all. It is entirely unilateral: God simply promises that he will never again destroy all life with a flood, that the rhythms of "seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, day and night" will continue without interruption as long as the earth endures (Genesis 8:22), and that the world will be preserved until his purposes for it are complete.

The sign of this covenant is the rainbow — set in the clouds as a perpetual, visible reminder, not primarily for Noah's benefit but for God's own: "I will remember my covenant" (Genesis 9:15-16). It functions as the pledge undergirding every other covenant that follows, because without God's commitment to preserve the created order, there would be no stable world within which Abraham's family, Israel's nation, David's throne, or Christ's incarnation could unfold. The Noahic covenant is the floor beneath the entire rest of the biblical story.

Fulfillment in Christ

Peter draws a direct line from Noah's flood to final judgment, and from God's patience then to his patience now: the Lord is "not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" (2 Peter 3:9), holding back final judgment just as he once held back the flood until the ark was ready. The world's preservation under the Noahic covenant is not an end in itself — it is the stage God sustains so that the gospel can go out and the full number of the redeemed can be gathered in before history closes. Isaiah later reaches back to this very covenant as the pattern for God's unbreakable commitment to his people: "as I have sworn that the waters of Noah shall no more go over the earth, so I have sworn that I will not be angry with you" (Isaiah 54:9). The flood that once purged the earth also prefigures, in Peter's reading, the washing of baptism that now saves through the resurrection of Jesus Christ (1 Peter 3:20-21) — judgment passed through so that new life can begin on the other side.

The Abrahamic Covenant

The Covenant

God's call of Abram in Genesis 12 begins with a command to leave his country and family, but it is anchored in a sweeping set of promises: a great nation, a great name, blessing for Abram and for those who bless him, and — reaching far beyond Abram's own household — blessing for "all the families of the earth" through him (Genesis 12:2-3). In Genesis 15, God formalizes this as a covenant through an ancient ceremony of cut animal halves, but with a striking twist: only a smoking firepot and flaming torch, representing God himself, pass between the pieces (Genesis 15:17), while Abram sleeps. Abram contributes nothing to the ceremony. The obligation, and the oath, are God's alone. In Genesis 17, the covenant is confirmed again and its sign is given: circumcision, a mark in the flesh of every male in Abraham's household, not a condition for entering the covenant but a seal of a covenant relationship God had already established by grace.

The promises themselves run in three main strands: descendants as countless as the stars and the sand, a specific land for those descendants to inherit, and blessing flowing outward from Abraham's family to every nation on earth — along with the striking promise that kings, including one line of kings in particular, would come from his line (Genesis 17:6). Taken together, these promises set the whole rest of the Old Testament narrative in motion: the story of Israel is the story of God keeping his word to this one man.

Fulfillment in Christ

Paul reads the Abrahamic covenant as fundamentally a covenant about Christ. In Galatians 3:16 he presses the wording of Genesis closely: God spoke of Abraham's "offspring," singular, "which is Christ" — the promise given to Abraham was, from the start, a promise about one particular descendant through whom the blessing to the nations would finally come. Galatians 3:8 goes further, calling Genesis 12:3 itself a preaching of the gospel in advance: "the Scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, preached the gospel beforehand to Abraham, saying, 'In you shall all the nations be blessed.'" And because the blessing comes by faith rather than by lineage or law-keeping, every believer — Jew or Gentile — who trusts in Christ as Abraham trusted God's promise becomes Abraham's true offspring and heir of this covenant's blessing (Galatians 3:29; Romans 4:16-17). What began as a promise to one childless man in Genesis 12 reaches, in Christ, every nation on earth exactly as God said it would.

The Mosaic Covenant

The Covenant

At Mount Sinai, God forms the freed slaves of Egypt into a covenant nation. The terms are unmistakably conditional: "Now therefore, if you will indeed obey my voice and keep my covenant, you shall be my treasured possession among all peoples... and you shall be to me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exodus 19:5-6). Israel accepts these terms explicitly — "All that the LORD has spoken we will do" (Exodus 19:8) — and the covenant is then sealed with blood, sprinkled on the people and on the altar (Exodus 24:8). The Ten Commandments and the rest of the law given at Sinai spell out what covenant faithfulness would look like, and Deuteronomy 28 later lays out, in detail, the blessings that would follow obedience and the curses that would follow its breach.

The sign of this covenant is the Sabbath — "a sign between me and the people of Israel forever" (Exodus 31:16-17), a weekly, built-in reminder both of God's rest after creation and of Israel's identity as his redeemed, covenant people. Unlike the Abrahamic, Davidic, or new covenants, the Mosaic covenant's blessings were never guaranteed unconditionally; they were always tied to the nation's obedience, which is precisely why Israel's history runs the course it does — centuries of partial faithfulness, prophetic warning, and finally exile, exactly as the covenant's own terms had specified.

Fulfillment in Christ

The New Testament treats the law given at Sinai as real, holy, and good (Romans 7:12) — and also as something no one could keep well enough to be justified by it (Romans 3:20). Paul calls the law a "guardian" that watched over Israel "until Christ came, in order that we might be justified by faith" (Galatians 3:24-25), and he states plainly that "Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes" (Romans 10:4) — not that the law was bad, but that it was never meant to be the final word, only the tutor that exposed humanity's need for a Savior. Jesus himself said he came "not to abolish the Law or the Prophets... but to fulfill them" (Matthew 5:17), and he did so perfectly, succeeding where Israel as a nation had failed. Where the law could condemn but not transform, Galatians 3:13 says Christ "redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" — taking on himself the very penalty the Mosaic covenant had pronounced on covenant-breakers, so that its curse would fall on him instead of on his people.

The Davidic Covenant

The Covenant

When David proposes building a temple for God, the prophet Nathan brings back a far larger promise than David had offered: God himself will build David a "house" — not a building, but a dynasty. The terms, delivered through Nathan in 2 Samuel 7:8-16, promise David an eternal throne, an eternal kingdom, and an enduring line of descendants — "your house and your kingdom shall be made sure forever before me. Your throne shall be established forever" (2 Samuel 7:16). Individual kings in David's line could be disciplined for unfaithfulness, even severely, but the covenant itself — the promise that a descendant of David would sit on an everlasting throne — could never be revoked. God describes the relationship in father-son terms: "I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son" (2 Samuel 7:14), language that reaches its fullest meaning only later, in Christ.

Unlike the Mosaic covenant, nothing in the Davidic covenant is made contingent on David's or his heirs' obedience. Its guarantee rests entirely on God's own character and word, and the ongoing survival of David's royal line — through centuries of good kings and bad, through exile, and even through the apparent end of the monarchy in 586 BC — testifies that this promise was never actually broken, only waiting for its true and final fulfillment.

Fulfillment in Christ

Every Gospel writer takes care to establish that Jesus descends from David; Matthew opens his Gospel with exactly that genealogy (Matthew 1:1). The angel Gabriel announces to Mary that her son will be given "the throne of his father David," that he "will reign over the house of Jacob forever," and that "of his kingdom there will be no end" (Luke 1:32-33) — a direct, deliberate echo of Nathan's words to David a thousand years earlier. Peter preaches the same connection at Pentecost, arguing from Psalm 16 that God had sworn an oath to David to set one of his descendants on his throne, and that this was fulfilled in the resurrection of Jesus, whom God "made both Lord and Christ" (Acts 2:29-36). And Revelation's closing chapter gives Jesus himself the title that gathers up the whole promise: "I am the root and the descendant of David, the bright morning star" (Revelation 22:16). The throne Nathan promised never passed away — it was simply waiting for the King who would never be deposed from it.

The New Covenant

The Covenant

Centuries before Christ, the prophet Jeremiah announced a covenant unlike any before it: "Behold, the days are coming, declares the LORD, when I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah, not like the covenant that I made with their fathers... my covenant that they broke" (Jeremiah 31:31-32). What makes this covenant new is not merely its timing but its nature — God promises, "I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts" (Jeremiah 31:33), accomplishing internally what the Mosaic law could only ever demand from the outside. Alongside that inward transformation comes a comprehensive promise of forgiveness — "I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more" (Jeremiah 31:34) — and direct, unmediated knowledge of God available to every covenant member, "from the least of them to the greatest."

This covenant, like the Abrahamic and Davidic covenants before it, is unconditional: it rests entirely on God's grace, entered by faith rather than earned by obedience to a law. Its sign is the Lord's Supper, instituted by Jesus himself the night before his death: "This cup that is poured out for you is the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20) — the sign given not in stone or circumcision or rainbow, but in bread and wine shared at a table, re-enacted by his people until he returns.

Fulfillment in Christ

Jesus does not merely teach about the new covenant — he inaugurates it, in person, at the Last Supper, identifying the cup he shares with his disciples as "the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20), language that deliberately recalls both the blood that sealed the Mosaic covenant at Sinai (Exodus 24:8) and Jeremiah's promise of a covenant that would not be broken like the old one. Hebrews makes the connection explicit and sustained: Jesus is "the mediator of a new covenant," and "because a death has occurred that redeems them from the transgressions committed under the first covenant," those who are called receive the promised eternal inheritance (Hebrews 9:15). Because this covenant is "enacted on better promises" (Hebrews 8:6) and because its sacrifice never needs repeating, Hebrews 8:13 can say plainly that in establishing it, Christ has made the first covenant obsolete. Every promise embedded in the covenants before it — Adam's restored fellowship, Noah's preserved world, Abraham's worldwide blessing, Israel's law fulfilled rather than abolished, David's eternal throne — converges and comes to rest here, in the covenant sealed not with animal blood but with the blood of the Son of God himself.
