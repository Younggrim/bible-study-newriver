Summary

A Song of Zion celebrating the security of God's holy city. It opens declaring that the LORD is great and greatly to be praised in the city of God, his holy mountain, beautiful in elevation and the joy of the whole earth (vv. 1-2). God has made himself known within its citadels as a fortress (v. 3), and when kings gathered against the city they were struck with astonishment, panic, and trembling like a woman in labor, fleeing in fear (vv. 4-7). What the worshipers had only heard about, they now witness firsthand in the city of the LORD, which God establishes forever (v. 8). Inside the temple, the people meditate on God's steadfast love, and his praise and righteousness reach to the ends of the earth (vv. 9-10). The psalm closes urging the people to walk about Zion, count its towers, and consider its ramparts, so they can tell the next generation that this God is their God forever, who will guide them (vv. 12-14).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Zion Song / Hymn of Praise
Attributed Author: Sons of Korah (A Song and Psalm for the sons of Korah)
Key Themes: The glory of Zion, God as defender of His city, the beauty of God's dwelling, generational testimony, God as eternal guide
Historical Context: Psalm 48 completes the triumphant trilogy of Songs of Korah (46-48) and is classified as a "Song of Zion" — psalms celebrating Jerusalem as God's chosen city (alongside Psalms 46, 76, 84, 87, 122). The psalm celebrates a specific military deliverance: enemy kings assembled against Jerusalem, saw its defenses (or God's defense of it), and fled in terror (vv. 4-7). This may refer to the defeat of Sennacherib (2 Kings 19:35-36) or to the coalition against Jehoshaphat (2 Chronicles 20).
Great Is the LORD (vv.1-3): Great is the LORD, and greatly to be praised in the city of our God, in the mountain of his holiness. The psalm's subject is a place, and the geography is described in terms borrowed from the mythology of the region, beautiful for situation, the joy of the whole earth, is mount Zion, on the sides of the north. Zion is not in the north and is not a notable mountain; the phrase belongs to the divine dwelling of Canaanite poetry, and the psalm assigns it to Jerusalem.
The Kings Were Troubled (vv.4-7): For lo, the kings were assembled, they passed by together. The defeat is described entirely as a reaction, with no battle reported, they saw it, and so they marvelled, they were troubled, and hasted away. Then the physical description, fear took hold upon them there, and pain, as of a woman in travail. And the comparison the section ends on is naval, thou breakest the ships of Tarshish with an east wind, which for an inland city is a borrowed image of unstoppable loss.
As We Have Heard, So Have We Seen (v.8): As we have heard, so have we seen in the city of the LORD of hosts. One verse, and it is the exact counterpart of Psalm 44:1, where the congregation had heard from its fathers and was now losing. Here the inherited report and the present experience agree. The psalter puts both claims in its collection without harmonizing them.
We Have Thought of Thy Lovingkindness (vv.9-11): We have thought of thy lovingkindness, O God, in the midst of thy temple. The word is meditated, and the location is the building, so what is described is deliberate reflection in a particular place. Then the reach of the reputation, according to thy name, O God, so is thy praise unto the ends of the earth. And the response asked for is regional, let mount Zion rejoice, let the daughters of Judah be glad.
Walk About Zion (vv.12-14): Walk about Zion, and go round about her, tell the towers thereof. The instruction is a survey, and it is oddly practical for a hymn: count the towers, mark the bulwarks, consider the palaces. The purpose is given in the next clause and it is educational, that ye may tell it to the generation following. And the closing verse moves from the fortifications to the one who is not among them, for this God is our God for ever and ever, he will be our guide even unto death.

Map & Geography

  • Zion (v.2, v.11, v.12): The hill on which Jerusalem/the Temple stood; often used poetically for God's dwelling.
  • Tarshish (v.7): Distant port city (possibly Spain); represents the farthest reaches of the known world.

Deep Dive

Setting: "A Song. A Psalm of the Sons of Korah." This is the third of the three "songs of Zion" in a row (46–48). Psalm 46 said God is "in the midst" of his city and it "shall not be moved." Psalm 47 crowned him "a great king over all the earth." Psalm 48 now walks through the city of that great King and celebrates a deliverance from enemy kings who came against it and ran. Zion was the hill in Jerusalem that David captured from the Jebusites (2 Sam 5:7). The name came to stand for the whole city and especially the temple mount, where God had chosen to put his name. No occasion is named. Matthew Henry lists the options: Jehoshaphat's victory over Moab, Ammon and Edom, when the Korahite singers went before the army (2 Chr 20), the sudden collapse of Sennacherib's Assyrian siege in Hezekiah's day, about 701 BC (2 Kgs 19:35–36), or one of David's victories. JFB leans toward the Jehoshaphat story, which has kings gathering together, a ships disaster in the same reign (2 Chr 20:35–37) and a return to the temple to give thanks (2 Chr 20:27–28). It remains a guess. The psalm moves from God's greatness in his city (vv1–3), to the rout of the kings (vv4–8), to worship in the temple (vv9–11), to a walk around the walls (vv12–14).

Great Is the LORD (vv1–3)

  • "Great is the LORD and greatly to be praised" (v1). The praise matches its object. A great God deserves great praise. The same line opens 96:4 and 145:3, which adds, "his greatness is unsearchable." Notice that the psalm begins with God, not with the city. Zion's glory is borrowed.
  • "In the city of our God! His holy mountain" (v1). The mountain is "holy" because God has set it apart as his dwelling. Zion was not an impressive mountain. The Mount of Olives just to the east is higher. Its height is spiritual.
  • "Beautiful in elevation, is the joy of all the earth" (v2). "Elevation" means its height or setting. Jerusalem sat on a ridge about 2,500 feet up in the hill country, and pilgrims always "went up" to it. Lamentations later quotes this line in grief, as passers-by mock the ruined city: "Is this the city that was called the perfection of beauty, the joy of all the earth?" (Lam 2:15). The "joy of all the earth" looks beyond Israel. God's city was meant to be good news for the whole world (Isa 2:2–3).
  • "Mount Zion, in the far north" (v2). This is a puzzle, because Zion is not in the north. The Hebrew is yarkete tsaphon, "the heights of Zaphon." Tsaphon means north, but it was also the name of a real mountain (Jebel Aqra, in modern Syria) that the Canaanites believed was the home of their god Baal, their "Olympus." Isaiah uses the same phrase when the king of Babylon boasts, "I will sit on the mount of assembly in the far reaches of the north" (Isa 14:13). The psalm seems to say: the true mountain of the gods is not Zaphon but Zion, where the true God lives. Other readers take it more simply as a poetic word for lofty and exalted (so JFB, who notes that ancient people thought of the north as the highest part of the earth), or as referring to the temple mount, which sat on the north side of the old city of David. The first reading has the best support among modern scholars, and all three agree on the point: Zion is the highest place on earth because God is there.
  • "The city of the great King" (v2). The title from 47:2, the one Assyria's emperors claimed (2 Kgs 18:19). Jesus quotes this verse when he forbids swearing "by Jerusalem, for it is the city of the great King" (Matt 5:35).
  • "Within her citadels God has made himself known as a fortress" (v3). "Citadels" are the fortified palaces and strong towers. Notice the order: the city has citadels, but its real defense is God. He has "made himself known," proved himself, by what he did. That is the event the next stanza describes.
  • Commentators: Henry says the setting "must needs be every way agreeable, when Infinite Wisdom chose it for the place of the sanctuary," and stresses that Jerusalem is praised first "for its relation to God." Guzik contrasts Mount Zion with Mount Sinai, where a fence kept people back (Exod 19:12), and points to Hebrews 12:22: believers "have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem." He cites Kidner, who reads "the far north" as describing earthly Zion in heavenly terms. JFB reads "the mountain of his holiness" as the church.

The Kings Assembled (vv4–8)

  • "For behold, the kings assembled; they came on together" (v4). "Assembled" can mean they met by appointment, an alliance. Several kings joined forces against Jerusalem. That fits the Moab-Ammon-Edom coalition against Jehoshaphat (2 Chr 20:1), or the vassal kings marching under Assyria (Isa 10:8: "Are not my commanders all kings?").
  • "As soon as they saw it, they were astounded" (v5). The Hebrew is a rapid string of short verbs: they saw, they were astounded, they panicked, they fled. No battle is described and no weapon is raised. They simply looked and ran. Henry noticed the reverse of Julius Caesar's famous boast, "I came, I saw, I conquered": here it is Veni, vidi, victus sum, "I came, I saw, I was defeated." Guzik makes the same point from Boice.
  • "Trembling took hold of them there, anguish as of a woman in labor" (v6). Labor pain is the Bible's picture for sudden, helpless agony that cannot be stopped once it starts (Isa 13:8; Jer 6:24; 1 Thess 5:3). These proud kings are reduced to helplessness. "There" points to the spot, as though the psalmist could show where it happened.
  • "By the east wind you shattered the ships of Tarshish" (v7). "Tarshish" was a distant port in the far west, probably in Spain. "Ships of Tarshish" were the large ocean-going trading ships able to make that voyage, the supertankers of their day (1 Kgs 10:22; Isa 2:16). The "east wind" was the scorching, violent wind off the desert, the wind that drove back the Red Sea (Exod 14:21) and wrecked ships at sea (Ezek 27:26). In Jehoshaphat's reign, a fleet he built at Ezion-geber to sail for gold "was wrecked" (1 Kgs 22:48; 2 Chr 20:37). The Tyndale notes suggest the psalm may have that wreck in mind. More likely it is a picture: the kings' armies were shattered as easily as a storm wrecks the mightiest ships. JFB reads it as a picture of God's control over all material powers, and Henry as a storm on a fleet, "when some are split, others shattered, all dispersed."
  • "As we have heard, so have we seen" (v8). This is one of the most important lines in the psalm. Israel had heard the stories of the exodus, of Jericho, of God's victories in the past (44:1). Now they have seen it with their own eyes. The faith passed down to them has been proved true in their own day. Job says something similar at the end of his ordeal: "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you" (Job 42:5).
  • "In the city of the LORD of hosts… which God will establish forever. Selah" (v8). "LORD of hosts" is the commander of heaven's armies, the title from 46:7. "Establish forever" rests on God's promise to David's house and to Zion (2 Sam 7:16; Ps 132:13–14). Jerusalem would fall to Babylon in 586 BC, so this promise looked beyond the earthly city to the one God would build (Heb 11:10; Rev 21:2).
  • Commentators: Henry on v8: "As we have heard done in former providences, in the days of old, so have we seen done in our own days." Guzik draws a lesson from Boice: children who have heard about God from their parents should one day be able to say this for themselves. He also cites Maclaren's observation that no cause for the rout is given and no sword is drawn. JFB thinks the kings were seized by a sudden conviction of God's presence, perhaps through the boldness of the prophets, as in 2 Chronicles 20:14–20.

In the Midst of Your Temple (vv9–11)

  • "We have thought on your steadfast love, O God" (v9). After the battle, worship. "Steadfast love" is chesed, God's loyal covenant love, the love that keeps its promises. "Thought on" is the verb damah, which basically means "to liken" or "compare," and from there "to ponder, picture." JFB catches the sense: they compared this new mercy with God's past dealings and saw they matched. They did not just enjoy the rescue; they meditated on what it showed about God's character.
  • "In the midst of your temple" (v9). The right place to think about God's love is in his presence, among his people. When Jehoshaphat's army came home, they went "to Jerusalem, with harps and lyres and trumpets, to the house of the LORD" (2 Chr 20:28).
  • "As your name, O God, so your praise reaches to the ends of the earth" (v10). God's "name" is his revealed character and reputation. Wherever his name is known, his praise should follow. The news of this victory would spread, and "the fear of God came on all the kingdoms of the countries when they heard that the LORD had fought against the enemies of Israel" (2 Chr 20:29).
  • "Your right hand is filled with righteousness" (v10). The right hand is the hand of strength and action. God's power is never arbitrary. When he acts, he acts rightly. Whatever he holds in his hand is righteous.
  • "Let Mount Zion be glad! Let the daughters of Judah rejoice" (v11). The "daughters of Judah" are the towns and villages of Judah, which in Hebrew can be called the "daughters" of a mother city (Num 21:25, "Heshbon and all its villages," literally "daughters"). Kidner and JFB both take it this way. The women of the towns may also be in view, since it was women who went out singing after victories (Exod 15:20; 1 Sam 18:6).
  • "Because of your judgments!" (v11). "Judgments" here are not court sentences but God's acts of justice, his rescue of the innocent and defeat of the aggressor. For God's people, his judgments are good news.
  • Commentators: Henry: "We must have God's lovingkindness always before our eyes." Guzik makes much of v9, passing on a Spurgeon sermon that gently rebukes church members who always talk about their illnesses, their business troubles or the church's faults, and urges them to talk about God's steadfast love instead. JFB on v9 reads "thought of" as "compared," and "the daughters of Judah" as the small towns or the people.

Walk About Zion (vv12–14)

  • "Walk about Zion, go around her, number her towers" (v12). Now the worshipers are invited on a tour. Walk around the walls. Count the towers. See that not one is missing. The enemy came and went, and the city stands untouched. It is the sort of thing people do after a storm, walking around the house to see what survived.
  • "Consider well her ramparts, go through her citadels" (v13). "Consider well" means set your heart on them, study them. The Tyndale notes call it a visual tour of Zion's strength. But the point is not the masonry.
  • "That you may tell the next generation" (v13). Here is the purpose of the tour. The walls are evidence, and the people are witnesses. They must pass on what they have seen. Faith in the Bible is always handed down: "We will not hide them from their children, but tell to the coming generation the glorious deeds of the LORD" (78:4; Deut 6:20–25). Verse 8 ("as we have heard") becomes v13 ("tell the next generation"). Those who have heard and seen must now speak.
  • "That this is God, our God forever and ever" (v14). The surprise comes at the end. The tour of towers and ramparts ends not with "this is a strong city" but with "this is God." The walls were never the real defense. The city disappears from view and only God remains.
  • "He will guide us forever" (v14). God is the shepherd who leads his flock (23:2–3; 80:1). The last word in Hebrew is unusual: 'al-mut, which literally reads "over death" or "unto death." The King James Version has "he will be our guide even unto death." The Septuagint and ESV read it with different vowels as 'olamot, "forever." Some scholars think it is a musical direction that belongs with the next psalm's title, like "Muth-labben" (title of Ps 9) or "Alamoth" (title of Ps 46). Fittingly, Psalm 49 is all about death. All the readings point the same way: God's guidance does not stop at the grave.
  • Commentators: Henry reads the real bulwarks of Zion as "the special presence of God in it," and on v14 writes, "If we take the Lord for our God, he will conduct and convey us safely to death, through death, and beyond death." Guzik likewise notes that the city fades from view and God himself becomes the focus, and passes on Trapp's caution that "even to death" does not mean God stops there. JFB: the survey shows "how well our God secures His fold."

Pointing to Christ

Psalm 48 celebrates a city that was beautiful because God lived in it. Jesus wept over that city, because it did not recognize the time of its visitation (Luke 19:41–44), and he spoke of his own body as the true temple (John 2:19–21). Through him, believers "have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem" (Heb 12:22), and the city of the great King turns out to be the one Abraham looked for, "whose designer and builder is God" (Heb 11:10). John saw it at the end, coming down out of heaven, with walls and gates and foundations to walk around and measure (Rev 21:10–21), and no temple in it, "for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb" (Rev 21:22). And the last line of the psalm, "he will guide us forever," even over death, is kept by the Good Shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep and says of them, "no one will snatch them out of my hand" (John 10:11, 28).

Reflection

  • Beauty Because God Dwells There (v.2) — Zion isn't praised for its own impressive height or size, but because God's presence makes it the joy of the whole earth. Do you find your significance in what you've built, or in whose presence you actually live in?
  • Enemies Flee Before God's Presence (vv.4-6) — The attacking kings don't even get to fight — they see and flee in panic before a single battle happens. What situation are you trying to fight in your own strength that might actually only need God to show up?
  • From Hearing to Seeing (v.8) — The psalmist moves from what he'd only heard about God to what he has personally witnessed for himself. Is your faith still mostly secondhand, borrowed from what others taught you, or has it become something you've genuinely seen God do?
  • Telling the Next Generation (vv.12-13) — The people are told to deliberately walk the city, count its towers, and take note — specifically so they can pass the story on. Who are you intentionally telling about what God has done in your life?
  • A Guide Even Through Death (v.14) — The psalm closes with the confidence that God will be their guide even to the very end. Does your trust in God extend all the way to your final days, or does it feel limited to the here and now?
