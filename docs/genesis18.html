1Then the LORD appeared to Abraham by the Oaks of Mamre in the heat of the day, while he was sitting at the entrance of his tent.

2And Abraham looked up and saw three men standing nearby. When he saw them, he ran from the entrance of his tent to meet them and bowed low to the ground.

3“My lord,” said Abraham, “if I have found favor in your sight, please do not pass your servant by.

4Let a little water be brought, that you may wash your feet and rest yourselves under the tree.

5And I will bring a bit of bread so that you may refresh yourselves. This is why you have passed your servant’s way. After that, you may continue on your way.” “Yes,” they replied, “you may do as you have said.”

6So Abraham hurried into the tent and said to Sarah, “Quick! Prepare three seahs of fine flour, knead it, and bake some bread.”

7Meanwhile, Abraham ran to the herd, selected a tender and choice calf, and gave it to a servant, who hurried to prepare it.

8Then Abraham brought curds and milk and the calf that had been prepared, and he set them before the men and stood by them under the tree as they ate.

9“Where is your wife Sarah?” they asked. “There, in the tent,” he replied.

10Then the LORD said, “I will surely return to you at this time next year, and your wife Sarah will have a son!” Now Sarah was behind him, listening at the entrance to the tent.

11And Abraham and Sarah were already old and well along in years; Sarah had passed the age of childbearing.

12So she laughed to herself, saying, “After I am worn out and my master is old, will I now have this pleasure?”

13And the LORD asked Abraham, “Why did Sarah laugh and say, ‘Can I really bear a child when I am old?’

14Is anything too difficult for the LORD? At the appointed time I will return to you—in about a year—and Sarah will have a son.”

15But Sarah was afraid, so she denied it and said, “I did not laugh.” “No,” replied the LORD, “but you did laugh.”

16When the men got up to leave, they looked out over Sodom, and Abraham walked along with them to see them off.

17And the LORD said, “Shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do?

18Abraham will surely become a great and powerful nation, and through him all the nations of the earth will be blessed.

19For I have chosen him, so that he will command his children and his household after him to keep the way of the LORD by doing what is right and just, in order that the LORD may bring upon Abraham what He has promised.”

20Then the LORD said, “The outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is great. Because their sin is so grievous,

21I will go down to see if their actions fully justify the outcry that has reached Me. If not, I will find out.”

22And the two men turned away and went toward Sodom, but Abraham remained standing before the LORD.

23Abraham stepped forward and said, “Will You really sweep away the righteous with the wicked?

24What if there are fifty righteous ones in the city? Will You really sweep it away and not spare the place for the sake of the fifty righteous ones who are there?

25Far be it from You to do such a thing—to kill the righteous with the wicked, so that the righteous and the wicked are treated alike. Far be it from You! Will not the Judge of all the earth do what is right?”

26So the LORD replied, “If I find fifty righteous ones within the city of Sodom, on their account I will spare the whole place.”

27Then Abraham answered, “Now that I have ventured to speak to the Lord—though I am but dust and ashes—

28suppose the fifty righteous ones lack five. Will You destroy the whole city for the lack of five?” He replied, “If I find forty-five there, I will not destroy it.”

29Once again Abraham spoke to the LORD, “Suppose forty are found there?” He answered, “On account of the forty, I will not do it.”

30Then Abraham said, “May the Lord not be angry, but let me speak further. Suppose thirty are found there?” He replied, “If I find thirty there, I will not do it.”

31And Abraham said, “Now that I have ventured to speak to the Lord, suppose twenty are found there?” He answered, “On account of the twenty, I will not destroy it.”

32Finally, Abraham said, “May the Lord not be angry, but let me speak once more. Suppose ten are found there?” And He answered, “On account of the ten, I will not destroy it.”

33When the LORD had finished speaking with Abraham, He departed, and Abraham returned home.

Summary

Three visitors — the LORD and two angels — arrive at Abraham's tent near Mamre, and he hurries to prepare a meal for them (vv. 1-8). One visitor promises that Sarah will bear a son within the year; overhearing from the tent, she laughs to herself in disbelief and is questioned about it (vv. 9-15). As the visitors set out toward Sodom, God reveals His intent to judge the city's grave sin (vv. 16-21). Abraham then intercedes on Sodom's behalf, repeatedly bargaining with God and successfully lowering the number of righteous people needed to spare the city from fifty down to ten (vv. 22-33).

Authorship & Background

Author: Moses (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 18 contains two remarkable scenes: the LORD's visit to Abraham at Mamre (vv.1-15) and Abraham's intercession for Sodom (vv.16-33). Three visitors appear — one is the LORD Himself (a theophany), and two are angels (who go on to Sodom in ch.19). The chapter reveals God's intimate friendship with Abraham, confirms Isaac's birth within a year, and shows Abraham as an intercessor — boldly yet humbly pleading for the righteous in Sodom.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: three visitors arriving at Mamre, one of them the LORD himself, met with lavish and immediate hospitality before anyone identifies who they are, a promise that Sarah will have a son within the year met by her own private laughter, a rhetorical question that answers the very doubt just raised, is any thing too hard for the LORD, God choosing to tell Abraham his plan for Sodom rather than hide it from him, and an intercession that talks God down from fifty righteous to ten without ever finding even that many in the city
The Three Visitors (vv.1-8): The LORD appears at Mamre (v.1). Abraham sees three men and runs to serve them (vv.2-3). Lavish hospitality: water, rest, bread, butter, milk, a calf (vv.4-8). Abraham stands by while they eat (v.8). This is the passage referenced in Hebrews 13:2 — "entertained angels unawares."
The Promise of Isaac (vv.9-15): "Where is Sarah?" (v.9). "I will certainly return... and Sarah shall have a son" (v.10). Sarah laughs within herself (v.12). The LORD's response: "Is any thing too hard for the LORD?" (v.14). Sarah denies laughing; the LORD corrects her (v.15).
Abraham's Intercession (vv.16-33): God reveals His plan to judge Sodom (vv.17- 21). "Shall I hide from Abraham?" (v.17) — God treats Abraham as a friend. Abraham intercedes: "Wilt thou also destroy the righteous with the wicked?" (v.23). He negotiates from 50 down to 10 righteous (vv.24-32). "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" (v.25). God agrees to spare the city for 10 righteous — but there aren't even 10.

Deep Dive

Setting: Abraham is 99 and Sarah 89, probably sometime around 2000 BC, though dates for the patriarchs are approximate. They are camped by the oaks of Mamre near Hebron, in the hill country of southern Canaan, where Abraham built an altar years before (13:18). It has been about twenty-four years since God first promised him offspring (12:4). Chapter 17 has just given the covenant of circumcision, changed Abram's name to Abraham and Sarai's to Sarah, and promised a son named Isaac "at this season next year" (17:21). Abraham laughed at that promise (17:17). Now the LORD comes in person, in the form of a traveler, and Sarah hears the promise for herself. The same visit turns toward Sodom, where Lot lives, and the chapter ends with one of the boldest prayers in the Bible. Chapter 18 sets a quiet tent, open to strangers, against a city whose outcry has reached heaven (ch 19).

Isaac's Birth Promised (vv1–21)

  • The ESV heading began back at 17:15. The ESV keeps the whole visit, from the promise of Isaac in chapter 17 through v21 here, under one heading. Its only new heading comes at v22.
  • "The LORD appeared to him" (v1). The narrator tells the reader up front who this is. Abraham, at first, sees only "three men" (v2). The time is "the heat of the day," early afternoon, when travelers would normally rest in shade, so strangers on the road at that hour would need help.
  • Who were the three? The text later separates them. Two "men" go on toward Sodom (v22) and arrive there as "the two angels" (19:1), while "the LORD" stays and talks with Abraham. So most readers take the visitors as the LORD and two angels. Christians have read the LORD's appearance in different ways. Many, from Justin Martyr in the second century to many Protestants today, see a preincarnate appearance of the Son, since "no one has ever seen God" the Father (John 1:18). Others say only that God took visible human form for the occasion, without identifying which person of the Trinity. Some church fathers saw a hint of the Trinity in the three visitors, the picture behind Rublev's famous icon, though the text treats one of the three as distinct from the other two. Jewish tradition generally reads the three as angels, sometimes named as Michael, Gabriel and Raphael, with one speaking for God. The text itself is clear on the main point: the LORD himself came to Abraham's tent.
  • "He ran… and bowed himself to the earth" (v2). A man of 99, in the midday heat, runs. The deep bow is the honor given to a superior, not necessarily worship.
  • "O Lord" (v3). The ESV footnote gives "My lord." The Hebrew consonants could be read either as "my lords" (addressing all three) or "my lord." The scribes who added the vowels pointed it as Adonai, the form reserved for God. The Tyndale notes take this as a signal that this is the LORD himself, while admitting it isn't clear how much Abraham understood at first. By the end of the scene he plainly knows.
  • "A little water… a morsel of bread" (vv4–5). This is the polite understatement of Near Eastern hospitality: offer a little, serve a feast. Washing a traveler's feet was the first kindness to anyone who had walked dusty roads in sandals.
  • "Three seahs of fine flour" (v6). A seah was a dry measure. The lexicon puts it at about 12 liters, which would make this more than 30 liters of the best flour, far more bread than three guests could eat. Estimates of the seah vary, but either way, this is a lavish welcome. "Quick" runs through vv6–7.
  • "A calf, tender and good… curds and milk" (vv7–8). Meat was a luxury in that world, usually eaten only at feasts and sacrifices. A calf is a feast for honored guests, like the fattened calf for the returning son (Luke 15:23). "Curds" (chemah) is curdled or soured milk, something like yogurt. Abraham "stood by them under the tree while they ate," the posture of a host who serves rather than sits.
  • Hospitality to strangers. Hebrews 13:2 almost certainly has this scene in mind: "some have entertained angels unawares." The Tyndale notes add that a shared meal often marked a covenant relationship. As God came near to fulfill his covenant promise, he came to eat at Abraham's table (compare Exod 24:9–11).
  • "Where is Sarah your wife?" (v9). The visitors know her name, her new name given only in 17:15, though no one has introduced her. That alone tells Abraham these are no ordinary travelers.
  • "I will surely return to you about this time next year" (v10). The Hebrew is literally "at the time of life," usually taken to mean "when the season comes round again." The promise in 17:21 is now given a date, and this time Sarah hears it. She is "listening at the tent door behind him," out of sight in the women's part of the tent.
  • "The way of women had ceased to be with Sarah" (v11). The narrator is blunt so the reader knows what Sarah knows: this birth is biologically impossible. Paul draws on this: Abraham "considered… the barrenness of Sarah's womb" and still did not waver (Rom 4:19).
  • "Sarah laughed to herself" (v12). The verb is tsachaq, "laugh," the same verb used when Abraham laughed (17:17) and the root of the name Isaac, Yitschaq, "he laughs." Her word "pleasure" (ednah) is a word for delight. The lexicon notes that here it refers to married intimacy. Her laugh is not mocking, but tired and bitter: after decades of waiting, it sounds too good to be true. Peter later cites her calling Abraham "my lord" in this verse as a sign of respect (1 Pet 3:6).
  • "Why did Sarah laugh?" (v13). The laugh was silent, inside the tent, and the LORD heard it. Notice what he does with her words. She said "my lord is old," but he reports her as saying "now that I am old." Jewish tradition noticed this and saw God leaving out a remark that might have hurt Abraham, softening it for the sake of peace in the home.
  • "Is anything too hard for the LORD?" (v14). This is the center of the scene. "Too hard" is pala, which the lexicon defines as "be beyond one's power, difficult to do," and also "be extraordinary, wonderful." The question comes back in Jeremiah's prayer, "Nothing is too hard for you" (Jer 32:17, 27). The angel's words to Mary, "nothing will be impossible with God" (Luke 1:37), are very close to the old Greek translation of this verse. "At the appointed time" (mo'ed) echoes 17:21. The promise is fulfilled in 21:1–2.
  • "I did not laugh… No, but you did laugh" (v15). Sarah lies "for she was afraid," which shows she now realizes who is speaking. The LORD corrects her firmly but does not cancel the promise. The faithfulness of God doesn't depend on the faith of the person receiving the promise, and it carries her faith along with it. Hebrews remembers her among the faithful: "By faith Sarah herself received power to conceive" (Heb 11:11). When Isaac is born, her laughter becomes joy: "God has made laughter for me" (21:6).
  • "They looked down toward Sodom" (v16). The view east from the hills near Hebron drops toward the Dead Sea basin. Abraham will stand here again and see the smoke rising (19:27–28).
  • "Shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do?" (v17). The LORD speaks in the reader's hearing, as if thinking aloud. Abraham is treated as a friend who is told what is coming, not a servant left in the dark. Scripture later calls him "Abraham your friend" (2 Chr 20:7; Isa 41:8; Jas 2:23), and God calls him a prophet (20:7). Compare Amos 3:7, "the Lord GOD does nothing without revealing his secret to his servants the prophets," and Jesus: "I have called you friends, for all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you" (John 15:15).
  • "All the nations of the earth shall be blessed in him" (v18). The promise of 12:3 is repeated as the reason God shares his plan. Abraham's family is meant to bless nations, not just survive among them. Paul calls this promise the gospel announced in advance (Gal 3:8).
  • "For I have chosen him" (v19). The ESV footnote says the Hebrew is "known" (yada). God's "knowing" here means choosing someone for a close relationship (compare Amos 3:2). The purpose of that choosing is that Abraham will "command his children and his household… to keep the way of the LORD by doing righteousness and justice." That pair, tsedaqah and mishpat, becomes a summary of what God asks of Israel (Isa 5:7; Jer 22:3). It also sets up the next scene, where Abraham appeals to God's own justice. The promise is sure, but God brings it to pass through a family that walks in his ways.
  • "The outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is great" (v20). "Outcry" is tse'aqah, the lexicon's word for a cry of distress, especially one God hears. It is the same word for Israel crying out under slavery in Egypt (Exod 3:7, 9). The cry rises from the victims of Sodom's sin. Ezekiel names the sins: "pride, excess of food, and prosperous ease, but did not aid the poor and needy," along with "abominations" (Ezek 16:49–50).
  • "I will go down to see" (v21). This echoes Babel, where "the LORD came down to see" (11:5). God is not short of information. It is the language of a judge who examines the evidence before he gives a verdict. The ESV footnote offers "they deserve destruction" as an alternative to "they have done altogether according to the outcry," and either way the verse speaks of careful, just judgment.
  • Commentators: Henry lists the views on the visitors, noting that some thought "one of them was the Son of God, the angel of the covenant." Guzik holds firmly that the LORD here is the preincarnate Son, based on John 1:18. JFB heads the section "Entertainment of Angels" but identifies the "chief stranger" as the Lord. The Tyndale notes say only "the Lord and two angels." On the hospitality, Henry writes, "Religion does not destroy, but improve, good manners," and Guzik notes that Abraham and Sarah served the guests themselves instead of leaving it to servants. Henry calls Sarah's laugh "a laughter of doubting and mistrust" and adds, "It is a shame to do amiss, but a greater shame to deny it." JFB suggests that "Long delay seems to have weakened faith," and that she then "added falsehood to distrust." Guzik notes that the Hebrew word for "hard" in v14 is the word for "Wonderful" in Isaiah 9:6. The lexicon shows they are related but not identical: v14 uses the verb pala, while Isaiah 9:6 uses the noun pele from the same root. The link is real, but it is a root connection, not the same word. On v19 Henry writes, "Those that expect family blessings must make conscience of family duty." On v21 he says God "never punishes upon report, or common fame," and JFB likewise reads "I will go down" as language put in human terms. JFB has no separate comment on vv4, 9, 11, 13–15 and 18–20.

Abraham Intercedes for Sodom (vv22–33)

  • "The men turned from there… but Abraham still stood before the LORD" (v22). The two angels go on, and the LORD stays. An old Jewish scribal tradition lists this verse among a handful of "corrections of the scribes." It says the text originally read "the LORD still stood before Abraham," as if God were waiting for Abraham to speak, and that scribes reversed it out of reverence. Scholars debate whether a change was really made. Either way, the scene is an invitation: God has told Abraham his plan and waits to hear what he will say.
  • "Abraham drew near" (v23). He doesn't ask God simply to pull Lot out, though his nephew is surely on his mind (13:12; 14:12). He asks a bigger question: "Will you indeed sweep away the righteous with the wicked?" He is concerned for Lot and for the whole city.
  • "Suppose there are fifty righteous" (v24). Abraham asks not just that the righteous be rescued but that the whole place be spared for their sake. He assumes a few righteous people can protect a city, a principle that runs through Scripture (Jer 5:1; Matt 5:13).
  • "Far be it from you" (v25). The Hebrew chalilah, said twice, is a strong exclamation. The lexicon explains it literally as "to the profane!", meaning that such a thing would be unthinkable, a profaning of God's name. Paul uses the same reflex in Greek, "By no means!" when he defends God's justice: "how could God judge the world?" (Rom 3:5–6).
  • "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (v25). This is the theological peak of the chapter. "What is just" is mishpat, the same word God used of Abraham's calling in v19. Abraham is not trying to teach God justice. He appeals to God's own character, the very thing God chose him to teach his children. Prayer here argues from who God is.
  • "I will spare the whole place for their sake" (v26). God agrees at once and without hesitation. Abraham is not pulling mercy out of a reluctant God. God is drawing out of Abraham the heart he wants his friend to have.
  • "I who am but dust and ashes" (v27). In Hebrew this is a rhyming pair, 'aphar va'epher. "Dust" recalls Adam's creation and his end (2:7; 3:19), and "ashes" stands for something worthless or burned. The lexicon notes that the pair occurs elsewhere only in Job (30:19; 42:6). Abraham's boldness and humility come in the same breath.
  • Fifty, forty-five, forty, thirty, twenty, ten (vv28–32). Abraham asks six times. Each time God agrees, and he never refuses or even pauses. The steps look like haggling, a familiar scene in a Near Eastern market, but the tone is reverent: "Oh let not the Lord be angry" (vv30, 32). The Tyndale notes suggest that Abraham probably overestimated how many righteous people lived in Sodom. In fact, the angels will find only Lot's household, and even his sons-in-law think he is joking (19:14).
  • "Suppose ten are found there" (v32). Why stop at ten? The text doesn't say. Some think ten was Abraham's guess at the size of Lot's household with its married daughters and sons-in-law (19:12–14). Later Jewish practice made ten the smallest group for public prayer, and some readers link that custom back to this verse, though that is tradition, not the text. Others think Abraham simply felt he had reached the limit of what he dared to ask. What matters is that God never said no. Abraham stopped asking before God stopped answering.
  • "And the LORD went his way… and Abraham returned to his place" (v33). The conversation ends quietly. Sodom is not spared, because not even ten are found. But the prayer is not wasted. When the city falls, "God remembered Abraham and sent Lot out of the midst of the overthrow" (19:29). God answered beyond what Abraham asked, saving the righteous out of the city even though he did not spare the city itself.
  • Does prayer change God's mind? Christians answer this in different ways. Some stress that God planned to spare Lot all along and used the conversation to shape Abraham. Others stress that God really does respond to his people's prayers, as he did after Moses interceded (Exod 32:11–14). Both views can agree on what the chapter shows. God invited this prayer (v17), honored every request, and acted in a way that was both just and merciful.
  • Commentators: All three see Abraham as a model intercessor. Henry imagines Abraham thinking, "Shall Sodom be destroyed, and I not speak one good word for it?" and draws the lesson that "though sin is to be hated, sinners are to be pitied and prayed for." On v25 Henry writes, "God is the Judge of all the earth; he gives charge to all, takes cognizance of all, and will pass sentence upon all." Guzik says effective prayer appeals to God's own character and is specific, and that God told Abraham his plan to shape him into an intercessor. He ties this to Christ, who always lives to intercede (Heb 7:25). On why Abraham stopped at ten, Henry and JFB both suggest God restrained him. Henry says, "Because God restrained his spirit from asking any further," and JFB offers that as one option. JFB's other option is that Abraham wrongly felt sure the city would be spared, and JFB adds bluntly that there were not even ten: "There was only one." Guzik goes the other way and wonders whether Abraham should have kept asking. JFB comments on v23 and v33 only, with no notes on vv22 or 24–32.

Pointing to Christ

The LORD who once sat under a tree at Abraham's tent later "became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), and again ate with sinners and travelers. The question "Is anything too hard for the LORD?" is answered with a laugh in Sarah's tent and answered again when an angel tells a virgin in Nazareth that "nothing will be impossible with God" (Luke 1:37). Abraham stood before the LORD and pleaded for a guilty city. Jesus is the greater intercessor who "always lives to make intercession" for his people (Heb 7:25). Abraham asked whether the Judge of all the earth would let the righteous die with the wicked. At the cross the Judge's own Son, the one truly righteous man, died for the wicked, "the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God" (1 Pet 3:18). Sodom could not be spared for lack of ten righteous people, but through the obedience of one many are made righteous (Rom 5:19).

Reflection

  • Hospitality That Became a Divine Encounter (vv.2-8) — Abraham runs to greet three strangers and lavishes them with food and rest, not realizing he's hosting the LORD Himself. Are you open enough to the people who cross your path that you might miss what God is doing through an unplanned interruption?
  • Nothing Too Hard for God (v.14) — When Sarah laughs privately at the thought of bearing a child in her old age, the LORD asks whether anything is too difficult for Him. What impossible situation in your life have you quietly written off instead of bringing to God?
  • Let Into God's Plans as a Friend (v.17) — Before judging Sodom, God decides not to hide His plans from Abraham, treating him as a confidant rather than a bystander. Do you pursue a closeness with God that goes beyond simply asking Him for things?
  • Bold, Humble Intercession (vv.23-32) — Abraham pleads for Sodom again and again, appealing to God's justice while acknowledging he is only dust and ashes. Who in your life or community needs you to keep interceding for them, even when the outcome feels unlikely?
