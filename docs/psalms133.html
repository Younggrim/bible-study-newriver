Summary

This short Song of Ascents, attributed to David, opens by declaring how good and pleasant it is when brothers live together in unity (v. 1). That unity is compared to precious oil poured on Aaron's head, running down his beard and robes, and to the dew of Hermon falling on the mountains of Zion (vv. 2-3a). The psalm closes by noting that it is there, in that unity, that the LORD has commanded his blessing — life forevermore (v. 3b).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Song of Ascents / Wisdom Psalm
Attributed Author: David (superscription: "A Song of degrees of David")
Key Themes: The goodness of unity, anointing that flows from head to body, refreshing dew from above, commanded blessing, brethren dwelling together
Historical Context: Psalm 133 is one of the most beloved short psalms — only 3 verses celebrating the beauty of unity among God's people. When pilgrims from all twelve tribes gathered in Jerusalem for festivals, this psalm captured the joy of togetherness. David (who unified the kingdom after years of civil war) was uniquely qualified to praise unity. The two images — anointing oil flowing down Aaron's beard and Hermon's dew descending on Zion — both move downward: blessing flows from above onto the gathered community. Where unity exists, God commands blessing. Unity doesn't earn blessing; it's where God designates it.
How Good and How Pleasant It Is (v.1): Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity. Three verses, and the first states the subject without arguing for it. Dwelling together had a literal sense for pilgrims sharing quarters at a feast, and a national sense for tribes that spent much of their history at odds; the psalm does not choose between them.
The Precious Ointment upon the Head (v.2): It is like the precious ointment upon the head, that ran down upon the beard, even Aaron's beard: that went down to the skirts of his garments. The comparison is the anointing of a high priest, and the whole force of it is in the running down: the oil is poured at one point and arrives everywhere, so unity is described as something that starts at the head and reaches the hem. It is an extravagant amount of oil, and the extravagance is intended.
As the Dew of Hermon (v.3a): As the dew of Hermon, and as the dew that descended upon the mountains of Zion. Hermon is in the far north and Zion is a hundred and twenty miles south, so dew from one does not fall on the other. The impossibility is the point rather than a mistake: the psalm is claiming that the moisture of the wettest mountain in the land arrives on the driest hill, which is what unity between distant parties would be.
There the LORD Commanded the Blessing (v.3b): For there the LORD commanded the blessing, even life for evermore. The last clause tells the reader where the two images were heading. There means the place where brethren dwell together, and what is said to be there is not a good atmosphere but a command already issued. The psalm ends by making unity the address at which a blessing was left.

Deep Dive

Setting: Psalm 133 is the fourteenth of the fifteen "Songs of Ascents" (Psalms 120–134), the pilgrim songs sung on the way up to Jerusalem for the three great yearly festivals: Passover, Pentecost (the Feast of Weeks) and Tabernacles (Deut 16:16). Its heading says "Of David," and it is the last of four Songs of Ascents linked to him (Pss 122, 124, 131, 133). Some think David wrote it when all the tribes came together at Hebron to make him king over a united Israel after years of civil war (2 Sam 5:1–3; 1 Chron 12:38–40). Others think the heading marks it as written in David's style or for his house, and that it was gathered into the pilgrim collection later. Either way, its setting in the collection is easy to picture. Families from every tribe, from Dan in the far north to Beersheba in the south, traveled for days, camped side by side and arrived in Jerusalem together to worship one God. In three verses the psalm says one thing: unity among God's people is good, it is beautiful, and it is where God pours out his blessing. It uses two pictures to say it, oil running down from a priest's head and dew falling on a mountain. Both pictures move downward. The blessing comes from above. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

How Good and Pleasant It Is (v1)

  • "Behold" (v1). The psalm opens by pointing: look at this. JFB notes that "behold" suits a scene the singers could actually see, "a meeting of the national brotherhood at the great festivals." The pilgrims arriving in Jerusalem were living what they sang.
  • "How good and pleasant it is" (v1). Two words, and they are not the same. "Good" (tov) means right, beneficial, the way things ought to be. It is the word God spoke over his creation (Gen 1:31). "Pleasant" (na'im) means delightful, lovely, sweet to experience. It is used of music (Ps 81:2) and of the "pleasant places" where the boundary lines fall (Ps 16:6). Some things are good but not pleasant, like discipline (Heb 12:11). Some are pleasant but not good. Unity among God's people is both. The repeated "how" (mah) is an exclamation: it is very good and very pleasant.
  • "When brothers dwell in unity" (v1). The Hebrew is literally "the dwelling of brothers also together" (shevet 'achim gam yachad). The little word gam, "also" or "indeed," adds weight: not just living near each other, but truly together. Deuteronomy uses almost the same phrase for brothers who share the family land rather than dividing it: "If brothers dwell together" (Deut 25:5). Genesis tells the opposite story more than once. Abram and Lot "could not dwell together" because of quarrels over pasture (Gen 13:6). Jacob and Esau could not either (Gen 36:7). Joseph's brothers "could not speak peacefully to him" (Gen 37:4). Israel's history was full of brothers at odds, and the nation itself split in two after Solomon (1 Kings 12). The psalm celebrates what was rare.
  • Who are the "brothers"? The word 'ach covers a real brother, a relative, a fellow tribesman or a fellow Israelite. The study notes point out that people can be brothers by blood, by shared work or by shared commitment. In the pilgrim setting it means the whole people of God, gathered from every tribe. The New Testament carries the word over to the church, where believers are "brothers" because they share one Father through Christ (Matt 23:8; Heb 2:11).
  • Unity is not sameness. The pilgrims came from different tribes, regions and walks of life. The psalm does not praise everyone becoming alike. It praises different people living together as one family. Paul makes the same point with the picture of one body with many members (1 Cor 12:12–27).
  • Commentators: Henry says the psalm commends brothers not merely keeping out of each other's way but "delighting in each other with mutual endearments." Living apart may avoid quarrels, he notes, but dwelling together in unity is the better good. He passes on the suggestion that David wrote it when the tribes met "unanimously to make him king," and he offers it as a conjecture. Guzik observes that "good" describes unity's rightness before God and "pleasant" its sweetness to those who share it. He notes that living close together is exactly what tests unity, and passes on Spurgeon's remark that such unity is rare enough to be worth stopping to look at. JFB ties the "behold" to the gathered festival crowds.

Like the Precious Oil on the Head (v2)

  • "It is like the precious oil on the head" (v2). In the ancient Near East, people poured scented olive oil on a guest's head as a sign of welcome and honor, and to refresh skin dried by sun and wind (Ps 23:5; Luke 7:46). The oil was soothing, and its fragrance filled the room. But this is not ordinary oil. "Precious" is literally "the good oil" (hashemen hatov), the same word "good" as in v1. The psalm makes clear which oil it means in the next line.
  • "On the beard of Aaron" (v2). Aaron was Moses' brother and the first high priest of Israel. At his ordination Moses "poured some of the anointing oil on Aaron's head and anointed him to consecrate him" (Lev 8:12; Exod 29:7). That oil was made from a special recipe of olive oil with myrrh, cinnamon, aromatic cane and cassia (Exod 30:22–25). It was holy. No one was allowed to copy it or use it for ordinary purposes, on pain of being cut off from the people (Exod 30:31–33). So the comparison says two things about unity: it is fragrant and delightful, and it is holy, something set apart for God.
  • "Running down on the beard... running down on the collar of his robes" (v2). The oil was poured generously, not dabbed. It ran from his head onto his beard and down to his garments. Exodus 29:21 also describes the oil and blood being sprinkled on Aaron's garments so that "he and his garments shall be holy." The repeated "running down" is the same Hebrew verb, yarad, "to go down." It appears a third time in v3, where the dew "falls." The whole psalm flows downward. Unity is not something people work up from below. It comes down from God, like oil from the head.
  • "The collar of his robes" (v2). The Hebrew is literally "the mouth of his garments" (pi middotav), meaning the opening at the neck. Older translations such as the KJV read "the skirts of his garments," as if the oil ran all the way to the hem. Most modern translations, including the ESV, take it as the collar, the neckline where the beard rests. Either way, the picture is of oil that does not stop at the head but covers the whole man.
  • Why the high priest? The high priest stood for the whole people before God. On his shoulders and over his heart he carried stones engraved with the names of the twelve tribes (Exod 28:9–12, 21, 29). When the oil ran down from his head over his garments, it ran over the names of all the tribes together. The image fits a psalm about the tribes living as one: the anointing on the head flowed down to all of them.
  • Commentators: Henry calls unity "fragrant as the holy anointing oil" and draws out the point that the oil was holy, so brotherly love must come from "a pure heart, devoted to God." He compares it to "ointment and perfume which rejoice the heart" (Prov 27:9). JFB notes the oil's rich blend of olive oil and spices and takes its abundance as a figure of the abundance of the Spirit's graces. Guzik passes on Kidner's observation that the oil did not stay on the head but reached the garments, and Kidner's point that true unity is given from above rather than manufactured. He also notes the old debate over collar or hem, and Boice's point that the verb "running down" is repeated, stressing that the blessing descends from God. The lexicon confirms that the same verb, yarad, stands behind all three "down" words in vv2–3.

Like the Dew of Hermon (v3)

  • "It is like the dew of Hermon" (v3). Mount Hermon stands at the far north of Israel, on the border of modern Lebanon and Syria, rising over 9,000 feet. It is snow-capped much of the year, and moist air coming in from the Mediterranean makes its dew famously heavy. Travelers in the 1600s and 1700s reported tents soaked through by Hermon's night dew even in the dry season. In a land with almost no summer rain, dew kept crops and pastures alive between April and October. It was a picture of life and blessing (Gen 27:28; Deut 33:28; Hos 14:5).
  • "Which falls on the mountains of Zion" (v3). Zion is the hill of Jerusalem, more than a hundred miles south of Hermon and far drier. Hermon's dew does not literally travel to Jerusalem. The study notes explain that Jerusalem's dew comes from moist winds off the Mediterranean. Most interpreters read the line as a striking picture: dew as rich as Hermon's falling on Zion. The far north and the center of worship are joined in one image, just as the tribes from the north and south were joined at the festivals. Some suggest "the mountains of Zion" here means the hills of the land more broadly, or that "Zion" stands for the whole land of Israel. The main point is plain either way: unity refreshes and makes things grow, the way dew brings green life to dry hills.
  • Oil and dew together. Both pictures come down from above. Both are gentle, quiet and abundant. Oil consecrates; dew refreshes. Unity among God's people does both: it sets them apart for God and brings life to them.
  • "For there the LORD has commanded the blessing" (v3). "There" points to Zion, where the brothers gather to worship. God does not simply allow blessing there. He "commanded" (tsivvah) it, the same verb used when God commanded his blessing on the land in the sabbath year (Lev 25:21) and promised to "command the blessing" on Israel's barns (Deut 28:8). Where God's people dwell together in unity before him, he has decreed that blessing will flow.
  • "Life forevermore" (v3). The blessing is life, chayyim, and it lasts "forevermore" ('ad ha'olam). This is the only place in the Songs of Ascents where the blessing is named this way. The Old Testament speaks of eternal life rarely and in hints (Ps 16:11; Dan 12:2). Here, at the end of a short psalm about brothers getting along, it reaches past this life altogether. The study notes put it simply: God gives life everlasting to human beings. Psalm 134, which follows, closes the collection with a blessing that also comes "from Zion" (134:3).
  • Commentators: Henry says the dew "cools the scorching heat of men's passions," and adds that both "Hermon and Zion will wither without this dew." He calls what God commands there "a complicated blessing, including all blessings," and says the blessing on those who dwell in love "is life for evermore." JFB takes the dew as fertilizing, so that unity bears fruit in good works, and reads "there" as Zion, the Church, the source of blessing. Guzik notes, with Clarke, the travelers' reports of Hermon's heavy dew, and passes on Poole's view that the dew is a figure for God's favor rather than a weather report. He adds that since believers will share eternal life together, they have every reason to pursue unity now.

Pointing to Christ

Psalm 133 pictures oil poured on the head of the high priest and running down over the names of all the tribes. Jesus is the great High Priest, and his name, "Christ," means "Anointed One." The Spirit came down on him at his baptism (Matt 3:16), and from him, the head, the Spirit flows to his whole body, the church (Acts 2:33; Eph 4:15–16). The unity the psalm celebrates is what Jesus prayed for on the night before he died: "that they may all be one, just as you, Father, are in me, and I in you" (John 17:21). It is not something his people manufacture. He made it by his death, breaking down the dividing wall between peoples and making "one new man in place of the two, so making peace" (Eph 2:14–16). His followers are called to "maintain the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace" (Eph 4:3), not to create it. And the blessing commanded on Zion, "life forevermore," is the eternal life he gives to all who belong to him (John 10:28), shared with every brother and sister in the city of the living God, "the heavenly Jerusalem" (Heb 12:22–23).

Reflection

  • Unity Is Both Good and Enjoyable (v.1) — The psalmist doesn't just call unity among God's people right, he calls it pleasant — genuinely good to experience. Is the unity in your church or family something you actually enjoy, or just something you tolerate?
  • Blessing That Flows From Above (v.2) — The image of oil poured on Aaron's head running down over his robes pictures blessing that originates above and flows downward onto the whole community. Are you waiting to manufacture unity yourself, or receiving it as something God pours out?
  • Where God Commands Blessing (v.3) — The psalm ends by declaring that it is in that place of unity where the Lord has commanded His blessing, life forevermore. What would change in your relationships if you believed unity itself was the place God chooses to bless?
