1These are the statutes and ordinances you must be careful to follow all the days you live in the land that the LORD, the God of your fathers, has given you to possess.

2Destroy completely all the places where the nations you are dispossessing have served their gods—atop the high mountains, on the hills, and under every green tree.

3Tear down their altars, smash their sacred pillars, burn up their Asherah poles, cut down the idols of their gods, and wipe out their names from every place.

4You shall not worship the LORD your God in this way.

5Instead, you must seek the place the LORD your God will choose from among all your tribes to establish as a dwelling for His Name, and there you must go.

6To that place you are to bring your burnt offerings and sacrifices, your tithes and heave offerings, your vow offerings and freewill offerings, as well as the firstborn of your herds and flocks.

7There, in the presence of the LORD your God, you and your households shall eat and rejoice in all you do, because the LORD your God has blessed you.

8You are not to do as we are doing here today, where everyone does what seems right in his own eyes.

9For you have not yet come to the resting place and the inheritance that the LORD your God is giving you.

10When you cross the Jordan and live in the land that the LORD your God is giving you as an inheritance, and He gives you rest from all the enemies around you and you dwell securely,

11then the LORD your God will choose a dwelling for His Name. And there you are to bring everything I command you: your burnt offerings and sacrifices, your tithes and special gifts, and all the choice offerings you vow to the LORD.

12And you shall rejoice before the LORD your God—you, your sons and daughters, your menservants and maidservants, and the Levite within your gates, since he has no portion or inheritance among you.

13Be careful not to offer your burnt offerings in just any place you see;

14you must offer them only in the place the LORD will choose in one of your tribal territories, and there you shall do all that I command you.

15But whenever you want, you may slaughter and eat meat within any of your gates, according to the blessing the LORD your God has given you. Both the ceremonially clean and unclean may eat it as they would a gazelle or deer,

16but you must not eat the blood; pour it on the ground like water.

17Within your gates you must not eat the tithe of your grain or new wine or oil, the firstborn of your herds or flocks, any of the offerings that you have vowed to give, or your freewill offerings or special gifts.

18Instead, you must eat them in the presence of the LORD your God at the place the LORD your God will choose—you, your sons and daughters, your menservants and maidservants, and the Levite within your gates. Rejoice before the LORD your God in all you do,

19and be careful not to neglect the Levites as long as you live in your land.

20When the LORD your God expands your territory as He has promised, and you crave meat and say, “I want to eat meat,” you may eat it whenever you want.

21If the place where the LORD your God chooses to put His Name is too far from you, then you may slaughter any of the herd or flock He has given you, as I have commanded you, and you may eat it within your gates whenever you want.

22Indeed, you may eat it as you would eat a gazelle or deer; both the ceremonially unclean and the clean may eat it.

23Only be sure not to eat the blood, because the blood is the life, and you must not eat the life with the meat.

24You must not eat the blood; pour it on the ground like water.

25Do not eat it, so that it may go well with you and your children after you, because you will be doing what is right in the eyes of the LORD.

26But you are to take your holy things and your vow offerings and go to the place the LORD will choose.

27Present the meat and blood of your burnt offerings on the altar of the LORD your God. The blood of your other sacrifices must be poured out beside the altar of the LORD your God, but you may eat the meat.

28Be careful to obey all these things I command you, so that it may always go well with you and your children after you, because you will be doing what is good and right in the eyes of the LORD your God.

29When the LORD your God cuts off before you the nations you are entering to dispossess, and you drive them out and live in their land,

30be careful not to be ensnared by their ways after they have been destroyed before you. Do not inquire about their gods, asking, “How do these nations serve their gods? I will do likewise.”

31You must not worship the LORD your God in this way, because they practice for their gods every abomination which the LORD hates. They even burn their sons and daughters in the fire as sacrifices to their gods.

32See that you do everything I command you; do not add to it or subtract from it.

1These are the statutes and judgments, which ye shall observe to do in the land, which the LORD God of thy fathers giveth thee to possess it, all the days that ye live upon the earth.

2Ye shall utterly destroy all the places, wherein the nations which ye shall possess served their gods, upon the high mountains, and upon the hills, and under every green tree:

3And ye shall overthrow their altars, and break their pillars, and burn their groves with fire; and ye shall hew down the graven images of their gods, and destroy the names of them out of that place.

4Ye shall not do so unto the LORD your God.

5But unto the place which the LORD your God shall choose out of all your tribes to put his name there, even unto his habitation shall ye seek, and thither thou shalt come:

6And thither ye shall bring your burnt offerings, and your sacrifices, and your tithes, and heave offerings of your hand, and your vows, and your freewill offerings, and the firstlings of your herds and of your flocks:

7And there ye shall eat before the LORD your God, and ye shall rejoice in all that ye put your hand unto, ye and your households, wherein the LORD thy God hath blessed thee.

8Ye shall not do after all the things that we do here this day, every man whatsoever is right in his own eyes.

9For ye are not as yet come to the rest and to the inheritance, which the LORD your God giveth you.

10But when ye go over Jordan, and dwell in the land which the LORD your God giveth you to inherit, and when he giveth you rest from all your enemies round about, so that ye dwell in safety;

11Then there shall be a place which the LORD your God shall choose to cause his name to dwell there; thither shall ye bring all that I command you; your burnt offerings, and your sacrifices, your tithes, and the heave offering of your hand, and all your choice vows which ye vow unto the LORD:

12And ye shall rejoice before the LORD your God, ye, and your sons, and your daughters, and your menservants, and your maidservants, and the Levite that is within your gates; forasmuch as he hath no part nor inheritance with you.

13Take heed to thyself that thou offer not thy burnt offerings in every place that thou seest:

14But in the place which the LORD shall choose in one of thy tribes, there thou shalt offer thy burnt offerings, and there thou shalt do all that I command thee.

15Notwithstanding thou mayest kill and eat flesh in all thy gates, whatsoever thy soul lusteth after, according to the blessing of the LORD thy God which he hath given thee: the unclean and the clean may eat thereof, as of the roebuck, and as of the hart.

16Only ye shall not eat the blood; ye shall pour it upon the earth as water.

17Thou mayest not eat within thy gates the tithe of thy corn, or of thy wine, or of thy oil, or the firstlings of thy herds or of thy flock, nor any of thy vows which thou vowest, nor thy freewill offerings, or heave offering of thine hand:

18But thou must eat them before the LORD thy God in the place which the LORD thy God shall choose, thou, and thy son, and thy daughter, and thy manservant, and thy maidservant, and the Levite that is within thy gates: and thou shalt rejoice before the LORD thy God in all that thou puttest thine hands unto.

19Take heed to thyself that thou forsake not the Levite as long as thou livest upon the earth.

20When the LORD thy God shall enlarge thy border, as he hath promised thee, and thou shalt say, I will eat flesh, because thy soul longeth to eat flesh; thou mayest eat flesh, whatsoever thy soul lusteth after.

21If the place which the LORD thy God hath chosen to put his name there be too far from thee, then thou shalt kill of thy herd and of thy flock, which the LORD hath given thee, as I have commanded thee, and thou shalt eat in thy gates whatsoever thy soul lusteth after.

22Even as the roebuck and the hart is eaten, so thou shalt eat them: the unclean and the clean shall eat of them alike.

23Only be sure that thou eat not the blood: for the blood is the life; and thou mayest not eat the life with the flesh.

24Thou shalt not eat it; thou shalt pour it upon the earth as water.

25Thou shalt not eat it; that it may go well with thee, and with thy children after thee, when thou shalt do that which is right in the sight of the LORD.

26Only thy holy things which thou hast, and thy vows, thou shalt take, and go unto the place which the LORD shall choose:

27And thou shalt offer thy burnt offerings, the flesh and the blood, upon the altar of the LORD thy God: and the blood of thy sacrifices shall be poured out upon the altar of the LORD thy God, and thou shalt eat the flesh.

28Observe and hear all these words which I command thee, that it may go well with thee, and with thy children after thee for ever, when thou doest that which is good and right in the sight of the LORD thy God.

29When the LORD thy God shall cut off the nations from before thee, whither thou goest to possess them, and thou succeedest them, and dwellest in their land;

Summary

Moses commands Israel to destroy every place where the Canaanite nations worshiped their gods, their altars, pillars, and carved images, once they take possession of the land (vv. 1-3). Unlike the pagan practice of worshiping anywhere, Israel is told to bring their offerings, tithes, and sacrifices only to the one place the LORD will choose to put His name, where the whole community will eat and rejoice before Him (vv. 4-14). Meat for ordinary meals may be eaten anywhere, but Israel is repeatedly warned not to eat blood and not to imitate the worship practices of the nations they are displacing, nor add to or take away from what God has commanded (vv. 15-32).

Authorship & Background

Author: Moses. Deuteronomy is the fifth and final book of the Pentateuch, written at the end of the 40 years of wilderness wandering (approximately 1406 BC). Hebrew title: "Devarim" — "Words/Things." Greek title: "Deuteronomion" — "Second Law" (a repetition/expansion of the Law for the new generation). The book consists of Moses' farewell speeches to Israel on the plains of Moab, just before they enter the Promised Land. Moses will not enter with them — these are his final words to the nation he has led for 40 years.
Historical Context: Chapter 12 begins the specific legislation section of Deuteronomy (chapters 12-26) — the detailed laws that will govern life in the Promised Land. The first and most fundamental law concerns worship: where and how Israel is to worship God. The chapter establishes the principle of centralized worship — Israel must worship only "in the place which the LORD your God shall choose" (v.5). This stands in direct contrast to Canaanite worship, which occurred at multiple sites: high mountains, hills, and under every green tree (v.2). God demands that all pagan worship sites be utterly destroyed and that Israel worship Him only at His chosen place. The phrase "the place which the LORD shall choose" occurs repeatedly (vv.5,11,14,18,21,26) — this will ultimately be Jerusalem (2 Chronicles 7:12). The chapter also addresses a practical concern: when the central sanctuary is far away, ordinary (non-sacrificial) slaughter of animals for food is permitted locally (vv.15,20-22). But sacred offerings must go to the chosen place. The overarching principle: do not worship God the way pagans worship their gods (vv.4,30-31). God determines how He is to be worshiped — not human preference or pagan precedent.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: every pagan worship site marked for total destruction, altars torn down, pillars broken, names erased, before a single word is said about how Israel itself should worship, a single chosen place established in deliberate contrast to every man doing what is right in his own eyes, ordinary meat permitted to be slaughtered locally for food while tithes, firstlings and vows are reserved strictly for the chosen place, a warning not to forsake the Levite repeated inside the very laws that seem to make room for eating apart from him, and a prohibition on drinking blood stated three times in eleven verses as though it were the instruction most often broken
Destroy All Pagan Worship Sites (vv.1-4): "These are the statutes and judgments" for life in the land (v.1). First command: "utterly destroy all the places" where the nations served their gods (v.2) — on high mountains, hills, under green trees. Tear down altars, break pillars, burn Asherim/ groves, hew down images, "destroy the names of them out of that place" (v.3). Then the pivotal statement: "Ye shall not do so unto the LORD your God" (v.4). Do not worship God at pagan sites or in pagan ways. God is not to be worshiped wherever you please or however you please.
The Place God Will Choose (vv.5-7): "But unto the place which the LORD your God shall choose out of all your tribes to put his name there" (v.5) — seek that place and come there. Bring burnt offerings, sacrifices, tithes, heave offerings, vows, freewill offerings, firstborn of herds and flocks (v.6). "There ye shall eat before the LORD your God, and ye shall rejoice" (v.7). Worship is not grim duty but joyful feasting in God's presence.
One Place of Worship, Not Every Man His Own (vv.8-14): Ye shall not do after all the things that we do here this day, every man whatsoever is right in his own eyes. That sentence is the hinge of the chapter and the phrase Judges will use as its epitaph. The wilderness arrangement was provisional because ye are not as yet come to the rest, and once the land is settled the rule changes: bring your offerings unto the place which the LORD your God shall choose. Not a ban on joy, the passage is emphatic that they shall rejoice before the LORD with their households and the Levite. A ban on improvisation, take heed to thyself that thou offer not thy burnt offerings in every place that thou seest.
Permission for Local Slaughter (vv.15-16): "Notwithstanding thou mayest kill and eat flesh in all thy gates" (v.15) — ordinary meat for food may be slaughtered anywhere. Both ritually clean and unclean persons may eat it — "as of the roebuck, and as of the hart" (KJV) / "as of the gazelle and as of the deer" (ESV) (v.15). It is treated as common food, not sacrifice. One prohibition: "ye shall not eat the blood; ye shall pour it upon the earth as water" (v.16).
The Tithe, the Levite, and the Blood (vv.17-28): The concession of the previous verses is fenced. What may be eaten at home is ordinary meat, not the tithe, the firstlings or the vows, which are to be eaten before the LORD in the chosen place. Then a clause easy to read past, take heed that thou forsake not the Levite as long as thou livest, because the Levite has no land and lives on exactly this. The prohibition on blood is repeated three times in eleven verses, thou shalt not eat the blood, for the blood is the life, pour it upon the earth as water. Three repetitions suggest it was the instruction most often ignored.
Do Not Worship God Like the Pagans (vv.29-32): When God cuts off the nations, "take heed to thyself that thou be not snared by following them" (v.30). Do not ask: "How did these nations serve their gods? even so will I do likewise" (v.30). "Thou shalt not do so unto the LORD thy God" (v.31). Why? "For every abomination to the LORD, which he hateth, have they done unto their gods; for even their sons and their daughters they have burnt in the fire to their gods" (v.31). Canaanite worship included child sacrifice. Final command: "What thing soever I command you, observe to do it: thou shalt not add thereto, nor diminish from it" (v.32). God alone determines how He is worshiped.

Setting: The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life. Chapters 5–11 laid the foundation of Moses' second sermon: the Ten Commandments, the call to love God with all one's heart, and the choice between blessing and curse. Now, at chapter 12, the sermon turns to the "statutes and rules" (v1), the detailed laws of chapters 12–26 for life in the land. Many scholars note that these laws follow the general order of the Ten Commandments, and this first block applies the first and second commandments: worship the LORD alone, and worship him in the way he chooses. The great theme of the chapter is "the place that the LORD your God will choose." In Canaan every hill had its shrine. Israel was to have one altar, at one place of God's choosing, where the whole nation would come to sacrifice, eat and rejoice before him.

The LORD's Chosen Place of Worship (vv1–28)

  • "These are the statutes and rules" (v1). A new heading, like a chapter title. "All the days that you live on the earth" means these laws are for life in the land, not only for the moment of conquest.
  • "On the high mountains and on the hills and under every green tree" (v2). Canaanite worship happened in the open air. Hilltops felt closer to the gods of sky and storm, and leafy trees, green even in the dry season, seemed full of life and fertility. These shrines are the "high places" that come up again and again in Kings, where even good kings are faulted because "the high places were not taken away" (1 Kgs 15:14; 2 Kgs 12:3).
  • "Tear down their altars… dash in pieces their pillars… burn their Asherim… destroy their name out of that place" (v3). This repeats 7:5 and adds one thing: their names. Shrines were often named for the god worshipped there. To destroy the name was to make sure no one remembered or invoked that god there again.
  • "You shall not worship the LORD your God in that way" (v4). This is the key line, and it returns at v31. The danger was not only worshipping other gods but worshipping the true God in a pagan way, using their sites, their methods and their assumptions. The golden calf was exactly that (9:16).
  • "Seek the place that the LORD your God will choose out of all your tribes to put his name and make his habitation there" (v5). The phrase "the place that the LORD will choose" comes up more than twenty times in Deuteronomy, yet the place is never named. At first it was wherever the tabernacle stood: Shiloh for a long period (Josh 18:1; Jer 7:12), and later Jerusalem, chosen in David's day (2 Sam 24:18–25; 2 Chr 6:6). To "put his name" there means God makes himself present and known there in a special way, while heaven remains his dwelling (1 Kgs 8:27–30).
  • Why one place? It guarded against the slow blending of Israel's worship with Canaan's local shrines. It bound the twelve tribes together as one people, meeting at one altar. And it taught that God sets the terms for approaching him. Many scholars think this law is the one King Josiah enforced when he closed the high places (2 Kgs 22–23), and some date the writing of Deuteronomy to his time. Others hold that the law came from Moses and was rediscovered and enforced then. The Tyndale notes add that local family worship continued, and Samuel offered sacrifices at several places while the ark had no settled home (1 Sam 7:17; 9:12).
  • "Your burnt offerings and your sacrifices, your tithes and the contribution… vow offerings… freewill offerings, and the firstborn" (v6). The list covers every kind of gift. Burnt offerings were wholly burned on the altar. Sacrifices here means peace or fellowship offerings, mostly eaten by the worshipper. Tithes were a tenth of the harvest (14:22–29). The contribution (literally "the lifting of your hand") was a set-apart gift. Vow and freewill offerings were voluntary.
  • "There you shall eat before the LORD your God, and you shall rejoice" (v7). This surprises some readers. Worship at the central place was a feast. Families ate the meat of their offerings together in God's presence, celebrating what he had given. "Rejoice" comes again in vv12 and 18. Deuteronomy commands joy more than any other book of the law.
  • "Everyone doing whatever is right in his own eyes" (v8). Moses admits that in the wilderness, worship had been irregular. Amos 5:25–26 suggests some Israelites carried idols even then. The same phrase becomes the sad refrain of Judges: "Everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judg 17:6; 21:25). The opposite is doing "what is right in the sight of the LORD" (vv25, 28).
  • "When he gives you rest from all your enemies around, so that you live in safety" (v10). "Rest" is a key idea in the Bible. The land would be Israel's rest after slavery and wandering. Full rest came only in David's time, and it was then that a permanent house for God was built (2 Sam 7:1; 1 Kgs 5:3–5). Hebrews 4:8–9 says Joshua did not give the final rest, so "there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God."
  • "You and your sons and your daughters, your male servants and your female servants, and the Levite" (v12). The whole household rejoices together, servants included. The Levite is singled out because the tribe of Levi had no land of its own (10:9). Their support depended on the people's faithfulness, so Moses says it twice: "Take care that you do not neglect the Levite as long as you live in your land" (v19).
  • "Take care that you do not offer your burnt offerings at any place that you see" (v13). It is natural to want to worship where something beautiful or striking catches the eye. That was exactly how the Canaanite shrines came to be.
  • "However, you may slaughter and eat meat within any of your towns" (v15). In the wilderness, every ox, sheep or goat killed for food had to be brought to the tabernacle as an offering (Lev 17:3–5), since the camp was always close by. Once Israel spread out across the land, that would be impossible. So ordinary meat could now be killed and eaten at home, like wild game. "The gazelle and the deer" were hunted, never sacrificed, so they were the model of ordinary, non-sacred meat. Because it was not a sacrifice, "the unclean and the clean may eat of it." Someone who was ritually unclean could not eat holy food (Lev 7:20) but could eat this.
  • "Only you shall not eat the blood; you shall pour it out on the earth like water" (v16). One rule remains. The ban on blood goes back to Noah (Gen 9:4) and comes before the law of Moses. Pouring it on the ground returned the life to God, its giver. Leviticus 17:11 gives the deeper reason: "the life of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it for you on the altar to make atonement for your souls." Blood was God's appointed means of atonement, not food. Even the early church asked Gentile believers to avoid blood for the sake of fellowship with Jewish believers (Acts 15:20, 29).
  • "You may not eat within your towns the tithe… or the firstborn… or any of your vow offerings" (v17). Holy food is different from ordinary food. Whatever had been given to God had to be eaten at his place, in his presence.
  • "Because you crave meat" (v20). The word is the same desire that brought disaster at Kibroth-hattaavah, the "graves of craving" (Num 11:4, 34). There God judged a greedy demand. Here he simply allows the appetite: "you may eat meat whenever you desire." Desire is not sin in itself. It depends on whether it is received as God's gift and within his limits.
  • "For the blood is the life, and you shall not eat the life with the flesh" (v23). Three times in vv23–25 Moses says, "you shall not eat it." The repetition shows how seriously this matters. Ancient pagan rites sometimes drank blood to take in an animal's life or power. Israel received life from God, not by consuming it.
  • "The blood of your sacrifices shall be poured out on the altar… but the flesh you may eat" (v27). For holy offerings, the blood goes on the altar, not the ground. The two settings make the line between ordinary meals and worship clear, while honoring life in both.
  • "When you do what is good and right in the sight of the LORD your God" (v28). The section ends with the phrase that answers v8. Not "right in his own eyes" but right in God's sight.
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry reads the chapter as an expansion of the second commandment and says God "is above all gods, and will not be worshipped as other gods are." He suggests God named no place himself, "lest the tribes should have quarrelled about it." On the feasting: "It is the will of God that we should serve him with gladness." And on the Levites: "It is a great mercy to have Levites near us." He says the offerings "received their virtue purely from that altar on which they were offered, as it typified Christ." JFB suggests Moses left the place unnamed to avoid stirring up tribal rivalry, and says it was later Shiloh and Jerusalem. It explains that the wilderness rule requiring all slaughter at the tabernacle was now relaxed, which it calls "a great boon." It adds that the poured-out blood was covered with earth, unlike pagan hunters who left it exposed as an offering to a god of the hunt (compare Lev 17:13). Guzik notes that nearly all killing of animals in the ancient world counted as a sacrifice to some god, so permission to slaughter for ordinary meals set Israel apart. He presses the command to rejoice: joy is an obligation, and when it doesn't come naturally, obedience still comes first. He also uses v5 to argue that private devotion can't replace gathering with God's people.

Warning Against Idolatry (vv29–32)

  • "Take care that you be not ensnared to follow them, after they have been destroyed before you" (v30). A strange danger: being drawn to the religion of a people whose gods had just failed to protect them. Yet it happened. Defeated gods kept their appeal, especially when the question was rain and harvest.
  • "Do not inquire about their gods, saying, 'How did these nations serve their gods?—that I also may do the same'" (v30). In the ancient world, people believed each land had its local gods, and newcomers should learn to keep them happy. When Assyria later resettled foreigners in Samaria, they were attacked by lions and concluded they didn't know "the law of the god of the land" (2 Kgs 17:24–28). Moses forbids that kind of curiosity. Some questions are a door to a trap.
  • "For they even burn their sons and their daughters in the fire to their gods" (v31). Child sacrifice was practiced in parts of the ancient world, linked especially with the god Molech (Lev 18:21; 20:2–5). Archaeologists have found burial grounds of infant remains with dedications at Phoenician colonies such as Carthage, though how many were sacrifices is debated. Tragically, Israel later did the same. Ahaz and Manasseh burned their own sons (2 Kgs 16:3; 21:6), and the valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem became so associated with it that its name, Gehenna, became a word for hell (Jer 7:31; Matt 5:22).
  • "Everything that I command you, you shall be careful to do. You shall not add to it or take from it" (v32). In Hebrew Bibles this verse begins chapter 13, but it fits both ways. It sums up the chapter: worship is to be shaped by what God says, not by human invention or by borrowing from others. Moses said the same in 4:2, and the Bible ends with a similar warning (Rev 22:18–19). Protestant traditions differ on how strictly to apply this to Christian worship. Some, especially Reformed churches, hold that worship should include only what Scripture commands. Others hold that it may include whatever Scripture does not forbid. Both take seriously that God, not his worshippers, sets the terms.
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry sums up v32: "God will have his own work done in his own way." JFB links v30 to the ancient belief that local gods demanded something from newcomers, and says Israel "too often endeavored to propitiate the deities of Canaan." It has no comment on vv31–32. Guzik says spiritual curiosity about other religions has wrecked many lives, notes the strange pull of gods already defeated by the LORD, and traces child sacrifice through Israel's later kings. He concludes that God's word alone sets the standard for worship.

Pointing to Christ

Israel was to bring every offering to the one place where God put his name. At a well near Mount Gerizim, a Samaritan woman asked Jesus where that place really was, on her mountain or in Jerusalem. He answered that the hour had come when true worshippers would worship the Father "in spirit and truth" (John 4:20–24). Jesus himself is the place God has chosen. He called his body the temple (John 2:19–21), and "in him all the fullness of God was pleased to dwell" (Col 1:19). His blood, the life poured out, is the atonement Leviticus 17:11 pointed to. And the joyful feast "before the LORD" looks ahead to the Lord's Supper and to the marriage supper of the Lamb, where the whole household of God will eat and rejoice in his presence.

Reflection

  • God Alone Defines True Worship (vv.2-4) — Israel is told to tear down every pagan altar and then bluntly warned not to worship the Lord that way either. Do you ever borrow methods or trends from the culture around you and call it worship, without asking whether God actually commanded it?
  • The Place God Chooses (v.5) — Instead of worshiping wherever felt convenient, Israel had to come to the site God Himself selected. Are you willing to meet God on His terms, or do you shape worship around your own comfort and preference?
  • No One Doing What's Right in His Own Eyes (v.8) — Moses warns against the very freedom the wilderness generation had grown used to — everyone acting on personal judgment. Where in your spiritual life have you substituted your own sense of what's right for what God has actually said?
  • Caring for Those Who Serve (v.19) — Israel is charged never to neglect the Levite, who depended entirely on the people's faithfulness for support. Who serves God in your life without much pay or recognition, and how are you supporting them?
  • Resisting the Pull of Pagan Curiosity (v.30) — God warns against even asking how the nations worshiped their gods, since curiosity so easily turns into imitation. What outside practices or trends are you tempted to import into your faith instead of simply following God's word?
