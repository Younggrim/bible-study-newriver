1When Abram was ninety-nine years old, the LORD appeared to him and said, “I am God Almighty. Walk before Me and be blameless.

2I will establish My covenant between Me and you, and I will multiply you exceedingly.”

3Then Abram fell facedown, and God said to him,

4“As for Me, this is My covenant with you: You will be the father of many nations.

5No longer will you be called Abram, but your name will be Abraham, for I have made you a father of many nations.

6I will make you exceedingly fruitful; I will make nations of you, and kings will descend from you.

7I will establish My covenant as an everlasting covenant between Me and you and your descendants after you, to be your God and the God of your descendants after you.

8And to you and your descendants I will give the land where you are residing—all the land of Canaan—as an eternal possession; and I will be their God.”

9God also said to Abraham, “You must keep My covenant—you and your descendants in the generations after you.

10This is My covenant with you and your descendants after you, which you are to keep: Every male among you must be circumcised.

11You are to circumcise the flesh of your foreskin, and this will be a sign of the covenant between Me and you.

12Generation after generation, every male must be circumcised when he is eight days old, including those born in your household and those purchased from a foreigner—even those who are not your offspring.

13Whether they are born in your household or purchased, they must be circumcised. My covenant in your flesh will be an everlasting covenant.

14But if any male is not circumcised, he will be cut off from his people; he has broken My covenant.”

15Then God said to Abraham, “As for Sarai your wife, do not call her Sarai, for her name is to be Sarah.

16And I will bless her and will surely give you a son by her. I will bless her, and she will be the mother of nations; kings of peoples will descend from her.”

17Abraham fell facedown. Then he laughed and said to himself, “Can a child be born to a man who is a hundred years old? Can Sarah give birth at the age of ninety?”

18And Abraham said to God, “O that Ishmael might live under Your blessing!”

19But God replied, “Your wife Sarah will indeed bear you a son, and you are to name him Isaac. I will establish My covenant with him as an everlasting covenant for his descendants after him.

20As for Ishmael, I have heard you, and I will surely bless him; I will make him fruitful and multiply him greatly. He will become the father of twelve rulers, and I will make him into a great nation.

21But I will establish My covenant with Isaac, whom Sarah will bear to you at this time next year.”

Summary

When Abram is ninety-nine, the Lord appears to him, identifies Himself as God Almighty, and calls Abram to walk before Him blamelessly as He establishes His covenant (vv. 1-2). God changes Abram's name to Abraham, meaning father of many nations, and promises to make him exceedingly fruitful, to give the land of Canaan to his descendants as an everlasting possession, and to be their God (vv. 3-8). God institutes circumcision as the sign of the covenant, to be performed on every male on the eighth day, including servants and foreigners in the household (vv. 9-14). Sarai's name is changed to Sarah, and God promises she will bear a son within the year despite her old age, a promise Abraham greets with laughter as he suggests Ishmael instead (vv. 15-18). God confirms that Sarah will bear a son named Isaac, through whom the everlasting covenant will continue, while also blessing Ishmael with numerous descendants (vv. 19-22). Abraham obeys immediately, circumcising himself, Ishmael, and every male in his household that same day (vv. 23-27).

Authorship & Background

Author: Moses (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 17 records the covenant of circumcision — 13 years after Ishmael's birth (Abram is now 99). God appears with a new name for Himself (El Shaddai — God Almighty), new names for Abram (Abraham) and Sarai (Sarah), a covenant sign (circumcision), and the specific promise that Sarah will bear the son of promise — Isaac. This chapter formalizes the Abrahamic covenant with its sign and specifies the heir.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: a new divine name, El Shaddai, introduced thirteen years after Ishmael's birth alongside new names for both Abram and Sarai, circumcision instituted as a sign cut into the flesh of every male at eight days old, Abraham's own laughter at the promise of a hundred-year-old father and a ninety-year-old mother met not with rebuke but with a name, Isaac, that means laughter itself, Ishmael blessed and made into a nation while the covenant itself is reserved specifically for a son not yet conceived, and every male in the household circumcised that same day with no recorded delay
God Almighty Appears (vv.1-2): "I am the Almighty God; walk before me, and be thou perfect" (v.1). El Shaddai — the God who is sufficient for every impossibility. "Perfect" = blameless, wholehearted.
The Covenant Expanded (vv.3-8): Abraham falls on his face (v.3). God's promises: father of many nations (v.4), new name (v.5), exceedingly fruitful (v.6), kings from him (v.6), everlasting covenant (v.7), the land of Canaan as everlasting possession (v.8).
Circumcision — The Covenant Sign (vv.9-14): Every male circumcised at eight days old (vv.10-12). A sign "in your flesh" of the everlasting covenant (v.13). The uncircumcised are "cut off" — covenant-breakers (v.14).
Sarah's Promise (vv.15-22): Sarai renamed Sarah ("princess") (v.15). She will bear a son (v.16). Abraham laughs — "Shall a child be born unto him that is an hundred years old?" (v.17). He asks for Ishmael (v.18). God insists: Sarah will bear Isaac (v.19). Ishmael blessed but the covenant is with Isaac (vv.20-21).
Immediate Obedience (vv.23-27): Abraham circumcises every male "in the selfsame day" (v.23). No delay. Complete obedience.

Map & Geography

  • Abraham is still at Mamre/Hebron during this chapter. The covenant renewal takes place in the same region where he has settled (cf. 13:18).
  • Ishmael's prophesied territory: "He shall dwell in the presence of all his brethren" (cf. 16:12) — the Arabian desert regions east and south of Canaan.

Deep Dive

Setting: Thirteen years have passed in silence since the end of chapter 16. Abram was 86 when Hagar bore Ishmael (16:16), and now he is 99. It has been twenty-four years since he left Haran at 75 (12:4) on the strength of God's promise of offspring, and in that time the only son he has is the one he and Sarai arranged through Hagar. In chapter 15 God sealed his promise with a covenant ceremony, passing alone between the pieces of the animals. Chapter 17 comes back to that covenant and fills it out. God gives himself a name, gives Abram and Sarai new names, gives the covenant a sign to be carried in the body, and, for the first time, says plainly that the promised son will be Sarai's own child, born within a year. The word "covenant" runs through the chapter thirteen times. This is the covenant chapter of Abraham's life, and Paul builds much of Romans 4 and Galatians 3 on it.

Abraham and the Covenant of Circumcision (vv1–14)

  • "When Abram was ninety-nine years old" (v1). The age is the point. Abram is past the age of fathering children by any natural reckoning, and Sarai is 89. God has waited until the promise is humanly impossible. Paul says Abraham "considered his own body, which was as good as dead (since he was about a hundred years old)" and still did not weaken in faith (Rom 4:19).
  • "The LORD appeared to Abram" (v1). The narrator uses the covenant name, the LORD (Yahweh), but in the speech that follows God calls himself by another name, and from v3 on the chapter calls him simply "God." The text doesn't say what form the appearance took, but when it ends "God went up from Abraham" (v22), so it was more than an inner impression.
  • "I am God Almighty" (v1). The Hebrew is El Shaddai. This is its first appearance in the Bible, and Exodus 6:3 says it was the name by which God made himself known to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. Its root meaning is uncertain. The ancient Greek translation outside the Pentateuch usually renders it "Almighty" (pantokratōr), and the ESV follows that. An old rabbinic explanation reads it as "the one who is sufficient," and some link it to a word for "mountain." The lexicon (BDB) lists these options and calls the origin "dubious." What is clear is how Genesis uses it: almost every time El Shaddai appears, it comes with the promise of children and fruitfulness (28:3; 35:11; 48:3–4; 49:25). It is the name of the God who can give life where there is none.
  • "Walk before me, and be blameless" (v1). To "walk before" someone is to live in their sight, as a servant in front of a king. Noah "walked with God" and was "blameless" (6:9), using the same word, tamim. It means whole, complete, with integrity. It is the word used for sacrificial animals "without blemish" (Exod 12:5). It doesn't mean sinless. Abram is far from sinless, as the Hagar episode showed. It means wholehearted, not divided between trusting God and taking matters into his own hands.
  • "That I may make my covenant between me and you" (v2). The command and the promise are tied together. The covenant is God's gift. He began it in chapter 15 without asking anything of Abram. Here he calls Abram to live in a way that fits it. The ancient Near East knew two kinds of covenants: a royal grant, a gift from a king to a loyal servant, and a treaty laying obligations on a lesser party. Many scholars see the Abrahamic covenant as the first kind, a grant secured by God's own word, with obligations that express the relationship rather than earn it.
  • "Then Abram fell on his face" (v3). This is the posture of worship and awe before a great king. He does it again in v17. Abram says nothing in the whole first half of the chapter. God does all the speaking.
  • "No longer shall your name be called Abram, but your name shall be Abraham" (v5). Abram means "exalted father," likely honoring his family line. For a childless man of 99, it must have sounded like a joke. God gives him a name that sounds even bolder: Abraham, which Genesis links to ab hamon, "father of a multitude." The lexicon says this is a wordplay. Linguistically Abraham is probably just a longer form of the same name. But the meaning God attaches to it is what matters. Every time someone called his name, Abraham would hear the promise.
  • "For I have made you the father of a multitude of nations" (v5). The verb is past tense. God speaks of the promise as already done. Paul quotes this verse and says Abraham believed the God "who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist" (Rom 4:17). "Nations" is broader than Israel. Ishmael's line, Esau's Edomites and the sons of Keturah (25:1–4) all come from Abraham. Paul sees the deeper fulfillment in believers from every nation who share Abraham's faith (Rom 4:16; Gal 3:7, 29).
  • "Kings shall come from you" (v6). This is the first promise of kings in Abraham's line. It is repeated to Sarah (v16) and Jacob (35:11), and it points to the kingdom of David, and through David to the King of kings.
  • "An everlasting covenant, to be God to you and to your offspring after you" (v7). Here is the heart of the covenant. Land and descendants matter, but the center is God himself: "to be God to you." This phrase becomes the refrain of the whole Bible: "I will be their God, and they shall be my people" (Jer 31:33). "Offspring" (literally "seed") is a singular word that can mean many descendants or one. Paul notices this and argues that the promise ultimately rests on one offspring, Christ (Gal 3:16).
  • "The land of your sojournings, all the land of Canaan" (v8). Abraham still lives in Canaan as a resident alien. By the end of his life he will own exactly one field and its cave, bought as a grave (ch 23). Hebrews says he lived "in tents" because "he was looking forward to the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God" (Heb 11:9–10).
  • "Every male among you shall be circumcised" (v10). Circumcision was not unique to Israel. Egyptian tomb art from centuries before Abraham shows it, and Jeremiah lists Egypt, Edom, Ammon and Moab as circumcising nations (Jer 9:25–26). Elsewhere it was usually done at puberty or before marriage, as a rite of manhood. In Israel it was done to babies, it was commanded by God, and it was tied to a covenant with him.
  • "It shall be a sign of the covenant" (v11). The word for sign, ot, is the same word used for the rainbow, the sign of God's covenant with Noah (9:12–13). The rainbow is set in the sky. This sign is set in the flesh, in the very part of the body tied to offspring, the thing the whole promise is about. A sign points to something beyond itself. Moses and the prophets soon say what it pointed to: "Circumcise therefore the foreskin of your heart" (Deut 10:16; also Deut 30:6; Jer 4:4).
  • "He who is eight days old" (v12). Leviticus ties the eighth day to the end of the mother's seven days of ceremonial uncleanness after birth (Lev 12:2–3). The child receives the sign long before he can understand it, as a member of a family God has claimed.
  • "Whether born in your house or bought with your money from any foreigner" (v12). The sign goes to every male in the household, including servants who were not Abraham's blood relatives. From the start, the covenant community was not purely a matter of ancestry. Later, a foreigner who wanted to keep the Passover could join Israel by receiving the same sign (Exod 12:48).
  • "Shall be cut off from his people; he has broken my covenant" (v14). There is a grim wordplay here. The verb karat, "cut off," is the same verb used for "cutting" a covenant (15:18), and for cutting off a foreskin (Exod 4:25). The man who refuses to be cut will himself be cut off. The Tyndale notes say "cut off" could mean being expelled from the community or put to death, but most often it warned that God himself would end that person's life early. Refusing the sign was not a small oversight. It meant rejecting the covenant itself. When Moses neglected to circumcise his son, the LORD sought to put him to death (Exod 4:24–26).
  • Circumcision and faith. Paul points out that God counted Abraham righteous in chapter 15, years before he was circumcised in chapter 17. So circumcision was "a seal of the righteousness that he had by faith while he was still uncircumcised" (Rom 4:11). The sign confirmed a relationship, it did not create one. That is why Paul fought so hard when some insisted Gentile believers must be circumcised to be saved (Acts 15:1; Gal 5:2–6).
  • Circumcision and baptism. Christians agree that baptism is the sign of the new covenant and that Colossians 2:11–12 connects it with circumcision. They differ on what follows from that. Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Anglican, Presbyterian and Reformed churches, and Methodists, see baptism as taking circumcision's place as the covenant sign given to believers' children. They point to the household baptisms in Acts (16:15, 33) and Peter's words "the promise is for you and for your children" (Acts 2:39). Baptist and many evangelical and Pentecostal churches see an important change: the new covenant is with those who know the Lord (Jer 31:34), and the circumcision Paul links to baptism in Colossians is "made without hands," the circumcision of the heart. So the sign follows a profession of faith. Both sides take Scripture seriously, and both agree that the sign on its own saves no one.
  • Commentators: Henry calls this the covenant of grace and reads "be thou perfect" as "upright and sincere," since sincerity is the gospel's measure of perfection. He adds that "the covenant of grace is everlasting," and plainly says that "circumcision therefore gives way to baptism." JFB agrees: circumcision "was the sign in the Old Testament Church as baptism is in the New." JFB explains El Shaddai as "all-sufficient," which follows the rabbinic derivation. The lexicon notes that modern scholars don't accept that derivation, though "all-sufficient" is still a fair description of what the name means in Genesis. Guzik reports several suggested meanings, including one from shad, "breast," as the God who nourishes. The lexicon doesn't list that derivation, and it is best taken as a devotional reflection. Guzik draws attention to the thirteen silent years as ordinary, faithful time in which faith grew. He also repeats a popular medical argument that a newborn's blood-clotting factors peak on the eighth day. The text gives no medical reason, and the figures usually quoted are disputed. On the name change, Henry reports that "some think" a letter from the name Jehovah was inserted into Abraham's name. It is a pious thought, not something the text claims. JFB has no comment on vv2, 6–7, 9 and 11–14.

Isaac's Birth Promised (vv15–27)

  • "As for Sarai your wife… Sarah shall be her name" (v15). For the first time God names Sarai directly in the promise. Up to now, Abram could reasonably think the heir might come some other way, through a servant (15:2–3) or through Hagar (16:2). Now God rules that out. The lexicon treats Sarai and Sarah as two forms of the same name, both meaning "princess." The change is not in the meaning so much as in the moment: God renames her, as he renamed Abraham, and gives her a place of her own in the covenant.
  • "I will bless her… she shall become nations; kings of peoples shall come from her" (v16). God repeats the blessing on Sarah twice. Everything promised to Abraham in vv4–6 is now promised to her. Sarah is not a bystander to the covenant. She is its mother. Isaiah later says, "Look to Abraham your father and to Sarah who bore you" (Isa 51:2).
  • "Abraham fell on his face and laughed" (v17). The laugh is the key to the next scene. The Hebrew verb is yitsḥaq, "he laughed," which is exactly the name God gives the child two verses later. Abraham's question is honest arithmetic: a man of 100 and a woman of 90. Was the laugh joy or doubt? Henry and JFB read it as delight, and Paul says Abraham "did not weaken in faith" when he considered his age and Sarah's barrenness (Rom 4:19–20). Many modern interpreters, and the Tyndale notes, hear disbelief, since Abraham immediately offers God an alternative. It may well be both: worship on his face, and a laugh that can't quite believe what he is hearing. Sarah will laugh too (18:12), and in the end, when the child is born, she says, "God has made laughter for me" (21:6).
  • "Oh that Ishmael might live before you!" (v18). This is a father's prayer for the son he has. Ishmael is 13, and Abraham loves him. The words "before you" echo God's opening command, "walk before me" (v1). Abraham asks that Ishmael might live in God's favor and presence.
  • "No, but Sarah your wife shall bear you a son, and you shall call his name Isaac" (v19). God says no to the substitute and yes to the father's heart. The covenant line will run through Isaac and his offspring, "an everlasting covenant." God's purpose doesn't run through the plan Abraham and Sarah made, but through the child only God can give. Paul reads this as the difference between "the children of the flesh" and "the children of the promise" (Rom 9:7–9; Gal 4:22–28).
  • "As for Ishmael, I have heard you" (v20). Another wordplay. "I have heard" is shamaʿ, the root of Ishmael's name, which means "God hears" (16:11). God hears the prayer for the boy named "God hears." He promises Ishmael fruitfulness, a great nation and "twelve princes," fulfilled in the list of Ishmael's twelve sons (25:13–16). Ishmael is not cast aside. He receives a real blessing, though not the covenant.
  • "At this time next year" (v21). After twenty-four years of waiting, God gives a date. The promise now has a calendar attached.
  • "That very day" (v23). Abraham obeys the same day, and the text says so twice (vv23, 26). He does it completely: "every male among the men of Abraham's house." That included the 318 trained men of his household (14:14). There is no record of argument or delay.
  • "Abraham was ninety-nine… Ishmael his son was thirteen" (vv24–25). The narrator lingers on the two ages. Abraham receives the sign of the covenant in old age, at the moment the promise is renewed, and Ishmael receives it as a boy. Josephus, the first-century Jewish historian, notes that the Arab peoples of his day circumcised at thirteen, following Ishmael's example. Ishmael carries the sign even though the covenant line will not run through him. The sign marks him as part of Abraham's household, but it does not make him the heir of promise. Paul makes a similar point about Israel: not all who carry the outward mark are children of the promise (Rom 9:6–8).
  • "All the men of his house… were circumcised with him" (v27). The chapter closes not with Abraham alone but with a whole household, servants and foreigners included, marked as belonging to God.
  • Commentators: Henry says Abraham's laugh "was a laughter of delight, not of distrust," and ties it to Jesus' words that Abraham "rejoiced that he would see my day" (John 8:56). JFB agrees: "not the sneer of unbelief, but a smile of delight at the improbability of the event." Both, as noted above, differ from the Tyndale notes. Henry reads "Sarai" as "my princess," honor confined to one family, and "Sarah" as a princess of multitudes, a reading Guzik also passes on from the Puritan Matthew Poole. The lexicon treats the two as simply older and newer forms of the same name, so the contrast shouldn't be pressed. On v18, Henry draws a lesson for parents to pray that their children may "live before" God. JFB calls the prayer the "natural solicitude of a parent," and says Ishmael received "common blessings" and may personally have shared in the covenant's benefits. Henry agrees: "Covenant blessings are reserved for Isaac." Guzik reads Abraham's obedience in vv23–27 as complete, immediate and costly, since it left every fighting man in the household sore and vulnerable for days (compare 34:25). JFB says nothing on vv21–27.

Pointing to Christ

Everything in this chapter leans forward. The promised "offspring" in whom all the nations would be blessed is, Paul says, "Christ" (Gal 3:16), and through him believers from every nation become "Abraham's offspring, heirs according to promise" (Gal 3:29). The son God promised was born of a woman who could not have children, by a word God spoke and kept; the greater Son was born of a virgin, and the angel who announced it told Mary that "nothing will be impossible with God" (Luke 1:37), while Mary herself sang of the mercy God "spoke to our fathers, to Abraham and to his offspring forever" (Luke 1:55). Jesus himself was circumcised on the eighth day (Luke 2:21), born under the covenant he came to fulfill. And the sign that cut away flesh pointed to something deeper. In Christ believers are "circumcised with a circumcision made without hands," by "the circumcision of Christ" (Col 2:11), his body given up on the cross. The God who said "I will be God to you" is the God who in Jesus came to dwell with his people, and who will one day say it for the last time: "they will be his people, and God himself will be with them as their God" (Rev 21:3).

Reflection

  • The All-Sufficient God (v.1) — God reveals Himself as El Shaddai, the Almighty, and calls Abraham to walk before Him and live wholeheartedly devoted, not merely blameless in behavior. What impossible situation in your life needs you to trust that God is sufficient for it?
  • A New Name, A New Identity (v.5) — God renames Abram to Abraham, giving him an identity rooted in the promise rather than anything he had accomplished. Are you living out of an old identity, or the new one God has given you?
  • Bringing Honest Doubt to God (v.17) — Abraham laughs at the idea of a hundred-year-old man and a ninety-year-old woman having a child, and God doesn't rebuke him for it. Are you willing to bring your genuine doubts to God instead of hiding them from Him?
  • Obedience Without Delay (v.23) — Abraham circumcises every male in his household that very same day, with no negotiation or procrastination. When God asks something of you, how quickly do you actually respond?
