Summary

Matthew opens with a genealogy tracing Jesus' lineage from Abraham through David to Joseph, arranged in three sets of fourteen generations to present him as Israel's promised Messiah (vv. 1-17). The account then turns to Mary, found to be pregnant by the Holy Spirit before her marriage to Joseph, and Joseph's plan to quietly end the engagement (vv. 18-19). An angel appears to Joseph in a dream, telling him not to be afraid to take Mary as his wife and to name the child Jesus, because he will save his people from their sins (vv. 20-21). Matthew notes this fulfills Isaiah's prophecy of a virgin bearing a son called Immanuel, God with us, and Joseph obeys, taking Mary home and naming the child at his birth (vv. 22-25).

Authorship & Background

Author: Matthew (also called Levi), a tax collector who became one of the twelve apostles (Matthew 9:9). He was an eyewitness to Jesus' ministry. Tax collectors were despised in Jewish society — they worked for Rome and were considered traitors and extortioners. Matthew's inclusion in the twelve is itself a demonstration of grace.
Date Written: Approximately AD 50-70, making it one of the earliest Gospels.
Audience: Written primarily for a Jewish audience. Matthew quotes the Old Testament more than any other Gospel (approximately 60 direct quotes and 100 allusions), constantly showing how Jesus fulfills Jewish Scripture and prophecy.
Purpose of Matthew's Gospel: To present Jesus as the promised Messiah — the King of the Jews, the fulfillment of the Law and the Prophets. Matthew begins with a genealogy to establish Jesus' legal right to David's throne.
Purpose of Chapter 1: The genealogy establishes Jesus' legal credentials as the Messiah — descended from Abraham (the covenant) and David (the throne). The birth narrative establishes His divine origin — conceived by the Holy Spirit, fulfilling Isaiah 7:14.
Classification: Gospel — Narrative
Key Themes: a genealogy opening the entire Gospel as a legal document rather than a story, three groups of fourteen imposed deliberately on the record rather than found in it, four women named by choice, Tamar, Rahab, Ruth and the wife of Uriah, before Mary breaks the pattern of the whole list entirely, a decent man's decision to divorce quietly reversed by an angel who names the child before he is born, for he shall save his people from their sins, and the first of Matthew's fulfilment formulas, quoting Isaiah on a virgin and a name Matthew stops to translate, God with us
The Genealogy (vv.1-17): The book opens with a legal document rather than a story, and its first line states the two claims the whole Gospel rests on, the son of David, the son of Abraham. The list is arranged in three groups of fourteen, from Abraham to David, David to the exile, the exile to Christ, which is a structure imposed on the record rather than found in it. Four women are named and the choice of them is deliberate: Tamar, who posed as a prostitute, Rahab, who was one, Ruth, a Moabitess, and her that had been the wife of Uriah, unnamed. Then the last link breaks the pattern of the whole list. Every other entry says begat. This one says Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom was born Jesus.
The Birth of Jesus Christ (vv.18-25): The account is told from Joseph's side throughout, and his first decision is a decent one made in the wrong direction, being a just man, and not willing to make her a publick example, was minded to put her away privily. The angel's instruction reverses it and gives the child's name with its meaning attached, for he shall save his people from their sins. Then the first of Matthew's fulfilment formulas, quoting Isaiah on the virgin and the name Emmanuel, which Matthew translates for a reader who might not have Hebrew, God with us. The chapter ends with obedience described in two flat clauses, he did as the angel of the Lord had bidden him, and called his name JESUS.

Deep Dive

Setting: Matthew, the tax collector Jesus called to follow him (9:9), wrote this Gospel, by early church tradition, with Jewish readers especially in mind. Most scholars date it between the 50s and 80s AD. More than any other Gospel it shows Jesus fulfilling the Old Testament: Matthew quotes it over sixty times and repeats the formula "this took place to fulfill what the Lord had spoken by the prophet." The book's first page is a family tree, which sounds dull to modern readers but would have gripped first-century Jews. Four centuries after Malachi, with Herod on the throne and Rome in charge, a Jewish reader needed to know one thing first: is this man really the son of David? Chapter 1 answers with a genealogy, and then shows how a child with no human father could be David's heir.

The Genealogy of Jesus Christ (vv1–17)

  • "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ" (v1). In Greek, biblos geneseōs. Genesis means "origin" or "beginning," and the phrase echoes the Greek Old Testament's "This is the book of the generations of Adam" (Gen 5:1; see also 2:4). Matthew's first words announce a new beginning. The phrase most directly heads the family list, but it suits the whole story of Jesus' origins.
  • "Jesus Christ" (v1). "Christ" is not a surname. It is the Greek for "Messiah," "anointed one," the title of Israel's promised King.
  • "The son of David, the son of Abraham" (v1). Two promises are in view. God promised David a son whose throne would last forever (2 Sam 7:12–16). He promised Abraham that in his offspring "all the nations of the earth" would be blessed (Gen 22:18). Matthew's Gospel ends with Jesus sending his followers to "all nations" (28:19). Jesus is David's King for Israel and Abraham's blessing for the world. The names are given in reverse order (Jesus, David, Abraham), and the list then runs Abraham, David, Jesus, a mirror pattern.
  • Why genealogies mattered. Jews kept careful family records, especially priestly and royal ones (Ezra 2:62; Neh 7:64). Josephus, the Jewish historian, cites his own priestly pedigree from public registers. A claim to be the Messiah stood or fell on descent from David.
  • "Judah and his brothers" (v2). The line runs through Judah, not the firstborn Reuben. Jacob had promised that "the scepter shall not depart from Judah" (Gen 49:10). The "brothers" are noted because all Israel's tribes come from them.
  • The women in the list. Genealogies normally traced fathers only. Matthew names four women before Mary, and each is surprising. Tamar (v3) disguised herself as a prostitute to get children from her father-in-law Judah after he wronged her (Gen 38). Rahab (v5) was a Canaanite prostitute in Jericho who hid Israel's spies and was spared (Josh 2; 6:25). Ruth (v5) was a Moabite, from a nation barred from Israel's assembly (Deut 23:3), who chose Israel's God (Ruth 1:16). "The wife of Uriah" (v6) was Bathsheba, with whom David committed adultery before arranging her husband's death (2 Sam 11). Three, perhaps all four, were Gentiles (Bathsheba married a Hittite). Several were touched by scandal. Matthew seems to say: God's plan has always included outsiders and has always worked through human sin and unlikely people. They also prepare for Mary, whose pregnancy would look scandalous too.
  • "Salmon the father of Boaz by Rahab" (v5). The Old Testament does not say whom Salmon married. Matthew's note that it was Rahab comes from a tradition otherwise unrecorded. Rahab lived around 1400 or 1200 BC and David around 1000 BC, so generations have likely been skipped between Salmon and David.
  • "David the king" (v6). Only David is called "king" in the list. He is the hinge of the genealogy.
  • "By the wife of Uriah" (v6). Matthew does not say "Bathsheba." He names the man she was taken from. It quietly keeps David's sin in view. The Messiah's royal line passed through a king's greatest failure.
  • Asaph and Amos (vv7, 10). The kings were Asa (1 Kings 15:9) and Amon (2 Kings 21:18). The oldest manuscripts of Matthew spell them "Asaph" and "Amos," the names of a psalmist and a prophet, and the ESV follows them. Most scholars think these were simply variant spellings. Some suggest Matthew was playfully linking the royal line with the Psalms and the Prophets, but that is speculation.
  • Missing kings (v8). "Joram the father of Uzziah" skips three generations: Ahaziah, Joash and Amaziah (2 Kings 8–14; 1 Chron 3:11–12). "Father of" in Hebrew genealogies can mean "ancestor of." These three kings descended from Athaliah, Ahab and Jezebel's daughter, who married into David's house (2 Kings 8:18, 26), which may be why they were left out. Matthew also skips Jehoiakim between Josiah and Jechoniah (v11).
  • "Josiah the father of Jechoniah and his brothers, at the time of the deportation to Babylon" (v11). Jechoniah (also called Jehoiachin or Coniah) was actually Josiah's grandson. The "deportation to Babylon" in 597–586 BC is the third great turning point in the list: Abraham, David, exile, and now Christ.
  • Jechoniah's curse. Jeremiah said of Jechoniah: "none of his offspring shall succeed in sitting on the throne of David" (Jer 22:30; see also 36:30 on his father Jehoiakim). Yet Matthew's royal line runs through him. Some see this as one reason Jesus' descent comes through Joseph legally rather than by blood: Jesus inherits the throne-right without being Jechoniah's physical offspring. Others note that God's later word to Jechoniah's grandson Zerubbabel, "I will make you like a signet ring" (Hag 2:23), reverses Jeremiah's image of Coniah as a discarded signet ring (Jer 22:24), suggesting the curse was lifted.
  • After the exile (vv12–16). Shealtiel and Zerubbabel are known from the Old Testament (Ezra 3:2; 1 Chron 3:17–19). Zerubbabel led the first return from exile and rebuilt the temple. The names from Abiud to Jacob (vv13–15) are unknown anywhere else. Matthew probably drew them from family records.
  • "Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born" (v16). The pattern "A was the father of B" breaks here. Matthew does not say Joseph was the father of Jesus. "Of whom" is feminine in Greek: Jesus was born of Mary. The grammar prepares for vv18–25.
  • "Fourteen generations" (v17). Matthew organizes history into three sets of fourteen. He leaves out names to make the pattern, which was common in ancient genealogies. Why fourteen? In Hebrew, letters double as numbers. David's name, DWD, adds up to 4 + 6 + 4 = 14. Many scholars think Matthew is signing the whole list with David's number. Three times fourteen is also six sevens, so Jesus would begin the seventh seven, the number of completeness. The counting does not quite work out evenly: one of the three groups has thirteen names unless someone is counted twice. Matthew's point is not mathematics but meaning: history has been moving on schedule toward the Christ.
  • Matthew and Luke. Luke's genealogy (Luke 3:23–38) runs backward from Jesus to Adam and differs from Matthew's between David and Joseph: Matthew goes through Solomon, Luke through David's son Nathan. Two main explanations are offered. One is that Matthew gives Joseph's royal, legal line (who was heir to the throne) and Luke his physical line, with a levirate marriage or adoption explaining the difference (Joseph's father is Jacob in Matthew and Heli in Luke). The other, popular since the Reformation, is that Luke traces Mary's line through her father Heli. Both are reasonable. The ancient church already knew the problem and offered the first answer, as Eusebius records.
  • Commentators: JFB says the four women include two Gentiles and three with "blots" on their record, a sign of the grace that would reach publicans and sinners. JFB explains the missing kings as the price of the "three fourteens," and points out the Jews preferred to say "migration" rather than the "too bitter" word "captivity." Henry insists, "It is not a needless genealogy": it proves Jesus belongs to the family the Messiah must come from, and shows how far the Son of God "stooped" to share a human family with sinners in it. Guzik reads Matthew's list as Joseph's legal line and Luke's as Mary's, and argues that this kept Jesus from inheriting the curse on Jehoiakim's line while giving him David's throne by legal right. He presents that as a view, not a settled fact. The Tyndale notes favor the gematria explanation for fourteen.

The Birth of Jesus Christ (vv18–25)

  • "Now the birth of Jesus Christ took place in this way" (v18). "Birth" is genesis again, linking back to v1. Luke tells the story from Mary's side. Matthew tells it from Joseph's.
  • "When his mother Mary had been betrothed to Joseph" (v18). Jewish marriage had two stages. Betrothal was a binding legal contract, usually around age twelve or thirteen for a girl. The couple were called husband and wife (vv19–20) and could only part by divorce, but they did not yet live together. About a year later came the wedding, when the groom took the bride to his home.
  • "Before they came together she was found to be with child from the Holy Spirit" (v18). Matthew tells the reader immediately what Joseph does not yet know. "Came together" refers to living together as husband and wife.
  • "Her husband Joseph, being a just man and unwilling to put her to shame, resolved to divorce her quietly" (v19). Joseph's dilemma is real. As a "just" (righteous) man, he could not simply go ahead and marry someone who appeared to have been unfaithful. The law treated a betrothed woman's unfaithfulness as adultery, which in theory carried the death penalty (Deut 22:23–24), though Roman rule had largely ended such executions. He could have exposed her publicly in court. Instead, he planned a quiet divorce, giving her a certificate before two witnesses without stating the reason. In Joseph, righteousness and mercy go together.
  • "As he considered these things" (v20). He did not act in a rush. He thought it over, and God met him in his thinking.
  • "An angel of the Lord appeared to him in a dream" (v20). God guides Joseph by dreams four or five times in these chapters (1:20; 2:13, 19, 22). Another Joseph, the son of Jacob, was also a man of dreams who went to Egypt and saved his family (Gen 37–50).
  • "Joseph, son of David" (v20). The angel addresses him by his royal descent. That is the point of the genealogy. Joseph will pass David's line to Jesus by taking Mary as his wife and naming the child, which in Jewish law made Jesus his legal son.
  • "Do not fear to take Mary as your wife" (v20). Joseph would bear the gossip too. Many in Nazareth would assume he was the father and had not waited, or that Mary had been unfaithful. Years later Jesus' opponents may hint at this: "We were not born of sexual immorality" (John 8:41).
  • "You shall call his name Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins" (v21). Jesus is the Greek form of Yeshua, a shortened form of Yehoshua (Joshua), meaning "the LORD saves." Naming was a father's right. Joseph is told to name him, adopting him as his own. "He" is emphatic in Greek: he himself will save. And the rescue is "from their sins," not first from Rome. "His people" begins with Israel but by the end of Matthew includes believers from every nation.
  • "All this took place to fulfill what the Lord had spoken by the prophet" (v22). This is the first of Matthew's ten or so fulfillment formulas. Matthew sees the whole Old Testament leaning forward toward Jesus.
  • "Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and they shall call his name Immanuel" (v23). This is Isaiah 7:14. In about 735 BC King Ahaz of Judah was terrified of an alliance of Syria and Israel. Isaiah offered him a sign from God, and Ahaz refused. God gave one anyway: a child would be born, and before he was old enough to choose good from evil, the two enemy kings would be gone (Isa 7:15–16).
  • "Virgin." The Hebrew word in Isaiah is almah, a young woman of marriageable age. It is not the specific Hebrew word for virgin (betulah), but it is never clearly used in the Old Testament of a married woman. Two centuries before Jesus, the Jewish translators of the Greek Old Testament rendered it parthenos, "virgin," and Matthew quotes that translation. Many interpreters think Isaiah's sign had a first fulfillment in his own day, in a child born then, and a full fulfillment in Jesus, whose birth to a virgin is unique. Others think Isaiah 7:14 points directly to the Messiah, especially since the same child is described as "Mighty God" two chapters later (Isa 9:6–7). Either way, Matthew reads the promise in the light of the whole of Isaiah 7–11, the "Immanuel" book.
  • "Immanuel (which means, God with us)" (v23). Matthew translates the Hebrew for his readers. Jesus is never called "Immanuel" as a personal name. It describes who he is. Matthew's Gospel begins with "God with us" and ends with Jesus' promise, "I am with you always, to the end of the age" (28:20). The whole book is framed by God's presence.
  • "He did as the angel of the Lord commanded him: he took his wife" (v24). Joseph's obedience is immediate and complete. He never speaks a single word in the Gospels. He just obeys.
  • "But knew her not until she had given birth to a son" (v25). "Knew" is the Bible's word for sexual relations (Gen 4:1). Joseph and Mary did not consummate the marriage until after Jesus' birth, so no one could question that Jesus was born of a virgin. Does "until" mean they had normal relations afterward? Most Protestants say yes, and point to Jesus' brothers and sisters (13:55–56). Catholic and Orthodox Christians teach Mary's perpetual virginity and note that "until" in Greek does not always imply a change afterward (as in 28:20). The verse itself is concerned with the time before the birth.
  • "And he called his name Jesus" (v25). The chapter ends with Joseph doing exactly what he was told, naming the child and so making him a son of David.
  • Commentators: JFB notes that betrothal was legally binding, so ending it took a written bill before witnesses, and that "the virgin" in v23 has the article, pointing to a particular woman. JFB adds that "till" "does not imply" what happened afterward, and that "firstborn" in the law only means no child came before. Guzik reads "until" as implying normal married life afterward and calls perpetual virginity unbiblical. On almah he grants it can mean "young woman," but argues the Septuagint's parthenos shows how Jews understood it before Christ. Henry says "it is the thoughtful, not the unthinking, whom God will guide," and that Jesus saves "not in their sins, but from their sins," from both the guilt and the power of sin.

Pointing to Christ

Matthew's first page is a long road of names, from Abraham through David to the exile, kings good and bad, outsiders and scandals, all leading to one child. He is David's heir, God's answer to every promise made to Abraham, and yet he came by a birth no genealogy could produce, conceived by the Holy Spirit. His name says what he came to do: save his people from their sins. His title says who he is: God with us.

Reflection

  • Grace in the Family Line (vv.3-6) — Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and Bathsheba appear in Jesus' genealogy alongside their scandal, deception, and disgrace. Does anything in your past make you feel disqualified from being used by God?
  • God's Hand in the Long Story (v.17) — Matthew arranges the genealogy into three neat sets of fourteen generations, showing centuries of ordinary and broken lives moving toward God's appointed moment. How does seeing God's patience across generations shape your trust in His timing for your own life?
  • Mercy Over the Letter of the Law (v.19) — Joseph had the legal right to expose Mary publicly but chose to protect her quietly instead. Where do you have the right to expose or punish someone but mercy would be the better path?
  • Obedience Without a Roadmap (v.20) — Joseph received his guidance through a dream, with no verse of Scripture that spoke directly to his situation. How do you seek and trust God's direction when the Bible doesn't give you a clear-cut answer?
  • Saved From Sin, Not Just Circumstance (v.21) — The angel names Jesus' mission precisely: to save people from their sins, not merely their troubles. What sin do you most need Jesus to save you from right now?
  • Emmanuel: God With Us (v.23) — The virgin birth fulfills Isaiah's promise that God Himself would come to dwell among His people. What does it mean to you personally that God wanted to be with you enough to become one of us?
