Summary

Joel opens with the word of the Lord describing a locust plague so severe that successive swarms strip the land bare, an event unlike anything the older generation has seen (vv. 1-4). He calls drunkards to weep because the wine supply is cut off, and pictures the land mourning like a young bride grieving her husband, with grain and drink offerings halted at the temple (vv. 5-10). Farmers and vinedressers are told to despair over ruined crops, and priests are urged to lament, call a fast, and gather the people to cry out to the Lord because the great and terrible Day of the Lord is near (vv. 11-15). The chapter closes with a picture of starving livestock and scorched pastures, and Joel himself cries out to God over the devastation caused by drought and fire (vv. 16-20).

Authorship & Background

Author: Joel son of Pethuel. Date debated (either 835 or 400 BC). Key theme: the Day of the LORD — judgment (locust plague as precursor) and restoration. Famous for: "I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh" (2:28) — fulfilled at Pentecost (Acts 2:16-21).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — The Locust Plague as Harbinger of the Day of the LORD
Key Themes: Unprecedented devastation, the call to mourn and fast, creation groaning, the Day of the LORD approaching, total economic and agricultural collapse
Historical Context: Chapter 1 opens with a devastating locust plague — so severe that nothing like it has occurred in living memory. Joel uses four different Hebrew words for locusts (v.4), suggesting wave after wave of destruction. The locusts strip everything: vines, fig trees, grain, oil, wine. But Joel sees beyond the natural disaster to its theological meaning: this is a foretaste of "the Day of the LORD" (v.15). The plague is both literal judgment and prophetic preview. Every sector of society is affected: drunkards (no wine), priests (no offerings), farmers (no harvest), even the animals and land itself groan.
The Unprecedented Plague: Tell Your Children (vv.1-4): Joel gives no king and no date, which is why the book is hard to place and easy to apply. He opens by asking the elders whether anything like this has happened in their days or their fathers', and instructs them to tell their children, so the event is being entered into memory as it happens. Verse 4 names four waves of locust in sequence, each finishing what the last left. Whether these are four species or four stages, the effect is a description of nothing remaining.
Call to Mourn: Drunkards, Priests, Farmers (vv.5-12): The summons goes out group by group, and each is addressed through what it has lost. The drinkers have no new wine. The farmers and vinedressers are told to be ashamed, their harvest gone. The priests are told to gird themselves and lament, because the grain and drink offerings are cut off from the house of God — the plague has stopped the sacrifices, so the disaster is liturgical as well as agricultural. The list of ruined crops in v.12 ends with joy itself withered.
Call to Fast and Cry Out (vv.13-14): The instruction moves from mourning to assembly: sanctify a fast, call a solemn meeting, gather the elders and all the inhabitants into the house of the LORD, and cry to Him. Nothing is promised at this point. The response asked for is corporate and public rather than private, which is how Israel handled catastrophe it understood as more than weather.
The Day of the LORD Is Near (vv.15-18): “Alas for the day! for the day of the LORD is at hand” turns the locust crisis into a lens. What is in front of them becomes a preview of something larger, and this is the theme the rest of the book develops. The detail in vv.17-18 stays close to the ground — seed rotting under the clods, barns broken down, cattle wandering because there is no pasture, flocks made desolate. The animals are described as groaning, which is the verse that most often stops a reader.
Joel's Prayer: Creation Cries to God (vv.19-20): The chapter ends with the prophet praying rather than preaching: “O LORD, to thee will I cry.” Fire has consumed the pastures and the watercourses are dried. Verse 20 puts the beasts of the field alongside him, crying to God as well. The chapter closes without an answer, which is left for chapter 2.

Setting: Joel is a prophet to Judah and Jerusalem. He speaks to elders and priests, mentions the temple and its offerings, and says nothing about a king. When did he prophesy? The book gives no date, and faithful scholars disagree. One view, held by Guzik and JFB, places him early, around 835 BC, during the boyhood of King Joash, when the priest Jehoiada led the nation (2 Chr 24:1–3). That would explain why no king is named, and the enemies Joel lists (Philistia, Tyre, Sidon, Egypt and Edom, 3:4, 19) fit that period before Assyria and Babylon. Joel's place among the early prophets in the Hebrew order of the Twelve points the same way. Another view, held by many modern scholars, places him after the exile, around 500–400 BC: there is no king, the temple is standing, the people have been "scattered… among the nations" (3:2), and the Greeks appear as slave buyers (3:6). The message does not depend on the date. A real catastrophe has hit the land, a locust plague followed by drought, and Joel reads it as a warning from God and a preview of the great "day of the LORD." Chapter 1 describes the disaster and calls the nation to its knees.

An Invasion of Locusts (vv1–12)

  • "The word of the LORD that came to Joel, the son of Pethuel" (v1). Joel means "the LORD is God," a whole confession of faith in a name, like Elijah's ("my God is the LORD"). Nothing else is known of him or his father. The opening phrase matters: the words are not Joel's opinions but the LORD's word.
  • "Hear this, you elders… Has such a thing happened in your days, or in the days of your fathers?" (v2). The oldest men in the land had never seen anything like it. Joel starts by making them stop and notice. Disasters can be explained away as bad luck. The prophet insists that this one means something.
  • "Tell your children of it… and their children to another generation" (v3). Israel was commanded to tell its children what God had done in the exodus (Exod 10:2; Deut 6:20–25). Now the story to pass on is a story of judgment. The plague of locusts in Egypt was told so that Israel would know the LORD (Exod 10:1–2), and so is this one.
  • The four locusts (v4). Joel uses four Hebrew words: gazam, "cutting" or "gnawing"; 'arbeh, "swarming," the common word for locust; yeleq, "hopping," perhaps the young wingless stage; and hasil, "destroying" or "finishing." Some think they are four stages of a locust's life, others four kinds of locust, others simply four waves of the same plague described with different words for effect. JFB favors "four species or stages," and the Tyndale notes say the point is the overwhelming completeness of the destruction. An old Jewish and Christian reading took them as four empires that would devour Israel, and JFB mentions it, but the chapter itself is clearly describing insects.
  • What a locust plague is. Desert locusts can gather in swarms of billions covering many square miles. A swarm can eat its own weight in green plants every day, stripping fields, vines and trees bare. A plague in Jerusalem in 1915 was documented with photographs: swarms darkened the sky, and within weeks the land was bare. Guzik cites accounts of it. The law of Moses had listed locusts among the curses for covenant unfaithfulness (Deut 28:38–42), and Solomon's temple prayer asked God to hear when locusts came (2 Chr 6:28).
  • "Awake, you drunkards, and weep… because of the sweet wine, for it is cut off" (v5). The first group Joel calls are those most attached to the good things now lost. The vines are gone, so the wine is gone. "Sweet wine" ('asis) is the fresh juice of new grapes. Joel's point is not mainly a rebuke of drinking but that the people most numbed by pleasure must wake up. Henry puts it well: "The more earthly delights we make needful to satisfy us, the more we expose ourselves to trouble."
  • "A nation has come up against my land, powerful and beyond number" (v6). Joel calls the locusts a "nation," an invading army. Proverbs observes that locusts "have no king, yet all of them march in rank" (Prov 30:27). The army picture prepares for chapter 2. "My land" is God speaking. The land was his gift and his possession (Lev 25:23).
  • "Its teeth are lions' teeth" (v6). A single locust is tiny, but a swarm chews through everything like a lion tearing prey. Revelation 9:7–8 borrows this image for its demonic locusts: "their teeth like lions' teeth."
  • "It has laid waste my vine and splintered my fig tree" (v7). The vine and fig tree were symbols of Israel itself (Hos 9:10; Isa 5:1–7) and of peace and prosperity, "every man under his vine and under his fig tree" (1 Kings 4:25; Mic 4:4). The locusts have eaten the bark, leaving the branches white. Guzik notes the possessive: even ruined, they are still "my vine" and "my fig tree." God has not given up his claim on his people.
  • "Lament like a virgin wearing sackcloth for the bridegroom of her youth" (v8). In Israel, betrothal was legally binding (Deut 22:23–24). This is a young woman whose husband-to-be has died before the wedding. Her joy was about to begin and has turned to grief. Sackcloth was rough cloth of goat or camel hair, worn in mourning.
  • "The grain offering and the drink offering are cut off from the house of the LORD" (v9). This is the worst of it. Every morning and evening the priests offered a lamb with flour, oil and wine (Exod 29:38–42; Num 28:3–8). With no grain and no grapes, the daily offering stopped. That offering was the sign of God's ongoing fellowship with his people. "The priests mourn," says Joel. JFB says it was not their lost income they mourned but that they could not offer what God required.
  • "The grain is destroyed, the wine dries up, the oil languishes" (v10). Grain, new wine and oil were the three staple products of the land and the three blessings God promised for obedience (Deut 7:13; 11:14). All three are gone. "The ground mourns," says Joel, as if the land itself grieves.
  • "Be ashamed, O tillers of the soil; wail, O vinedressers" (v11). "Be ashamed" here means to be disappointed, to have hopes dashed. Wheat and barley, the two grain harvests (barley in spring, wheat in early summer), have both failed.
  • "Pomegranate, palm, and apple… and gladness dries up from the children of man" (v12). The orchards fail too. The "apple" may be the apricot or another fruit. Joel closes the section with a wordplay: in Hebrew, "dries up" (hobish) sounds like "be ashamed" (hobishu, v11). The crops dry up and joy dries up with them. Harvest was Israel's great season of rejoicing (Ps 4:7; Isa 9:3), and now there will be none.
  • Commentators: Henry sees God humbling a people by the smallest of creatures: "Armies of insects were coming upon the land to eat the fruits of it." He adds that "all who labour only for the meat that perishes, will, sooner or later, be ashamed of their labour." JFB reads v4 as "Four species or stages of locusts, rather than four different insects," with "the primary reference" to "literal locusts." It explains that a betrothed virgin was already regarded as married (v8). Guzik reads the four words as successive waves and stresses that Joel is describing a present disaster, not a future threat. He also notes, if Joel is dated to Athaliah's time, that temple offerings were still going on even under a wicked queen, until there was nothing left to offer.

A Call to Repentance (vv13–20)

  • "Put on sackcloth and lament, O priests… pass the night in sackcloth" (v13). The priests are called to lead. They are to spend the night at the temple in mourning clothes, like Ahab when he humbled himself (1 Kings 21:27). The reason given is again the offerings: "withheld from the house of your God." What troubles the prophet most is not hunger but the break in worship.
  • "Consecrate a fast; call a solemn assembly" (v14). To "consecrate" (qaddeshu) a fast is to set it apart as holy. A "solemn assembly" ('atsarah) is a sacred gathering when ordinary work stops (Lev 23:36). JFB calls it "a day of restraint." The whole community, elders and "all the inhabitants of the land," is to come to the temple and "cry out to the LORD." Fasting says that seeking God matters more than food, which, in a famine, was also simply true. Joel 2:12–17 repeats the call and goes deeper.
  • "Alas for the day! For the day of the LORD is near" (v15). This is the first mention of the book's great theme. "The day of the LORD" is the time God steps into history to judge and to save. Amos had warned that it would be darkness for an unfaithful people (Amos 5:18–20). The locusts are not the day itself but a sign that it is coming, a small preview of something far greater.
  • "As destruction from the Almighty it comes" (v15). The Hebrew plays on sound: "destruction" is shod, "the Almighty" is Shaddai. It comes as shod from Shaddai. Isaiah 13:6 uses exactly the same line. The God who is all-powerful in blessing is also all-powerful in judgment.
  • "Is not the food cut off… joy and gladness from the house of our God?" (v16). Israel's feasts were times of eating before the LORD with joy (Deut 12:7; 16:11). With no harvest there could be no feast. Joel says "our God": he stands among the people, not above them.
  • "The seed shrivels under the clods" (v17). The drought is now part of the disaster. Even the seed for next year has died in the ground. The Hebrew of this verse has several rare words and is hard to translate, but the picture is clear: empty storehouses, crumbling barns and no hope of a next harvest.
  • "How the beasts groan! The herds of cattle are perplexed" (v18). The animals wander confused, looking for grass that isn't there. Even sheep, which survive on little, suffer. Creation suffers with its human stewards when they sin (Gen 3:17–18; Rom 8:20–22).
  • "To you, O LORD, I call" (v19). Joel stops describing and prays. He does what he has called the nation to do. "Fire has devoured the pastures" may mean the scorching heat of drought, as JFB takes it, or actual wildfires sweeping through dry grass, which often followed drought.
  • "Even the beasts of the field pant for you because the water brooks are dried up" (v20). The word "pant" is the one used in Psalm 42:1: "As a deer pants for flowing streams, so pants my soul for you, O God." The animals are crying to God. The question hanging over the chapter is whether the people will. JFB says the beasts "tacitly reprove" the people who had not yet called on God.
  • Commentators: Henry says the fast must be "a day in which people must be kept from their common employments," and he sees "even the inferior creatures suffering for our transgression." He then turns it into a rebuke: the animals' "crying to God in those cases, shames the stupidity of those who cry not to God in any case." JFB calls the day of the LORD in v15 "the day of His anger," a foretaste of the final day, and says the elders are summoned because those who led in guilt should lead in repentance. Guzik notes that the day of the LORD points finally to Christ's return but applies in a lesser sense to judgments like this. He adds Jesus' words about the tower of Siloam (Luke 13:1–5): disasters are not a puzzle about who is to blame but a call for everyone to repent. The Tyndale notes add the shod/Shaddai wordplay and the link between "pant" and Psalm 42:1.

Pointing to Christ

Joel 1 ends with an empty altar, dry ground and animals crying out to God. The daily offering, the sign that God was among his people, had stopped. The New Testament tells of the sacrifice that never runs out. Jesus "offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins" (Heb 10:12), and no locust or drought can cut it off. Joel calls the people to fast and cry out because there is nowhere else to go. That call still stands, and those who cry out will find, as Joel will promise, that "everyone who calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved" (2:32; Rom 10:13). The groaning creation of v20 waits, as Paul says, for "the freedom of the glory of the children of God" (Rom 8:21).

Reflection

  • Compounding Loss (v.4) — Joel describes four successive waves of locusts, each devouring what the last left behind. Where in your life has one unaddressed compromise multiplied into something far bigger?
  • Worship Interrupted (v.9) — The priests mourn because there's nothing left to bring as an offering; the disaster has reached even into worship. When hardship disrupts your normal rhythms of faith, does it draw you closer to God or expose neglect that was already there?
  • Gathering in Crisis (v.14) — Joel calls the whole community to fast and cry out to God together rather than face the disaster alone. When trouble hits your family or community, is your instinct to gather others in prayer or to withdraw?
  • A Preview of the Day of the LORD (v.15) — Joel reads this present disaster as a warning sign of a far greater reckoning still to come. Do the hard seasons of your life sharpen your awareness of eternity, or do you let them pass without a second thought?
  • Creation's Instinct for God (vv.19-20) — The chapter ends with the parched land and panting animals crying out to the LORD. If creation knows enough to turn to God in its drought, what keeps you from doing the same in yours?
