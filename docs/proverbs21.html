Summary

This chapter's proverbs circle around God's sovereignty over human plans, opening with the striking claim that the king's heart is like a stream of water in the Lord's hand, directed wherever he pleases (v. 1). Doing what is right and just is valued above religious sacrifice (v. 3), and living with a quarrelsome spouse is likened to a leaky roof in a storm or cramped quarters on a rooftop (vv. 9, 19). The chapter warns that the sluggard's own laziness will be his ruin, craving all day while refusing to work (vv. 25-26). It closes by declaring that no wisdom, understanding, or plan can succeed against the Lord, and that while a horse may be prepared for battle, victory itself belongs to him (vv. 30-31).

Authorship & Background

Author: Primarily Solomon (1:1, 10:1, 25:1), with sections by Agur (ch.30) and King Lemuel (ch.31). Compiled over several centuries. Proverbs is wisdom literature — practical instruction for godly living. Hebrew title: 'Mishlei' — 'Proverbs/Comparisons.'
Historical Context: Chapter 21 opens and closes with powerful sovereignty statements (vv.1, 30-31): God controls kings' hearts and determines battle outcomes. The chapter addresses God's absolute supremacy over human power, the superiority of justice over ritual sacrifice (v.3), the contentious wife (vv.9, 19), the sluggard's self-destruction (vv.25-26), and the futility of opposing God (v.30). These proverbs consistently redirect confidence from human ability to divine sovereignty.
Themes in This Chapter: God's Sovereignty Over Human Power (vv.1, 30-31); Justice Over Sacrifice (v.3); The Contentious Woman (vv.9, 19); Laziness and Its Consequences (vv.5, 17, 25-26); No Wisdom Against the LORD (v.30)
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: the boldest sovereignty claim in the whole collection opening the chapter, the king's heart is in the hand of the LORD, as the rivers of water, he turneth it whithersoever he will, a priority the prophets spent entire books arguing, to do justice and judgment is more acceptable to the LORD than sacrifice, a saying about a brawling wife repeated almost word for word twice in the same chapter, evidence the collection was assembled rather than composed in one sitting, guarded speech named as guarding the soul itself, and a closing image of a horse prepared for battle set against the plain admission that safety belongs to the LORD alone
The King's Heart and the Weighing of Ways (vv.1-8): The opening verse claims more than any other saying in the collection, the king's heart is in the hand of the LORD, as the rivers of water, he turneth it whithersoever he will, which places the most powerful human will inside another's. Verse 2 puts the reader in the same position, every way of a man is right in his own eyes, but the LORD pondereth the hearts. Verse 3 states the priority the prophets spent whole books on, to do justice and judgment is more acceptable to the LORD than sacrifice. The block closes on the thoughts of the diligent, on treasure got by a lying tongue, and on the way of a man being froward and strange.
The Housetop Corner and the Price of Pleasure (vv.9-20): This block collects the chapter's domestic and economic complaints. It is better to dwell in a corner of the housetop than with a brawling woman in a wide house, a saying repeated almost word for word at verse 19 with the wilderness in place of the roof, which is one sign that these chapters were assembled rather than composed. Between them stand the ear stopped at the cry of the poor, the gift that pacifies anger, and the pair of verses on appetite, he that loveth pleasure shall be a poor man, and there is treasure and oil in the dwelling of the wise, but a foolish man spendeth it up.
Guarded Speech, and the Horse Prepared for Battle (vv.21-31): The chapter ends on speech and on the limits of preparation. Whoso keepeth his mouth and his tongue keepeth his soul from troubles is the plainest sentence in it. The desire of the slothful killeth him, and the sacrifice of the wicked is abomination, which repeats the priority set at verse 3. The last two verses close the chapter where the first opened it, on the futility of setting anything against God, there is no wisdom, nor understanding, nor counsel, against the LORD, and then the image the chapter is remembered for, the horse is prepared against the day of battle, but safety is of the LORD.

Deep Dive

Setting: The collection headed "The proverbs of Solomon" continues (10:1–22:16; see Proverbs 10 on how it was gathered and how to read a proverb). The ESV has no headings inside it, so the headings below simply group the sayings in order. Chapter 21 is framed by God's rule. It opens with the king's heart in the LORD's hand, turned like a stream of water (v1), and closes with the horse made ready for battle while "the victory belongs to the LORD" (v31). In between, God weighs hearts (v2), prefers justice to sacrifice (v3), and sees into the house of the wicked (v12). Two sayings about a quarrelsome wife (vv9, 19), a set on the sluggard's craving (vv25–26), and warnings about wealth gained by lies or lost to pleasure fill out the chapter.

The King's Heart and the Weighed Heart (vv1–4)

  • "The king's heart is a stream of water in the hand of the LORD; he turns it wherever he will" (v1). "Stream" is peleg, an irrigation channel. JFB notes the same word in Psalm 1:3. A farmer in the Near East directed water down little channels with a hoe, opening one, blocking another. The king, the most powerful person in the land, is as easily steered by God as that water. The Tyndale notes: it seemed a king could do anything, but God guides his heart. Scripture is full of it: God moved Pharaoh to honor Joseph (Gen 41), Persian kings to rebuild Jerusalem (Ezra 1:1; 7:27), and Artaxerxes to grant Nehemiah's request (Neh 2:8). Henry: "the Lord rules every heart as he sees fit."
  • "Every way of a man is right in his own eyes, but the LORD weighs the heart" (v2). Nearly identical to 16:2 (which has "spirits"). Egyptian art pictured a god weighing the heart of the dead against a feather. Proverbs uses the same picture, but the LORD weighs the living heart now. Henry: "We are partial in judging ourselves and our actions." Paul: "It is the Lord who judges me" (1 Cor 4:4).
  • "To do righteousness and justice is more acceptable to the LORD than sacrifice" (v3). Not that sacrifice was unimportant, but that ritual without right living is empty. Samuel told Saul, "to obey is better than sacrifice" (1 Sam 15:22). The prophets said it again and again (Isa 1:11–17; Hos 6:6; Mic 6:6–8), and Jesus quoted Hosea twice (Matt 9:13; 12:7). The Tyndale notes: God wants worship from a sincere heart that leads to good actions. Henry: many think "outward devotions will excuse unrighteousness." Guzik points to the priest and Levite who passed by the wounded man (Luke 10:31–32).
  • "Haughty eyes and a proud heart, the lamp of the wicked, are sin" (v4). The Hebrew consonants can be read "lamp" (ner) or "plowed field" (nir), which is why the King James has "the plowing of the wicked." Most modern versions, the ESV, JFB and Guzik read "lamp," a picture of a person's life and prosperity (13:9; 20:20). The wicked person's pride is what lights his life, and it is all sin. Henry: "Sin is the pride, the ambition, the glory, the joy, and the business of wicked men." "Haughty eyes" are first on the list of things God hates (6:17).
  • Commentators: Guzik notes on v1 that God usually turns hearts by arranging circumstances, not by force, and gives Cyrus as an example (Isa 45:1). JFB and Guzik both prefer "lamp" in v4. Henry and the Tyndale notes agree on v3 that God wants the heart behind the worship.

Hasty Riches and Twisted Ways (vv5–8)

  • "The plans of the diligent lead surely to abundance, but everyone who is hasty comes only to poverty" (v5). Steady planning and work against shortcuts. Guzik passes on Waltke's contrast: the sluggard fails by doing nothing, the hasty man by not thinking. Henry: "The really diligent employ foresight as well as labour." Compare 28:20, 22.
  • "The getting of treasures by a lying tongue is a fleeting vapor and a snare of death" (v6). "Vapor" is hevel, the word Ecclesiastes uses again and again ("vanity of vanities," Eccl 1:2), breath that vanishes. The Hebrew text reads literally "seekers of death," and the old Greek translation has "snares of death," which the ESV follows. JFB keeps the Hebrew: those who chase such gain are chasing death. Henry agrees: "While men seek wealth by unlawful practices, they seek death."
  • "The violence of the wicked will sweep them away, because they refuse to do what is just" (v7). "Sweep away" is the picture of dragging fish off in a net (Hab 1:15). Their own violence is the net. JFB: their sins come back on them. Guzik notes they are judged both for what they did and for the justice they refused to do.
  • "The way of the guilty is crooked, but the conduct of the pure is upright" (v8). The word for "guilty" is rare, and the line can also read "the way of a man is crooked and strange," which Henry follows: "The way of mankind by nature is froward and strange." Either way, a life shows what a heart is.
  • Commentators: Henry and JFB both keep the Hebrew "seekers of death" in v6, while the ESV follows the Greek. Guzik applies v7 to Judas, whose sin ended in his own destruction.

The Rooftop and the Scoffer (vv9–13)

  • "It is better to live in a corner of the housetop than in a house shared with a quarrelsome wife" (v9). Israelite houses had flat roofs used for work, sleeping in hot weather and prayer (Acts 10:9). A corner of the roof was exposed to sun, wind and rain. Better that than a spacious house with constant fighting. JFB: the "wide house" is a "house of fellowship," big enough for several families. The verse is repeated word for word in 25:24, and v19 says it again in desert terms. The Tyndale notes: a beautiful home can't make up for a quarrelsome marriage. The proverb is addressed to men, but the principle applies to husbands just as well.
  • "The soul of the wicked desires evil; his neighbor finds no mercy in his eyes" (v10). The wicked aren't merely weak but hungry for evil. Guzik passes on the point that people often sin eagerly, not only from weakness. JFB: he won't spare even a friend in his way. The Tyndale notes: evil people are bad, and no argument will change them.
  • "When a scoffer is punished, the simple becomes wise; when a wise man is instructed, he gains knowledge" (v11). A near repeat of 19:25. The simple learn by watching the scoffer punished. The wise learn from teaching alone. JFB: the simple through "terror," the wise through teaching.
  • "The Righteous One observes the house of the wicked; he throws the wicked down to ruin" (v12). "The Righteous One" (tsaddiq) is unusual. Most read it as God, who sees what happens behind closed doors and brings it down (Ps 37:35–38). Others read it as a righteous person who watches the wicked and sees where they end up (Ps 73:17). The Tyndale notes give both views, and JFB thinks the second line implies God. The ESV capitalizes it as a title for God.
  • "Whoever closes his ear to the cry of the poor will himself call out and not be answered" (v13). The punishment fits the sin. JFB: the principle of retribution (Matt 7:2). The rich man ignored Lazarus at his gate and then cried out in torment without relief (Luke 16:19–26). James: "judgment is without mercy to one who has shown no mercy" (Jas 2:13). Henry includes underpaying workers in "stop their ears at the cry of the poor."
  • Commentators: Guzik and the Tyndale notes both mention the two readings of v12, and Henry takes the righteous man's view: good men "envy not the prosperity of evil-doers" because they see where it ends. Guzik and Clarke both point to the priest and Levite again on v13.

Bribes, Justice and the Ransom (vv14–18)

  • "A gift in secret averts anger, and a concealed bribe, strong wrath" (v14). Another honest observation, like 17:8 and 18:16. "Concealed" is literally "in the bosom," the fold of a robe (17:23). A gift can calm an angry person: Jacob's gifts to Esau (Gen 32:20) and Abigail's to David (1 Sam 25:18–35). But secret bribery corrupts justice, as v15 makes clear. JFB: secrecy heightens the effect, because the one bribed doesn't want his motive known. Henry asks why, if a gift can calm anger, "the fear of God, and the command of Christ" can't do it better.
  • "When justice is done, it is a joy to the righteous but terror to evildoers" (v15). The same event, justice done, is joy to one and terror to the other. The Tyndale notes: good people love justice and bad people fear it. Henry: "There is true pleasure only in the practice of religion."
  • "One who wanders from the way of good sense will rest in the assembly of the dead" (v16). "The dead" is rephaim, the shades in the underworld (2:18; 9:18). "Rest" is ironic. JFB: as at a journey's end, death becomes his unchanging home. "Wanders" pictures drifting, not a dramatic leap. Guzik uses Solomon himself, who slowly drifted from wisdom in old age (1 Kgs 11:4). Henry adds a note of hope: there is hope even for wanderers "in the all-sufficient Saviour."
  • "Whoever loves pleasure will be a poor man; he who loves wine and oil will not be rich" (v17). Wine and fragrant oil were luxuries for feasts (Amos 6:6). Enjoying them isn't the problem. Loving them is. The Tyndale notes: spending on luxury stops people saving. Compare v20.
  • "The wicked is a ransom for the righteous, and the traitor for the upright" (v18). "Ransom" (kopher) is the price paid to free someone. The idea is that the trouble meant for the righteous falls on the wicked instead, as 11:8 says. Haman was hanged on the gallows he built for Mordecai (Esth 7:10), which JFB cites. Egypt was given as Israel's ransom (Isa 43:3). Guzik warns, following Waltke, against pressing the word too far. The proverb does not mean the wicked pay for the sins of the righteous. That ransom only Christ could pay (Mark 10:45).
  • Commentators: Guzik notes v14 neither approves nor condemns gifts but notes they work, and lists Jacob, Abigail and Hezekiah. JFB, Henry and the Tyndale notes all read v18 as the wicked falling into the trouble they planned for others.

Treasure, Strongholds and the Guarded Mouth (vv19–24)

  • "It is better to live in a desert land than with a quarrelsome and fretful woman" (v19). The rooftop of v9 becomes a whole wilderness. JFB notes the "wilderness" was pastureland, but uninhabited. Henry, more broadly: "Unbridled passions spoil the comfort of all relations."
  • "Precious treasure and oil are in a wise man's dwelling, but a foolish man devours it" (v20). The wise save. The fool "swallows" everything as soon as he gets it. JFB: he swallows it "greedily." Joseph stored grain in the good years for the famine (Gen 41:48–49). The prodigal son "devoured" his inheritance (Luke 15:30).
  • "Whoever pursues righteousness and kindness will find life, righteousness, and honor" (v21). "Kindness" is hesed, loyal love. Pursue righteousness and you find it, and life and honor too. Jesus: "seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you" (Matt 6:33). The Tyndale notes tie righteousness and hesed to God's covenant (Exod 34:6–7). Henry: faith in "the mercy of God in Christ" leads a person to pursue them.
  • "A wise man scales the city of the mighty and brings down the stronghold in which they trust" (v22). Wisdom beats walls. Ecclesiastes tells of a poor wise man who saved a city by his wisdom (Eccl 9:14–16). David's men took Jerusalem, a city everyone thought impregnable, by going up the water shaft (2 Sam 5:6–8). JFB: "Wisdom is better than strength." Guzik applies it to spiritual warfare: the weapons of the faith "destroy strongholds" (2 Cor 10:4).
  • "Whoever keeps his mouth and his tongue keeps himself out of trouble" (v23). A play on "keep" again: guard your mouth and it guards you. Compare 13:3 and 18:21. JFB cites James 3:6–10. Henry: "It is our great concern to keep our souls from being entangled and disquieted."
  • "'Scoffer' is the name of the arrogant, haughty man who acts with arrogant pride" (v24). A definition, almost a dictionary entry. Three words for pride pile up: "arrogant," "haughty," "arrogant pride." The scoffer's mockery comes from pride. Pharaoh's "Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice?" (Exod 5:2) is Guzik's example. Henry: "Pride and haughtiness make men passionate."
  • Commentators: Henry and JFB both read v20 as wise saving against foolish waste. Guzik's spiritual application of v22 matches the Tyndale notes' broader point that wisdom is better than strength.

Craving, Sacrifice and the Day of Battle (vv25–31)

  • "The desire of the sluggard kills him, for his hands refuse to labor" (v25). He wants everything and will do nothing, and the unmet wanting kills him. JFB: his desire for ease leads to starvation.
  • "All day long he craves and craves, but the righteous gives and does not hold back" (v26). The sluggard (still the subject) is always wanting. The righteous person, who has worked, has enough to give. Generosity is the mark of the godly (11:24–25). Paul: let the thief "labor, doing honest work with his own hands, so that he may have something to share with anyone in need" (Eph 4:28). JFB: covetousness marks the sluggard, generosity the righteous.
  • "The sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination; how much more when he brings it with evil intent" (v27). The first line repeats 15:8. Worship from an unrepentant heart is detestable to God. Worse still is using worship as a cover for sin, like Absalom asking leave to pay a vow in Hebron to launch his revolt (2 Sam 15:7–10). JFB: "Hypocrisy is more hateful than open wrongdoing." Henry: "When holiness is pretended, but wickedness intended, that especially is an abomination."
  • "A false witness will perish, but the word of a man who hears will endure" (v28). "Will endure" is literally "will speak forever" or "speak successfully." The man who listens carefully and reports only what he heard is the one whose testimony stands. JFB: he "heareth" and so speaks sincerely and is believed.
  • "A wicked man puts on a bold face, but the upright gives thought to his ways" (v29). "Puts on a bold face" means he hardens his face, brazen and unashamed, like the adulteress in 7:13. JFB: he is obstinate. The upright, by contrast, think about where they are going.
  • "No wisdom, no understanding, no counsel can avail against the LORD" (v30). Three words for human wisdom, all negated. The book that praises wisdom so highly now sets its limit: no plan succeeds against God. Ahithophel's counsel was "as if one consulted the word of God," yet God defeated it (2 Sam 16:23; 17:14). The leaders gathered "against the LORD and against his Anointed" only did what God's plan had predestined (Acts 4:25–28).
  • "The horse is made ready for the day of battle, but the victory belongs to the LORD" (v31). Horses and chariots were the tanks of the ancient world. Israel was told not to multiply horses (Deut 17:16) and sang, "Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God" (Ps 20:7). "Victory" is teshu'ah, "salvation" or "deliverance." Preparation is not wrong. The horse is still made ready. But the outcome belongs to God. Henry: means should be used, but "our safety and salvation are only of the Lord."
  • Commentators: Guzik and Henry both say vv30–31 call for using the means without trusting them. The Tyndale notes read v31 as military power being powerless without the Lord's blessing. JFB cites Psalm 33:17: "The war horse is a false hope for salvation."

Pointing to Christ

Proverbs 21 begins and ends with God in control, of kings' hearts (v1) and of the day of battle (v31). The cross is the greatest case of both. Pilate and Herod thought they held power, but they did only what God's "hand and plan had predestined to take place" (Acts 4:27–28). No wisdom or counsel availed against the LORD (v30), and the victory, the salvation, was his alone (v31). Verse 18 speaks of the wicked as a "ransom" for the righteous, but the gospel turns it inside out: the Righteous One became the ransom for the wicked, "the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God" (1 Pet 3:18; Mark 10:45). He is also the one who did "righteousness and justice" perfectly (v3), and so made the final sacrifice that was not an abomination but "a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God" (Eph 5:2).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes v.1's stunning claim: God doesn't merely observe kings — He directs them as effortlessly as directing water flow. On v.31, he states the balance perfectly: "Prepare the horse — that's your job. But know that safety is from God — that's His job. Do your part and trust Him with His."
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • God Directs Even the Most Powerful Hearts (v.1) — A king's heart is described as being in the LORD's hand, turned wherever He wills. What situation involving someone else's power over you needs to be entrusted to God's sovereignty instead of your anxiety?
  • Justice Matters More Than Ritual (v.3) — Doing what is right and just pleases God more than religious offerings. Where might you be substituting religious activity for actually treating people justly?
  • Mercy Shown Is Mercy Received (v.13) — Refusing to hear the cry of the poor means your own cry may go unheard later. Who is crying out for help around you that you've been tuning out?
  • No Plan Can Prevail Against God (v.30) — All human wisdom and counsel fail when set against the LORD's purpose. What are you resisting that you should instead surrender to God's will?
  • Preparation and Dependence Together (v.31) — The horse is made ready for battle, yet victory ultimately comes from the LORD. Are you doing your part while still trusting God for the outcome, or leaning fully on one and neglecting the other?
