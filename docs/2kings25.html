1So in the ninth year of Zedekiah’s reign, on the tenth day of the tenth month, Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon marched against Jerusalem with his entire army. They encamped outside the city and built a siege wall all around it.

2And the city was kept under siege until King Zedekiah’s eleventh year.

3By the ninth day of the fourth month, the famine in the city was so severe that the people of the land had no food.

4Then the city was breached; and though the Chaldeans had surrounded the city, all the men of war fled by night by way of the gate between the two walls near the king’s garden. They headed toward the Arabah,

5but the army of the Chaldeans pursued the king and overtook him in the plains of Jericho, and his whole army deserted him.

6The Chaldeans seized the king and brought him up to the king of Babylon at Riblah, where they pronounced judgment on him.

7And they slaughtered the sons of Zedekiah before his eyes. Then they put out his eyes, bound him with bronze shackles, and took him to Babylon.

8On the seventh day of the fifth month, in the nineteenth year of Nebuchadnezzar’s reign over Babylon, Nebuzaradan captain of the guard, a servant of the king of Babylon, entered Jerusalem.

9He burned down the house of the LORD, the royal palace, and all the houses of Jerusalem—every significant building.

10And the whole army of the Chaldeans under the captain of the guard broke down the walls around Jerusalem.

11Then Nebuzaradan captain of the guard carried into exile the people who remained in the city, along with the deserters who had defected to the king of Babylon and the rest of the population.

12But the captain of the guard left behind some of the poorest of the land to tend the vineyards and fields.

13Moreover, the Chaldeans broke up the bronze pillars and stands and the bronze Sea in the house of the LORD, and they carried the bronze to Babylon.

14They also took away the pots, shovels, wick trimmers, dishes, and all the articles of bronze used in the temple service.

15The captain of the guard also took away the censers and sprinkling bowls—anything made of pure gold or fine silver.

16As for the two pillars, the Sea, and the movable stands that Solomon had made for the house of the LORD, the weight of the bronze from all these articles was beyond measure.

17Each pillar was eighteen cubits tall. The bronze capital atop one pillar was three cubits high, with a network of bronze pomegranates all around. The second pillar, with its network, was similar.

18The captain of the guard also took away Seraiah the chief priest, Zephaniah the priest of second rank, and the three doorkeepers.

19Of those still in the city, he took a court official who had been appointed over the men of war, as well as five royal advisors. He also took the scribe of the captain of the army, who had enlisted the people of the land, and sixty men who were found in the city.

20Nebuzaradan captain of the guard took them and brought them to the king of Babylon at Riblah.

21There at Riblah in the land of Hamath, the king of Babylon struck them down and put them to death. So Judah was taken into exile, away from its own land.

22Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon appointed Gedaliah son of Ahikam, the son of Shaphan, over the people he had left behind in the land of Judah.

23When all the commanders of the armies and their men heard that the king of Babylon had appointed Gedaliah as governor, they came to Gedaliah at Mizpah—Ishmael son of Nethaniah, Johanan son of Kareah, Seraiah son of Tanhumeth the Netophathite, and Jaazaniah son of the Maacathite, as well as their men.

24And Gedaliah took an oath before them and their men, assuring them, “Do not be afraid of the servants of the Chaldeans. Live in the land and serve the king of Babylon, and it will be well with you.”

25In the seventh month, however, Ishmael son of Nethaniah, the son of Elishama, who was a member of the royal family, came with ten men and struck down and killed Gedaliah, along with the Judeans and Chaldeans who were with him at Mizpah.

26Then all the people small and great, together with the commanders of the army, arose and fled to Egypt for fear of the Chaldeans.

27On the twenty-seventh day of the twelfth month of the thirty-seventh year of the exile of Judah’s King Jehoiachin, in the year Evil-merodach became king of Babylon, he released King Jehoiachin of Judah from prison.

28And he spoke kindly to Jehoiachin and set his throne above the thrones of the other kings who were with him in Babylon.

29So Jehoiachin changed out of his prison clothes, and he dined regularly at the king’s table for the rest of his life.

30And the king provided Jehoiachin a daily portion for the rest of his life.

18And the captain of the guard took Seraiah the chief priest, and Zephaniah the second priest, and the three keepers of the door:

19And out of the city he took an officer that was set over the men of war, and five men of them that were in the king’s presence, which were found in the city, and the principal scribe of the host, which mustered the people of the land, and threescore men of the people of the land that were found in the city:

1So King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon came against Jerusalem with his whole army and set up camp outside it. They built siege ramps all around it. He arrived on the tenth day of the tenth month in the ninth year of Zedekiah’s reign.

2The city remained under siege until King Zedekiah’s eleventh year.

3By the ninth day of the fourth month the famine in the city was so severe the residents had no food.

4The enemy broke through the city walls, and all the soldiers tried to escape. They left the city during the night. They went through the gate between the two walls, which is near the king’s garden. (The Babylonians were all around the city.) Then they headed for the rift valley.

5But the Babylonian army chased after the king. They caught up with him in the rift valley plains of Jericho, and his entire army deserted him.

6They captured the king and brought him up to the king of Babylon at Riblah, where he passed sentence on him.

7Zedekiah’s sons were executed while Zedekiah was forced to watch. The king of Babylon then had Zedekiah’s eyes put out, bound him in bronze chains, and carried him off to Babylon.

8On the seventh day of the fifth month, in the nineteenth year of King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon, Nebuzaradan, the captain of the royal guard, who served the king of Babylon, arrived in Jerusalem.

9He burned down the Lord’s temple, the royal palace, and all the houses in Jerusalem, including every large house.

10The whole Babylonian army that came with the captain of the royal guard tore down the walls that surrounded Jerusalem.

11Nebuzaradan, the captain of the royal guard, deported the rest of the people who were left in the city, those who had deserted to the king of Babylon, and the rest of the craftsmen.

12But he left behind some of the poor of the land and gave them fields and vineyards.

13The Babylonians broke the two bronze pillars in the Lord’s temple, as well as the movable stands and the big bronze basin called “The Sea.” They took the bronze to Babylon.

14They also took the pots, shovels, trimming shears, pans, and all the bronze utensils used by the priests.

15The captain of the royal guard took the golden and silver censers and basins.

16The bronze of the items that King Solomon made for the Lord’s temple—including the two pillars, the big bronze basin called “The Sea,” the twelve bronze bulls under “The Sea,” and the movable stands—was too heavy to be weighed.

17Each of the pillars was about 27 feet high. The bronze top of one pillar was about 4½ feet high and had bronze latticework and pomegranate-shaped ornaments all around it. The second pillar with its latticework was like it.

18The captain of the royal guard took Seraiah, the chief priest, and Zephaniah, the priest who was second in rank, and the three doorkeepers.

19From the city he took a eunuch who was in charge of the soldiers, five of the king’s advisers who were discovered in the city, an official army secretary who drafted citizens for military service, and 60 citizens from the people of the land who were discovered in the city.

20Nebuzaradan, captain of the royal guard, took them and brought them to the king of Babylon at Riblah.

21The king of Babylon ordered them to be executed at Riblah in the territory of Hamath. So Judah was deported from its land.

22Now King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon appointed Gedaliah son of Ahikam, son of Shaphan, as governor over the people whom he allowed to remain in the land of Judah.

23All the officers of the Judahite army and their troops heard that the king of Babylon had appointed Gedaliah to govern. So they came to Gedaliah at Mizpah. The officers who came were Ishmael son of Nethaniah, Johanan son of Kareah, Seraiah son of Tanhumeth the Netophathite, and Jaazaniah son of the Maacathite.

24Gedaliah took an oath so as to give them and their troops some assurance of safety. He said, “You don’t need to be afraid to submit to the Babylonian officials. Settle down in the land and submit to the king of Babylon. Then things will go well for you.”

25But in the seventh month Ishmael son of Nethaniah, son of Elishama, who was a member of the royal family, came with 10 of his men and murdered Gedaliah, as well as the Judeans and Babylonians who were with him at Mizpah.

26Then all the people, from the youngest to the oldest, as well as the army officers, left for Egypt, because they were afraid of what the Babylonians might do.

27In the thirty-seventh year of the exile of King Jehoiachin of Judah, on the twenty-seventh day of the twelfth month, King Evil Merodach of Babylon, in the first year of his reign, pardoned King Jehoiachin of Judah and released him from prison.

28He spoke kindly to him and gave him a more prestigious position than the other kings who were with him in Babylon.

29Jehoiachin took off his prison clothes and ate daily in the king’s presence for the rest of his life.

Summary

In Zedekiah's ninth year Nebuchadnezzar besieges Jerusalem, and by the eleventh year famine is so severe in the city that the wall is finally breached (vv. 1-4). Zedekiah flees but is captured near Jericho, forced to watch his sons executed, then blinded and taken in bronze shackles to Babylon (vv. 5-7). Nebuzaradan, the captain of the guard, burns the temple, the palace, and every great house in Jerusalem, tears down the city walls, and deports most of the remaining population, leaving only the poorest to work the land (vv. 8-12). He also carries off or breaks up all the temple's bronze, silver, and gold articles, including the great pillars and the bronze sea, and executes the chief priest Seraiah and other leading officials at Riblah (vv. 13-21). Gedaliah is appointed governor over those left in the land, but he is soon assassinated by Ishmael, and the remaining people flee to Egypt in fear of Babylonian reprisal (vv. 22-26). The book closes on a faint note of hope: in the thirty-seventh year of his exile, King Jehoiachin is released from prison by Evil-merodach of Babylon, given a seat of honor, and allowed to eat regularly at the king's table for the rest of his life (vv. 27-30).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Jeremiah or a prophetic school. Originally one book with 1 Kings. Covers approximately 250 years (850-586 BC) from Elijah's translation through the Babylonian exile.
Historical Context: Chapter 25 records the catastrophic end of the kingdom of Judah — the destruction of Jerusalem and Solomon's Temple by Nebuchadnezzar in 586 BC. This is the darkest chapter in Israel's history: the city of David reduced to rubble, the Temple of God burned to the ground, the Davidic king blinded and chained, the people scattered to Babylon, Egypt, and oblivion. Everything God promised to David, everything Solomon built, everything the prophets warned about — it all culminates here. The siege lasted approximately eighteen months (January 588 to July 586 BC), and famine became so severe that "there was no bread for the people of the land" (v.3). When the walls were breached, Zedekiah fled toward the Jordan but was captured near Jericho — the very place where Israel first entered the Promised Land. He watched his sons executed, then had his eyes put out — the last thing he ever saw was the death of the royal line.
Yet the chapter does not end in total darkness. In an epilogue (vv.27-30) set 37 years after the exile, Jehoiachin — the deposed king taken captive in 597 BC — is released from prison by Evil-merodach (Amel-Marduk), Nebuchadnezzar's successor. He is given a seat above other captive kings and provided daily provisions. This small act of mercy functions as a theological signal: the Davidic line survives. The promises to David are not annulled. In the midst of judgment's aftermath, a descendant of David sits at a king's table. The author ends 2 Kings not with the destruction but with this whispered hope — the same hope that will eventually produce the return under Cyrus and, ultimately, the Messiah from David's line. The exile is not the end; it is a death from which resurrection will come.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: An eighteen-month siege ending in a breach and a king's flight by night through a garden gate, sons killed in front of Zedekiah as the last thing his own eyes ever see before they are put out, temple bronze and gold catalogued item by item in a grief that is almost liturgical in its detail, leaders executed at Riblah while the poorest of the land are left to tend the vineyards and fields, and the book's very last notice, a captive king raised up out of prison and given a seat above every other captive king in Babylon
The Fall of Jerusalem (vv.1-12): Nebuchadnezzar besieges Jerusalem in the ninth year of Zedekiah's reign, tenth month (January 588 BC). The siege lasts until the eleventh year, fourth month (July 586 BC) — approximately eighteen months of encirclement, starvation, and bombardment. When famine overwhelms the city (v.3), a breach is made in the walls (v.4). Zedekiah and his army flee by night through a gate "between two walls, which is by the king's garden" — heading toward the Jordan Valley (the Arabah). The Chaldean army pursues and overtakes him in the plains of Jericho; his army scatters (v.5). He is brought before Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah (in modern Lebanon), where his sons are slaughtered before his eyes, his eyes are then put out, and he is bound in chains and taken to Babylon (v.7). One month later, Nebuzaradan (captain of the guard) arrives and systematically destroys Jerusalem: the Temple is burned, the palace is burned, every great house is burned, and the walls are torn down (vv.8-10). The remaining population is deported, except the very poorest who are left "to be vinedressers and husbandmen" (v.12).
The Temple Plundered and the Aftermath (vv.13-30): The destruction of the Temple furnishings is recorded in painful detail: the bronze pillars (Jachin and Boaz), the bronze sea, the stands, the pots, shovels, snuffers, dishes, fire pans, and bowls — everything of bronze, gold, and silver is taken to Babylon (vv.13-17). The detail is liturgical grief — each item represented God's presence and provision, now stripped away. Key leaders are executed at Riblah (vv.18-21): Seraiah the chief priest, Zephaniah the second priest, doorkeepers, military officers, royal advisers, and sixty citizens. "So Judah was carried away out of their land" (v.21). Gedaliah is appointed governor over the remnant but is assassinated by Ishmael — a member of the royal family (vv.22-25). The surviving remnant flees to Egypt in fear (v.26) — against Jeremiah's explicit warning (Jeremiah 42-43). The book ends with the epilogue: 37 years later, Evil-merodach releases Jehoiachin, speaks kindly to him, elevates his position, gives him a place at the royal table, and provides daily provisions for the rest of his life (vv.27-30).

Setting: Jerusalem, 588–586 BC, with a closing scene in Babylon in 561 BC. Ten years earlier Nebuchadnezzar took King Jehoiachin, the leading citizens and the craftsmen to Babylon and put Jehoiachin's uncle Zedekiah on the throne (24:10–17). Zedekiah swore loyalty in the LORD's name, then broke his oath and rebelled, trusting Egypt (24:20; Ezek 17:15). Jeremiah told him again and again to surrender and live (Jer 38:17–18). He would not. This is the last chapter of Kings, and it tells the end of everything the book has followed: the city, the temple, the line of kings and Israel's life in the land. Jeremiah 39 and 52 tell the same story, almost word for word in places, and Lamentations is the poetry of grief written over the ruins.

Fall and Captivity of Judah (vv1–21)

  • "In the ninth year of his reign, in the tenth month, on the tenth day of the month" (v1). January 588 BC. Kings rarely gives exact days, but in this chapter it gives them over and over, as if each date were burned into memory. They were. After the exile the Jews kept fasts on the anniversaries: the tenth month for the siege's start, the fourth month for the wall breached, the fifth for the temple burned, the seventh for Gedaliah's murder (v25). Zechariah names exactly these four fasts (Zech 8:19). Jewish communities still keep the fast of the fifth month, Tisha B'Av, the ninth of Av, as a day of mourning.
  • "They built siegeworks all around it" (v1). A siege wall and towers ringed the city, so no one could get out and no food could get in. Ezekiel, in Babylon, was told to act out this very siege on a clay brick on the day it began (Ezek 4:1–3; 24:1–2). Ezekiel 24:1–2 records the same date.
  • "So the city was besieged till the eleventh year" (v2). About eighteen months. There was a break when an Egyptian army marched up and the Babylonians withdrew for a time (Jer 37:5–11). The people thought they were saved. Jeremiah told them the Babylonians would be back. They came back.
  • "The famine was so severe in the city that there was no food" (v3). Lamentations describes the horror in detail: children begging for bread, and mothers so starved they cooked their own children (Lam 4:4, 10). This was one of the curses Moses had warned would come if Israel broke the covenant (Deut 28:52–57). JFB notes the same link to the curses on apostasy.
  • "A breach was made in the city" (v4). July 586 BC, on the ninth day of the fourth month. Some scholars date the fall to 587 BC, depending on how the regnal years are counted.
  • "By the way of the gate between the two walls, by the king's garden… in the direction of the Arabah" (v4). The escape route ran out the southeast corner of the city near the Pool of Siloam, as JFB says, down the Kidron Valley and east toward the Jordan Valley (the Arabah). Ezekiel had acted this out too, digging through a wall and leaving at night with his baggage (Ezek 12:3–12).
  • "Overtook him in the plains of Jericho, and all his army was scattered" (v5). Guzik points out the irony: Jericho was where Israel first entered the land under Joshua (Josh 6). The last king of Judah is caught on the same ground where the conquest began. The story of Israel in the land ends where it started.
  • "Brought him up to the king of Babylon at Riblah, and they passed sentence on him" (v6). Riblah in Syria was Nebuchadnezzar's field headquarters, the same place where Pharaoh Neco had put Jehoahaz in chains (23:33). "Passed sentence" is literally "spoke judgment with him." Zedekiah was tried as a vassal who broke his oath. Chronicles stresses that the oath had been sworn by God (2 Chr 36:13).
  • "They slaughtered the sons of Zedekiah before his eyes, and put out the eyes of Zedekiah" (v7). This was a common punishment for rebel kings, and the Tyndale notes observe how deliberately cruel it was: the last thing he saw was his sons killed. Two prophecies that seemed to contradict each other both came true. Jeremiah said Zedekiah would "see the king of Babylon eye to eye" (Jer 32:4; 34:3). Ezekiel said he would be brought to Babylon, "yet he shall not see it, and he shall die there" (Ezek 12:13). He saw Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah, was blinded, and never saw Babylon. JFB makes the same point from Ezekiel.
  • "In the fifth month, on the seventh day… Nebuzaradan, the captain of the bodyguard" (v8). About a month after the wall fell, Nebuchadnezzar sent his senior officer to finish the work. Nebuzaradan's name appears on a Babylonian list of Nebuchadnezzar's officials, the Tyndale notes say. His title, rab-tabbachim, is the same one Potiphar held in Egypt (Gen 37:36). The word tabbach can mean "cook" or "butcher," and by extension a royal guard or executioner. Jeremiah 52:12 gives the date as the tenth day. JFB suggests one date is when he arrived and the other when he set the fire.
  • "He burned the house of the LORD" (v9). Kings says it in one plain line. Solomon's temple, about 375 years old, the place God said he would put his name (1 Kings 9:3), is gone. Henry: "Solomon's temple is burnt to the ground." Ezekiel had seen the glory of the LORD leave the temple before this happened, rising from the threshold and departing east over the Mount of Olives (Ezek 10:18–19; 11:22–23). The building that burned was already empty.
  • "Broke down the walls around Jerusalem" (v10). The walls would lie in ruins for almost 140 years, until Nehemiah rebuilt them (Neh 1:3; 6:15).
  • "The deserters who had deserted to the king of Babylon" (v11). Jeremiah had urged people to surrender to save their lives (Jer 21:9; 38:2). Some did. They went into exile with the rest, but alive.
  • "Some of the poorest of the land to be vinedressers and plowmen" (v12). Babylon needed someone to keep the land producing. Jeremiah adds that Nebuzaradan gave these poor people vineyards and fields (Jer 39:10). The landless ended up with the land.
  • The bronze (vv13–17). Solomon's great bronze work, made by Hiram of Tyre (1 Kings 7:13–47), is broken up and carted away as scrap. The two pillars had names, Jachin ("he establishes") and Boaz ("in him is strength") (1 Kings 7:21). The "sea" was an enormous basin for the priests' washing. The narrator lingers over these pieces, giving their height, the capitals and the pomegranates, the way a family lists what was lost in a fire. "Beyond weight" (v16) is what was said when Solomon made them (1 Kings 7:47). What Solomon could not weigh, Babylon hauls away. The ESV footnote gives a cubit as about 18 inches, so each pillar stood about 27 feet high. The Tyndale notes say 1 Kings 7:16 and Jeremiah 52:22 give the capital as five cubits; the "three" here may measure only the lattice part. Guzik notes, citing an older writer, that verse after verse begins with "and," like a clock counting down.
  • No mention of the ark. The ark of the covenant is not in any list of things taken or destroyed. Scripture does not say what happened to it. A later Jewish tradition says Jeremiah hid it (2 Maccabees 2:4–8), but that is legend, not Scripture. Jeremiah himself had said a day would come when no one would miss it or make another (Jer 3:16).
  • "Seraiah the chief priest and Zephaniah the second priest" (v18). Seraiah was a grandson of Hilkiah, who found the book of the law under Josiah (1 Chr 6:13–14). He is executed, but his son Jehozadak is taken into exile (1 Chr 6:15), and Jehozadak's son Joshua becomes high priest after the return (Hag 1:1). Ezra, the great scribe, was also a descendant of this Seraiah (Ezra 7:1). JFB calls him Ezra's grandfather, which the dates do not allow, since Ezra came to Jerusalem 128 years later. Zephaniah the second priest had carried messages between Zedekiah and Jeremiah (Jer 21:1; 37:3). The "keepers of the threshold" were, in JFB's words, "not mere porters, but officers of high trust."
  • "Put them to death at Riblah" (vv19–21). In 597 BC the leaders had been deported. Now they are executed. "The people of the land" were the landowning men of Judah who had made and unmade kings (21:24; 23:30). Sixty of them die here.
  • "So Judah was taken into exile out of its land" (v21). Short and final, and a deliberate echo of the verdict on the northern kingdom: "So Israel was exiled from their own land" (17:23). Moses had warned that if Israel turned to other gods, "you shall be plucked off the land" (Deut 28:63–64). God did what he said he would do.
  • Commentators: Henry describes Jerusalem as both the royal city and the holy city, and says both glories departed here, writing "Ichabod—Where is the glory?" over it. Guzik reads Zedekiah's capture near Jericho as a grim bookend to the conquest, and notes that the exile ended about 860 years in the land, lost through idolatry. JFB explains the escape route by the Pool of Siloam and sees Ezekiel 12:13 fulfilled in a king who came to Babylon but "did not see" it. JFB skips the bronze furnishings (vv13–17) altogether.

Gedaliah Made Governor of Judah (vv22–26)

  • "Gedaliah the son of Ahikam, son of Shaphan" (v22). This family appears all through Josiah's reform and Jeremiah's ministry. Shaphan read the found book to Josiah (22:10). Ahikam saved Jeremiah's life from a mob (Jer 26:24). Now Ahikam's son is made governor, and Nebuchadnezzar puts Jeremiah in his care (Jer 39:14; 40:5–6). A clay seal impression found at Lachish reads "belonging to Gedaliah, who is over the house," probably the same man before the fall.
  • "At Mizpah" (v23). Jerusalem was rubble, so the government moved to Mizpah, a few miles north in Benjamin. It was an old place of national gathering, where Samuel called Israel to repent (1 Sam 7:5–6).
  • The captains (v23). These were Judean army officers who had scattered into the countryside during the siege. Jeremiah 40–41 tells the longer story. A seal with the name "Jaazaniah, servant of the king" was found at Tell en-Nasbeh, which many think is Mizpah, as the Tyndale notes mention.
  • "Do not be afraid… serve the king of Babylon, and it shall be well with you" (v24). Gedaliah repeats Jeremiah's message almost word for word (Jer 27:11; 29:7). Babylon was the rod of God's judgment, and the way to live was to accept it, not fight it. Guzik makes the same point: resisting Babylon now meant resisting God's discipline.
  • "Ishmael… of the royal family, came with ten men and struck down Gedaliah" (v25). Johanan had warned Gedaliah that Ishmael was sent by the king of Ammon to kill him, but Gedaliah refused to believe it (Jer 40:13–16). Ishmael killed him at a meal (Jer 41:1–2). He was of David's family and may have resented a non-royal governor serving Babylon. JFB suggests envy, or a bribe from Baalis king of Ammon. The fast of the seventh month, still kept by Jews as the Fast of Gedaliah, remembers this day.
  • "All the people… went to Egypt, for they were afraid of the Chaldeans" (v26). They asked Jeremiah to pray for guidance and promised to obey whatever God said. God told them to stay in the land, and they went to Egypt anyway, dragging Jeremiah with them (Jer 42–43). Kings tells it in one verse, and the irony is crushing. Moses had warned that if Israel broke the covenant, God would bring them back to Egypt (Deut 28:68). The people the LORD brought out of Egypt walk back into it by their own choice.
  • Commentators: Henry describes the remnant under Gedaliah as scattered and broken. Guzik calls Gedaliah a godly friend of Jeremiah and says Egypt proved a worse choice than submission to Babylon. JFB notes that they went "in spite of Jeremiah's dissuasions."

Jehoiachin Released from Prison (vv27–30)

  • "In the thirty-seventh year of the exile of Jehoiachin" (v27). That is 561 BC, twenty-five years after the temple burned. Jehoiachin is about fifty-five, after thirty-seven years as a prisoner. Nebuchadnezzar has just died after a reign of forty-three years.
  • "Evil-merodach king of Babylon" (v27). His Babylonian name was Amel-Marduk, "man of Marduk." He reigned only two years before he was murdered. New kings sometimes released prisoners at the start of their reign as a show of goodwill.
  • "Graciously freed" (v27). The ESV footnote gives the Hebrew idiom: he "lifted up the head of" Jehoiachin. It is the same phrase used when Pharaoh restored his cupbearer in Joseph's story (Gen 40:13, 20). There, too, a man in a foreign prison was raised up by a king.
  • "Spoke kindly to him and gave him a seat above the seats of the kings who were with him" (v28). Babylon held several captive kings. David's heir is given the place of honor among them. Babylonian ration tablets found in the ruins of Nebuchadnezzar's palace list oil supplied to "Yaukin, king of the land of Judah" and his five sons, so the text's picture of a royal prisoner with a household is confirmed.
  • "Jehoiachin put off his prison garments… dined regularly at the king's table" (v29). Compare Mephibosheth, Saul's grandson, who ate at David's table "like one of the king's sons" (2 Sam 9:11–13). The roles are turned around: now David's heir eats at a pagan king's table, by grace.
  • "As long as he lived" (v30). That is where the book of Kings ends. There is no word of return, no new temple, no restored throne. But David's line is still alive, and a foreign king has lifted its head. The Tyndale notes suggest the scene was meant as a quiet hope that God would restore a repentant people, as Solomon had prayed (1 Kings 8:46–53) and Jeremiah promised after seventy years (Jer 29:10–14). Jeremiah 52 ends with the very same paragraph.
  • Commentators: Henry speaks of "the countenance which, after thirty-seven years' imprisonment, was given to Jehoiachin." JFB sees God's providence toward the house of David in it, and says Jehoiachin had "a seat at the royal table." Guzik reads the ending as a deliberate note of grace: the land is empty and the people are in exile, but God is not finished with David's house.

Pointing to Christ

Kings ends in ashes, with the temple burned, the walls down, the king blinded, the people scattered and a remnant walking back to Egypt. The one light left is a son of David let out of prison to eat at a king's table. It is a small light, but it is a real one, because God had sworn that David's line would not fail (2 Sam 7:16). Jehoiachin's name appears in the genealogy that ends with "Jesus… who is called Christ" (Matt 1:12, 16). The true Son of David did more than eat at a king's table by favor. He became the temple that was destroyed and raised in three days (John 2:19–21), and he spreads his own table for exiles, people raised up from prison and given new clothes, who will eat in his kingdom forever (Luke 22:29–30).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes that the detailed inventory of Temple items destroyed serves as liturgical grief — the author catalogs what was lost the way a person in mourning remembers the details of what they loved. He also highlights the significance of ending 2 Kings with Jehoiachin's release rather than Jerusalem's destruction: the author deliberately structures hope as the final word, signaling that God's covenant with David endures even through the darkest judgment.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Sin's Consequences Are Not Abstract (vv.1-7) — The exile meant real famine, a breached city, and a king watching his sons killed before his own eyes were put out. Do you take God's warnings about sin as seriously as their actual, tangible cost?
  • One Reckless Leader Can Undo Patient Progress (vv.22-25) — Gedaliah offered the shattered remnant a stable path forward, but one jealous man's violence destroyed it and sent the people fleeing to Egypt against God's explicit warning. Are you guarding what you're building from being wrecked by someone else's ambition, or your own?
  • God's Last Word Is Never Judgment (v.27) — The book could have ended at the temple's destruction, but instead it closes 37 years later with Jehoiachin released, given a seat at the king's table, and David's royal line preserved intact. Whatever has been lost or broken in your life, are you still looking for the mercy God writes beyond it?
  • Daily Provision, Even in Exile (v.30) — God gave the imprisoned king a continual, daily allowance for the rest of his life, echoing the manna once given in the wilderness. In your own season of displacement or consequence, can you trust God for today's bread even without seeing the whole future?
