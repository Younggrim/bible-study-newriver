1In the twenty-seventh year of Jeroboam’s reign over Israel, Azariah son of Amaziah became king of Judah.

2He was sixteen years old when he became king, and he reigned in Jerusalem fifty-two years. His mother’s name was Jecoliah; she was from Jerusalem.

3And he did what was right in the eyes of the LORD, just as his father Amaziah had done.

4Nevertheless, the high places were not taken away; the people continued sacrificing and burning incense there.

5And the LORD afflicted the king with leprosy until the day he died, so that he lived in a separate house while his son Jotham had charge of the palace and governed the people of the land.

6As for the rest of the acts of Azariah, along with all his accomplishments, are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Judah?

7And Azariah rested with his fathers and was buried near them in the City of David. And his son Jotham reigned in his place.

8In the thirty-eighth year of Azariah’s reign over Judah, Zechariah son of Jeroboam became king of Israel, and he reigned in Samaria six months.

9And he did evil in the sight of the LORD, as his fathers had done. He did not turn away from the sins that Jeroboam son of Nebat had caused Israel to commit.

10Then Shallum son of Jabesh conspired against Zechariah, struck him down and killed him in front of the people, and reigned in his place.

11As for the rest of the acts of Zechariah, they are indeed written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Israel.

12So the word of the LORD spoken to Jehu was fulfilled: “Four generations of your sons will sit on the throne of Israel.”

13In the thirty-ninth year of Uzziah’s reign over Judah, Shallum son of Jabesh became king, and he reigned in Samaria one full month.

14Then Menahem son of Gadi went up from Tirzah to Samaria, struck down and killed Shallum son of Jabesh, and reigned in his place.

15As for the rest of the acts of Shallum, along with the conspiracy he led, they are indeed written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Israel.

16At that time Menahem, starting from Tirzah, attacked Tiphsah and everyone in its vicinity, because they would not open their gates. So he attacked Tiphsah and ripped open all the pregnant women.

17In the thirty-ninth year of Azariah’s reign over Judah, Menahem son of Gadi became king of Israel, and he reigned in Samaria ten years.

18And he did evil in the sight of the LORD, and throughout his reign he did not turn away from the sins that Jeroboam son of Nebat had caused Israel to commit.

19Then Pul king of Assyria invaded the land, and Menahem gave Pul a thousand talents of silver in order to gain his support and strengthen his own grip on the kingdom.

20Menahem exacted this money from each of the wealthy men of Israel—fifty shekels of silver from each man—to give to the king of Assyria. So the king of Assyria withdrew and did not remain in the land.

21As for the rest of the acts of Menahem, along with all his accomplishments, are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Israel?

22And Menahem rested with his fathers, and his son Pekahiah reigned in his place.

23In the fiftieth year of Azariah’s reign over Judah, Pekahiah son of Menahem became king of Israel and reigned in Samaria two years.

24And he did evil in the sight of the LORD and did not turn away from the sins that Jeroboam son of Nebat had caused Israel to commit.

25Then his officer, Pekah son of Remaliah, conspired against him along with Argob, Arieh, and fifty men of Gilead. And at the citadel of the king’s palace in Samaria, Pekah struck down and killed Pekahiah and reigned in his place.

26As for the rest of the acts of Pekahiah, along with all his accomplishments, they are indeed written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Israel.

27In the fifty-second year of Azariah’s reign over Judah, Pekah son of Remaliah became king of Israel, and he reigned in Samaria twenty years.

28And he did evil in the sight of the LORD and did not turn away from the sins that Jeroboam son of Nebat had caused Israel to commit.

29In the days of Pekah king of Israel, Tiglath-pileser king of Assyria came and captured Ijon, Abel-beth-maacah, Janoah, Kedesh, Hazor, Gilead, and Galilee, including all the land of Naphtali, and he took the people as captives to Assyria.

30Then Hoshea son of Elah led a conspiracy against Pekah son of Remaliah. In the twentieth year of Jotham son of Uzziah, Hoshea attacked Pekah, killed him, and reigned in his place.

31As for the rest of the acts of Pekah, along with all his accomplishments, they are indeed written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Israel.

32In the second year of the reign of Pekah son of Remaliah over Israel, Jotham son of Uzziah became king of Judah.

33He was twenty-five years old when he became king, and he reigned in Jerusalem sixteen years. His mother’s name was Jerusha daughter of Zadok.

34And he did what was right in the eyes of the LORD, just as his father Uzziah had done.

35Nevertheless, the high places were not taken away; the people continued sacrificing and burning incense there. Jotham rebuilt the Upper Gate of the house of the LORD.

36As for the rest of the acts of Jotham, along with his accomplishments, are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Judah?

37(In those days the LORD began to send Rezin king of Aram and Pekah son of Remaliah against Judah.)

38And Jotham rested with his fathers and was buried with them in the City of David his father. And his son Ahaz reigned in his place.

1In the twenty and seventh year of Jeroboam king of Israel began Azariah son of Amaziah king of Judah to reign.

2Sixteen years old was he when he began to reign, and he reigned two and fifty years in Jerusalem. And his mother’s name was Jecholiah of Jerusalem.

3And he did that which was right in the sight of the LORD, according to all that his father Amaziah had done;

4Save that the high places were not removed: the people sacrificed and burnt incense still on the high places.

5And the LORD smote the king, so that he was a leper unto the day of his death, and dwelt in a several house. And Jotham the king’s son was over the house, judging the people of the land.

6And the rest of the acts of Azariah, and all that he did, are they not written in the book of the chronicles of the kings of Judah?

7So Azariah slept with his fathers; and they buried him with his fathers in the city of David: and Jotham his son reigned in his stead.

8In the thirty and eighth year of Azariah king of Judah did Zachariah the son of Jeroboam reign over Israel in Samaria six months.

9And he did that which was evil in the sight of the LORD, as his fathers had done: he departed not from the sins of Jeroboam the son of Nebat, who made Israel to sin.

10And Shallum the son of Jabesh conspired against him, and smote him before the people, and slew him, and reigned in his stead.

11And the rest of the acts of Zachariah, behold, they are written in the book of the chronicles of the kings of Israel.

12This was the word of the LORD which he spake unto Jehu, saying, Thy sons shall sit on the throne of Israel unto the fourth generation. And so it came to pass.

13Shallum the son of Jabesh began to reign in the nine and thirtieth year of Uzziah king of Judah; and he reigned a full month in Samaria.

14For Menahem the son of Gadi went up from Tirzah, and came to Samaria, and smote Shallum the son of Jabesh in Samaria, and slew him, and reigned in his stead.

15And the rest of the acts of Shallum, and his conspiracy which he made, behold, they are written in the book of the chronicles of the kings of Israel.

16Then Menahem smote Tiphsah, and all that were therein, and the coasts thereof from Tirzah: because they opened not to him, therefore he smote it; and all the women therein that were with child he ripped up.

17In the nine and thirtieth year of Azariah king of Judah began Menahem the son of Gadi to reign over Israel, and reigned ten years in Samaria.

18And he did that which was evil in the sight of the LORD: he departed not all his days from the sins of Jeroboam the son of Nebat, who made Israel to sin.

19And Pul the king of Assyria came against the land: and Menahem gave Pul a thousand talents of silver, that his hand might be with him to confirm the kingdom in his hand.

20And Menahem exacted the money of Israel, even of all the mighty men of wealth, of each man fifty shekels of silver, to give to the king of Assyria. So the king of Assyria turned back, and stayed not there in the land.

21And the rest of the acts of Menahem, and all that he did, are they not written in the book of the chronicles of the kings of Israel?

22And Menahem slept with his fathers; and Pekahiah his son reigned in his stead.

23In the fiftieth year of Azariah king of Judah Pekahiah the son of Menahem began to reign over Israel in Samaria, and reigned two years.

24And he did that which was evil in the sight of the LORD: he departed not from the sins of Jeroboam the son of Nebat, who made Israel to sin.

25But Pekah the son of Remaliah, a captain of his, conspired against him, and smote him in Samaria, in the palace of the king’s house, with Argob and Arieh, and with him fifty men of the Gileadites: and he killed him, and reigned in his room.

26And the rest of the acts of Pekahiah, and all that he did, behold, they are written in the book of the chronicles of the kings of Israel.

27In the two and fiftieth year of Azariah king of Judah Pekah the son of Remaliah began to reign over Israel in Samaria, and reigned twenty years.

28And he did that which was evil in the sight of the LORD: he departed not from the sins of Jeroboam the son of Nebat, who made Israel to sin.

29In the days of Pekah king of Israel came Tiglath-pileser king of Assyria, and took Ijon, and Abel-beth-maachah, and Janoah, and Kedesh, and Hazor, and Gilead, and Galilee, all the land of Naphtali, and carried them captive to Assyria.

30And Hoshea the son of Elah made a conspiracy against Pekah the son of Remaliah, and smote him, and slew him, and reigned in his stead, in the twentieth year of Jotham the son of Uzziah.

31And the rest of the acts of Pekah, and all that he did, behold, they are written in the book of the chronicles of the kings of Israel.

32In the second year of Pekah the son of Remaliah king of Israel began Jotham the son of Uzziah king of Judah to reign.

33Five and twenty years old was he when he began to reign, and he reigned sixteen years in Jerusalem. And his mother’s name was Jerusha, the daughter of Zadok.

34And he did that which was right in the sight of the LORD: he did according to all that his father Uzziah had done.

35Howbeit the high places were not removed: the people sacrificed and burned incense still in the high places. He built the higher gate of the house of the LORD.

36Now the rest of the acts of Jotham, and all that he did, are they not written in the book of the chronicles of the kings of Judah?

37In those days the LORD began to send against Judah Rezin the king of Syria, and Pekah the son of Remaliah.

38And Jotham slept with his fathers, and was buried with his fathers in the city of David his father: and Ahaz his son reigned in his stead.

Summary

Azariah (Uzziah) becomes king of Judah and reigns fifty-two years, doing what is right yet leaving the high places standing; the LORD strikes him with leprosy, forcing his son Jotham to govern the household and judge the land (vv. 1-7). In the north, Israel spirals into instability: Zechariah, the last of Jehu's dynasty, is assassinated by Shallum after only six months, fulfilling God's word to Jehu, and Shallum is himself killed within a month by Menahem, whose brutal reign includes paying tribute to Pul of Assyria (vv. 8-22). Pekahiah succeeds his father Menahem but is murdered by his own captain Pekah, who seizes the throne and later loses Israelite territory to Tiglath-pileser of Assyria (vv. 23-31). The chapter closes in Judah with Jotham's generally faithful reign, though the high places remain and the LORD begins sending Rezin of Aram and Pekah of Israel against Judah (vv. 32-38).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Jeremiah or a prophetic school. Originally one book with 1 Kings. Covers approximately 250 years (850-586 BC) from Elijah's translation through the Babylonian exile.
Historical Context: Chapter 15 spans approximately fifty years (c. 790-740 BC) and records one of the most turbulent periods in Israel's history. While Judah enjoys relative stability under the long reigns of Azariah/Uzziah (52 years) and Jotham (16 years), the northern kingdom descends into political chaos. Five kings reign in rapid succession — Zechariah (6 months), Shallum (1 month), Menahem (10 years), Pekahiah (2 years), and Pekah (20 years) — with four of the five transitions accomplished by assassination. This instability fulfills the pattern warned about since Jeroboam I: a kingdom built on rebellion against God's anointed will be consumed by rebellion from within.
The most significant geopolitical development is the emergence of Assyria as an aggressive imperial power. Tiglath-pileser III (called "Pul" in v.19) begins the systematic conquest of the region. His invasion during Menahem's reign marks the beginning of the end for Israel. Within a generation, the northern kingdom will cease to exist. The prophets Isaiah, Hosea, Amos, and Micah all ministered during this period, warning both kingdoms of the approaching judgment. The chapter reads like a countdown clock — each king bringing Israel closer to the catastrophe of 722 BC.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: A long and mostly faithful reign remembered chiefly for a leprosy contracted the moment a king tried to burn incense that belonged only to the priests, four generations promised to Jehu's dynasty fulfilled exactly and no further, five kings in rapid succession and four of five transitions accomplished by assassination, a brutality that rips open pregnant women in a city that only refused to open its gates, and storm clouds already gathering over Jotham's otherwise stable and faithful Judah
Azariah/Uzziah of Judah (vv.1-7): Azariah (also called Uzziah) reigned fifty-two years — one of Judah's longest reigns. He "did that which was right in the sight of the LORD" (v.3), yet the high places remained. His most notable feature in this account is his leprosy: "the LORD smote the king, so that he was a leper unto the day of his death" (v.5). Second Chronicles 26:16-21 explains the cause — Uzziah entered the Temple to burn incense, usurping the priestly role, and was struck with leprosy on the spot. He lived in a "several house" (a separate/quarantined dwelling) while his son Jotham governed. Isaiah's prophetic call came "in the year that king Uzziah died" (Isaiah 6:1) — the earthly king's death revealed the heavenly King's glory. Uzziah's leprosy is a sobering reminder that even long, successful reigns can end in divine judgment when pride crosses sacred boundaries.
The Collapse of Israel's Monarchy (vv.8-31): The northern kingdom disintegrates in a cascade of violence. Zechariah, the last of Jehu's dynasty, reigns only six months before Shallum assassinates him — fulfilling God's promise to Jehu that his sons would sit on the throne "unto the fourth generation" (v.12; cf. 2 Kings 10:30). God's word is precise: four generations, no more. Shallum himself lasts only one month before Menahem kills him. Menahem's brutality is shocking — he rips open pregnant women at Tiphsah (v.16) because the city refused to open its gates. His reign is characterized by violence and vassalage: he pays Tiglath-pileser a thousand talents of silver (approximately 37 tons) to secure his throne, taxing Israel's wealthy fifty shekels each. Pekahiah inherits his father's compromised throne for two years before his own captain, Pekah, murders him. Under Pekah, Tiglath-pileser conquers the northern territories — Ijon, Abel-beth-maachah, Hazor, Gilead, Galilee, and all Naphtali (v.29). This is the first deportation of Israelites to Assyria. Finally, Hoshea assassinates Pekah — the last king of Israel, who will preside over the nation's final fall.
Jotham of Judah (vv.32-38): In stark contrast to Israel's chaos, Judah under Jotham is stable and faithful. He "did that which was right in the sight of the LORD" (v.34), built the upper gate of the Temple, and reigned sixteen years. Yet even Judah faces gathering threats: "In those days the LORD began to send against Judah Rezin the king of Syria, and Pekah the son of Remaliah" (v.37). This is the beginning of the Syro-Ephraimite crisis that will dominate chapter 16 and Isaiah 7. The storm clouds are forming even over faithful Judah.

Setting: The middle of the eighth century BC, roughly 790–732 BC. In the south, Judah enjoys a long, stable reign under Azariah (also called Uzziah). In the north, Israel has just come through its richest years under Jeroboam II (793–753 BC), the era of Amos and Hosea. Chapter 14 ended with Jeroboam's death. Chapter 15 then races through the collapse that follows: five kings of Israel in about twenty years, four of them murdered, and the Assyrian empire waking up under Tiglath-pileser III. It reads like a fast-moving list, and that is the point. The writer pairs Judah's two long, steady reigns at the start and end of the chapter with Israel's chaos in between. Hosea's words describe it exactly: "They made kings, but not through me" (Hos 8:4); "all their kings have fallen" (Hos 7:7).

Azariah Reigns in Judah (vv1–7)

  • Two names (v1). He is Azariah here and in vv6–8, 17, 23 and 27, and Uzziah in vv13, 30, 32 and 34, as in Chronicles, Isaiah and Amos. Azariah means "the LORD has helped" and Uzziah "the LORD is my strength." The two names are close in sound in Hebrew, and kings often had a personal name and a throne name.
  • "In the twenty-seventh year of Jeroboam… sixteen years old… fifty-two years" (vv1–2). The dates in Kings are notoriously hard to fit together because reigns overlapped. Most scholars think Azariah was co-regent with his father Amaziah for many years, after Amaziah's humiliating defeat by Israel (14:8–14), and that the fifty-two years count from the start of that co-regency, about 792 to 740 BC. His long reign was the high point of Judah's power since Solomon.
  • "He did what was right in the eyes of the LORD… Nevertheless, the high places were not taken away" (vv3–4). The same formula was used of his father and grandfather (12:2–3; 14:3–4). The "high places" were local hilltop shrines. Some were used for worship of the LORD outside Jerusalem, which the law forbade (Deut 12:5–14), and many slid into Canaanite practice. A good king left them alone. No king of Judah removed them until Hezekiah (18:4).
  • "The LORD touched the king, so that he was a leper" (v5). Kings gives no reason. Chronicles fills it in: at the height of his success Uzziah became proud and went into the temple to burn incense, a duty reserved for the priests, and leprosy broke out on his forehead while he raged at them (2 Chr 26:16–21). The Hebrew word for leprosy covered a range of skin diseases, not only what we call leprosy today. Whatever it was, it made him unclean, and the law required such a person to live "outside the camp" (Lev 13:46).
  • "He lived in a separate house" (v5). Literally "a house of freedom," probably meaning a house where he was released from his public duties. He could not enter the temple or hold court. "Jotham the king's son was over the household, governing the people of the land": his son served as regent, probably from about 750 BC. Assyrian records of Tiglath-pileser's campaign around 743–738 BC may mention an "Azriyau," whom some scholars identify with Azariah leading a western coalition, though others doubt it.
  • "They buried him with his fathers in the city of David" (v7). Chronicles adds that he was buried "in the burial field that belonged to the kings, for they said, 'He is a leper'" (2 Chr 26:23), near the royal tombs but not in them. In 1931 a stone plaque from around the first century AD was found on the Mount of Olives, in Aramaic: "Here were brought the bones of Uzziah, king of Judah. Do not open." His bones had evidently been moved, perhaps during later building work, and the memory of his leprosy still marked his grave centuries later.
  • The year King Uzziah died. Isaiah dates his great vision of the LORD "in the year that King Uzziah died," about 740 BC (Isa 6:1). The proud king who forced his way into the holy place died a leper, and in the same year Isaiah saw the true King on his throne in the temple, and cried, "Woe is me! For I am lost; for I am a man of unclean lips." Amos also dated his prophecy "in the days of Uzziah," two years before a remembered earthquake (Amos 1:1; Zech 14:5).
  • Commentators: Henry: "It was happy for the kingdom that a good reign was a long one," and he judges that Uzziah "did for the most part that which was right." Guzik uses Chronicles to show how success bred pride, and he connects the king's sad end to Isaiah's call: a great earthly king fell, and Isaiah saw the greater King. JFB thinks Judah's throne may have been empty for some years after Amaziah's death and that "a regency was appointed during Azariah's minority," a different way of solving the chronology from the co-regency most modern scholars prefer.

Zechariah Reigns in Israel (vv8–12)

  • "Zechariah the son of Jeroboam reigned over Israel in Samaria six months" (v8). After forty-one years of his father's strong rule, the son lasted half a year. The northern kingdom had no fixed dynasty in the way Judah had David's line, and power belonged to whoever could seize it.
  • "He did not depart from the sins of Jeroboam the son of Nebat" (v9). This refrain appears for every northern king. Jeroboam son of Nebat, the first king of the north, set up golden calves at Bethel and Dan to keep his people from going to Jerusalem (1 Kgs 12:26–33). Every king after him kept them.
  • "Struck him down at Ibleam" (v10). The ESV follows the Greek here. The Hebrew reads "before the people," meaning he was murdered in public. If Ibleam is right, the irony is grim: Ibleam was where Jehu, Zechariah's great-great-grandfather, had Ahaziah of Judah killed (9:27). Hosea had prophesied, "I will punish the house of Jehu for the blood of Jezreel" (Hos 1:4).
  • "Your sons shall sit on the throne of Israel to the fourth generation" (v12). God promised Jehu this for wiping out Ahab's house (10:30). Jehu, Jehoahaz, Jehoash, Jeroboam II and Zechariah make four generations after Jehu, and the promise was kept to the letter. The writer pauses to point it out: God's word stands, both in blessing and in its limits. Jehu's dynasty was the longest in the north, about ninety years. After it, the kingdom lasted only about thirty more.
  • Commentators: Guzik and Henry both stress the fulfilment of God's word to Jehu. Guzik, citing Trapp, notes that God keeps his promises even to men who were far from godly. JFB connects Zechariah's continued calf worship to the end of the dynasty foretold to Jehu.

Shallum Reigns in Israel (vv13–16)

  • "Shallum the son of Jabesh… reigned one month in Samaria" (v13). "Son of Jabesh" may mean his father's name, or that he came from Jabesh-gilead, east of the Jordan. Gilead men keep appearing in these coups (v25), and Hosea called Gilead "a city of evildoers, tracked with blood" (Hos 6:8).
  • "Menahem the son of Gadi came up from Tirzah" (v14). Tirzah was the old capital of the north before Omri built Samaria (1 Kgs 16:23–24). Menahem was probably a military commander based there. The man who killed to take the throne was killed after one month.
  • "Menahem sacked Tiphsah… and he ripped open all the women in it who were pregnant" (v16). The best-known Tiphsah was far away on the Euphrates (1 Kgs 4:24), which hardly fits. Some Greek manuscripts read "Tappuah," a town in the hills of Ephraim near Tirzah, which most scholars think is the place meant. The town refused to recognise him, so he made an example of it. Ripping open pregnant women was a horror of ancient war that the prophets condemned in foreign nations (2 Kgs 8:12; Amos 1:13). Here an Israelite king does it to Israelites. Hosea warned the same would be done to Samaria (Hos 13:16).
  • Commentators: Guzik notes that such atrocities were common among Israel's neighbours, which makes it worse that Israel's own king copied them. JFB places the attack on the town for "refusing to open their gates to him." Henry sums up these verses: "This history shows Israel in confusion."

Menahem Reigns in Israel (vv17–22)

  • "He reigned ten years in Samaria" (v17). About 752–742 BC. Menahem is the only king in the chapter who died a natural death and passed the throne to his son.
  • "Pul the king of Assyria came against the land" (v19). Pul is Tiglath-pileser III (744–727 BC), who used the name Pulu as king of Babylon. The two names appear side by side in 1 Chronicles 5:26. He was the founder of Assyria's great age of conquest. He introduced a standing army and the policy of deporting conquered peoples and resettling them elsewhere, which is how Israel would eventually disappear (17:6).
  • "Menahem gave Pul a thousand talents of silver, that he might help him to confirm his hold on the royal power" (v19). A talent weighed about 75 pounds (34 kg), so this was over 30 tons of silver. Menahem was not only buying off an invader; he was buying Assyria's support to keep his shaky throne. Tiglath-pileser's own annals list "Menihimme of Samaria" among the kings who paid him tribute, one of the clearest links between the Bible and Assyrian records.
  • "Fifty shekels of silver from every man" (v20). A shekel was about 0.4 oz (11 g). At fifty shekels each, a thousand talents needed roughly 60,000 wealthy landowners. That gives a sense of the prosperous class Amos condemned for their ivory beds and stolen land (Amos 6:4; Isa 5:8). Some scholars note that fifty shekels was about the price of a slave in Assyrian records. "So the king of Assyria turned back": for now. Hosea saw this as the folly it was: "Ephraim went to Assyria… but he is not able to cure you" (Hos 5:13; 8:9).
  • Commentators: JFB reconciles this with Hosea's charge that Ephraim sought help from Assyria. Guzik sees it as the beginning of Israel's dependence on Assyria and, citing Knapp, links the tax on the rich to the injustice the prophets denounced. The Tyndale notes put the silver at about thirty-seven tons.

Pekahiah Reigns in Israel (vv23–26)

  • "Pekahiah the son of Menahem… reigned two years" (v23). About 742–740 BC. Same evil, same refrain.
  • "Pekah the son of Remaliah, his captain, conspired against him with fifty men of the people of Gilead" (v25). "Captain" (shalish) was a high officer, probably the king's personal aide, the third man in a chariot. Pekah came from Gilead and had Gileadite backing. He killed the king inside the palace citadel. "With Argob and Arieh": the Hebrew is unclear whether these were the king's supporters killed with him or Pekah's accomplices. JFB thinks they were the king's friends. Some scholars think the names are misplaced from the place names of v29.

Pekah Reigns in Israel (vv27–31)

  • "He reigned twenty years" (v27). Pekah's actual sole reign in Samaria was only about 740–732 BC. Many scholars think the twenty years count from about 752 BC, when Pekah may have set up a rival government in Gilead while Menahem ruled in Samaria. Hosea may allude to the divided kingdom when he speaks of "Israel and Ephraim" stumbling (Hos 5:5).
  • "Tiglath-pileser king of Assyria came and captured Ijon, Abel-beth-maacah, Janoah, Kedesh, Hazor, Gilead, and Galilee, all the land of Naphtali" (v29). This was the campaign of 734–732 BC. Pekah had joined Rezin of Damascus in an alliance against Assyria and tried to force Judah to join (16:5; Isa 7:1–9). Assyria answered by stripping Israel of its north (Galilee) and east (Gilead). The towns listed run from the far north southward, the invasion route. "He carried the people captive to Assyria": the first major deportation of Israelites. Assyrian records confirm it.
  • Galilee of the nations. Isaiah wrote of this very disaster: "In the former time he brought into contempt the land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali, but in the latter time he has made glorious the way of the sea… Galilee of the nations. The people who walked in darkness have seen a great light" (Isa 9:1–2). The first region of Israel to go into darkness would be the first to see the light. Matthew quotes it when Jesus begins preaching in Galilee (Matt 4:12–16).
  • "Hoshea the son of Elah made a conspiracy against Pekah… and reigned in his place" (v30). About 732 BC. Tiglath-pileser claimed in his annals: "Pekah their king they deposed and I placed Hoshea as king over them." Hoshea would be Israel's last king (ch 17). "The twentieth year of Jotham" counts from Jotham's co-regency with his father.
  • Commentators: JFB notes that Tiglath-pileser gives no genealogy in his records, which he takes as a sign that he was a usurper, and points to his campaigns in the Assyrian annals. Guzik sees v29 as the step into vassal status, and, citing Knapp, recalls Pekah's slaughter of the men of Judah (2 Chr 28:6), violence that came back on his own head. Henry contrasts these kings with Judah's: "The imperfections of true believers are very different from the allowed wickedness of ungodly men."

Jotham Reigns in Judah (vv32–38)

  • "Jotham the son of Uzziah… was twenty-five years old… and he reigned sixteen years" (vv32–33). About 750–735 BC, counting from the start of his regency, with a further overlap with his son Ahaz. "Jerusha the daughter of Zadok": possibly from the high priestly family, which might explain Jotham's respect for the temple.
  • "He did what was right… according to all that his father Uzziah had done" (v34). Chronicles adds a careful exception: "except he did not enter the temple of the LORD" (2 Chr 27:2). He learned from his father's sin. It also says "the people still followed corrupt practices," and that Jotham "became mighty, because he ordered his ways before the LORD his God" (2 Chr 27:2, 6).
  • "He built the upper gate of the house of the LORD" (v35). This was probably a gate on the north side of the temple court (Jer 20:2; Ezek 9:2), perhaps linking the temple and palace areas. Chronicles adds building on the wall of Ophel, cities and forts in Judah, and victory over the Ammonites (2 Chr 27:3–5).
  • "In those days the LORD began to send Rezin the king of Syria and Pekah… against Judah" (v37). The threat that will dominate Ahaz's reign (ch 16; Isa 7) begins under Jotham. The writer says plainly that "the LORD began to send" them. Even the schemes of foreign kings are under his hand, and this was discipline, because Judah, like Israel, kept its high places.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Jotham showed great respect to the temple." Guzik, citing Knapp, notes that a gate between palace and temple was better protection than any wall around the kingdom, and stresses that Jotham's strength came from keeping his life in order before God. JFB takes the upper gate as a court gate, not the temple building, and v37 as the first sign of the Israel–Syria alliance that broke into open war only under Ahaz.

Pointing to Christ

The chapter is a parade of kings who could not save: a good king struck with leprosy for grasping at the priesthood, and five northern kings who took the throne by blood and lost it the same way. The year the leper king died, Isaiah saw "the King, the LORD of hosts" (Isa 6:5). Jesus is the King who is also rightly a priest (Heb 7:1–3, 26), who did not grasp at what was not his but "emptied himself" (Phil 2:6–7), and who touched lepers and made them clean (Mark 1:40–42). And the region Tiglath-pileser emptied first, "Galilee of the nations," is where that King began to preach, the great light dawning on people who sat in darkness.

Guzik also highlights that Uzziah's leprosy shows God takes holiness seriously even with otherwise faithful servants.

Reflection

  • Presuming on God's Space (v.5) — Despite a long, faithful reign, Azariah was struck with leprosy for stepping into ground reserved for the priesthood (2 Chronicles 26 explains why). Does years of experience with God ever tempt you to redraw His boundaries on your own terms?
  • God's Word Fulfilled to the Letter (v.12) — Zechariah's assassination fulfilled, exactly as promised generations earlier, that Jehu's line would rule only four generations. Do you trust that what God has spoken will come to pass precisely as He said, in His timing?
  • Violence Escalating Unchecked (v.16) — Menahem's brutal conquest of Tiphsah shows how quickly a nation without restraint descends into savagery. What happens in a life, a family, or a culture when there's no higher authority left to answer to?
  • Buying Peace Instead of Facing It (vv.19-20) — Menahem paid off Assyria to secure his throne, but the tribute only delayed a far bigger reckoning. Where are you purchasing short-term relief instead of dealing honestly with a problem?
  • Pressure Even on the Faithful (v.37) — Even under Jotham's obedient rule, God allowed enemies to begin pressing against Judah. When trouble rises despite your faithfulness, can you consider that God might be preparing you rather than punishing you?
