1When Jesus had finished saying these things, He left Galilee and went into the region of Judea beyond the Jordan.

2Large crowds followed Him, and He healed them there.

3Then some Pharisees came and tested Him by asking, “Is it lawful for a man to divorce his wife for any reason?”

4Jesus answered, “Have you not read that from the beginning the Creator ‘made them male and female,’

5and said, ‘For this reason a man will leave his father and mother and be united to his wife, and the two will become one flesh’?

6So they are no longer two, but one flesh. Therefore what God has joined together, let man not separate.”

7“Why then,” they asked, “did Moses order a man to give his wife a certificate of divorce and send her away?”

8Jesus replied, “Moses permitted you to divorce your wives because of your hardness of heart. But it was not this way from the beginning.

9Now I tell you that whoever divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, and marries another woman commits adultery.”

10His disciples said to Him, “If this is the case between a man and his wife, it is better not to marry.”

11“Not everyone can accept this word,” He replied, “but only those to whom it has been given.

12For there are eunuchs who were born that way; others were made that way by men; and still others live like eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven. The one who can accept this should accept it.”

13Then little children were brought to Jesus for Him to place His hands on them and pray for them. And the disciples rebuked those who brought them.

14But Jesus said, “Let the little children come to Me, and do not hinder them! For the kingdom of heaven belongs to such as these.”

15And after He had placed His hands on them, He went on from there.

16Just then a man came up to Jesus and inquired, “Teacher, what good thing must I do to obtain eternal life?”

17“Why do you ask Me about what is good?” Jesus replied. “There is only One who is good. If you want to enter life, keep the commandments.”

18“Which ones?” the man asked. Jesus answered, “‘Do not murder, do not commit adultery, do not steal, do not bear false witness,

19honor your father and mother, and love your neighbor as yourself.’”

20“All these I have kept,” said the young man. “What do I still lack?”

21Jesus told him, “If you want to be perfect, go, sell your possessions and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. Then come, follow Me.”

22When the young man heard this, he went away in sorrow, because he had great wealth.

23Then Jesus said to His disciples, “Truly I tell you, it is hard for a rich man to enter the kingdom of heaven.

24Again I tell you, it is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of God.”

25When the disciples heard this, they were greatly astonished and asked, “Who then can be saved?”

26Jesus looked at them and said, “With man this is impossible, but with God all things are possible.”

27“Look,” Peter replied, “we have left everything to follow You. What then will there be for us?”

28Jesus said to them, “Truly I tell you, in the renewal of all things, when the Son of Man sits on His glorious throne, you who have followed Me will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel.

29And everyone who has left houses or brothers or sisters or father or mother or wife or children or fields for the sake of My name will receive a hundredfold and will inherit eternal life.

30But many who are first will be last, and the last will be first.

Summary

Pharisees test Jesus by asking about divorce, and he responds that God's original design joined man and woman as one flesh and that Moses permitted divorce only because of hardness of heart, adding that divorcing one's wife except for sexual immorality and remarrying commits adultery (vv. 1-9). When the disciples react that it is better not to marry, Jesus speaks of those who remain single for the kingdom's sake (vv. 10-12). He welcomes little children brought to him despite the disciples' objections, saying the kingdom of heaven belongs to such as these (vv. 13-15). A rich young man asks what he must do to inherit eternal life, and when told to keep the commandments and then to sell his possessions and follow Jesus, he goes away sorrowful because of his great wealth, prompting Jesus to say it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of God (vv. 16-24). When Peter asks what the disciples will receive for having left everything, Jesus promises them thrones in the renewed world and assures that many who are first will be last, and the last first (vv. 25-30).

Authorship & Background

Author: Matthew (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 19 marks Jesus' departure from Galilee toward Jerusalem — the final journey to the cross. It contains three major encounters: the Pharisees' question about divorce (vv.3-12), Jesus blessing the children (vv.13-15), and the rich young ruler (vv.16-30).
The Divorce Question: The Pharisees were testing Jesus by asking Him to take sides in a rabbinic debate. The school of Shammai allowed divorce only for sexual immorality; the school of Hillel allowed it for almost any reason (including burning dinner). Jesus goes back to Genesis — to God's original design — and declares that marriage is permanent by divine intent.
The Rich Young Ruler: A man who had everything the world values — youth, wealth, moral uprightness, and religious devotion. Yet he lacked the one thing necessary: willingness to surrender everything to follow Jesus. He is the only person in the Gospels who came to Jesus and walked away sad.
Classification: Gospel — Narrative
Key Themes: a test question about divorce answered by going behind Moses to Genesis itself, what therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder, Moses' provision named a concession to hardness of heart rather than a permission, disciples rebuking people who bring children to Jesus for the second time in three chapters and being corrected again, a rich young man who alone in the Gospels asks what lack I yet and alone walks away sorrowful for his great possessions, and an answer that removes the whole matter from human capacity entirely, with God all things are possible
What Therefore God Hath Joined (vv.1-12): The question is put as a test and in the terms of a live rabbinic dispute, is it lawful for a man to put away his wife for every cause? The answer goes behind Moses to Genesis, quoting both creation accounts, and draws the conclusion, what therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder. Moses' provision is described as a concession to hardness of heart rather than a permission. The exception Matthew records is fornication. The disciples' reaction is telling, if it be so, it is not good to marry, and the reply neither denies nor generalizes it, all men cannot receive this saying, he that is able to receive it, let him receive it.
Suffer the Little Children (vv.13-15): Three verses, and the disciples are wrong twice in three chapters about the same group. They rebuke the people bringing children, and the correction is short, suffer little children, and forbid them not, to come unto me, with the reason given as a statement about the kingdom rather than about children, for of such is the kingdom of heaven. He laid his hands on them, and departed thence.
The Rich Young Man (vv.16-30): Matthew alone tells us he was young, and alone records the exchange about which commandments, and alone has the questioner ask what lack I yet? The instruction is total, go and sell that thou hast, and give to the poor, and Matthew's note on the outcome is one of the saddest sentences in the Gospel, he went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions. Then the camel and the needle's eye, the disciples' astonishment, and the answer that removes the matter from human capacity, with God all things are possible. Peter asks what they will get, and the reply promises thrones and then immediately unsettles it, but many that are first shall be last, and the last shall be first.

Deep Dive

Setting: Early in AD 30, a few months before the crucifixion. Jesus has finished his teaching on life in the church (ch 18) and now leaves Galilee for the last time. He travels south through "Judea beyond the Jordan," the region east of the river usually called Perea, on the way to Jerusalem (20:17; 21:1). Perea was ruled by Herod Antipas, the same ruler who had imprisoned and beheaded John the Baptist over his marriage to his brother's wife (14:3–4). That makes the first question of the chapter, about divorce, more dangerous than it looks. Chapters 19–20 form a journey section, and through it runs one theme: what the kingdom looks like for marriage, children, wealth and reward, and how often it turns human ideas of status upside down.

Teaching About Divorce (vv1–12)

  • "When Jesus had finished these sayings" (v1). This is the formula Matthew uses to close each of the five teaching blocks (7:28; 11:1; 13:53; 26:1). The story moves on toward Jerusalem.
  • "Is it lawful to divorce one's wife for any cause?" (v3). The debate turned on Deuteronomy 24:1, where a man may divorce his wife if he finds "some indecency" in her. The school of Rabbi Shammai read the phrase narrowly, as sexual sin or something close to it. The school of Rabbi Hillel read it broadly. The Mishnah records Hillel's followers allowing divorce if a wife spoiled a meal, and Rabbi Akiva later allowing it if a man found someone prettier (m. Gittin 9:10). "For any cause" was the Hillel position, and it was the more popular one. The question "tested" Jesus because any answer would offend someone, and in Antipas's territory a strict answer could be costly, as John had found.
  • "Have you not read…" (v4). Jesus goes behind Moses to the creation. He joins Genesis 1:27 ("male and female he created them") and Genesis 2:24 ("a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh"). Marriage is not a human arrangement that the law then regulates. It is God's design from the start.
  • "What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate" (v6). The verb "joined together" (syzeugnymi) means to yoke together, as two animals share one yoke. God is the one who joins, so no human being has authority to undo what he has done.
  • "Why then did Moses command…?" (v7). The Pharisees push back with the law itself. But Deuteronomy 24:1–4 does not command divorce. It assumes divorce was happening and regulates it. The certificate protected the woman, giving her proof that she was free to remarry and not an adulteress. The actual command in that passage is that a man may not take back a wife he has divorced after she has married someone else.
  • "Because of your hardness of heart Moses allowed you" (v8). Jesus corrects their word: Moses allowed, he did not command. "Hardness of heart" (sklērokardia) means stubborn resistance to God. The law made room for human sin to limit the damage it caused. It did not make divorce God's will. Malachi had already said, "the LORD the God of Israel… hates divorce" (Mal 2:16, many translations; the Hebrew is difficult, and the ESV reads it as "the man who does not love his wife but divorces her").
  • "Except for sexual immorality" (v9). The word is porneia, a broad term covering sexual sin of all kinds, including adultery. Matthew alone records this exception (here and 5:32). Mark and Luke give the saying without it (Mark 10:11–12; Luke 16:18), likely because in Jewish life adultery already ended a marriage as a matter of course and needed no mention, while Matthew, writing for Jewish believers, spells it out. Christians read this differently. Many Protestants hold that sexual unfaithfulness allows divorce and remarriage, and most add desertion by an unbelieving spouse from 1 Corinthians 7:15. The Roman Catholic Church holds that a valid marriage cannot be dissolved, and reads porneia as marriages that were unlawful from the start, such as within forbidden degrees of kinship. Some evangelicals take a similar "no remarriage" view. All agree that Jesus set the bar far higher than any rabbi of his day, and that faithful, lifelong marriage is God's design.
  • "It is better not to marry" (v10). The disciples' shock shows how strict Jesus' teaching sounded. Even the stricter school of Shammai allowed more than this.
  • "Not everyone can receive this saying, but only those to whom it is given" (v11). "This saying" most likely means the disciples' remark that it is better not to marry. Jesus agrees that some are called to singleness, but it is a gift given, not a rule for all.
  • "Eunuchs… from birth… made eunuchs by men… made themselves eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom" (v12). A eunuch was a castrated man, often a royal official (Acts 8:27). Jesus names three kinds: men born unable to marry or father children, men castrated by others, and those who give up marriage to serve God's kingdom. The third is figurative. It means choosing a single life for the kingdom, as Jesus himself did and Paul did (1 Cor 7:7–8, 32–35). The early theologian Origen is said to have taken this literally in his youth, which the church rejected. In a culture that expected every man to marry and father children, Jesus honors singleness as a real calling.
  • Commentators: JFB says Moses as civil lawgiver "tolerated a relaxation of the strictness of the marriage bond" for a hard-hearted people, "not as approving of it, but to prevent still greater evils." On v12 it describes those who "to do God's work better, deliberately choose this state," and adds simply, "Such was Paul." Guzik explains the Hillel and Shammai debate, and notes that Jesus refused to be drawn into rabbinic argument and went back to Genesis. He holds that porneia and an unbelieving spouse's desertion (1 Cor 7:15) are the only grounds for divorce, and that unhappiness or falling out of love are not. Henry urges that "the married state should be entered upon with great seriousness and earnest prayer."

Let the Children Come to Me (vv13–15)

  • "Children were brought to him that he might lay his hands on them and pray" (v13). Jewish parents customarily brought children to rabbis and elders for a blessing. The laying on of hands for blessing goes back to Jacob blessing Joseph's sons (Gen 48:14–15). Luke says some were infants (Luke 18:15).
  • "The disciples rebuked the people" (v13). They probably thought they were protecting a busy teacher on his way to Jerusalem from interruptions by people of no importance. They had just been told that whoever receives a child receives Jesus (18:5), and they have already forgotten. Mark adds that Jesus was "indignant" (Mark 10:14).
  • "Let the little children come to me… for to such belongs the kingdom of heaven" (v14). The kingdom belongs to those who come with nothing to offer and receive it as a gift (18:3–4). Placing this scene right before the rich young man is pointed. Children come with empty hands and are welcomed. The rich man comes with full hands and leaves sad.
  • Infant baptism. This passage is often read at infant baptisms and dedications. It does not mention baptism, and those who baptize infants and those who do not both draw on it: the first group stresses that Jesus welcomed even infants into his blessing, and the second that he blessed them without baptizing them. Both agree that Jesus treats children as people who matter to God.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Little children may be brought to Christ as needing, and being capable of receiving blessings from him." Guzik notes that children were drawn to Jesus, which says something about the kind of person he was. JFB defers to its notes on Luke 18.

The Rich Young Man (vv16–30)

  • "Behold, a man came up" (v16). Matthew calls him "young" (v20), Luke a "ruler" (Luke 18:18), and all three say he was rich. So he is often called the rich young ruler. Mark adds that he ran up and knelt, and that "Jesus, looking at him, loved him" (Mark 10:17, 21). He was earnest, not hostile.
  • "What good deed must I do to have eternal life?" (v16). He thinks of eternal life as something earned by an achievement, one more item to complete.
  • "Why do you ask me about what is good? There is only one who is good" (v17). Mark and Luke have "Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone." Matthew's wording differs a little, but the point is the same. Jesus turns the man's attention from good deeds to the good God. Some read this as Jesus denying his own goodness, but he is not. He is asking the man to think about what he is saying. If only God is good, and Jesus is good, then the man is standing before more than a teacher.
  • "If you would enter life, keep the commandments" (v17). Jesus does not mock the question. God's commands do describe the life that pleases him. But he is leading the man to see his need.
  • "Which ones?" (v18). Jesus lists five of the Ten Commandments from the "second table," the ones about other people (Exod 20:12–16), and adds Leviticus 19:18, "love your neighbor as yourself." Notably absent are the commands about God, and the last commandment, "you shall not covet."
  • "All these I have kept. What do I still lack?" (v20). He likely kept them outwardly, as Paul once did ("as to righteousness under the law, blameless," Phil 3:6). Yet he knows something is missing.
  • "If you would be perfect, go, sell what you possess and give to the poor… and come, follow me" (v21). "Perfect" (teleios) means complete or whole, the word Jesus used in 5:48. He puts his finger on the one thing the man loves more than God. The commandments he skipped were the ones the man was breaking: his wealth was his god, and he coveted it. The call is personal. Jesus did not tell every follower to sell everything; Zacchaeus gave half (Luke 19:8), and wealthy disciples like Joseph of Arimathea served him with their means (27:57). But he does call every follower to put nothing ahead of him (10:37–39).
  • "He went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions" (v22). He is one of the few people in the Gospels personally invited to follow Jesus, and the only one we are told turned and walked away. Jesus lets him go.
  • "Only with difficulty will a rich person enter" (v23). Riches are not sin, but they make it hard to feel need, and the kingdom is for the needy (5:3).
  • "Easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle" (v24). The camel was the largest animal in Palestine and the needle's eye the smallest opening. It is a deliberately impossible picture. The Babylonian Talmud uses a similar figure, an elephant through a needle's eye, for something that cannot happen. Two attempts to soften it have no good support. The idea of a small gate in Jerusalem called "the Needle's Eye," through which a camel could squeeze if unloaded, appears only from the medieval period on and has no ancient evidence. A few late manuscripts read kamilos, "rope," for kamēlos, "camel," a one-letter change, but that is a scribe's attempt to make the saying easier.
  • "Who then can be saved?" (v25). The disciples are "greatly astonished." Many Jews assumed wealth was a sign of God's blessing (Deut 28:1–14). If even the blessed rich cannot get in, who can?
  • "With man this is impossible, but with God all things are possible" (v26). That is the answer. No one, rich or poor, saves himself. Salvation is God's work, and he can change even a heart held by money. The words echo the angel's question to Sarah, "Is anything too hard for the LORD?" (Gen 18:14).
  • "See, we have left everything and followed you. What then will we have?" (v27). Peter sees the contrast with the rich man and asks, a little too eagerly, what they will get. Jesus does not scold him but answers generously, and then warns him in the next parable (20:1–16).
  • "In the new world" (v28). The Greek is palingenesia, "regeneration" or "rebirth." It appears only here and in Titus 3:5, where it is used of personal new birth. Here it means the renewal of all creation at the end, what Peter later calls "the time for restoring all the things" (Acts 3:21; compare Rev 21:5). The ESV's "new world" catches the sense.
  • "You who have followed me will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel" (v28). The twelve apostles stand for a renewed Israel. "Judging" may mean ruling, like the judges of the Old Testament, as well as giving verdicts. Luke places a similar promise at the Last Supper (Luke 22:28–30). Judas was among the twelve who heard it, a sobering reminder that hearing the promise is not the same as keeping faith.
  • "Will receive a hundredfold and will inherit eternal life" (v29). Mark adds two things: "now in this time… with persecutions," and "in the age to come eternal life" (Mark 10:30). The hundredfold is not a promise of wealth. Those who lose family and homes for Jesus gain a new family of brothers, sisters and mothers in the church (12:49–50), and homes opened to them across the world, along with suffering.
  • "Many who are first will be last, and the last first" (v30). The saying closes the section and opens the next, where it is repeated in reverse at the end of the parable of the workers (20:16). Those the world ranks first, like the rich young man, may be last. The children and the poor may be first. And disciples who start counting their rewards should take care.
  • Commentators: Henry writes that "Christ knew that covetousness was the sin which most easily beset this young man," and on v24, "It is utterly impossible for a man that sets his heart upon his riches, to get to heaven." Guzik agrees that Jesus listed the commands about neighbors and stopped short of the one the man was breaking, and stresses that the call to sell everything was aimed at this man's idol, not a rule for every Christian. He reads the camel saying as deliberately comic exaggeration of the impossible and lists Zacchaeus, Joseph of Arimathea and Barnabas as rich men who followed. He understands the hundredfold mainly as spiritual and relational gain, since no one literally gains a hundred mothers. JFB defers vv16–30 to its notes on Luke 18.

Pointing to Christ

The chapter is full of people who think they know how the kingdom works: Pharisees looking for loopholes, disciples shooing away children, a rich man ready to do one more good deed, and Peter keeping count. Jesus answers each of them by pointing to God: to what God joined at creation, to the God who alone is good, to the God for whom all things are possible. And he quietly stands in God's place, as the one children must come to and the one the rich man must follow. He who calls the rich to give everything was himself rich and "for your sake became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Cor 8:9). The camel goes through the needle's eye only because God does the impossible, and he did it through his Son.

Reflection

  • Marriage by God's Design (vv.4-6) — Jesus answers a trap question about divorce by pointing back to God's original design: one man and one woman, joined for life. Are you treating marriage — your own or others' — as permanent by God's intent, or as negotiable by human convenience?
  • Kingdom Belongs to the Childlike (v.14) — Jesus welcomes the children the disciples tried to turn away, saying the kingdom belongs to such as these. What childlike trust or humility have you lost that you need to recover?
  • The One Thing Withheld (v.22) — A man who kept every commandment walks away sorrowful because he cannot surrender his wealth to follow Jesus. What is the "one thing" Jesus would put His finger on in your life?
  • Salvation Is God's Work (v.26) — When the disciples wonder who can possibly be saved, Jesus says it is impossible for people but possible for God. Are you resting in what God has done, or still trying to earn your way?
  • The Kingdom's Reversal (v.30) — Jesus warns that many who are first will be last, and the last first. How does your definition of success line up with the kingdom's upside-down values?
