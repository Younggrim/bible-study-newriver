Summary

A wise son listens to his father's instruction, while a mocker refuses correction, and careful speech is repeatedly tied to a well-guarded life (vv. 1-3). The lazy person craves and gets nothing, while diligence brings prosperity, and wealth gained by dishonest shortcuts shrinks away while wealth earned through labor grows (vv. 4, 11). Hope delayed makes the heart sick, but a desire fulfilled is like a tree of life, and refusing correction leads to poverty and shame while welcoming it leads to honor (vv. 12-13, 18). Companionship matters: walking with the wise makes a person wise, while associating with fools brings harm (v. 20). The chapter closes on parental discipline, teaching that withholding correction from a child is a form of hatred, while loving discipline is applied early (v. 24).

Authorship & Background

Author: Primarily Solomon (1:1, 10:1, 25:1), with sections by Agur (ch.30) and King Lemuel (ch.31). Compiled over several centuries. Proverbs is wisdom literature — practical instruction for godly living. Hebrew title: 'Mishlei' — 'Proverbs/Comparisons.'
Historical Context: Chapter 13 continues the antithetical proverbs, with notable themes of speech and its consequences (vv.1-3), wealth and its proper acquisition (vv.7-8, 11, 22), the power of desire and hope (vv.4, 12, 19), discipline and parenting (v.24), and the influence of companions (v.20). This chapter contains some of the most quotable proverbs in the collection, including "hope deferred maketh the heart sick" (v.12) and "he that spareth his rod hateth his son" (v.24). The theme of generational wealth (v.22) connects individual wisdom to family legacy.
Themes in This Chapter: Speech and Its Consequences (vv.1-3, 5); Desire, Diligence, and Fulfillment (vv.4, 11-12, 19); The Influence of Companions (v.20); Wealth and Inheritance (vv.7-8, 11, 22); Discipline and Parenting (v.24)
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: a son who hears instruction set against a scorner who will not from the very first verse, two observations about money placed deliberately against each other, the ransom of a man's life are his riches, and there is that maketh himself rich, yet hath nothing, hope deferred maketh the heart sick, but when the desire cometh, it is a tree of life, offered as the chapter's most quoted line, a plain sentence on companionship, he that walketh with wise men shall be wise, but a companion of fools shall be destroyed, and a much-argued verse on discipline, he that spareth his rod hateth his son, closing on sufficiency rather than plenty
Words, Wealth and Discipline (vv.1-12): The block opens with a son hearing instruction and a scorner who will not, and runs through the keeping of the mouth, he that keepeth his mouth keepeth his life. It contains two observations about money that pull against each other on purpose, the ransom of a man's life are his riches, and there is that maketh himself rich, yet hath nothing. And it closes on the verse most often quoted from the chapter, hope deferred maketh the heart sick, but when the desire cometh, it is a tree of life.
Instruction, Company and the Rod (vv.13-25): The second half runs on who a person learns from. He that walketh with wise men shall be wise, but a companion of fools shall be destroyed, which is the chapter's plainest sentence. The block includes the much-argued verse on discipline, he that spareth his rod hateth his son, and a line about inheritance that reaches past one lifetime, a good man leaveth an inheritance to his children's children. The closing verse is about sufficiency rather than plenty, the righteous eateth to the satisfying of his soul.

Setting: The collection headed "The proverbs of Solomon" continues (10:1–22:16; see Proverbs 10 on how it was gathered and how to read a proverb). The ESV has no headings inside it, so the headings below simply group the sayings in order. Chapter 13 opens, like chapter 10, with a son and his father, and closes with a father and the rod. In between run familiar threads: guarding the mouth, the diligent and the lazy, money gained honestly or quickly. Two sayings about desire, hope deferred and desire fulfilled, sit at the center (vv12, 19). The chapter also shows how Proverbs balances itself: a saying that hard work brings plenty (v4) stands near one admitting that injustice can sweep away a poor man's harvest (v23).

Listening, Speaking and Working (vv1–6)

  • "A wise son hears his father's instruction, but a scoffer does not listen to rebuke" (v1). The first line has no verb in Hebrew, literally "a wise son, a father's discipline," as if the two belong together by nature. The scoffer (lets, as in 9:7–8) does not listen even to "rebuke," a stronger word than instruction. Guzik notes the point: the scoffer won't respond to any correction, however strong. The Tyndale notes say gaining wisdom requires discipline and correction.
  • "From the fruit of his mouth a man eats what is good, but the desire of the treacherous is for violence" (v2). Good words feed their speaker (12:14). The treacherous have an appetite too, but it is for violence, and Guzik observes that in the end they taste the violence they planned for others.
  • "Whoever guards his mouth preserves his life; he who opens wide his lips comes to ruin" (v3). "Guards" is the word for a watchman. Guzik compares the mouth to a city gate that needs a guard, and notes that people have two ears and one tongue. "Opens wide his lips" pictures someone who never closes them. JFB says loose talk can provoke others to violence. David prayed, "Set a guard, O LORD, over my mouth" (Ps 141:3), and James develops the same theme (Jas 3:1–12), as the Tyndale notes point out.
  • "The soul of the sluggard craves and gets nothing, while the soul of the diligent is richly supplied" (v4). "Soul" (nephesh) here means appetite, desire. The sluggard is full of wishes. "Richly supplied" is literally "made fat." Desire without effort stays desire (21:25).
  • "The righteous hates falsehood, but the wicked brings shame and disgrace" (v5). The second line can also be read "acts shamefully and disgracefully," or "makes himself a stench." The verb (baʾash) means "to stink," the word Jacob used: "you have made me stink to the inhabitants of the land" (Gen 34:30). Lying makes a person foul to others.
  • "Righteousness guards him whose way is blameless, but sin overthrows the wicked" (v6). Righteousness and sin are pictured almost as people: one a bodyguard, the other a wrecker. JFB calls it a common theme in Proverbs.
  • Commentators: Guzik and JFB both stress how speech protects or endangers a life (v3). Guzik adds the city-gate picture and the observation about ears and tongue, and the Tyndale notes connect James 3.

Wealth, Light and Hope (vv7–12)

  • "One pretends to be rich, yet has nothing; another pretends to be poor, yet has great wealth" (v7). The verbs mean "makes himself rich" and "makes himself poor." The saying can describe pretenders: one puts on a show of wealth, the other hides his money to avoid giving or taxes. JFB: "In opposite ways men act hypocritically." It can also be read as a deeper truth: some are rich in things and poor toward God, and some give everything away and are rich indeed (Luke 12:21; 2 Cor 6:10). Guzik takes the second reading and sees Jesus as the supreme example: he became poor so that others might become rich (2 Cor 8:9). The Tyndale notes: things are not always what they seem.
  • "The ransom of a man's life is his wealth, but a poor man hears no threat" (v8). A rich man may have to pay off a kidnapper, a blackmailer or a lawsuit. A poor man has nothing worth taking, so no one bothers to threaten him. Money solves some problems and creates others. Guzik offers this reading alongside JFB's, which reads the second line as "the poor man doesn't listen to rebuke" and so stays poor. The Hebrew word geʿarah usually means "rebuke," as in v1, so both readings are possible.
  • "The light of the righteous rejoices, but the lamp of the wicked will be put out" (v9). A lamp burning in a house was a sign that someone lived there and the family continued. To have one's lamp put out meant death or the end of the family line (20:20; 24:20; Job 18:5–6). "Rejoices" is unusual for a light. It pictures a bright, cheerful flame. JFB: the righteous shine lastingly, while the wicked's lamp is small and soon fails.
  • "By insolence comes nothing but strife, but with those who take advice is wisdom" (v10). "Insolence" is zadon, the pride of 11:2. Pride cannot take advice, so it ends up fighting. JFB: contention comes from self-conceit.
  • "Wealth gained hastily will dwindle, but whoever gathers little by little will increase it" (v11). The Hebrew reads "wealth from vanity," from hebel (breath, vapor, the "vanity" of Ecclesiastes): money from nothing, from schemes or fraud. The Greek and Latin read "wealth gained hastily," with a small change of letters, and the ESV follows them. Either way, the saying fits get-rich-quick schemes, gambling and fraud: what comes fast goes fast. "Little by little" is literally "by hand," earned by work. Guzik: God's blessing rests on honest labor. JFB: "laboriously."
  • "Hope deferred makes the heart sick, but a desire fulfilled is a tree of life" (v12). One of the most human sayings in the book. "Deferred" means drawn out, delayed. Waiting long for something good wears the heart down, and its fulfillment brings new life. The "tree of life" is the third of four uses in Proverbs (3:18; 11:30; 15:4). Abraham and Sarah waited twenty-five years for Isaac. Hannah wept for years before Samuel (1 Sam 1:7–8). Guzik suggests Christians should help others see good hopes fulfilled where they can. JFB: fulfillment is a "cause of happiness."
  • Commentators: Guzik and JFB take v7 differently: JFB reads hypocrisy for gain, and Guzik reads true and false riches, with Christ as the example. They also differ on v8, and Guzik gives both readings. On v11 both see honest, steady labor as what lasts.

The Word, Good Sense and Faithful Messengers (vv13–19)

  • "Whoever despises the word brings destruction on himself, but he who reveres the commandment will be rewarded" (v13). "The word" and "the commandment" may mean the father's teaching, but the terms naturally point to God's word. Guzik takes them as Scripture. "Brings destruction on himself" may mean he becomes bound by a pledge, owing a debt he must pay.
  • "The teaching of the wise is a fountain of life, that one may turn away from the snares of death" (v14). Repeated in 14:27, where "the fear of the LORD" takes the place of "the teaching of the wise." Good teaching is a spring of water in a dry land and a warning sign beside a hidden trap.
  • "Good sense wins favor, but the way of the treacherous is their ruin" (v15). The second line in Hebrew reads "the way of the treacherous is ʾetan," which normally means "enduring" or "perennial," like an ever-flowing stream (Deut 21:4). The KJV renders it "hard," a rough, rocky road. The ESV follows the Greek translation ("their ruin"). Either way, the treacherous make life harder for themselves. Guzik notes that sinners often struggle more than the righteous, though the righteous bear their own costs.
  • "Every prudent man acts with knowledge, but a fool flaunts his folly" (v16). "Flaunts" is literally "spreads out," like a merchant laying out his goods. The fool puts his foolishness on display.
  • "A wicked messenger falls into trouble, but a faithful envoy brings healing" (v17). The Tyndale notes explain that most messages were carried by word of mouth. A messenger who distorted the message could start a war or ruin a deal. A faithful one brought "healing," the same word as 12:18. Guzik applies it to anyone entrusted with God's message (2 Cor 5:20).
  • "Poverty and disgrace come to him who ignores instruction, but whoever heeds reproof is honored" (v18). Guzik observes that Proverbs names several causes of poverty (laziness, refusing instruction, injustice), and not every poor person brought it on themselves (v23).
  • "A desire fulfilled is sweet to the soul, but to turn away from evil is an abomination to fools" (v19). The two lines seem unrelated at first. The link is desire. Fools want good things, but they will not give up the evil that blocks the way. Turning from it disgusts them. JFB: success requires self-denial, "which fools refuse."
  • Commentators: JFB and Guzik both read v19 as fools clinging to evil, Guzik saying they are attached to it. Guzik's application of v17 to anyone carrying God's truth goes beyond the text, but it fits the picture.

Companions, Inheritance and the Rod (vv20–25)

  • "Whoever walks with the wise becomes wise, but the companion of fools will suffer harm" (v20). The second line has a wordplay: "companion" is roʿeh, and "suffer harm" is yeroaʿ, sounding alike. Run with fools and you'll be ruined with them. Henry: "He that would be himself wise must walk with those that are so," and "multitudes are brought to ruin by bad company." Paul quotes a Greek poet with the same idea: "Bad company ruins good morals" (1 Cor 15:33). The Tyndale notes: the people we spend time with shape us. Guzik: our choice of friends both reveals and shapes where we are headed.
  • "Disaster pursues sinners, but the righteous are rewarded with good" (v21). Disaster is pictured as a hunter on the trail. JFB suggests the second line may read "He (God) will repay good." Guzik connects Jesus' promise that not even a cup of cold water given to a disciple goes unrewarded (Matt 10:42).
  • "A good man leaves an inheritance to his children's children, but the sinner's wealth is laid up for the righteous" (v22). A good life can bless two generations ahead. Guzik notes the inheritance is more than money: prayers, example and counsel. The sinner, meanwhile, piles up wealth that ends in someone else's hands (Job 27:16–17; Eccl 2:26). Israel leaving Egypt with Egyptian silver and gold is one picture of it (Exod 12:35–36).
  • "The fallow ground of the poor would yield much food, but it is swept away through injustice" (v23). "Fallow ground" (nir) is newly broken soil. The poor farmer's land could feed his family well, but injustice takes it: crooked judges, unfair taxes, powerful neighbors moving boundary stones (Isa 5:8; Mic 2:2). The Tyndale notes say this verse balances the many sayings that hard work brings plenty: sometimes it doesn't, because the world is unjust. Guzik offers both readings, injustice or laziness. JFB takes it as the honest poor doing better than wicked strivers. This is one of the places where Proverbs admits that its patterns can be broken by human sin.
  • "Whoever spares the rod hates his son, but he who loves him is diligent to discipline him" (v24). This is the best-known saying on child discipline. "Discipline" (musar) is the book's word for training and correction. The "rod" (shebet) is a shepherd's staff, a ruler's scepter and a tool for correction. "Diligent" is the verb shachar again, "seeks him early" (8:17): a loving parent pursues the child's good from the start, before bad habits harden. The saying does not mean "love equals hitting." It says that refusing to correct a child is not love but neglect. The Tyndale notes connect 19:18; 23:13–14; 29:17, and say that failing to discipline can come from laziness.
  • Reading v24 well. Christians differ on whether "the rod" commands physical punishment or stands for discipline in general. Many hold that careful, calm physical correction can be part of it. Others note that "rod" is often a picture of authority and correction, and that the point is loving, consistent discipline by whatever means. All agree that Scripture never permits harshness or abuse: "Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger" (Eph 6:4; Col 3:21). God himself is the model: "the Lord disciplines the one he loves" (Heb 12:6, quoting 3:12).
  • "The righteous has enough to satisfy his appetite, but the belly of the wicked suffers want" (v25). The chapter ends at the dinner table. JFB takes this as comparative prosperity, not contentment. Guzik says a wicked way of life tends to create its own lack, while contentment is a feast (15:15).
  • Commentators: Henry and Guzik both make v20 about the power of company. On v24 Guzik argues that withholding discipline harms a child even when it feels kind, and that the rod includes but is not limited to physical correction. He balances it with Ephesians 6:4 and the tenderness of Proverbs' own parents. JFB says not disciplining is like hating, while love "diligently seeks for him all useful discipline." On v23 JFB, Guzik and the Tyndale notes each read the verse somewhat differently, which matches the difficult Hebrew.

Pointing to Christ

Proverbs 13 is full of longing: hope deferred, desire fulfilled, the poor man's field swept away by injustice. Israel waited centuries for the promised King, and "when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son" (Gal 4:4). In Christ the long-deferred hope became "a tree of life." He is the "faithful envoy" who brings healing (v17), sent from the Father with a message he delivered perfectly (John 12:49–50). He is the companion of fools who did not become one, the friend of sinners who makes them wise (1 Cor 1:30). And he is the one who became poor while being rich (v7), so that his people inherit what no sinner's hoard can match, "an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading" (1 Pet 1:4).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes v.10's sweeping claim: "Only by pride cometh contention." He suggests applying this as a diagnostic: in every argument, ask "where is the pride?" On v.12, he notes that this proverb validates emotional pain — God acknowledges that waiting causes genuine heartsickness. On v.20, Guzik states that this is perhaps the most practically actionable proverb: choose your friends wisely, because you will become like them.

Reflection

  • Guarding Your Words (v.3) — Whoever guards his mouth preserves his life, but whoever opens his lips wide comes to ruin. Are your words building safety around your life, or exposing you to trouble you didn't need?
  • The Ache of Waiting (v.12) — Hope deferred makes the heart sick, but a desire fulfilled is a tree of life. Where has delayed hope left you discouraged, and can you trust God with the waiting itself?
  • Chosen Companions Shape You (v.20) — Walking with the wise makes a person wise, while a companion of fools comes to harm. Are the people you spend the most time with pulling you toward wisdom or away from it?
  • Building for Generations (v.22) — A good person leaves an inheritance not just to their children but to their children's children. What are you building today — financially, spiritually, in character — that will outlast you?
  • Discipline as an Act of Love (v.24) — Sparing correction is framed as a failure to love, while diligent discipline is true care. Where might you be avoiding a hard correction with someone you love, out of comfort rather than love?
