1Therefore, since Christ suffered in His body, arm yourselves with the same resolve, because anyone who has suffered in his body is done with sin.

2Consequently, he does not live out his remaining time on earth for human passions, but for the will of God.

3For you have spent enough time in the past carrying out the same desires as the Gentiles: living in debauchery, lust, drunkenness, orgies, carousing, and detestable idolatry.

4Because of this, they consider it strange of you not to plunge with them into the same flood of reckless indiscretion, and they heap abuse on you.

5But they will have to give an account to Him who is ready to judge the living and the dead.

6That is why the gospel was preached even to those who are now dead, so that they might be judged as men in the flesh, but live according to God in the spirit.

7The end of all things is near. Therefore be clear-minded and sober, so that you can pray.

8Above all, love one another deeply, because love covers over a multitude of sins.

9Show hospitality to one another without complaining.

10As good stewards of the manifold grace of God, each of you should use whatever gift he has received to serve one another.

11If anyone speaks, he should speak as one conveying the words of God. If anyone serves, he should serve with the strength God provides, so that in all things God may be glorified through Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory and the power forever and ever. Amen.

12Beloved, do not be surprised at the fiery trial that has come upon you, as though something strange were happening to you.

13But rejoice that you share in the sufferings of Christ, so that you may be overjoyed at the revelation of His glory.

14If you are insulted for the name of Christ, you are blessed, because the Spirit of glory and of God rests on you.

15Indeed, none of you should suffer as a murderer or thief or wrongdoer, or even as a meddler.

16But if you suffer as a Christian, do not be ashamed, but glorify God that you bear that name.

17For it is time for judgment to begin with the family of God; and if it begins with us, what will the outcome be for those who disobey the gospel of God?

18And, “If it is hard for the righteous to be saved, what will become of the ungodly and the sinner?”

19So then, those who suffer according to God’s will should entrust their souls to their faithful Creator and continue to do good.

Summary

Because Christ suffered in the flesh, Peter calls believers to arm themselves with the same resolve, choosing to live for God's will rather than human passions like their pagan neighbors do (vv. 1-4). He reminds them that judgment is coming for all, urges them to be serious and prayerful, to love one another deeply, and to use whatever gift they've received to serve others as faithful stewards of God's grace (vv. 7-11). Peter tells them not to be surprised by the fiery trials they face, but to rejoice in sharing Christ's sufferings, since suffering as a Christian brings no shame (vv. 12-16). He closes by explaining that judgment begins with the household of God, and that those who suffer according to God's will should entrust their souls to their faithful Creator while continuing to do good (vv. 17-19).

Authorship & Background

Author: Peter the Apostle (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Historical Context: Chapter 4 addresses living differently from the pagan world (vv.1-6), practical community life in light of the end (vv.7-11), and rejoicing in suffering for Christ (vv.12-19). Peter's readers faced social ostracism for refusing to participate in pagan practices. He encourages them: your former life is over, suffering for Christ is an honor, and judgment begins with God's house.
Classification: Epistle — General
Key Themes: A former life declared sufficient rather than merely regrettable, love covering a multitude of sins ranked above every other instruction, gifts administered as a stewardship of grace rather than a personal possession, fiery trials met with rejoicing instead of surprise, and judgment beginning at the house of God before it reaches anyone else
Living Differently (vv.1-6): Arm yourself with Christ's mindset toward suffering (v.1). Stop living for human desires; live for God's will (v.2). Your past pagan life is enough (v.3). Former friends think it strange you've changed and slander you (v.4). They will answer to God (v.5).
Community Life (vv.7-11): The end is near — be sober and prayerful (v.7). "Above all things have fervent charity" — love covers sins (v.8). Practice hospitality without complaining (v.9). Use your gifts to serve others as stewards of God's grace (v.10). Speak God's words; serve in God's strength — so God gets the glory (v.11).
Suffering as a Christian (vv.12-19): Don't be surprised by fiery trials (v.12). Rejoice — you share Christ's sufferings and will share His glory (v.13). If reproached for Christ's name, you're blessed (v.14). Don't suffer as a criminal — but if as a Christian, glorify God (vv.15-16). Judgment begins at God's house (v.17). Commit your soul to a faithful Creator (v.19).

Deep Dive

Setting: Peter writes from Rome ("Babylon," 5:13) in the early 60s AD, probably shortly before Nero's persecution broke out in AD 64. His readers are mostly Gentile believers scattered across five Roman provinces in what is now northern and central Turkey (1:1). They are not yet facing a state campaign against them, but they are mocked, slandered and pushed out of the social life of their towns because they no longer join in the festivals, feasts and temple meals of their neighbors. Chapter 3 ended with Christ, who "suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous" (3:18), now risen, ascended and ruling over every power (3:22). Chapter 4 draws the conclusion: since Christ suffered and won, his people can face suffering with his mindset, live for God's will, serve each other with the gifts God gives, and stand in the fire without surprise or shame.

Stewards of God's Grace (vv1–11)

  • "Since therefore Christ suffered in the flesh" (v1). "Therefore" picks up 3:18, the argument Peter interrupted with Noah and baptism. Christ's suffering is both the payment for sin (2:24; 3:18) and the pattern for his people (2:21).
  • "Arm yourselves with the same way of thinking" (v1). The verb hoplizō is a soldier's word, from hoplon, a weapon; a hoplite was an armed foot soldier. It appears only here in the New Testament. What believers arm themselves with is not a sword but a settled attitude (ennoia, "thought, intention"): the mind of Christ, who was willing to suffer to do his Father's will. Paul uses the same picture of putting on armor (Rom 13:12; Eph 6:11–17).
  • "Whoever has suffered in the flesh has ceased from sin" (v1). This is one of the harder lines in the letter, and there are three main readings. (1) It speaks of Christ: he suffered and was done with sin's burden once for all (Rom 6:10). (2) It speaks of the believer united to Christ in his death, who has broken with sin's rule (Rom 6:6–7). (3) It is a practical observation: someone who has chosen to suffer rather than sin has made a decisive break, and sin has lost its grip. Peter cannot mean that suffering makes anyone sinless, since he still has to warn these same readers about sin (v15). Most commentators combine the second and third: a believer who accepts suffering for doing right has settled the question of which master he serves.
  • "No longer for human passions but for the will of God" (v2). Two ways to spend "the rest of the time in the flesh," the years left in this body. The contrast is total: one life is driven by what people crave, the other by what God wants.
  • "The time that is past suffices" (v3). A dry, almost ironic understatement: "You have had enough time doing that already." The six words that follow paint a pagan city's social life. "Sensuality" and "passions" cover sexual license; "drunkenness, orgies, drinking parties" describe the banquets of trade guilds and religious festivals, where heavy drinking was normal; "lawless idolatry" (athemitos, "forbidden, wicked") names the worship of the gods that went with all of it. For these converts, cutting off idolatry meant cutting off much of their social calendar.
  • "What the Gentiles want to do" (v3). Peter writes to mostly Gentile believers but calls unbelievers "the Gentiles." In his eyes the church is now the people of God (2:9–10), and "Gentile" has become a word for those outside it, as it was for Israel.
  • "They are surprised when you do not join them in the same flood of debauchery" (v4). "Join them" is literally "run together with," a picture of a crowd rushing along. "Flood" (anachysis) is a pouring out, an overflow, like a river spilling its banks. "Debauchery" (asōtia) is reckless, wasteful living, the word used of the prodigal son's "reckless living" (Luke 15:13). Old friends find the change baffling, then offensive. "Malign" is blasphēmeō: they speak abusively, and JFB notes the slander falls not only on the believers but on the God and faith behind them.
  • "They will give account to him who is ready to judge the living and the dead" (v5). The slanderers think they are judging the Christians. In fact they will answer to the Judge, who is "ready," already standing at the door (James 5:9). "The living and the dead" is early creedal language for the whole human race (Acts 10:42; 2 Tim 4:1).
  • "The gospel was preached even to those who are dead" (v6). This is not a second chance after death, and it is probably not the same event as 3:19. The likeliest reading, held by JFB, Henry and the Tyndale notes, is that Peter means believers who heard the gospel while alive and have since died. Their pagan neighbors may have sneered that their faith did them no good, since they died like everyone else. Peter answers: "though judged in the flesh the way people are," that is, though they suffered death, the human verdict, "they might live in the spirit the way God does." Death is not the last word for them. Some older interpreters connect this verse to 3:19 and Christ's descent to the dead. Others read "the dead" as the spiritually dead, which JFB explicitly rejects.
  • "The end of all things is at hand" (v7). With Christ's death, resurrection and ascension, the last chapter of history has begun. Every New Testament writer speaks this way (Rom 13:11–12; James 5:8; 1 John 2:18). "At hand" is not a date but a posture: the next great event is his return, and nothing else must happen first. Peter himself will later explain why the Lord seems slow (2 Pet 3:8–9).
  • "Self-controlled and sober-minded for the sake of your prayers" (v7). The end does not call for panic or date-setting but for clear heads. "Sober-minded" is the same word Peter used in 1:13 and will use again in 5:8, the opposite of the drunkenness of v3. Peter had once fallen asleep in Gethsemane when told to watch and pray (Mark 14:37–38). He knows what it costs to be unready.
  • "Above all, keep loving one another earnestly" (v8). "Earnestly" is ektenēs, "stretched out, strained," the picture of a runner leaning into the tape. The same root describes Jesus praying "more earnestly" in Gethsemane (Luke 22:44) and the church praying "earnestly" for Peter in prison (Acts 12:5). Peter already called for this kind of love in 1:22.
  • "Love covers a multitude of sins" (v8). This draws on Proverbs 10:12: "Hatred stirs up strife, but love covers all offenses." In context it means that love does not keep score. It forgives, bears with and refuses to broadcast the faults of others, especially under the pressure of persecution, when tempers are short and a church can tear itself apart. It does not mean love earns forgiveness for one's own sins, a reading JFB rejects outright. James closes his letter with a similar line (James 5:20).
  • "Show hospitality to one another without grumbling" (v9). There were no church buildings, and inns were few, expensive and often immoral. Traveling preachers and believers driven from home depended on Christian homes (3 John 5–8; Heb 13:2). Hospitality also cost something: food, space and privacy. Peter adds "without grumbling," because a door opened with a sigh is only half open.
  • "As each has received a gift" (v10). "Gift" is charisma, a grace-gift. Every believer has one; no one is left out. Peter does not give a full list as Paul does (Rom 12:6–8; 1 Cor 12), but sorts them into two broad kinds in v11: speaking and serving.
  • "Good stewards of God's varied grace" (v10). A steward (oikonomos) managed a household or estate for an owner, often a trusted slave. The gifts are not owned, only managed, and the steward will give account (Luke 12:42–48). "Varied" is poikilos, "many-colored," the same word Peter used of "various trials" in 1:6. The trials come in many colors, and so does the grace that meets them.
  • "Whoever speaks, as one who speaks oracles of God" (v11). "Oracles" (logia) means words from God himself, the term used for the Old Testament Scriptures (Acts 7:38; Rom 3:2). The teacher is not to air opinions but to speak as one handling God's own words, with reverence and care.
  • "Whoever serves, as one who serves by the strength that God supplies" (v11). "Supplies" (chorēgeō) originally meant paying for the chorus in a Greek play, a wealthy patron footing the bill generously. God funds the work he assigns.
  • "In order that in everything God may be glorified through Jesus Christ" (v11). The goal of every gift is not the gifted person's reputation but God's glory. The doxology that follows, "To him belong glory and dominion forever and ever. Amen," closes this major section of the letter. A few scholars once thought the letter originally ended here, but there is no manuscript evidence for that, and Paul also places doxologies mid-letter (Rom 11:36; Eph 3:21).
  • Commentators: Henry puts v1 memorably: "The strongest and best arguments against sin, are taken from the sufferings of Christ." On v3 he says conversion "alters the mind, judgment, affections, and conversation." JFB links "arm yourselves" to Ephesians 6, and on v6 rejects reading "the dead" as the spiritually dead. Guzik offers two options for v6, the faithful dead who heard Christ's announcement and Christians who had already died, while JFB and the Tyndale notes settle on the second. On v10 Guzik uses the "many-colored" sense of poikilos and says grace that stops with the one who received it is grace wasted. Henry's lesson on v11: in all duties, aim at God's glory "as our chief end."

Suffering as a Christian (vv12–19)

  • "Beloved, do not be surprised at the fiery trial" (v12). "Fiery trial" is pyrōsis, a burning. In Revelation 18:9, 18 it is the burning of Babylon. Here, as in the Greek of Proverbs 27:21, it is the fire of a refiner's furnace that tests metal. Peter already used the image in 1:7: faith "tested by fire" is more precious than gold. "When it comes upon you" may also point to something worse on the horizon. Within a few years Nero would blame Christians for the fire of Rome, and Peter himself would die in the persecution that followed, according to early church tradition.
  • "As though something strange were happening to you" (v12). "Surprised" and "strange" both come from xenos, "foreign," the same root as "surprised" in v4. The pagans find the Christians' new life strange; the Christians must not find the world's hostility strange. Jesus said it would come: "In the world you will have tribulation" (John 16:33; 15:18–20).
  • "Rejoice insofar as you share Christ's sufferings" (v13). "Share" is koinōneō, to have fellowship or partnership in something. Suffering for Christ is a kind of fellowship with him (Phil 3:10). The joy is now ("rejoice") and later ("rejoice and be glad when his glory is revealed"); the second is a doubled, exultant word. The apostles had already lived this: they left the council "rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for the name" (Acts 5:41). Peter was one of them.
  • "If you are insulted for the name of Christ, you are blessed" (v14). This echoes Jesus' last beatitude: "Blessed are you when others revile you… on my account" (Matt 5:11–12). Peter heard it from Jesus himself.
  • "The Spirit of glory and of God rests upon you" (v14). "Rests upon" echoes Isaiah 11:2, where the Spirit of the LORD "shall rest upon" the coming Messiah. The Spirit who rested on Jesus rests on those who suffer for his name. The Spirit of glory may also recall the glory-cloud that rested on the tabernacle: the despised believer is where God's presence dwells. Some later manuscripts (followed by the KJV) add, "on their part he is evil spoken of, but on your part he is glorified." The earliest manuscripts lack it, and modern translations leave it out.
  • "Let none of you suffer as a murderer or a thief or an evildoer or as a meddler" (v15). There is no credit in suffering for wrongdoing (2:20). The last word is surprising next to "murderer." "Meddler" is allotriepiskopos, found nowhere earlier in Greek literature and possibly coined by Peter. It joins allotrios, "belonging to another," with episkopos, "overseer": a self-appointed supervisor of other people's affairs. A new convert who lectured neighbors, interfered in their family business or picked fights over their gods would bring hostility on himself and on the gospel.
  • "If anyone suffers as a Christian" (v16). "Christian" appears only three times in the New Testament (Acts 11:26; 26:28; here). It was first used at Antioch, most likely by outsiders, and possibly as a sneer, like "Christ-people." By the early second century the Roman governor Pliny, writing to the emperor Trajan about Christians in this very region (Bithynia, 1:1), treated bearing the name as a crime in itself. Peter tells believers to wear the label without shame and to "glorify God in that name."
  • "It is time for judgment to begin at the household of God" (v17). "Household of God" is the church, God's spiritual house (2:5). Peter draws on Ezekiel 9:6, where the judging angels are told, "begin at my sanctuary," and Malachi 3:1–3, where the Lord comes to his temple to refine his people like silver. The suffering of believers is not God's anger falling on them. It is the refining fire of 1:7 and v12, purifying his own house first.
  • "What will be the outcome for those who do not obey the gospel of God?" (v17). If the refining fire is this hot for God's children, the fire that falls on those who reject him will be far worse. The argument runs from the lesser to the greater.
  • "If the righteous is scarcely saved" (v18). Peter quotes Proverbs 11:31 in its Greek (Septuagint) form. "Scarcely" means "with difficulty," not "barely" or "uncertainly." Salvation is secure because it rests on God's power (1:5), but the road home runs through trial and fire, and God brings his people through it. If that is the path for the righteous, "what will become of the ungodly and the sinner?"
  • "Entrust their souls to a faithful Creator while doing good" (v19). "Entrust" is paratithēmi, which the lexicon defines as to deposit something with another or commit it to their care. It is the word Jesus spoke from the cross, quoting Psalm 31:5: "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit" (Luke 23:46). The believer does what Jesus did (2:23). "Creator" appears only here in the New Testament. The God who made all things is able to keep what is deposited with him, and he is "faithful," so he will. The verse ends with action, not just trust: "while doing good." Suffering is no excuse to stop serving.
  • "Those who suffer according to God's will" (v19). Suffering for doing right is not an accident or a sign that God has lost control. Peter has said it twice before: "if that should be God's will" (3:17; compare 1:6).
  • Commentators: All three agree that the trial is refining, not punishing. Guzik pictures the same fire refining gold while burning up straw, and stresses that believers never bear God's punishment because Christ already bore it. JFB calls the meddler a "self-constituted bishop," a fair rendering of the two halves of allotriepiskopos, and on v14 says the Spirit who rested on Christ rests on his people. JFB also notes the dispute over the v14 addition. Henry is blunt about v15: "There is no comfort in sufferings, when we bring them upon ourselves by our own sin and folly." On v18 Guzik explains "scarcely saved" as the cost of discipleship rather than doubt about salvation, which fits JFB's note that the righteous are saved with difficulty but securely. Guzik points out that "entrust" is a banking word for a deposit left with a trusted friend, which the lexicon supports. Henry: "The only way to keep the soul well, is, to commit it to God by prayer, and patient perseverance in well-doing."

Pointing to Christ

The chapter opens and closes with Jesus. It begins with "Christ suffered in the flesh" (v1) and ends with believers doing what he did on the cross: entrusting themselves to the Father (v19; Luke 23:46). In between, every instruction takes its shape from him. His people arm themselves with his mind, serve with the strength God supplies "through Jesus Christ," share his sufferings and bear his name. The Spirit who rested on him rests on them. The fire they walk through is real, but it is refining fire, and the one who went through the fire of judgment for them stands on the far side, ready to reveal his glory.

Reflection

  • Arming Yourself With Christ's Mindset (vv.1-2) — Peter says believers should adopt Christ's attitude toward suffering so they stop living for human passions and live instead for God's will. Whose desires are actually steering your choices right now?
  • Sober and Prayerful in Light of the End (v.7) — Knowing the end of all things is near, Peter calls believers to be clear-minded and prayerful rather than distracted or complacent. Does the shortness of life produce urgency in your prayer life, or does it barely register?
  • Love That Covers a Multitude of Sins (v.8) — Above everything else, Peter prioritizes fervent love that overlooks others' faults instead of exposing them. Is your love for others fervent, or has it settled into lukewarm tolerance?
  • Stewarding Your Gift for Others (v.10) — Every believer has received a gift meant to serve others as a steward of God's grace, not as private property. Are you using what God gave you to build others up, or keeping it to yourself?
  • Rejoicing in the Fiery Trial (vv.12-13) — Peter tells believers not to be surprised by painful trials but to rejoice, since they share in Christ's sufferings and will share in his glory. Can you find any reason to rejoice in what you're currently walking through?
  • Entrusting Your Soul to a Faithful Creator (v.19) — Those who suffer according to God's will are told to entrust their souls to him as a faithful Creator. Are you still gripping control of your circumstances, or are you willing to release them into his hands?
