1One Sabbath Jesus was passing through the grainfields, and His disciples began to pick the heads of grain, rub them in their hands, and eat them.

2But some of the Pharisees asked, “Why are you doing what is unlawful on the Sabbath?”

3Jesus replied, “Have you not read what David did when he and his companions were hungry?

4He entered the house of God, took the consecrated bread and gave it to his companions, and ate what is lawful only for the priests to eat.”

5Then Jesus declared, “The Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath.”

6On another Sabbath Jesus entered the synagogue and was teaching, and a man was there whose right hand was withered.

7Looking for a reason to accuse Jesus, the scribes and Pharisees were watching Him closely to see if He would heal on the Sabbath.

8But Jesus knew their thoughts and said to the man with the withered hand, “Get up and stand among us.” So he got up and stood there.

9Then Jesus said to them, “I ask you, which is lawful on the Sabbath: to do good or to do evil, to save life or to destroy it?”

10And after looking around at all of them, He said to the man, “Stretch out your hand.” He did so, and it was restored.

11But the scribes and Pharisees were filled with rage and began to discuss with one another what they might do to Jesus.

12In those days Jesus went out to the mountain to pray, and He spent the night in prayer to God.

13When daylight came, He called His disciples to Him and chose twelve of them, whom He also designated as apostles:

14Simon, whom He named Peter, and his brother Andrew; James and John; Philip and Bartholomew;

15Matthew and Thomas; James son of Alphaeus and Simon called the Zealot;

16Judas son of James, and Judas Iscariot, who became a traitor.

17Then Jesus came down with them and stood on a level place. A large crowd of His disciples was there, along with a great number of people from all over Judea, Jerusalem, and the seacoast of Tyre and Sidon.

18They had come to hear Him and to be healed of their diseases, and those troubled by unclean spirits were healed.

19The entire crowd was trying to touch Him, because power was coming from Him and healing them all.

20Looking up at His disciples, Jesus said: “Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the kingdom of God.

21Blessed are you who hunger now, for you will be filled. Blessed are you who weep now, for you will laugh.

22Blessed are you when people hate you, and when they exclude you and insult you and reject your name as evil because of the Son of Man.

23Rejoice in that day and leap for joy, because great is your reward in heaven. For their fathers treated the prophets in the same way.

24But woe to you who are rich, for you have already received your comfort.

25Woe to you who are well fed now, for you will hunger. Woe to you who laugh now, for you will mourn and weep.

26Woe to you when all men speak well of you, for their fathers treated the false prophets in the same way.

27But to those of you who will listen, I say: Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you,

28bless those who curse you, pray for those who mistreat you.

29If someone strikes you on one cheek, turn to him the other also. And if someone takes your cloak, do not withhold your tunic as well.

30Give to everyone who asks you, and if anyone takes what is yours, do not demand it back.

31Do to others as you would have them do to you.

32If you love those who love you, what credit is that to you? Even sinners love those who love them.

33If you do good to those who do good to you, what credit is that to you? Even sinners do the same.

34And if you lend to those from whom you expect repayment, what credit is that to you? Even sinners lend to sinners, expecting to be repaid in full.

35But love your enemies, do good to them, and lend to them, expecting nothing in return. Then your reward will be great, and you will be sons of the Most High; for He is kind to the ungrateful and wicked.

36Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful.

37Do not judge, and you will not be judged. Do not condemn, and you will not be condemned. Forgive, and you will be forgiven.

38Give, and it will be given to you. A good measure, pressed down, shaken together, and running over will be poured into your lap. For with the measure you use, it will be measured back to you.”

39Jesus also told them a parable: “Can a blind man lead a blind man? Will they not both fall into a pit?

40A disciple is not above his teacher, but everyone who is fully trained will be like his teacher.

41Why do you look at the speck in your brother’s eye but fail to notice the beam in your own eye?

42How can you say, ‘Brother, let me take the speck out of your eye,’ while you yourself fail to see the beam in your own eye? You hypocrite! First take the beam out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother’s eye.

43No good tree bears bad fruit, nor does a bad tree bear good fruit.

44For each tree is known by its own fruit. Indeed, figs are not gathered from thornbushes, nor grapes from brambles.

45The good man brings good things out of the good treasure of his heart, and the evil man brings evil things out of the evil treasure of his heart. For out of the overflow of the heart the mouth speaks.

46Why do you call Me ‘Lord, Lord,’ but do not do what I say?

47I will show you what he is like who comes to Me and hears My words and acts on them:

48He is like a man building a house, who dug down deep and laid his foundation on the rock. When the flood came, the torrent crashed against that house but could not shake it, because it was well built.

49But the one who hears My words and does not act on them is like a man who built his house on ground without a foundation. The torrent crashed against that house, and immediately it fell—and great was its destruction!”

1And it came to pass on the second sabbath after the first, that he went through the corn fields; and his disciples plucked the ears of corn, and did eat, rubbing them in their hands.

2And certain of the Pharisees said unto them, Why do ye that which is not lawful to do on the sabbath days?

3And Jesus answering them said, Have ye not read so much as this, what David did, when himself was an hungred, and they which were with him;

4How he went into the house of God, and did take and eat the shewbread, and gave also to them that were with him; which it is not lawful to eat but for the priests alone?

5And he said unto them, That the Son of man is Lord also of the sabbath.

6And it came to pass also on another sabbath, that he entered into the synagogue and taught: and there was a man whose right hand was withered.

7And the scribes and Pharisees watched him, whether he would heal on the sabbath day; that they might find an accusation against him.

8But he knew their thoughts, and said to the man which had the withered hand, Rise up, and stand forth in the midst. And he arose and stood forth.

9Then said Jesus unto them, I will ask you one thing; Is it lawful on the sabbath days to do good, or to do evil? to save life, or to destroy it?

10And looking round about upon them all, he said unto the man, Stretch forth thy hand. And he did so: and his hand was restored whole as the other.

11And they were filled with madness; and communed one with another what they might do to Jesus.

12And it came to pass in those days, that he went out into a mountain to pray, and continued all night in prayer to God.

13And when it was day, he called unto him his disciples: and of them he chose twelve, whom also he named apostles;

14Simon, (whom he also named Peter,) and Andrew his brother, James and John, Philip and Bartholomew,

15Matthew and Thomas, James the son of Alphaeus, and Simon called the Zealot,

16And Judas the brother of James, and Judas Iscariot, which also was the traitor.

17And he came down with them, and stood in the plain, and the company of his disciples, and a great multitude of people out of all Judaea and Jerusalem, and from the sea coast of Tyre and Sidon, which came to hear him, and to be healed of their diseases;

18And they that were vexed with unclean spirits: and they were healed.

19And the whole multitude sought to touch him: for there went virtue out of him, and healed them all.

20And he lifted up his eyes on his disciples, and said, Blessed be ye poor: for yours is the kingdom of God.

21Blessed are ye that hunger now: for ye shall be filled. Blessed are ye that weep now: for ye shall laugh.

22Blessed are ye, when men shall hate you, and when they shall separate you from their company, and shall reproach you, and cast out your name as evil, for the Son of man’s sake.

23Rejoice ye in that day, and leap for joy: for, behold, your reward is great in heaven: for in the like manner did their fathers unto the prophets.

24But woe unto you that are rich! for ye have received your consolation.

25Woe unto you that are full! for ye shall hunger. Woe unto you that laugh now! for ye shall mourn and weep.

26Woe unto you, when all men shall speak well of you! for so did their fathers to the false prophets.

27But I say unto you which hear, Love your enemies, do good to them which hate you,

28Bless them that curse you, and pray for them which despitefully use you.

29And unto him that smiteth thee on the one cheek offer also the other; and him that taketh away thy cloke forbid not to take thy coat also.

30Give to every man that asketh of thee; and of him that taketh away thy goods ask them not again.

31And as ye would that men should do to you, do ye also to them likewise.

32For if ye love them which love you, what thank have ye? for sinners also love those that love them.

33And if ye do good to them which do good to you, what thank have ye? for sinners also do even the same.

34And if ye lend to them of whom ye hope to receive, what thank have ye? for sinners also lend to sinners, to receive as much again.

35But love ye your enemies, and do good, and lend, hoping for nothing again; and your reward shall be great, and ye shall be the children of the Highest: for he is kind unto the unthankful and to the evil.

36Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful.

37Judge not, and ye shall not be judged: condemn not, and ye shall not be condemned: forgive, and ye shall be forgiven:

38Give, and it shall be given unto you; good measure, pressed down, and shaken together, and running over, shall men give into your bosom. For with the same measure that ye mete withal it shall be measured to you again.

39And he spake a parable unto them, Can the blind lead the blind? shall they not both fall into the ditch?

40The disciple is not above his master: but every one that is perfect shall be as his master.

41And why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother’s eye, but perceivest not the beam that is in thine own eye?

42Either how canst thou say to thy brother, Brother, let me pull out the mote that is in thine eye, when thou thyself beholdest not the beam that is in thine own eye? Thou hypocrite, cast out first the beam out of thine own eye, and then shalt thou see clearly to pull out the mote that is in thy brother’s eye.

43For a good tree bringeth not forth corrupt fruit; neither doth a corrupt tree bring forth good fruit.

44For every tree is known by his own fruit. For of thorns men do not gather figs, nor of a bramble bush gather they grapes.

45A good man out of the good treasure of his heart bringeth forth that which is good; and an evil man out of the evil treasure of his heart bringeth forth that which is evil: for of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaketh.

46And why call ye me, Lord, Lord, and do not the things which I say?

47Whosoever cometh to me, and heareth my sayings, and doeth them, I will shew you to whom he is like:

48He is like a man which built an house, and digged deep, and laid the foundation on a rock: and when the flood arose, the stream beat vehemently upon that house, and could not shake it: for it was founded upon a rock.

49But he that heareth, and doeth not, is like a man that without a foundation built an house upon the earth; against which the stream did beat vehemently, and immediately it fell; and the ruin of that house was great.

1Jesus was going through the grain fields on a Sabbath, and his disciples picked some heads of wheat, rubbed them in their hands, and ate them.

2But some of the Pharisees said, “Why are you doing what is against the law on the Sabbath?”

3Jesus answered them, “Haven’t you read what David did when he and his companions were hungry—

4how he entered the house of God, took and ate the sacred bread, which is not lawful for any to eat but the priests alone, and gave it to his companions?”

5Then he said to them, “The Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath.”

6On another Sabbath, Jesus entered the synagogue and was teaching. Now a man was there whose right hand was withered.

7The experts in the law and the Pharisees watched Jesus closely to see if he would heal on the Sabbath, so that they could find a reason to accuse him.

8But he knew their thoughts, and said to the man who had the withered hand, “Get up and stand here.” So he rose and stood there.

9Then Jesus said to them, “I ask you, is it lawful to do good on the Sabbath or to do evil, to save a life or to destroy it?”

10After looking around at them all, he said to the man, “Stretch out your hand.” The man did so, and his hand was restored.

11But they were filled with mindless rage and began debating with one another what they would do to Jesus.

14Simon (whom he named Peter), and his brother Andrew; and James, John, Philip, Bartholomew,

24But woe to you who are rich, for you have received your comfort already.

26Woe to you when all people speak well of you, for their ancestors did the same things to the false prophets.

Summary

Jesus defends his disciples for picking grain on the Sabbath and heals a man's withered hand on the Sabbath, declaring himself Lord of the Sabbath and provoking the Pharisees' rage (vv. 1-11). After a night of prayer, he chooses twelve apostles from his disciples (vv. 12-16), then, before a great crowd, delivers the Sermon on the Plain: blessings on the poor, hungry, weeping, and persecuted, paired with woes on the rich, full, and well-regarded (vv. 20-26). He commands radical love for enemies, generosity without expectation of return, and mercy instead of judgment (vv. 27-38), then warns against hypocrisy in correcting others and teaches that good and bad fruit reveal the heart (vv. 39-45). The chapter closes with the parable of two builders: the one who hears and obeys Jesus' words is like a house founded on rock, secure against the storm, while the one who does not is like a house built without foundation (vv. 46-49).

Authorship & Background

Author: Luke (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 6 contains the Sabbath controversies (vv.1-11), the choosing of the twelve apostles (vv.12-16), and Luke's version of the Sermon on the Mount — often called the "Sermon on the Plain" because Luke says Jesus "stood in the plain" (v.17). It is shorter than Matthew's version (Matthew 5-7) but includes unique material — particularly the "woes" (vv.24-26) that balance the blessings.
Classification: Gospel — Narrative and Discourse
Key Themes: Two Sabbath confrontations, a night of prayer before choosing twelve men, blessings paired with woes found only here, poverty and hunger named without spiritualizing, love of enemies as the distinguishing mark, and two builders distinguished by what they dug
Sabbath Controversies (vv.1-11): Two incidents in succession, and both turn on the same claim. The disciples pluck grain as they walk, and the defense offered is David eating the shewbread — an argument from precedent — followed by something else entirely: the Son of man is Lord also of the sabbath. The second incident is a withered hand healed while they watch to see whether he will, and the question put to them first is whether it is lawful to do good on the sabbath. Verse 11's “filled with madness” records the answer they gave.
A Night of Prayer, and the Twelve Chosen (vv.12-16): Luke alone notes that he continued all night in prayer before naming the twelve. The most consequential appointment of the ministry is preceded by the longest prayer Luke records, and the list that follows includes Simon called the Zealot and Judas Iscariot, described in the same breath as the man which also was the traitor. Nothing suggests the night of prayer was meant to avoid that.
Blessings and Woes (vv.17-26): Luke sets this sermon on level ground, which is why it is often called the Sermon on the Plain, and his version is shorter and blunter than Matthew's. “Blessed be ye poor”, not poor in spirit; hunger and weeping are named without qualification. The four woes that follow are found only here, aimed at the rich, the full, the laughing and the well-spoken-of. Pairing them with the blessings makes the passage a set of contrasts rather than a list of ideals.
Love Your Enemies (vv.27-38): The demands are concrete and unhedged: love your enemies, bless them that curse you, offer the other cheek, lend without expecting return. The reasoning in vv.32-34 is comparative — loving those who love you is what everyone does, so it distinguishes nothing. Mercy is grounded in God's own, and the closing image is commercial: good measure, pressed down, shaken together, running over. The measure you use is the measure you receive.
The Blind Guide, the Beam, and the Tree (vv.39-45): A short sequence of sayings about qualification to teach. A blind man cannot lead the blind, and a disciple does not rise above his master, so who does the teaching matters. The beam and the mote follow, with the order of operations stated plainly — remove your own first, and then you will see clearly enough to help. The tree and its fruit close the section: what a man says comes out of what he is.
Two Builders and the Rock (vv.46-49): The sermon ends on the gap between calling him Lord and doing what he says. Luke's version of the two builders differs from Matthew's in a telling way: the wise builder digs deep and lays a foundation on rock, so the difference is effort that is invisible until the flood arrives. Both houses face the same water. Only the excavation distinguishes them.

Map & Geography

  • A plain (v.17): Luke's "Sermon on the Plain" was delivered on a level place, likely near Capernaum in Galilee. Crowds came from Judea, Jerusalem, and the coast of Tyre and Sidon.
  • A mountain (v.12): Before choosing the twelve apostles, Jesus went to a mountain to pray all night — likely a hill near the Sea of Galilee.

Setting: About AD 28–29, in Galilee, still early in Jesus' public ministry. Chapter 5 ended with three clashes with the Pharisees and scribes, over forgiveness, eating with sinners and fasting, and with Jesus' picture of new wine that will not fit old wineskins. Chapter 6 adds two more clashes, both over the Sabbath, and the leaders begin to talk about what to do with him. Jesus then spends a night in prayer, chooses twelve apostles and gives the Sermon on the Plain, Luke's shorter counterpart to the Sermon on the Mount (Matt 5–7). It is the clearest statement in Luke of what life in the kingdom looks like.

Jesus Is Lord of the Sabbath (vv1–5)

  • "On a Sabbath, while he was going through the grainfields" (v1). Some later manuscripts read "on the second-first Sabbath," an odd Greek word no one has explained with certainty. The earliest manuscripts lack it, and the ESV leaves it out. The grain was probably barley or wheat, ripening in spring.
  • "Plucked and ate some heads of grain, rubbing them in their hands" (v1). Only Luke mentions the rubbing, a doctor's eye for detail. Picking a little grain by hand while walking through a neighbor's field was allowed (Deut 23:25). Theft was not the issue.
  • "Why are you doing what is not lawful to do on the Sabbath?" (v2). The law forbade work on the Sabbath (Exod 20:8–11) but did not define every kind of work. The oral tradition, later written down in the Mishnah, listed thirty-nine categories of forbidden work, including reaping, threshing and winnowing. On that reading, picking was reaping, rubbing was threshing and blowing away the husks was winnowing. The disciples had broken the tradition, not Moses.
  • "Have you not read what David did" (vv3–4). The question has a sting, since these were men who prided themselves on reading Scripture. In 1 Samuel 21:1–6, David, fleeing Saul, received from the priest at Nob the bread of the Presence, twelve loaves set before the Lord each week and eaten only by priests (Lev 24:5–9). Scripture records it without blame. Human need came before a ceremonial rule. There is also a quiet comparison: David was God's anointed king, not yet on the throne and hunted by those in power. So is the Son of David.
  • "The Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath" (v5). This is a staggering claim. God made the Sabbath and called it "my Sabbaths" (Exod 31:13). To be its lord is to stand where God stands. Jesus does not abolish the Sabbath. He claims the right to say what it is for. Codex Bezae, an early manuscript with many additions, puts after v5 a story of Jesus seeing a man working on the Sabbath. Scholars treat it as a later insertion, and the ESV does not include it.
  • Commentators: JFB calls v5 "as naked a claim to" the authority of the Lawgiver at Sinai "as could well be made." Henry, writing as a Puritan who kept Sunday strictly, says that under Christ "the sabbath day is to be turned into a Lord's day," and that works of necessity and mercy are lawful on it. Guzik counts the four kinds of forbidden work the Pharisees saw in one handful of grain, and reads the title lord of the Sabbath as a direct claim to deity.

A Man with a Withered Hand (vv6–11)

  • "His right hand was withered" (v6). Only Luke says it was the right hand, the hand for work. A man with a shriveled right hand could hardly earn a living.
  • "The scribes and the Pharisees watched him… so that they might find a reason to accuse him" (v7). "Watched" means watched closely, even spied on. They do not doubt that Jesus can heal. They are counting on it. Rabbis of the time allowed medical help on the Sabbath only when life was in danger, and a withered hand could wait until Sunday.
  • "He knew their thoughts… 'Come and stand here'" (v8). Jesus does not heal quietly in a corner. He brings the man to the front where everyone can see. The question is going to be answered in public.
  • "Is it lawful on the Sabbath to do good or to do harm, to save life or to destroy it?" (v9). Jesus reframes the question. Not doing good, when it is in one's power, is itself a kind of harm. The irony is sharp: he is about to restore a life, while they are sitting in the synagogue planning to destroy his.
  • "After looking around at them all" (v10). Jesus looks each man in the face. Mark 3:5 adds that he looked "with anger, grieved at their hardness of heart."
  • "Stretch out your hand" (v10). The one thing the man could not do is what he is told to do. As he obeys, the hand is restored. Jesus does no work that could be called work. He speaks, and it is done.
  • "They were filled with fury" (v11). The word is anoia, literally "mindlessness," a rage that has lost its reason. A man has been healed in front of them and all they can see is a broken rule. Mark 3:6 says they began plotting with the Herodians "how to destroy him."
  • Commentators: Henry has two sharp lines on v11: "Anger is a short madness, malice is a long one," and "They were mad at Christ, mad at the people, mad at themselves." JFB likewise reads anoia as "senseless rage," and on v9 says that to neglect an opportunity to do good "is to incur the guilt of doing evil." Guzik points out that the leaders, in their fury and plotting, broke the spirit of the Sabbath far worse than Jesus did by healing, and suggests they may even have brought the man there as bait.

The Twelve Apostles (vv12–16)

  • "All night he continued in prayer to God" (v12). This is the only place in the Gospels where Jesus is said to pray all night. The choice of the Twelve was the foundation of the church (Eph 2:20), and Jesus made it on his knees. Luke keeps showing Jesus praying at turning points (3:21; 9:18, 28; 22:41). Even the Son chose to depend on his Father.
  • "He called his disciples and chose from them twelve, whom he named apostles" (v13). "Disciple" means learner, and there were many. "Apostle" (apostolos) means "one sent," an envoy who carries the authority of the one who sends him. Twelve matches the twelve tribes of Israel (22:30). Jesus is forming a renewed people of God.
  • "Simon, whom he named Peter" (v14). "Peter" is Greek for "rock" (Aramaic Kepha, Cephas). His name is always first in the lists, and Judas always last.
  • Bartholomew (v14). His name means "son of Tolmai." Many think he is Nathanael (John 1:45–49), since John never names Bartholomew and the other Gospels pair him with Philip, who brought Nathanael to Jesus.
  • "Matthew" (v15). The tax collector Levi of 5:27.
  • "Simon who was called the Zealot" (v15). The Zealot party as an organized revolt came later, in the 60s, but "zealot" already described men passionate for the law and for Israel's freedom from Rome. So Jesus chose a former tax collector, who worked for Rome's system, and a nationalist who hated it. Only Jesus could hold them together.
  • "Judas the son of James" (v16). He is probably the "Thaddaeus" of Matthew 10:3 and Mark 3:18, a different man from Judas Iscariot and from Jude, the brother of Jesus. John 14:22 calls him "Judas (not Iscariot)."
  • "Judas Iscariot, who became a traitor" (v16). "Iscariot" probably means "man of Kerioth," a town in southern Judea, which would make him the only Judean among the Galileans. "Became" is honest. He was chosen after a night of prayer, given the same calling and the same opportunities, and he turned.
  • Commentators: Henry notes that we think half an hour in private prayer a great deal, while Christ spent the whole night, and that "ministers are to be ordained with prayer more than ordinarily solemn." Guzik sees in the all-night prayer the dependence Jesus lived in as a man, and notes the names fall into pairs, perhaps reflecting how he later sent them out two by two. JFB adds only that he went out, probably from Capernaum, and gives Matthew 10 as the parallel.

Jesus Ministers to a Great Multitude (vv17–19)

  • "He came down with them and stood on a level place" (v17). Matthew sets his sermon "on the mountain" (Matt 5:1). Many think they are the same sermon, and a level place on a hillside fits both descriptions. Others think Jesus preached similar material more than once, as traveling teachers did. Luke's version is about a quarter as long and shares much of its order with Matthew's: blessings first, love of enemies, judging, fruit and two builders last.
  • "From all Judea and Jerusalem and the seacoast of Tyre and Sidon" (v17). People come from Jewish lands to the south and from Gentile coastal cities to the northwest, now in Lebanon. The kingdom's reach is already wider than Israel.
  • "Power came out from him and healed them all" (v19). Luke speaks of power going out from Jesus as something real (8:46). The tense pictures healing after healing, one person after another.
  • Commentators: Henry draws the lesson that those without good preaching nearby "had better travel far for it than be without it," and sees in the healings "a fulness of grace in Christ." JFB argues the Lukan sermon is a different discourse from Matthew's, and reads "healed them all" as "kept healing." Guzik thinks the "level place" and Matthew's "mountain" can describe the same plateau, and notes that power going out from Jesus implies the healing cost him something.

The Beatitudes (vv20–23)

  • "He lifted up his eyes on his disciples" (v20). The sermon is spoken first to those already following him, though the crowd is listening (7:1). It describes the life of people who belong to the kingdom.
  • "Blessed are you who are poor" (v20). "Blessed" (makarios) means more than happy. It is the state of someone God has favored, whatever the circumstances look like. Matthew says "poor in spirit." Luke's plain "poor" fits his Gospel's concern for the materially poor (1:53; 4:18). The two belong together. In the Old Testament "the poor" often meant the humble faithful who had nothing to lean on but God (Ps 34:6; Isa 61:1). Luke's beatitudes speak directly, "you who are poor," as a word to people standing in front of Jesus.
  • "Hungry now… weep now" (v21). The word "now" marks the hinge between this age and the age to come. Hunger will be satisfied and tears turned to laughter (Ps 126:2; Isa 25:8). These are not rewards for being poor, but God's promise to people who look to him and not to their circumstances.
  • "When people hate you… exclude you… spurn your name as evil, on account of the Son of Man" (v22). "Exclude" probably means cutting off from synagogue and community, which happened to believers early (John 9:22). "Your name" may be the name "Christian" itself, which became a term of abuse (1 Pet 4:14–16). The cause matters: "on account of the Son of Man." Suffering for one's own faults is not in view.
  • "Rejoice in that day, and leap for joy" (v23). "Leap" is the word used of John the Baptist leaping in Elizabeth's womb (1:41, 44). The joy is not a grim endurance. It rests on a great reward in heaven and on keeping company with the prophets, who were treated the same way (Jer 20:2; 2 Chr 24:20–21).
  • Commentators: JFB reads Luke's "poor" as "the poor of this world," and calls "laugh" and "leap for joy" livelier words than Matthew's. Guzik explains blessedness as a deep joy from God that does not depend on comfort, and argues that the promise is spiritual, not a program for social revolution. He also notes the force of the word for poor, used of beggars who own nothing.

Jesus Pronounces Woes (vv24–26)

  • "But woe to you who are rich" (v24). Only Luke has these four woes, set exactly against the four blessings. "Woe" is the cry of a prophet grieving over coming judgment (Isa 5:8–22; Amos 6:1). It is a lament as much as a threat.
  • "For you have received your consolation" (v24). "Received" is a business word for receiving payment in full, with a receipt. People who make wealth their comfort have been paid already, and there is nothing more to come. The rich man and Lazarus (16:25) is this verse turned into a story.
  • "Woe to you who are full now… who laugh now" (v25). Satisfaction and laughter are not sins. The woe is on those for whom this life's fullness is everything, so that they feel no need of God.
  • "When all people speak well of you" (v26). Being liked by everyone can be a warning sign. False prophets were popular because they told people what they wanted to hear (Jer 5:31; Mic 2:11).
  • Commentators: JFB points to Micah 2:11 on the court paid to false prophets, and reads "shall hunger" as a craving that never ends. Guzik reads the woes as a warning given in compassion, exposing how self-sufficiency keeps people from God. The Tyndale notes place them in the line of the prophets' woes against oppressors.

Love Your Enemies (vv27–36)

  • "But I say to you who hear, Love your enemies" (v27). "Love" here is agapē, love as a settled commitment to another's good. It is shown in four actions: do good, bless, pray, and in the verses that follow, give. Feelings are not the starting point. Proverbs already said to feed a hungry enemy (Prov 25:21), but making enemy-love the center of a whole way of life was new.
  • "To one who strikes you on the cheek, offer the other also" (v29). A slap on the face was an insult more than an injury. Jesus forbids personal revenge and the tit-for-tat that keeps hatred going. He is not abolishing the state's duty to punish crime (Rom 13:1–4), and Jesus himself protested when struck (John 18:22–23). The point is what a disciple does with personal wrongs.
  • "Give to everyone who begs from you" (v30). Jesus does not mean handing over whatever is demanded regardless of harm (2 Thess 3:10 sets limits). He means a generous, open-handed spirit that does not cling to possessions.
  • "As you wish that others would do to you, do so to them" (v31). The Golden Rule. Rabbi Hillel, a generation earlier, taught a negative form: do not do to a neighbor what is hateful to oneself. Jesus' positive form asks more, because it calls for action and not just restraint.
  • "What benefit is that to you?" (vv32–34). "Benefit" (charis, usually "grace") means credit or thanks. Loving those who love back is ordinary human trade. "Even sinners" do it. Kingdom love goes past what the other person deserves, which is what grace is.
  • "Lend, expecting nothing in return" (v35). The law already forbade charging interest to a poor Israelite (Exod 22:25; Lev 25:35–37). Jesus goes further: lend as if it were a gift.
  • "You will be sons of the Most High, for he is kind to the ungrateful and the evil" (v35). A son shows the family likeness. God gives sun and rain to people who never thank him (Matt 5:45). When believers love enemies, they look like their Father. "Most High" echoes 1:32, where Jesus is called "the Son of the Most High."
  • "Be merciful, even as your Father is merciful" (v36). Matthew 5:48 has "perfect." Luke's "merciful" names the part of God's perfection in view: his mercy (Exod 34:6).
  • Commentators: Henry calls charity "the bond of perfectness" and reads v36 as the summary of the whole section: in mercy God is to be imitated. Guzik insists that love for enemies is shown in deeds, not sentiment, and reads the slap on the cheek as personal insult, not a ban on justice by the state. He contrasts Hillel's negative form of the Golden Rule with Jesus' positive one. JFB gives only Matthew 5 as the parallel here.

Judging Others (vv37–42)

  • "Judge not, and you will not be judged" (v37). Jesus is not banning all moral judgment, since he goes on to tell disciples to recognize bad fruit (vv43–45) and to help a brother with a speck (v42). He forbids a harsh, fault-finding spirit that condemns others while excusing self. "Condemn not… forgive" makes the point clearer.
  • "Good measure, pressed down, shaken together, running over" (v38). The picture comes from a grain market. A generous seller fills the measure, presses it down, shakes it to settle it, and pours until it spills. "Lap" is the fold of a robe pulled up over the belt to make a pocket. God's generosity overflows like that.
  • "With the measure you use it will be measured back to you" (v38). This is a principle of God's dealing, not a formula for getting rich. Mercy shown meets mercy, and harshness meets harshness (James 2:13).
  • "Can a blind man lead a blind man?" (v39). Matthew uses this saying of the Pharisees (Matt 15:14). Here it warns disciples: a teacher who cannot see his own faults will lead his students into the same ditch.
  • "Everyone when he is fully trained will be like his teacher" (v40). "Fully trained" is the word for mending nets (Mark 1:19) or setting a bone, to put in proper order. A student becomes like his teacher, so choosing a teacher matters, and so does becoming one carefully.
  • "The speck… the log" (vv41–42). The picture is meant to be absurd and funny: a man with a beam sticking out of his eye, offering to perform delicate surgery on someone else's. "Hypocrite" (hypokritēs) was the word for an actor playing a part. Jesus does not say "leave the speck alone." He says deal with the log first, and then help.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Those with a very ill grace censure the faults of others who are not aware of their own faults." JFB calls vv37–38 "much fuller and more graphic" than Matthew's version, and on v40 warns that disciples of blind leaders share "one common ruin" with them. Guzik notes the humor of the log, and uses David as an example: quick to condemn the man in Nathan's story while blind to his own sin (2 Sam 12:1–7).

A Tree and Its Fruit (vv43–45)

  • "Each tree is known by its own fruit" (v44). Fruit does not make the tree good or bad. It shows what kind of tree it already is. Figs and grapes were the most common fruit in the land, and thorny shrubs grew everywhere, so the picture was close at hand.
  • "Out of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaks" (v45). "Treasure" means a storehouse. In Scripture the heart is the center of thought, will and desire. Words are the overflow of what is stored there. Real change starts inside, which is why the kingdom needs new hearts (Ezek 36:26) and not just better manners.
  • Commentators: Guzik draws the point that speech reveals the heart, and the Tyndale notes add that what a person takes in and treasures shapes what comes out.

Build Your House on the Rock (vv46–49)

  • "Why do you call me 'Lord, Lord,' and not do what I tell you?" (v46). Calling Jesus "Lord" is meant to be the language of a servant. To say it and ignore him is to contradict oneself.
  • "Dug deep and laid the foundation on the rock" (v48). Luke stresses the digging. Building on rock took effort, going down through the soil until hitting bedrock. Hearing is easy, and doing takes the slow work of laying a foundation.
  • "When a flood arose, the stream broke against that house" (v48). In Galilee dry creek beds could become torrents after heavy rain. Floods in Scripture often picture trial and judgment.
  • "Immediately it fell, and the ruin of that house was great" (v49). Both houses looked fine in good weather. The difference was hidden until the storm. Ezekiel was told his hearers listened to him like a pleasant song but did not obey (Ezek 33:31–32).
  • Commentators: Guzik contrasts verbal confession with obedience and stresses that the foundation is unseen until the storm tests it. Henry treats the sermon's end as a call to build on Christ's words by doing them. JFB gives only the parallel in Matthew 7.

Pointing to Christ

Luke 6 shows a Lord who has the right to say what the Sabbath is for and uses that right to feed the hungry and heal a withered hand. He prays all night and chooses twelve men, including the one who will betray him. The sermon that follows describes his own life. He became poor, hungry and weeping, and he was hated and cast out on account of his name. He loved his enemies, blessed those who cursed him and prayed for those who nailed him to a cross (23:34). He is the kind Father's Son, showing mercy to the ungrateful, and his words are the rock that holds when the flood comes.

Reflection

  • Rules Over People (v.11) — The Pharisees responded with rage when Jesus healed on the Sabbath, valuing rule-keeping over the man in front of them. Where might you be prioritizing being technically correct over showing compassion to someone who needs it?
  • Prayer Before Major Decisions (v.12) — Jesus spent an entire night in prayer before choosing His twelve apostles. How much prayer typically precedes your own significant decisions, and what would change if you brought that level of dependence on God to your next one?
  • Woes to Comfort and Applause (vv.24-26) — Luke's woes single out the rich, the full, the laughing, and the well-spoken-of — exactly what our culture chases. How loosely do you actually hold comfort, security, and other people's approval?
  • Loving Your Enemies (v.27) — Jesus commands love and good toward those who are hostile to us, not just tolerance. Who is one person who has wronged you, and what specific act of love or prayer could you direct toward them this week?
  • Mercy and Generosity as God's Economy (vv.36-38) — Jesus grounds mercy toward others in the mercy God has already shown us, and promises that generosity is met with generosity. Where are you withholding mercy or generosity out of a fear of not having enough?
  • Hearing vs. Doing (vv.46-49) — The two builders heard the same words from Jesus; only one obeyed them, and the storm exposed the difference. What teaching of Jesus have you heard many times but still haven't put into practice?
