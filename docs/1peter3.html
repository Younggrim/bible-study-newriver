7Husbands, in the same way, treat your wives with consideration as a delicate vessel, and with honor as fellow heirs of the gracious gift of life, so that your prayers will not be hindered.

8Finally, all of you, be like-minded and sympathetic, love as brothers, be tenderhearted and humble.

Summary

Peter instructs wives to win over unbelieving husbands through respectful conduct rather than words, and to seek the inner beauty of a gentle and quiet spirit rather than outward adornment, citing Sarah as an example (vv. 1-6). Husbands are told to live considerately with their wives, honoring them as co-heirs of the gift of life (v. 7). Peter then calls all believers to unity, sympathy, and humility, repaying evil with blessing rather than retaliation (vv. 8-12), and encourages them to keep a clear conscience and be ready to give a gentle, respectful defense of their hope when questioned about it (vv. 13-17). He closes by pointing to Christ, who suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring people to God, and connects this to Noah's ark and baptism as a pledge of a clean conscience (vv. 18-22).

Authorship & Background

Author: Peter the Apostle (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Historical Context: Chapter 3 continues the theme of godly conduct in relationships: wives and husbands (vv.1-7), the whole community (vv.8-12), suffering for righteousness (vv.13-17), and Christ's victorious suffering (vv.18-22). The chapter contains one of the most debated passages in the NT — Christ preaching to "spirits in prison" (vv.19-20).
Classification: Epistle — General
Key Themes: A wife's conduct trusted to win a husband where words have failed, an inner adornment of a meek and quiet spirit set against outward ornament, blessing returned for evil instead of evil for evil, an answer for one's hope demanded but required to be gentle, and a disputed descent to spirits in prison tied to baptism as the answer of a good conscience
Wives and Husbands (vv.1-7): Wives: win unbelieving husbands through conduct, not words (vv.1-2). Adorn the inner person — "a meek and quiet spirit" — not just the outer (vv.3-4). Sarah's example (vv.5-6). Husbands: live with wives "according to knowledge," honor them as co-heirs, "that your prayers be not hindered" (v.7).
Community Conduct (vv.8-12): Be unified, compassionate, loving, humble (v.8). Don't repay evil with evil but bless (v.9). Quotes Psalm 34: refrain your tongue, do good, seek peace (vv.10-11). God watches the righteous and opposes evildoers (v.12).
Suffering for Righteousness (vv.13-17): Who will harm you if you do good? (v.13). If you suffer for righteousness, you're blessed (v.14). Don't fear; sanctify Christ as Lord in your hearts (v.15). "Be ready always to give an answer" — with gentleness and respect (v.15). Keep a good conscience (v.16). Better to suffer for doing good than evil (v.17).
Christ's Victorious Suffering (vv.18-22): Christ suffered once for sins — the just for the unjust — to bring us to God (v.18). He preached to "spirits in prison" (v.19) — those disobedient in Noah's day (v.20). Baptism now saves — not physical washing but "the answer of a good conscience toward God" through Christ's resurrection (v.21). Christ is now at God's right hand with all powers subject to Him (v.22).

Deep Dive

Setting: Peter writes from Rome ("Babylon," 5:13) in the early 60s AD to mostly Gentile churches across what is now northern and central Turkey. They are slandered and pushed to the edges of their communities for turning from the gods of their families. In 2:13 Peter began a series of instructions about living under authority in that hostile world, using the familiar pattern of an ancient "household code": citizens under the emperor (2:13–17), slaves under masters (2:18–25), and now wives and husbands (3:1–7). He has just held up Christ, who suffered unjustly without reviling and "bore our sins in his body on the tree" (2:24). Chapter 3 applies that pattern to the home, then to the whole church facing hostility, and ends with one of the most debated passages in the New Testament: Christ's proclamation to "the spirits in prison," Noah's ark and baptism, and Christ's victory over every power.

Wives and Husbands (vv1–7)

  • "Likewise, wives, be subject to your own husbands" (v1). "Likewise" connects this to 2:13 and 2:18: the same Christlike posture now applied to marriage. "Be subject" (hypotassō) is a word for taking one's place in an order. It is used of Jesus submitting to his parents (Luke 2:51) and of the whole church submitting to Christ (Eph 5:24), so it does not imply that the one who submits is worth less. "Your own husbands" matters: Peter is not telling women in general to submit to men in general.
  • "So that even if some do not obey the word" (v1). Peter has in mind a wife who has become a Christian while her husband has not. In the Roman world that was a serious problem. A wife was expected to follow her husband's gods. The writer Plutarch advised brides to worship only their husband's gods and to shut the door on foreign religions. A Christian wife was already defying her husband in the deepest matter of all. Peter does not tell her to give up her faith. He tells her how to live so the faith itself is commended.
  • "They may be won without a word by the conduct of their wives" (v1). A pun: those who "do not obey the word" may be won "without a word." Not without the gospel, but without nagging or preaching at him. "Won" (kerdainō) is a merchant's word for gaining a profit, the word Paul uses for winning people to Christ (1 Cor 9:19–22). A changed life speaks where arguments would only harden.
  • "When they see your respectful and pure conduct" (v2). "See" is the same word as in 2:12, where outsiders "see your good deeds." Literally "respectful" is "in fear," which in this letter usually means reverence for God (1:17; 2:17–18). Her respect for her husband grows out of her fear of God.
  • "Do not let your adorning be external" (v3). "Adorning" is kosmos, the root of "cosmetics." Wealthy Roman women spent fortunes on elaborate braided hairstyles woven with gold and pearls, imported wigs and dyes, jewelry and expensive fabrics. Roman moralists themselves mocked it. Peter is not banning braids or rings, any more than "the clothing you wear" bans clothing. He is talking about where a woman looks for her worth. Paul says something very similar (1 Tim 2:9–10).
  • "The hidden person of the heart with the imperishable beauty of a gentle and quiet spirit" (v4). "Imperishable" is the same word used of the inheritance (1:4) and the seed of the word (1:23). Gold and good looks fade. This beauty lasts. "Gentle" (praus) is the word Jesus used of himself: "I am gentle and lowly in heart" (Matt 11:29). "Quiet" means calm and settled, not silent or timid. It describes a heart at rest in God, the opposite of the anxiety and fear Peter warns against in v6.
  • "Which in God's sight is very precious" (v4). The word is polytelēs, "costly," the word for the "very expensive" ointment poured on Jesus (Mark 14:3). The beauty the world cannot see is what God values highest.
  • "The holy women who hoped in God" (v5). Peter points to the wives of Israel's patriarchs. Their adornment came from hope in God, which is the theme of the whole letter (1:3, 13, 21; 3:15).
  • "As Sarah obeyed Abraham, calling him lord" (v6). The reference is Genesis 18:12, where Sarah, laughing to herself at the promise of a son, says "my lord is old." It was an ordinary, respectful way for a wife to refer to her husband. Peter draws a general attitude from a passing remark. It is an honest example, because Sarah was not a perfect wife (Gen 16:1–6), and Abraham was not a perfect husband. Twice he put her in danger by passing her off as his sister (Gen 12:10–20; 20:1–18), and Sarah did not betray him, though God protected her.
  • "You are her children, if you do good and do not fear anything that is frightening" (v6). Gentile women become "daughters of Sarah," just as Gentile believers are children of Abraham (Gal 3:7, 29). "Frightening" is ptoēsis, "a fluttering," panic or terror, used only here in the New Testament. It recalls Proverbs 3:25 in the Greek Old Testament: "Do not be afraid of sudden terror." A Christian wife with a hostile husband might well feel afraid. Peter wants her settled in God, not ruled by fear. Submission here is a free choice made in trust, not a terrified surrender.
  • Submission has limits. Peter has just shown, in the case of a wife choosing Christ against her husband's religion, that submission does not mean obeying a husband in everything. No passage in Scripture asks a wife to sin because her husband says so, or to stay in danger. Peter himself said, "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29).
  • "Likewise, husbands, live with your wives in an understanding way" (v7). Peter gives husbands only one verse, but it is radical for its time. Roman law gave a husband great power over his wife and expected nothing from him in return. Peter tells him to "live with" her "according to knowledge": to know her, study her, and treat her with understanding. Paul tells husbands to love their wives "as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" (Eph 5:25).
  • "Showing honor to the woman as the weaker vessel" (v7). "Vessel" (skeuos) means a container or tool. Both husband and wife are vessels in God's hands, as Paul calls himself and all believers (Acts 9:15; 2 Tim 2:21). "Weaker" is not about intelligence, character or spiritual strength. Most commentators take it as physical strength, and in Peter's world it also meant legal and social vulnerability. The point is that the stronger party must protect and honor, not exploit. Fine china is "weaker" than a cast-iron pan, and that is why it is handled with care.
  • "Since they are heirs with you of the grace of life" (v7). This is the key. In God's eyes husband and wife are equals: co-heirs of the same eternal life (Gal 3:28). Whatever differences of role exist in marriage, they exist between equal heirs.
  • "So that your prayers may not be hindered" (v7). "Hindered" is literally "cut into," as a road is cut to block an army. A man who mistreats his wife will find his relationship with God damaged. Some read "your" as the couple's shared prayers, others as the husband's own. Either way, the way a husband treats his wife matters to God.
  • Commentators: Henry: "The wife must discharge her duty to her own husband, though he obey not the word," and "a true Christian's chief care lies in right ordering his own spirit." He concludes on v7, "That you may pray much, live holily; and that you may live holily, be much in prayer." JFB quotes an early commentator, Oecumenius: "Unspoken acting is more powerful than unperformed speaking." He says the adornments are forbidden only when loved, adding, "Under costly attire there may be a humble mind." JFB calls the fear of v6 "fluttering alarm," which matches the lexicon's "a fluttering" for ptoēsis. Guzik says submission never implies inferior personhood and is limited by God's own commands. He notes Sarah obeyed even when Abraham was wrong, but distinguishes her obedience from simply being afraid. He reads "weaker" as physical, not spiritual.

Suffering for Righteousness' Sake (vv8–22)

  • "Finally, all of you" (v8). "Finally" closes the household instructions, and Peter turns to the whole church. Five qualities in a row: "unity of mind" (agreement in purpose), "sympathy" (literally feeling with someone), "brotherly love" (philadelphia, as in 1:22), "a tender heart" (deep inner compassion), and "a humble mind." In Greek and Roman culture humility was a slave's attitude, not a virtue. Christians made it one because of Jesus (Phil 2:3–8).
  • "Do not repay evil for evil or reviling for reviling, but on the contrary, bless" (v9). This is exactly what Jesus did: "When he was reviled, he did not revile in return" (2:23). And it is what he taught: "Bless those who curse you" (Luke 6:28; compare Rom 12:14, 17). To "bless" means to speak good of someone and ask God's good for them.
  • "For to this you were called, that you may obtain a blessing" (v9). "Obtain" is literally "inherit," the inheritance of 1:4. The one who blesses others will be blessed by God. It also recalls Isaac's sons: Esau wanted to inherit the blessing and lost it (Heb 12:17).
  • "Whoever desires to love life and see good days" (vv10–12). A quotation from Psalm 34:12–16, a psalm Peter already used in 2:3 ("taste and see that the Lord is good"). The title of Psalm 34 says David wrote it after escaping from the Philistine king by pretending to be mad (1 Sam 21:10–15). It is a song of God's care for a hunted man, which suits Peter's readers. "Keep his tongue from evil" ties to the slander they face. "Seek peace and pursue it": peace does not happen on its own. It has to be chased.
  • "The eyes of the Lord are on the righteous… but the face of the Lord is against those who do evil" (v12). Peter stops the quotation halfway through Psalm 34:16. The psalm goes on, "to cut off the memory of them from the earth." Peter may have left that out deliberately. He still hopes their enemies may be won (2:12; 3:1).
  • "Now who is there to harm you if you are zealous for what is good?" (v13). "Zealous" is zēlōtēs, the word for the Zealots, Jewish revolutionaries. One of Jesus' disciples was "Simon the Zealot" (Luke 6:15). Peter wants believers to be zealots for good. The question expects the answer "usually, no one." Most people do not attack those who do good, though Peter knows that sometimes they do.
  • "But even if you should suffer for righteousness' sake, you will be blessed" (v14). The form of the Greek suggests a real but not constant possibility: "even if it should happen." "Blessed" echoes Jesus: "Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake" (Matt 5:10). Peter heard that sermon.
  • "Have no fear of them, nor be troubled, but in your hearts honor Christ the Lord as holy" (vv14–15). From Isaiah 8:12–13. When Assyria threatened Judah, God told Isaiah, "Do not fear what they fear… But the LORD of hosts, him you shall regard as holy. Let him be your fear." Peter puts "Christ" where Isaiah has "the LORD," another quiet statement of Jesus' deity. Some later manuscripts read "the Lord God," but the oldest manuscripts read "Christ," as the ESV does. The cure for fear of people is a greater fear of God, a reverence that sets Christ apart as Lord at the center of the heart.
  • "Always being prepared to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you" (v15). "Defense" is apologia, the word for a speech in court answering charges. Paul used it of his defense before the Jerusalem crowd and before Agrippa (Acts 22:1; 26:2). It gives us the word "apologetics." But Peter means more than formal debate. Anyone might ask, a neighbor, a husband, a magistrate. Notice what they ask about: not your rules but your hope. A hopeful life in a hopeless world raises questions.
  • "Yet do it with gentleness and respect" (v15). The manner matters as much as the answer. "Respect" is literally "fear," and may mean reverence before God, respect toward the questioner, or both. The Peter who once cut off a man's ear (John 18:10) now urges gentleness.
  • "Having a good conscience" (v16). A clear conscience means the slander is false. Peter wants the accusation to have nothing to stick to, so that "those who revile your good behavior in Christ may be put to shame." The shame they meant for believers will rebound on them (2:12).
  • "It is better to suffer for doing good, if that should be God's will, than for doing evil" (v17). "If that should be God's will" means suffering is not random. It is under God's hand. This prepares for the great example that follows: Christ suffered for doing good, and it was God's will.
  • "Christ also suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous" (v18). "Also" links him to suffering believers, but his suffering was unique. "Once" (hapax) means once for all, never to be repeated (Heb 9:26–28; 10:10). "The righteous for the unrighteous" is substitution in four words. Some manuscripts read "died" instead of "suffered," which the ESV notes. Both are well attested, and the meaning is the same.
  • "That he might bring us to God" (v18). "Bring" (prosagō) was used of bringing someone into a king's presence, and the related noun is Paul's word for "access" to God (Rom 5:2; Eph 2:18). The whole purpose of the cross is relationship. Christ died to bring people home to God.
  • "Being put to death in the flesh but made alive in the spirit" (v18). "Flesh" and "spirit" here are not two parts of Jesus (body dies, soul lives on). They describe two realms or conditions. In the realm of ordinary human life Jesus was killed. In the realm of the Spirit, the new life of the age to come, he was raised. The ESV uses lowercase "spirit"; some versions capitalize it as "the Spirit," since Scripture says the Spirit raised Jesus (Rom 8:11). The Greek can be read either way.
  • "In which he went and proclaimed to the spirits in prison" (v19). This verse has puzzled readers from the beginning. Martin Luther said of it, "a wonderful text and a more obscure passage perhaps than any other in the New Testament, so that I do not know for a certainty just what Peter means." There are three main views, and faithful Christians hold each one.
  • View 1: Christ preached through Noah. Before the flood, the pre-existent Christ, by the Spirit, preached through Noah, "a herald of righteousness" (2 Pet 2:5), to the people of Noah's day, who refused to listen and whose spirits are now in prison. This was Augustine's view and is held by many Reformed interpreters. It fits "the Spirit of Christ" speaking through prophets (1:11). JFB takes this view, and Matthew Henry's reading is close to it. Its weakness is that "went" naturally suggests something Christ did after his death, which is what v18 has just described.
  • View 2: Christ announced his victory to fallen angels. After his death and resurrection, Christ proclaimed his triumph to evil spirits imprisoned for rebelling in Noah's time. In the New Testament "spirits" without further description usually means non-human spirits. 2 Peter 2:4–5 and Jude 6 speak of angels kept "in chains" in the same breath as Noah. A popular Jewish book of the time, 1 Enoch, told at length how "the sons of God" in Genesis 6:1–4 were angels who sinned and were imprisoned. Peter's readers would likely have known this story. On this view "proclaimed" (kēryssō) means announced as a herald, not offered salvation, and v22 completes the thought: "angels, authorities, and powers having been subjected to him." This is the most common view among scholars today, and the one Guzik and the Tyndale notes favor.
  • View 3: Christ descended to the dead. Between his death and resurrection Christ went to the realm of the dead. The early church taught that he went there to bring out the Old Testament faithful, and Catholic, Orthodox and Lutheran traditions see this as part of the creed's line "he descended into hell" (or "to the dead"). Some have added that he gave the dead of Noah's day a second chance. The Tyndale notes object that the New Testament nowhere teaches a chance to respond to the gospel after death (Heb 9:27), and most interpreters in all traditions reject that addition.
  • What all the views agree on. Whatever exactly happened, Christ's death and resurrection were a victory over sin, death and every hostile power, and Peter's readers, facing hostile powers of their own, are on the winning side.
  • "Because they formerly did not obey, when God's patience waited in the days of Noah" (v20). Many read Genesis 6:3 as God giving the world 120 more years before the flood, and Noah built the ark in full view of a mocking world. God's patience is still waiting, giving people time to repent (2 Pet 3:9).
  • "A few, that is, eight persons, were brought safely through water" (v20). Noah, his wife, his three sons and their wives (Gen 7:13). Peter's readers were a small minority too. "Through water" can mean through the water as a danger or by means of the water. The same flood that destroyed the world carried the ark.
  • "Baptism, which corresponds to this, now saves you" (v21). "Corresponds" is antitypos, "antitype," the real thing that the earlier event pictured. The flood was a judgment that separated those inside the ark from those outside. Baptism is the believer's passage through death into new life (Rom 6:3–4).
  • "Not as a removal of dirt from the body but as an appeal to God for a good conscience" (v21). Peter immediately guards against misunderstanding. Water does not save by washing skin. "Appeal" is eperōtēma, used only here in the New Testament. It originally meant a question or request, and in the second century it was used for a formal pledge in a contract. So it may mean an appeal to God for a clean conscience, or a pledge to God from a good conscience, the believer's answer to the questions asked at baptism ("Do you turn to Christ?"). The lexicon supports both. Either way, what saves is not the ritual but the person turning to God in faith.
  • Different views of baptism. Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran and Anglican traditions read "baptism now saves you" as teaching that God truly gives saving grace through baptism, received by faith. Baptist and many evangelical traditions read it as baptism being the outward sign and pledge of a salvation received by faith, saving in the sense that it expresses and seals the inner reality. All agree with Peter's qualifier: baptism does not work like magic, and its power comes "through the resurrection of Jesus Christ."
  • "Who has gone into heaven and is at the right hand of God" (v22). Peter watched Jesus taken up into heaven (Acts 1:9) and preached on Pentecost that he is exalted "at the right hand of God" (Acts 2:33–35), quoting Psalm 110:1, the most quoted Old Testament verse in the New. The right hand is the place of authority.
  • "With angels, authorities, and powers having been subjected to him" (v22). The same verb as the "be subject" of 2:13, 2:18 and 3:1. Believers are called to submit. Christ has had every power in the universe submitted to him (Eph 1:20–22; Col 2:15). The chapter that began with wives submitting ends with the cosmos submitting to Christ. Whatever powers hassle these small churches, from local slanderers to Nero to unseen spiritual forces, are already under his feet.
  • Commentators: Henry: "He that knew no sin, suffered instead of those who knew no righteousness," and the design of Christ's sufferings was "to reconcile us to God, and to bring us to eternal glory." On baptism he warns, "Let us beware that we rest not upon outward forms." Henry takes the spirits in prison as the disobedient people of Noah's day, now in "the prison of hell," and JFB says Christ preached to them by his Spirit through Noah. JFB says firmly that no "conversion of the spirits in prison" is mentioned. Guzik prefers the fallen-angel view, with Christ announcing judgment and victory, but quotes G. Campbell Morgan that the text does not reveal the message or its result, so no one should be dogmatic. All three insist that the outward water does not save in itself. JFB reads eperōtēma as the baptismal "interrogation" with its answer, close to the "pledge" sense in the lexicon. He also notes that the oldest manuscripts read "Christ" in v15, and Guzik agrees. Guzik points out that "bring us to God" uses the word for being brought into a king's presence.

Pointing to Christ

The chapter turns on v18: "the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God." Christ is the model for the believing wife who wins her husband without a word, for the husband who uses his strength to honor rather than dominate, and for the church that blesses those who revile it. Christ did all of that first: he did not revile in return, he gave himself for his bride, and he suffered once for all. But he is more than a model. His death brings people into God's presence. His resurrection makes baptism more than water. His ascension puts every authority and power beneath him. The fearful are told to "honor Christ the Lord as holy," because the one who sits at God's right hand is the one who suffered for them.

Reflection

  • Inner Beauty of a Gentle Spirit (v.4) — Peter values the imperishable beauty of a gentle and quiet spirit far above outward appearance. Where are you investing more energy — your image or your character?
  • Honoring Your Spouse for the Sake of Prayer (v.7) — Husbands are told to treat their wives with honor and understanding so that nothing hinders their prayers. Is there unresolved conflict in a close relationship that's quietly blocking your relationship with God?
  • Returning Blessing for Insult (v.9) — Believers are called to repay evil and insult not in kind but with blessing, since that's the very thing they were called to. When you're wronged, is your instinct to retaliate or to bless?
  • Ready to Give a Reason for Your Hope (v.15) — Peter tells believers to always be prepared to explain their hope, but to do it with gentleness and respect. If someone asked you today why you have hope, could you answer clearly and humbly?
  • Suffering for Good, Not for Wrong (v.17) — If suffering comes, Peter says it's far better to suffer for doing right than for doing wrong. Is your current hardship the result of righteousness or of your own poor choices?
  • Christ's Suffering That Brings Us to God (v.18) — Christ, the righteous one, suffered once for sins so that he might bring us to God, not merely forgive us from a distance. Are you living in that nearness to God, or keeping him at arm's length?
