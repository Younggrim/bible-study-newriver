Summary

A wisdom psalm describing the character required to dwell in God's presence. It opens with the question of who may stay in God's tent and live on his holy hill (v. 1), then answers with a list of qualities: walking blamelessly, doing what is right, and speaking truth honestly from the heart (v. 2). Such a person does not slander others, harm a neighbor, or spread reproach, but honors those who fear the LORD while despising those who reject him, and keeps promises even when it costs something (vv. 3-4). This person also refuses to profit from exploitative lending or bribery against the innocent, and the psalm concludes that whoever lives this way will never be shaken (v. 5).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Torah/Entrance Liturgy Psalm
Attributed Author: David — "A Psalm of David"
Key Themes: Requirements for dwelling with God, ethical character, truthful speech, integrity in relationships, financial justice, keeping vows
Historical Context: Psalm 15 is an "entrance liturgy" — likely used at the gates of the tabernacle/temple when worshippers asked who may enter God's presence. The question (v.1) receives a ten-point ethical answer (vv.2-5) rather than a ritual one. Notably, none of the qualifications are ceremonial — they are all moral and relational. This is remarkable: access to God is determined by character, not ritual compliance. This may have been composed when David brought the Ark to Jerusalem (2 Sam 6) and established tabernacle worship there. Compare Isaiah 33:14-16 and Psalm 24:3-5 for parallel entrance liturgies.
Who Shall Abide in Thy Tabernacle (v.1): LORD, who shall abide in thy tabernacle, who shall dwell in thy holy hill. One verse, and it is a question of the kind a pilgrim arriving at the sanctuary gate would ask, which is probably its original setting. Psalm 24:3 asks the same question in almost the same words and answers it more briefly.
He That Walketh Uprightly (vv.2-5a): The answer is a list and every item is conduct rather than belief, which is the point of it: he that walketh uprightly, and worketh righteousness, and speaketh the truth in his heart. Then the negatives, he that backbiteth not with his tongue, nor doeth evil to his neighbour, nor taketh up a reproach against his neighbour. Three of the ten concern speech about other people. Two are unexpectedly specific and costly, he that sweareth to his own hurt, and changeth not, that is, keeps a bargain that turns out badly for him, and he that putteth not out his money to usury. The section stops in the middle of verse 5 because the qualifications end there.
He That Doeth These Things Shall Never Be Moved (v.5b): Half a verse, and it is the answer to the question in verse 1. He that doeth these things shall never be moved. Nothing is promised about prosperity or long life, only stability, and it is attached to the list rather than to the temple: the person described is secure whether or not he is standing on the holy hill.

Deep Dive

Setting: The title reads simply "A Psalm of David." It may have been sung by worshipers arriving at the sanctuary, a kind of question-and-answer at the gate. Psalm 24:3–6 follows the same pattern ("Who shall ascend the hill of the LORD?"), and Isaiah 33:14–16 echoes it. In David's day the LORD's dwelling was a tent: the ark of the covenant sat in a tent David pitched for it in Jerusalem (2 Sam 6:17), while the older tabernacle stood at Gibeon (1 Chr 16:39). Psalm 14 ended with the verdict that "there is none who does good" and a longing for salvation from Zion. Psalm 15 asks the next question: who can actually live with God on Zion? The answer is a portrait of a person of integrity, especially in speech and money, the two areas where Psalms 12 and 14 found people most corrupt. A later Jewish tradition in the Talmud (Makkot 24a) says that David here summed up the Law's many commands in eleven. The psalm is short and plain, but it is not a list of entry requirements to earn God's favor. It describes the kind of person who belongs in his presence. It opens with a question (v1), gives the answer in ten or so lines (vv2–5a), and closes with a promise (v5b).

Who Shall Dwell on Your Holy Hill? (v1)

  • "O LORD, who shall sojourn in your tent?" (v1). "Sojourn" (gur) is a word for living somewhere as a guest or resident foreigner, not as an owner. Abraham "sojourned" in Canaan (Gen 20:1). In the ancient world a guest in someone's tent was under the host's protection: to harm a guest was a disgrace to the host. To sojourn in God's tent means to be welcomed into his house and kept safe there. Psalm 23:6 has the same hope: "I shall dwell in the house of the LORD forever."
  • "Who shall dwell on your holy hill?" (v1). "Dwell" (shakan) is a word for settling down and living somewhere. It gives us the later Jewish word Shekinah, God's dwelling presence. The "holy hill" is Zion, where God set his King (2:6) and from which he answers prayer (3:4). The parallel lines move from a temporary stay to a settled home.
  • The question is serious. God is holy. Psalm 5:4 said, "evil may not dwell with you." When the people of Beth-shemeth were struck for looking into the ark, they cried, "Who is able to stand before the LORD, this holy God?" (1 Sam 6:20). The question of Psalm 15 is not, "Who is allowed in the building?" It is, "Who can live close to a holy God?"
  • Commentators: Henry says the psalm's aim is to show "the way to heaven," and that "if we would be happy, we must be holy and honest." He also notes that the question asks "not, who by name, but who by description." JFB reads "abide" as "sojourn," under God's protection, and the tabernacle as "seat of the ark." Guzik passes on Maclaren's comment that in the ancient world "a guest was sheltered from all harm," and notes that in David's day the ark and the tabernacle were in two places, Jerusalem and Gibeon.

Blameless in Walk and Word (vv2–3)

  • "He who walks blamelessly" (v2). "Blamelessly" (tamim) means whole, complete, with integrity. It is used of Noah (Gen 6:9), of Abraham's calling ("walk before me, and be blameless," Gen 17:1), and of sacrificial animals "without blemish." It does not mean sinless. It means wholehearted and consistent, the same person in private and in public. JFB: "in a complete manner, as to all parts of conduct," not as to degree.
  • "And does what is right" (v2). Literally "works righteousness." Faith shows up in actions.
  • "And speaks truth in his heart" (v2). This is the opposite of 12:2, where people spoke "with flattering lips and a double heart." Here truth goes all the way down. What he says matches what he thinks. Jesus said, "out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks" (Matt 12:34).
  • "Who does not slander with his tongue" (v3). The Hebrew verb (ragal) comes from the word for "foot." It pictures someone walking around from house to house, carrying gossip. Leviticus 19:16 uses a related word: "You shall not go around as a slanderer among your people."
  • "And does no evil to his neighbor" (v3). "Neighbor" (rea) means anyone near, a companion or fellow citizen. The person God welcomes does not harm those closest to him.
  • "Nor takes up a reproach against his friend" (v3). "Takes up" could mean starting a slur or simply picking one up and passing it on. Both are excluded. It takes two people for gossip to spread, the one who speaks and the one who listens and repeats.
  • The order matters. Verses 2–3 alternate between positive (walks, does, speaks) and negative (does not slander, does no evil, does not take up). Three of the first six lines are about speech. After Psalm 12's world of lies, this is pointed.
  • Commentators: JFB says "in his heart" means "sincerely," and on v3, "He neither slanders nor spreads slander." Guzik calls gossip and backbiting one of the most damaging sins in the church, and passes on Boice that "more damage has been done to the church and its work by gossip, criticism, and slander" than by almost anything else. Guzik also makes a helpful distinction: under the old covenant a right walk was the condition of fellowship with God, while in Christ a right walk is the fruit of a fellowship already given by grace (1 John 1:6–7).

Honoring Those Who Fear the LORD (v4)

  • "In whose eyes a vile person is despised" (v4). This sounds harsh, but the point is the scale of values. Most societies flatter the powerful, even when they are wicked (12:8, "vileness is exalted"). The person who belongs to God does not admire people just because they are rich, famous or successful. "Vile" here means rejected, someone God has rejected because of his wickedness. It is not permission to treat people with contempt. It is a refusal to honor evil.
  • "But who honors those who fear the LORD" (v4). The flip side: he gives real honor to godly people, even if they are poor and unknown. King David himself honored the prophets and the faithful, and James warns churches against fawning over the rich and dishonoring the poor (Jas 2:1–9).
  • "Who swears to his own hurt and does not change" (v4). He makes a promise, it turns out to cost him, and he keeps it anyway. The classic example is Joshua's oath to the Gibeonites. Israel was tricked into a treaty, but they kept their word (Josh 9:15–20), and God later punished Saul for breaking it (2 Sam 21:1–2). The ancient Greek translation reads "who swears to his neighbor," taking the Hebrew letters slightly differently, but the ESV follows the standard Hebrew. Jesus took this further: let your yes be yes and your no be no (Matt 5:37).
  • Commentators: JFB: "Love and hate are regulated by a regard to God," and he reads "sweareth . . . to his hurt" as an oath that harms the one who swears it. Guzik passes on Spurgeon that believers should be as honest in paying respect as in paying their bills, and Trapp's example that Joshua and the elders kept their oath to the Gibeonites "though to their inconvenience."

Money and Justice (v5a)

  • "Who does not put out his money at interest" (v5). The Hebrew word for interest (neshek) comes from a verb meaning "to bite." Interest was seen as taking a bite out of a borrower. The Law of Moses forbade charging interest to a poor fellow Israelite: "If you lend money to any of my people with you who is poor... you shall not exact interest from him" (Exod 22:25; Lev 25:35–37; Deut 23:19–20). Loans in Israel were mostly to people in trouble, after a bad harvest or a disaster, not business loans to fund growth. Charging interest meant profiting from a neighbor's misfortune. Nehemiah later rebuked wealthy Jews for doing exactly that (Neh 5:1–13).
  • How Christians apply it. Christians have read this in different ways. For centuries the church forbade all lending at interest. Most Christians today distinguish between exploiting the poor and lending in a commercial economy, while still holding that profiting from another's need is wrong. The heart of the command is mercy toward the vulnerable.
  • "And does not take a bribe against the innocent" (v5). Bribery corrupts justice. The Law warned, "a bribe blinds the clear-sighted and subverts the cause of those who are in the right" (Exod 23:8; Deut 16:19). This line is especially aimed at judges and officials. A person who belongs to God will not sell out an innocent person for money.
  • The two money lines go together. Both are about refusing to gain at another's expense. Psalm 14:4 described evildoers who "eat up my people as they eat bread." Psalm 15 describes the opposite kind of person.
  • Commentators: JFB links "usury" to a word meaning "to bite" and says, "All gains made by the wrongful loss of others are forbidden," and "Bribery of all sorts is denounced." Guzik argues the real issue is greed overriding justice to the poor, and passes on Kidner that usury is condemned in Scripture not in general but as exploiting a brother's misfortune. He also points to Nehemiah 5.

He Shall Never Be Moved (v5b)

  • "He who does these things shall never be moved" (v5). The psalm asked who may dwell with God. It ends by promising that such a person will stand. "Moved" (mot) is the word the wicked man boasted about in 10:6 ("I shall not be moved") and the believer feared in 13:4 ("because I am shaken"). Real stability does not come from self-confidence or success. It comes from belonging to God and living his way. Psalm 16:8 will add the deeper reason: "Because he is at my right hand, I shall not be shaken."
  • "Does these things." The psalm ends with doing, not only knowing. Jesus ended the Sermon on the Mount the same way: the one "who hears these words of mine and does them" builds on rock and stands in the storm (Matt 7:24–25).
  • Does anyone actually meet this standard? Read straight after Psalm 14 ("none who does good"), Psalm 15 raises the obvious question. No one keeps it perfectly. The psalm is not an exam to pass before God lets someone in. It is a description of what God's people look like, and of what he is shaping them to be. It also points beyond any ordinary person to the one who did.
  • Commentators: JFB: "Such persons admitted to God's presence and favor shall never be moved." Henry says such a person "is like Zion-hill itself, which cannot be moved" (125:1). Guzik says that the psalm's demands, seen honestly, drive believers to Christ, whose perfect righteousness is credited to them, and passes on G. Campbell Morgan's warning: "To continue in sin is to frustrate the very purpose of God in grace."

Pointing to Christ

Only one man has ever fully answered Psalm 15. Jesus walked blamelessly, spoke truth in his heart, never slandered, kept his word to his own hurt and never took a bribe. He alone could ascend God's holy hill by right. And he did more than walk in. He made a way for others. Hebrews says that by his own blood Christ entered "once for all into the holy places" (Heb 9:12), so that sinners can now "draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith" (Heb 10:22). Those who come through him are welcomed as guests in God's tent and kept there. And the Spirit he gives begins to shape them into the person this psalm describes: truthful, loyal, generous and unshakable.

Reflection

  • Access to God Is About Character, Not Ceremony (v.1) — The question of who may dwell with God is answered with ethics rather than ritual requirements. Does your pursuit of God focus more on right behavior toward others than on religious performance?
  • Truth That Starts Internally (v.2) — The person who may dwell with God speaks truth from the heart, not merely with the lips. Are you honest with yourself, or do you allow self-deception to go unchallenged?
  • Keeping Your Word Even When It Costs You (v.4) — The qualified person keeps a promise even after circumstances make it painful to fulfill. Is there a commitment you are tempted to abandon now that it has become inconvenient?
  • Integrity Produces Stability (v.5) — The psalm promises that the person of integrity will never be shaken. Does your life show the settledness that comes from consistent character, or the instability that comes from compromise?
