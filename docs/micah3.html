Summary

Micah confronts Israel's leaders, who should uphold justice but instead oppress the people so violently it is likened to stripping their skin and eating their flesh (vv. 1-3); when trouble comes, the Lord will hide His face from them (v. 4). He condemns prophets who cry "peace" for pay but turn on anyone who won't pay them, promising they will lose their visions to darkness and shame (vv. 5-7). By contrast, Micah is filled with the Lord's Spirit and power to expose Jacob's sin (v. 8), rebuking corrupt leaders, priests, and prophets who profit from bribery yet falsely assume the Lord guarantees their safety (vv. 9-11). Because of this corruption, Zion will be plowed like a field and Jerusalem reduced to rubble (v. 12).

Authorship & Background

Author: Micah of Moresheth. Prophesied approximately 735-700 BC to both Israel and Judah. Contemporary with Isaiah. Key theme: social justice, true religion, and Messianic hope. Famous verses: 'But thou, Bethlehem Ephratah... out of thee shall he come forth' (5:2) and 'What doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?' (6:8).
Classification: Prophetic Indictment — Against Corrupt Leaders and False Prophets
Key Themes: Leadership accountability, corrupt judges, false prophets, true prophetic authority, Jerusalem's coming destruction
Historical Context: Chapter 3 intensifies Micah's indictment by naming the guilty parties directly: the rulers ("heads of Jacob"), the judges, the priests, and the prophets. Each leadership class has failed in its God-given role. The rulers who should protect the people instead devour them. The prophets who should speak truth instead speak for profit. The priests who should teach God's law instead sell their services. This chapter likely dates to the reign of either Ahaz (a wicked king) or early in Hezekiah's reign before his reforms. The corruption was systemic—every institution meant to serve the people and honor God had been co-opted for personal gain. Micah stands as the lone truthful voice, empowered by God's Spirit (v. 8), against an entire establishment of self-serving leaders. The chapter concludes with one of the most shocking prophecies in the Old Testament: "Zion shall be plowed as a field" (v. 12). This prophecy was so memorable that it was cited 100 years later to save Jeremiah's life (Jeremiah 26:18). The elders recalled that Hezekiah had responded to Micah's warning with repentance rather than execution—and they argued Jeremiah deserved the same mercy.
Rulers Who Eat the Flesh of My People (vv.1-4): The guilty are named by office rather than left general: the heads of Jacob and princes of Israel, whose job was to know judgment. The imagery is cannibalism — they pluck off the skin, break the bones, chop them in pieces as for the pot — which is as violent as the prophets get about economic exploitation. The sentence in v.4 is silence: they will cry to the LORD and he will not hear them, because they have behaved ill in their doings.
Prophets Who Divine for Money (vv.5-7): The second class indicted are prophets who bite with their teeth and cry peace, and who declare war against anyone who does not put something in their mouths. The message follows the payment. Their punishment fits the trade: night instead of vision, darkness instead of divination, the sun going down on them, and they shall cover their lips because there is no answer from God.
But I Am Full of Power by the Spirit (v.8): Against the hired prophets Micah sets himself in a single verse: truly I am full of power by the Spirit of the LORD, and of judgment, and of might, to declare to Jacob his transgression. The contrast is content rather than sincerity — what marks him is willingness to name the sin, which is precisely what the paid prophets would not do.
Building Zion with Blood (vv.9-11): The summary indictment gathers all three classes: heads who judge for reward, priests who teach for hire, prophets who divine for money. The line that condemns them is their confidence — they lean on the LORD and say is not the LORD among us, none evil can come upon us. The offense is not unbelief but a belief in protection held while doing all of this.
Zion Shall Be Plowed as a Field (v.12): The verdict is one verse and it names the temple: Zion plowed as a field, Jerusalem become heaps, the mountain of the house as the high places of the forest. It was remembered. A century later the elders quoted this verse to save Jeremiah's life in Jeremiah 26:18, recalling that Hezekiah had answered Micah with repentance rather than execution — which is the only recorded case of a king responding to a prophecy this severe by changing course.

  • Zion (v.10, v.12): The hill on which Jerusalem/the Temple stood; often used poetically for God's dwelling.

Setting: Jerusalem, probably in the years before Hezekiah's reforms, about 730–715 BC. Micah's first round of messages (chs 1–2) ended with a bright promise of the LORD breaking out at the head of his flock. Now the second round begins, again with the word "Hear" (3:1; compare 1:2; 6:1). Chapter 3 is Micah's sharpest attack on the men who ran the nation: the rulers and judges who should have upheld justice, the prophets who sold their messages for meals, and the priests who taught for a fee. It ends with the most shocking sentence in the book, that Zion itself would be ploughed like a field. A century later that one verse saved the prophet Jeremiah's life, when elders reminded a mob that King Hezekiah had heard it and repented (Jer 26:17–19). The ESV gives the chapter a single heading.

Rulers and Prophets Denounced (vv1–12)

  • "And I said: Hear, you heads of Jacob and rulers of the house of Israel!" (v1). "Heads" and "rulers" (qatsin, a word for commanders or magistrates) are the leaders who sat in judgment at the city gate. "Jacob" and "Israel" here mean the whole people of God, though Micah's target is Jerusalem. "And I said" links back to chapter 2. Whatever the false prophets promised, here is what the true prophet said.
  • "Is it not for you to know justice?" (v1). Justice (mishpat) is the key word of this chapter (vv1, 8, 9) and of the whole book (6:8). It means more than fair verdicts. It is a society set right, the weak protected, each person given what is due. These men were appointed precisely to know it and do it (Deut 16:18–20). Their office made their sin worse, because they knew better.
  • "You who hate the good and love the evil" (v2). This is a complete moral inversion. Amos, a generation earlier, had begged Israel's north to do the opposite: "Hate evil, and love good, and establish justice in the gate" (Amos 5:15). Isaiah: "Woe to those who call evil good and good evil" (Isa 5:20).
  • "Who tear the skin from off my people and their flesh from off their bones" (vv2–3). Micah's picture is a butcher's shop. Skin flayed, bones cracked open for marrow, meat chopped for the stewpot. The leaders were not literally cannibals, but their treatment of the poor was exactly as total and as merciless. The land grabs of chapter 2 left people with nothing. Psalm 14:4 uses the same picture: evildoers "eat up my people as they eat bread." "My people" appears twice. These were God's people being devoured, not theirs. Ezekiel later uses the same image of shepherds who eat the flock instead of feeding it (Ezek 34:2–3).
  • "Then they will cry to the LORD, but he will not answer them" (v4). "Then" means when the judgment comes. JFB rightly says this cry is the cry of pain, not of repentance. Men who refused to hear the cry of the poor will find God refuses to hear them (Prov 21:13). "He will hide his face from them." This is the reverse of the priestly blessing, "the LORD make his face to shine upon you" (Num 6:25). God had warned that he would hide his face if his people turned away (Deut 31:17–18).
  • "The prophets who lead my people astray, who cry 'Peace' when they have something to eat" (v5). The Hebrew is vivid: "who bite with their teeth and cry 'Peace.'" A good meal earns a good prophecy. "Peace" (shalom) means wholeness and prosperity, the message every audience wants to hear. Jeremiah later fought the same kind of prophets, who "have healed the wound of my people lightly, saying, 'Peace, peace,' when there is no peace" (Jer 6:14).
  • "But declare war against him who puts nothing into their mouths" (v5). Literally "they sanctify war," calling a holy war down on the man who will not pay. The same prophet who blessed the generous host would curse the stingy one. Their message changed with their income. JFB explains it as proclaiming war as if it were God's holy judgment.
  • "Therefore it shall be night to you, without vision, and darkness to you, without divination" (v6). A fitting judgment: men who misused God's word will lose it altogether. "Divination" (qesem) was a pagan practice forbidden in Israel (Deut 18:10). That Micah uses it of Judah's prophets shows how far they had sunk. The Tyndale notes compare them to the court prophets of Assyria and Mari, paid to tell kings what they wanted to hear. Amos warned of the same famine, "not a famine of bread… but of hearing the words of the LORD" (Amos 8:11).
  • "The sun shall go down on the prophets, and the day shall be black over them" (v6). When disaster strikes, their predictions of peace will be exposed, and they will have nothing left to say.
  • "They shall all cover their lips, for there is no answer from God" (v7). Literally "cover the moustache." Covering the upper lip was a sign of shame and mourning, the gesture required of a leper (Lev 13:45; Ezek 24:17, 22). The prophets will be like lepers, silent and unclean. Saul experienced this silence before his death: "the LORD did not answer him, either by dreams, or by Urim, or by prophets" (1 Sam 28:6).
  • "But as for me, I am filled with power, with the Spirit of the LORD, and with justice and might" (v8). The contrast is emphatic in Hebrew: "But I, on the other hand." The false prophets were full of food. Micah is full of God's Spirit. He names four things that fill him: power (the strength to speak), the Spirit of the LORD (the source of it all), justice (a true sense of right and wrong, the very thing the rulers lacked in v1), and might (courage). He is not boasting in himself. He is saying where his message comes from, and why he cannot be bought.
  • "To declare to Jacob his transgression and to Israel his sin" (v8). The test of a true prophet in Israel was not success or popularity but whether he spoke God's word, including the hard parts (Deut 18:18–22; Jer 23:21–22, 28–29). Micah's job description was to name the sin no one else would name. Isaiah received the same charge: "Declare to my people their transgression" (Isa 58:1).
  • "Hear this, you heads of the house of Jacob… who detest justice and make crooked all that is straight" (v9). Micah turns to rulers, priests and prophets together. "Detest" is the strong word used for what is abominable. The men appointed to keep the path straight have twisted every part of it.
  • "Who build Zion with blood and Jerusalem with iniquity" (v10). Jerusalem in these years was growing fast. Hezekiah would later extend the city walls and build the famous water tunnel (2 Chr 32:5, 30). But Micah says the new houses of the rich were built on stolen land and with the lives of the poor. Habakkuk later echoes him: "Woe to him who builds a town with blood" (Hab 2:12). So does Jeremiah, against King Jehoiakim's new palace (Jer 22:13).
  • "Its heads give judgment for a bribe; its priests teach for a price; its prophets practice divination for money" (v11). All three offices God gave his people for their good had become businesses. Judges were forbidden to take bribes (Exod 23:8; Deut 16:19). Priests were to teach God's law to the people as part of their calling (Lev 10:11; Mal 2:7), supported by the tithes, not by fees per lesson. Prophets were to speak what God gave, whatever the cost. Henry puts it well: "Magistracy and ministry are two great ordinances of God, for good to his church," and here both were corrupted together.
  • "Yet they lean on the LORD and say, 'Is not the LORD in the midst of us? No disaster shall come upon us'" (v11). This is the most dangerous kind of religion: corruption combined with confidence. They pointed to the temple. God lives here, so this city can never fall. Isaiah and the psalms did celebrate God's presence in Zion (Ps 46:5; Isa 12:6), and the city would be spared from Assyria in 701 BC. But they had turned a promise into a superstition. Jeremiah later faced the same slogan: "Do not trust in these deceptive words: 'This is the temple of the LORD'" (Jer 7:4).
  • "Therefore because of you Zion shall be plowed as a field" (v12). "Because of you" points at the leaders. Their sin will bring down the whole city. A ploughed field means a city gone, its stones cleared away and the ground farmed over. "Jerusalem shall become a heap of ruins," the same fate Micah pronounced on Samaria (1:6). Jerusalem had looked down on Samaria. Now it hears the same sentence.
  • "And the mountain of the house a wooded height" (v12). The temple mount, the holiest place on earth for Israel, would become an overgrown hill, like the pagan "high places" Micah condemned in 1:5. It was fulfilled when Babylon destroyed the city in 586 BC (Lam 5:18, "Mount Zion… lies desolate; jackals prowl over it"), and again when Rome destroyed it in AD 70. JFB passes on a Jewish tradition from the Talmud and Maimonides that a Roman officer literally ran a plough over the temple site after its destruction.
  • The verse that saved Jeremiah. This verse is the only time one Old Testament prophet is quoted word for word, by name and with his hometown, in another prophet's book. When Jeremiah was on trial for his life for predicting the temple's fall, elders stood up and said: "Micah of Moresheth prophesied in the days of Hezekiah… 'Zion shall be plowed as a field…' Did Hezekiah king of Judah and all Judah put him to death? Did he not fear the LORD and entreat the favor of the LORD, and did not the LORD relent of the disaster?" (Jer 26:18–19). Micah's preaching was heard. The threatened judgment was delayed for over a century because a king repented.
  • The chapter break hides a contrast. The next verse, 4:1, begins: "It shall come to pass in the latter days that the mountain of the house of the LORD shall be established as the highest of the mountains." The same phrase, "the mountain of the house," ends chapter 3 in ruins and begins chapter 4 in glory. The hill that will be ploughed is the hill God will one day raise above every other.
  • Commentators: JFB reads the flesh and bones as robbing countrymen of everything they had, and notes that Ezekiel uses the same pot image for the judgment that answers it (Ezek 24:3–5): "Men cannot expect to do ill and fare well." On v8 he explains "might" as moral courage and links the Spirit's power with Luke 24:49 and Acts 1:8. He notes that teaching and judging were meant to be given freely. Henry divides the chapter into a word to the princes, a word to the "false flattering prophets," and a word to both "acting in conjunction for the ruin of the kingdom." He sees Micah's boldness grounded in his commission from God. Guzik argues that leaders exist for the sake of the people, not the people for the leaders. He cites Waltke, who says the grotesque imagery is meant to wake up deadened consciences. On v8 he quotes Spurgeon's image of a mill wheel turning without grinding any grain, activity without the Spirit's power. With Boice, he highlights that Micah, unlike most prophets, was actually heard in his lifetime.

Pointing to Christ

Micah stood against shepherds who ate the flock, prophets who sold their words, and priests who taught for pay. Jesus is the answer to all three. He is the Good Shepherd who does not devour his sheep but "lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11). He is the true Prophet, filled with the Spirit like Micah but without measure (John 3:34; Luke 4:18), who spoke the Father's words whatever it cost him. He is the High Priest who gives freely what Micah's priests sold: "you received without paying; give without pay" (Matt 10:8). He also repeated Micah's warning. Weeping over the same city, he said, "there will not be left here one stone upon another" (Matt 24:2). But the mountain Micah saw ploughed is the mountain from which the word of the LORD goes out to the nations (4:1–2), and from Jerusalem the risen Christ sent his gospel to the ends of the earth (Luke 24:47).

Reflection

  • Leaders Who Devour Instead of Protect (vv.2-3) — Micah pictures corrupt rulers consuming the very people they were appointed to care for, like meat prepared for a pot. If you have any authority or influence over others — at work, at home, in your church — are you using it to serve them or to serve yourself?
  • Preaching Peace for a Price (v.5) — the false prophets tailor their message to whoever feeds them, promising peace to those who pay and declaring war on those who don't. Are you ever tempted to soften the truth because saying it plainly would cost you something?
  • Empowered by the Spirit, Not by Approval (v.8) — Micah's authority to confront sin comes from being filled with God's Spirit, not from popularity or position. Where do you need the courage to speak what's true even if it costs you approval?
  • Presuming on God's Presence (v.11) — corrupt leaders lean on the LORD and assume nothing bad can happen to them simply because they claim His name. Are you counting on God's favor while living in a way that contradicts His character?
