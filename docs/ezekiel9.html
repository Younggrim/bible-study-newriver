10But as for Me, I will not look on them with pity, nor will I spare them. I will bring their deeds down upon their own heads.”

11Then the man clothed in linen with the writing kit at his side reported back, “I have done as You commanded.”

Summary

God summons six executioners and a scribe dressed in linen carrying a writing kit (vv. 1-2). The scribe is sent through Jerusalem to mark the foreheads of everyone who grieves over the city's abominations (v. 4). The executioners are then commanded to kill everyone without that mark, showing no pity, beginning at the sanctuary itself (vv. 5-7); the slaughter starts with the elders standing before the temple. As the killing spreads, Ezekiel cries out and asks whether God intends to destroy the entire remnant of Israel (v. 8).

Authorship & Background

Author: Ezekiel son of Buzi (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Vision — The Marking and the Slaughter
Key Themes: The mark of preservation (those who grieve sin), judgment starting at the sanctuary, no sparing (old, young, women, children), the righteous remnant marked, God's response to Ezekiel's protest
Historical Context: Following the four abominations of chapter 8, God now responds with judgment. Six "men" (divine executioners/angels) appear with weapons; a seventh (the scribe in linen) carries a writing kit. The scribe's job: Mark (Hebrew "tav" = the last letter of the alphabet, shaped like an X or + in ancient Hebrew) the foreheads of those who "sigh and cry" over the city's sins. Those with the mark are spared; those without it are killed. The instruction: "begin at my sanctuary" (v.6) — judgment starts with the religious leadership (the very elders doing abominations in ch.8). This is the OT precedent for Revelation 7:3 (sealing the 144,000).
Six Men with Slaughter Weapons (vv.1-2): A loud voice gives an order to unnamed hearers, cause them that have charge over the city to draw near, and six men come from the way of the higher gate which lieth toward the north, every man with a slaughter weapon in his hand. With them is a seventh, clothed with linen, which is priestly dress, and carrying a writer's inkhorn by his side. The instrument that distinguishes him from the other six is a pen.
The Glory at the Threshold, and the Mark on the Foreheads (vv.3-4): The glory of the God of Israel goes up from the cherub whereupon he was, to the threshold of the house, and that movement is the first stage of a departure completed in chapter 11. The order to the man with the inkhorn is to go through the midst of the city and set a mark upon the foreheads of the men that sigh and that cry for all the abominations that be done in the midst thereof. The people spared are identified by grief rather than by conduct. The Hebrew word for the mark is taw, the last letter of the alphabet, written in that period as a cross, and Revelation 7 takes up the same idea of sealing foreheads before judgment.
The Slaughter, Beginning at the Sanctuary (vv.5-7): The instruction to the six is the hardest set of clauses in the book: go after him through the city, and smite, let not your eye spare, neither have ye pity, slay utterly old and young, both maids, and little children, and women, with the one exception, but come not near any man upon whom is the mark. And then the place to start, and begin at my sanctuary. The narration confirms that they did, then they began at the ancient men which were before the house, which are the seventy of the previous chapter. Judgment beginning with the people responsible for worship is the principle 1 Peter 4:17 restates.
Ezekiel's Protest, and the Answer (vv.8-11): He falls on his face and intercedes, Ah Lord GOD, wilt thou destroy all the residue of Israel in thy pouring out of thy fury upon Jerusalem. It is the second time he objects in the book and he gets no comfort. The answer restates the charge, the iniquity of the house of Israel and Judah is exceeding great, and the land is full of blood, and the city full of perverseness, and quotes the elders' own sentence back as the reason, for they say, The LORD hath forsaken the earth, and the LORD seeth not. The chapter ends with the man in linen filing a report in nine words, I have done as thou hast commanded me.

Setting: The same vision continues, September 592 BC. Ezekiel's body is in his house in Babylonia, with the elders of the exiles sitting before him, but in the Spirit he stands in the temple courts in Jerusalem (8:1–3). In chapter 8 he was led through four scenes of idolatry, each worse than the last, ending with twenty-five men at the temple door with their backs to God, bowing to the sun. That chapter ended: "though they cry in my ears with a loud voice, I will not hear them" (8:18). Chapter 9 opens with God himself crying out, not to hear, but to summon executioners. This is a vision, not a report of what happened that day. It shows in picture form what God would do when Babylon took the city six years later, and it shows something just as important: that in the middle of judgment God knows and protects his own.

Idolaters Killed (vv1–11)

  • One heading, three movements. The ESV gives the chapter one heading. It moves from the summons and the mark (vv1–4), to the slaughter (vv5–7), to Ezekiel's plea and God's answer (vv8–11).
  • "Then he cried in my ears with a loud voice" (v1). The wording deliberately echoes 8:18. The people would cry in God's ears and he would not hear. Now God cries in the prophet's ears and the destroyers come at once.
  • "Bring near the executioners of the city" (v1). The Hebrew word (pequddot) means those appointed to "visit" or "attend to" the city. The same root (paqad) can mean to visit in kindness or to visit in punishment. Here it is the second. The ESV's "executioners" makes the sense plain.
  • "Six men came from the direction of the upper gate, which faces north" (v2). In the vision these are heavenly agents, angels in human form, as angels often appear in Scripture (Gen 18:2; Judg 13:6). They come from the north, where the "image of jealousy" stood (8:3, 5) and from where Babylon's army would come (Jer 1:14–15). The "upper gate" had been built by King Jotham (2 Kings 15:35). It was at "the upper Benjamin Gate of the house of the LORD" that the priest Pashhur had put Jeremiah in the stocks for prophesying the city's fall (Jer 20:2).
  • "A man clothed in linen, with a writing case at his waist" (v2). Linen was the clothing of priests, especially of the high priest on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:4), and of heavenly messengers (Dan 10:5; 12:6–7). The writing case (qeset ha-sopher) was a scribe's kit: a palette with a slot for reed pens and wells for ink, carried in the belt. Scribes in Egypt and Mesopotamia are often pictured with one. This man is not armed. He is there to write and to mark. Interpreters differ on who he is. Many Jewish and Christian readers take him as the chief of the angels. A long Christian tradition, including both Matthew Henry and JFB, sees in him a picture of Christ, the priestly mediator who marks his own. The text itself does not name him.
  • "They went in and stood beside the bronze altar" (v2). This was the great altar of burnt offering in the inner court, where sacrifices for sin were made every day. The very place of atonement is now the place where judgment waits to begin.
  • "The glory of the God of Israel had gone up from the cherub on which it rested to the threshold of the house" (v3). This is the first step of the glory's departure. God's presence had been enthroned above the cherubim on the ark in the Most Holy Place (1 Sam 4:4; Ps 80:1). Now it moves to the doorway of the temple. In 10:4 it is seen there again, in 10:18–19 it moves to the east gate, and in 11:22–23 it leaves the city for the Mount of Olives. Like a man walking slowly out of his own house, God leaves step by step, as if reluctant to go. In 43:1–5 Ezekiel will see it come back.
  • "Put a mark on the foreheads of the men who sigh and groan" (v4). The word for "mark" is taw, which is also the name of the last letter of the Hebrew alphabet. In the old Hebrew script of Ezekiel's day, that letter was written as an X or a cross-shaped mark. Some early Christian writers, among them Tertullian and Jerome, saw in this a foreshadowing of the sign of the cross. That is a later reflection, not something Ezekiel's first readers would have known, but the connection is striking. In Ezekiel's world, a mark on the forehead showed whom a person belonged to, as slaves and devotees of a god were sometimes marked.
  • The Passover pattern. The scene closely follows the night of the exodus. There, blood on the doorposts marked the houses the destroyer would pass over (Exod 12:7, 13, 23). Here a mark on the forehead does the same. Rahab's scarlet cord in Jericho worked in a similar way (Josh 2:18–21). The difference is that this time the judgment is on Israel, not Egypt.
  • "Who sigh and groan over all the abominations" (v4). In Hebrew the two verbs rhyme (ne'enaḥim we-ne'enaqim), and they describe deep, audible grief. Those spared are not simply those who stayed out of idolatry, but those whose hearts were broken by it. Lot is called "righteous Lot, greatly distressed by the sensual conduct of the wicked" in Sodom (2 Pet 2:7–8). Jeremiah wept for his city (Jer 9:1). Ezekiel himself is told to groan in 21:6–7. This is a remnant defined by grief over sin.
  • "Pass through the city after him, and strike" (v5). The order matters. The marker goes first and the destroyers follow. God's people are secured before judgment falls.
  • "Kill old men outright, young men and maidens, little children and women" (v6). This is hard to read. It is a vision of a judgment that God carried out through the Babylonian conquest, and 2 Chronicles 36:17 describes it in almost the same words: Nebuchadnezzar "had no compassion on young man or virgin, old man or aged." Scripture does not soften it. It does say plainly that God does not take pleasure in the death of anyone (18:23, 32) and that these warnings came over many years. Readers have always struggled with the deaths of children in such judgments, and the Bible does not invite us to be casual about them. Abraham's question stands behind it: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Gen 18:25).
  • "But touch no one on whom is the mark" (v6). The protection is exact. Not one marked person is lost. Revelation picks up this scene directly: before judgment falls, an angel seals "the servants of our God on their foreheads" (Rev 7:3), and the locusts are told to harm "only those people who do not have the seal of God on their foreheads" (Rev 9:4).
  • "And begin at my sanctuary" (v6). Judgment starts where the light was greatest and the sin was worst. "So they began with the elders who were before the house", probably the men of 8:16 at the temple door, or the seventy of 8:11. Peter quotes the principle: "it is time for judgment to begin at the household of God" (1 Pet 4:17). Jesus said the same about privilege and accountability: "Everyone to whom much was given, of him much will be required" (Luke 12:48).
  • "Defile the house, and fill the courts with the slain" (v7). A dead body made anyone who touched it unclean (Num 19:11–16), and bodies in the temple would defile it completely. When the wicked queen Athaliah was executed, the priests took care to have her led outside the temple first (2 Kings 11:15). Now God himself orders his house defiled. It is already defiled by idols, so there is nothing left to protect. A building is not holy once God has left it. "Go out." The destroyers move from the temple into the city.
  • "I was left alone, I fell upon my face, and cried, 'Ah, Lord GOD!'" (v8). Ezekiel has been described as hard-faced (3:8–9), but here his heart breaks. "Ah" ('ahah) is the same cry of anguish as in 4:14. "Will you destroy all the remnant of Israel?" He fears no one will be left. This is intercession, in the line of Abraham pleading for Sodom (Gen 18:23–33) and Moses pleading after the golden calf (Exod 32:11–14). Ezekiel cries the same way again in 11:13 when one of the leaders dies in front of him.
  • "The guilt of the house of Israel and Judah is exceedingly great" (v9). God does not rebuke the prophet for praying, but he explains. "The land is full of blood, and the city full of injustice." The word for injustice (mutteh) means something bent or twisted, justice perverted. The charge of 7:23 and 8:17 is repeated: idolatry came with bloodshed. "For they say, 'The LORD has forsaken the land, and the LORD does not see.'" This is the elders' excuse from 8:12, quoted back. Their unbelief was the root of their sin.
  • "My eye will not spare… I will bring their deeds upon their heads" (v10). "Upon their heads" means they get back what they themselves have done. The punishment is the fruit of their own deeds (Prov 1:31).
  • "I have done as you commanded me" (v11). The chapter ends not with the destroyers' report but with the marker's. His work is the last word. Every one of the mourners was found and marked. God's answer to Ezekiel's fear that all would be destroyed is not an argument but this report: the remnant is safe.
  • Commentators: All three read the mark as God's seal of protection on his own, and link it to the Passover. Henry says, "A work of grace in the soul is to God a mark upon the forehead," and that God "will set a mark upon his mourners, will book their sighs and bottle their tears" (compare Ps 56:8). Guzik takes the mark as the letter taw, cross-shaped in that era, and connects it with the sealing in Revelation 7 and the counterfeit mark of Revelation 13. JFB and Henry both identify the man in linen with Christ, JFB calling him "the intercessory High Priest in heaven" and saying his report in v11 is "the characteristic of Messiah." Guzik sees him rather as the leader of the angelic group, but compares his report to Jesus' words "having accomplished the work that you gave me to do" (John 17:4). On v6 Henry writes, "When judgments are abroad they commonly begin at the house of God," and adds that "neither the sacredness of the place nor the eminency of their place in it" will protect the guilty. JFB says the judgment starts at the sanctuary because "the greatest abominations" happened there. On v8 Henry says Ezekiel prayed so that "he might stand in the gap to turn away the wrath of God," and JFB notes that God does not fully answer the question but shows that his justice did not exceed their sin.

Pointing to Christ

In the vision, a man dressed as a priest walks through a doomed city, finds every one of those who grieve over sin, and marks them before judgment falls. Then he reports, "I have done as you commanded me." Whatever the man in linen was in Ezekiel's vision, the gospel shows the reality. Jesus is the high priest who knows those who are his (John 10:14; 2 Tim 2:19), and he said to the Father on the night before he died, "I have guarded them, and not one of them has been lost" (John 17:12). The mark that protects his people is not ink but his own blood, the true Passover (1 Cor 5:7). And unlike the man in linen, he did not stand aside while judgment fell. He took it. It began at the house of God, and he stood in its place.

Reflection

  • Marked for Grieving Over Sin (v.4) — The mark of protection goes to those who sigh and cry over the evil happening around them, not merely those who oppose it in words. Does the sin you see around you still genuinely grieve you, or have you grown numb to it?
  • Judgment Begins With Those Closest to God (v.6) — God directs the executioners to start at His own sanctuary, holding the religious leadership most accountable first. If you carry any spiritual responsibility for others, are you living in a way that could withstand being examined first?
  • Bringing an Honest Question to God (v.8) — Ezekiel cries out and questions whether God intends to wipe out everyone who remains, and God receives the question rather than silencing it. When God's actions are hard to understand, do you bring your honest questions to Him instead of burying them?
  • Mercy Completed Before Judgment Falls (v.11) — The man in linen finishes marking the faithful and reports back before the executioners ever begin their work. Can you trust that God identifies and protects what is His before any judgment is carried out?
