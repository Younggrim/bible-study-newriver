1As Jesus left the temple and was walking away, His disciples came up to Him to point out its buildings.

2“Do you see all these things?” He replied. “Truly I tell you, not one stone here will be left on another; every one will be thrown down.”

3While Jesus was sitting on the Mount of Olives, the disciples came to Him privately. “Tell us,” they said, “when will these things happen, and what will be the sign of Your coming and of the end of the age?”

4Jesus answered, “See to it that no one deceives you.

5For many will come in My name, claiming, ‘I am the Christ,’ and will deceive many.

6You will hear of wars and rumors of wars, but see to it that you are not alarmed. These things must happen, but the end is still to come.

7Nation will rise against nation, and kingdom against kingdom. There will be famines and earthquakes in various places.

8All these are the beginning of birth pains.

9Then they will deliver you over to be persecuted and killed, and you will be hated by all nations because of My name.

10At that time many will fall away and will betray and hate one another,

11and many false prophets will arise and deceive many.

12Because of the multiplication of wickedness, the love of most will grow cold.

13But the one who perseveres to the end will be saved.

14And this gospel of the kingdom will be preached in all the world as a testimony to all nations, and then the end will come.

15So when you see standing in the holy place ‘the abomination of desolation,’ spoken of by the prophet Daniel (let the reader understand),

16then let those who are in Judea flee to the mountains.

17Let no one on the housetop come down to retrieve anything from his house.

18And let no one in the field return for his cloak.

19How miserable those days will be for pregnant and nursing mothers!

20Pray that your flight will not occur in the winter or on the Sabbath.

21For at that time there will be great tribulation, unseen from the beginning of the world until now, and never to be seen again.

22If those days had not been cut short, nobody would be saved. But for the sake of the elect, those days will be cut short.

23At that time, if anyone says to you, ‘Look, here is the Christ!’ or ‘There He is!’ do not believe it.

24For false Christs and false prophets will appear and perform great signs and wonders to deceive even the elect, if that were possible.

25See, I have told you in advance.

26So if they tell you, ‘There He is, in the wilderness,’ do not go out, or, ‘Here He is, in the inner rooms,’ do not believe it.

27For just as the lightning comes from the east and flashes as far as the west, so will be the coming of the Son of Man.

28Wherever there is a carcass, there the vultures will gather.

29Immediately after the tribulation of those days: ‘The sun will be darkened, and the moon will not give its light; the stars will fall from the sky, and the powers of the heavens will be shaken.’

30At that time the sign of the Son of Man will appear in heaven, and all the tribes of the earth will mourn. They will see the Son of Man coming on the clouds of heaven with power and great glory.

31And He will send out His angels with a loud trumpet call, and they will gather His elect from the four winds, from one end of the heavens to the other.

32Now learn this lesson from the fig tree: As soon as its branches become tender and sprout leaves, you know that summer is near.

33So also, when you see all these things, you will know that He is near, right at the door.

34Truly I tell you, this generation will not pass away until all these things have happened.

35Heaven and earth will pass away, but My words will never pass away.

36No one knows about that day or hour, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father.

37As it was in the days of Noah, so will it be at the coming of the Son of Man.

38For in the days before the flood, people were eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage, up to the day Noah entered the ark.

39And they were oblivious until the flood came and swept them all away. So will it be at the coming of the Son of Man.

40Two men will be in the field: one will be taken and the other left.

41Two women will be grinding at the mill: one will be taken and the other left.

42Therefore keep watch, because you do not know the day on which your Lord will come.

43But understand this: If the homeowner had known in which watch of the night the thief was coming, he would have kept watch and would not have let his house be broken into.

44For this reason, you also must be ready, because the Son of Man will come at an hour you do not expect.

45Who then is the faithful and wise servant, whom the master has put in charge of his household, to give the others their food at the proper time?

46Blessed is that servant whose master finds him doing so when he returns.

47Truly I tell you, he will put him in charge of all his possessions.

48But suppose that servant is wicked and says in his heart, ‘My master will be away a long time.’

49And he begins to beat his fellow servants and to eat and drink with drunkards.

50The master of that servant will come on a day he does not expect and at an hour he does not anticipate.

51Then he will cut him to pieces and assign him a place with the hypocrites, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.

Summary

Jesus predicts that the temple's buildings will be utterly thrown down, and when the disciples ask privately about the sign of his coming and the end of the age, he begins the Olivet Discourse, warning of false messiahs, wars, famines, and earthquakes as only the beginning of birth pains, and of persecution, betrayal, and the gospel being preached to all nations before the end comes (vv. 1-14). He warns of the abomination of desolation standing in the holy place and describes a tribulation unlike any before, urging those in Judea to flee immediately and warning against false christs and false prophets performing signs (vv. 15-28). After that tribulation, he says, the sun and moon will darken and the Son of Man will appear in the sky with power and great glory, sending his angels to gather his elect (vv. 29-31). Using the parable of the fig tree, he says these signs mean the end is near, though he insists no one — not even the angels or the Son, but only the Father — knows the exact day or hour (vv. 32-36). He compares his return to the days of Noah, when people were caught unprepared, and urges constant watchfulness, closing with the parable of the faithful servant who is ready and the wicked servant who is not (vv. 37-51).

Authorship & Background

Author: Matthew (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 24 is the Olivet Discourse — Jesus' longest prophetic teaching, delivered on the Mount of Olives overlooking the Temple. The disciples asked three questions (v.3): (1) When will the Temple be destroyed? (2) What will be the sign of your coming? (3) What will be the sign of the end of the age? Jesus answers all three, weaving together near-future events (the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70) and far-future events (His second coming).
The Temple's Destruction: Jesus predicted that "not one stone" would be left upon another (v.2). This was fulfilled literally in AD 70 when Roman general Titus destroyed Jerusalem and the Temple. The Romans even pried apart the stones to recover gold that had melted between them during the fire.
Interpretive Approaches: This chapter is interpreted differently across traditions: (1) Preterist — most was fulfilled in AD 70; (2) Futurist — most awaits future fulfillment; (3) Historicist — fulfilled progressively through church history; (4) Idealist — principles applicable to every generation. The study notes present the text without forcing one system.
The Key Command: "Watch" (vv.42-44). Regardless of interpretive framework, Jesus' practical application is clear: be ready, be faithful, be watchful. No one knows the day or hour — so live every day as if it could be today.
Classification: Gospel — Narrative and Discourse
Key Themes: a prediction that not one stone shall be left upon another answered by three joined questions about timing and signs, wars, famines and earthquakes named explicitly as only the beginning of sorrows rather than the end, a specific sign, the abomination of desolation, triggering practical instructions for a single day, flee to the mountains, a fig tree's season readable while the day and hour remain unknown to anyone but the Father, Noah's flood used as a pattern stressing ordinary normality rather than open wickedness, and a servant's fate turning entirely on how he treats a delay rather than on whether he believes a return is coming
Not One Stone Upon Another (vv.1-14): A disciple points out the buildings and the reply is that not one stone shall be left upon another. The question that follows has three parts and Matthew keeps them joined, when shall these things be? and what shall be the sign of thy coming, and of the end of the world? The first answer is a warning against being deceived, for many shall come in my name, saying, I am Christ. Wars, famines, pestilences and earthquakes are called the beginning of sorrows, not the end. Then persecution, betrayal, cooling love, and the one condition given for the end, this gospel of the kingdom shall be preached in all the world, and then shall the end come.
The Abomination, and the Flight (vv.15-28): The sign given is a specific one, the abomination of desolation, spoken of by Daniel the prophet, with an aside to the reader rather than the hearers, whoso readeth, let him understand. What follows is practical instruction for a day: flee to the mountains, do not go back into the house for anything, and two clauses of sympathy, woe unto them that are with child, and pray that your flight be not in the winter, neither on the sabbath day. Then false Christs with signs and wonders, and a warning against chasing reports, believe it not, for as the lightning cometh out of the east, and shineth even unto the west, so shall also the coming of the Son of man be.
The Fig Tree, and No Man Knoweth (vv.29-44): The sun darkened, the moon not giving light, the stars falling, and then the Son of man coming in the clouds with power and great glory. The fig tree parable says the season can be read, and the next sentence says the date cannot, of that day and hour knoweth no man, no, not the angels of heaven, but my Father only. Then Noah is used as the pattern, and what is stressed is normality rather than wickedness, they were eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage, until the day that Noah entered into the ark. Two in a field, two at a mill, one taken and one left. The instruction drawn from all of it is one word, watch.
The Faithful and Evil Servant (vv.45-51): The discourse ends with a servant left in charge of a household, and the difference between the two versions of him is what he does with a delay. The faithful one is found giving them meat in due season. The evil one says in his heart, My lord delayeth his coming, and begins to smite his fellowservants, and to eat and drink with the drunken. The point is not that the second disbelieves the return but that he has priced it as distant. The chapter closes on the sentence that leads straight into the three parables of chapter 25.

Setting: Tuesday evening of Passover week, spring of AD 30. Jesus has spent the day in the temple courts, silencing his opponents and pronouncing woe on the scribes and Pharisees. Now he walks out of the temple for the last time and crosses the Kidron Valley to the Mount of Olives, which looks straight across at the temple mount. There, with Peter, James, John and Andrew (Mark 13:3), he gives the teaching known as the Olivet Discourse, the fifth and last teaching block in Matthew, running through chapter 25. Mark 13 and Luke 21 give parallel versions. This is one of the most debated chapters in the Bible, mostly over one question: which parts speak of the Roman destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, and which speak of Christ's return at the end of history. Whatever view a reader holds, the chapter's own aim is plain: to keep disciples faithful, alert and unafraid.

Jesus Foretells Destruction of the Temple (vv1–2)

  • "His disciples came to point out to him the buildings of the temple" (v1). Herod the Great began rebuilding the temple around 20 BC, and work on the surrounding complex was still going on in Jesus' day (John 2:20). It was finished only in AD 63, seven years before it was destroyed. Josephus describes white stones some forty feet long and a gold-plated front that dazzled in the sun.
  • "There will not be left here one stone upon another that will not be thrown down" (v2). In AD 70, after a long siege, Roman soldiers under Titus burned the temple. Josephus says Titus then ordered the city and temple razed. Foundation stones of the retaining walls remain, including the Western Wall, but the temple itself was destroyed.
  • Commentators: Guzik points out that this prophecy was fulfilled literally, and takes that as a reason to read the rest of the chapter literally too. Henry: "Christ foretells the utter ruin and destruction coming upon the temple." JFB gives no comment on any of this chapter in Matthew and sends readers to its notes on Mark 13.

Signs of the End of the Age (vv3–14)

  • "When will these things be, and what will be the sign of your coming and of the end of the age?" (v3). The disciples ask two things: when the temple will fall, and what will signal Jesus' coming and the end. They probably assumed both would happen together. "Coming" is parousia, a word used of the official arrival or visit of a king or high official, and it becomes the New Testament's word for Christ's return (1 Thess 4:15; 2 Pet 3:4). Much of the debate about this chapter comes from how Jesus' answer handles their two questions.
  • The main ways of reading the chapter. (1) Many read vv4–35 as speaking mostly of AD 70, with vv36–51 (or from v29 on) turning to the second coming. (2) Many futurists, including dispensational interpreters, read most of the chapter, especially vv15–31, as a future time of tribulation just before Christ returns, with AD 70 at most a preview. (3) A large number of interpreters see a "near and far" pattern common in the prophets: the fall of Jerusalem is a real fulfillment and also a pattern of the final end, so parts of the chapter apply to both. (4) A fuller preterist view (from Latin praeter, "past") places almost all of it in the first century. Faithful Christians hold each of these. All agree that Jesus will return in person and in glory.
  • "See that no one leads you astray" (v4). Jesus' first concern is deception. "Many will come in my name, saying, 'I am the Christ'" (v5). Josephus describes a series of false prophets and would-be deliverers in the decades before AD 70, such as Theudas, who promised to part the Jordan, and an Egyptian who led thousands to the Mount of Olives promising the city walls would fall (Acts 5:36; 21:38). The church has seen such figures in every century since.
  • "Wars and rumors of wars… but the end is not yet" (v6). Wars, famines and earthquakes are not signs that the end has come. They are the normal groaning of a fallen world. There was famine under Claudius (Acts 11:28), earthquake at Laodicea in AD 60, and civil war in Rome in 68–69. Jesus says, "See that you are not alarmed."
  • "The beginning of the birth pains" (v8). The prophets used labor pains as a picture of the suffering that comes before God's new age (Isa 26:17–19; Mic 4:9–10). Birth pains hurt, but they lead to new life, and they come in waves that grow stronger.
  • "They will deliver you up to tribulation and put you to death" (v9). "Deliver up" is the word used of Jesus being handed over (17:22; 20:18). His people will share his path. Within a few years Stephen and James were killed (Acts 7; 12:2), and under Nero, around AD 64, Christians in Rome were put to death in crowds.
  • "Many will fall away… the love of many will grow cold" (vv10–12). "Fall away" is skandalizō, to stumble, as in the parable of the sower, where shallow faith withers "when tribulation or persecution arises" (13:21). "Lawlessness" (anomia) is life without regard for God's law. The danger is not only outside but inside: betrayal, false teaching and love growing cold (Rev 2:4).
  • "The one who endures to the end will be saved" (v13). "Endures" (hypomenō) means to remain under a load, to stay put. Those who keep trusting him to the end show that their faith was real (10:22).
  • "This gospel of the kingdom will be proclaimed throughout the whole world… and then the end will come" (v14). "World" (oikoumenē) means the inhabited world. Paul could write within thirty years that it was bearing fruit "in the whole world" (Col 1:6). Jesus gives the church its task for the waiting time: not guessing dates, but preaching to the nations (28:19–20).
  • Commentators: Guzik says the troubles of vv4–14 are real but are not the decisive sign of the end. They are labor pains that grow more frequent and intense. He reads the spread of the gospel to all nations as the church's duty in the meantime. Henry notes that Jesus warns his disciples first of all "to stand on their guard against false teachers." The Tyndale notes leave open whether Jesus meant the impostors before AD 70, those of the whole church age, or a future time of trouble.

The Abomination of Desolation (vv15–28)

  • "The abomination of desolation spoken of by the prophet Daniel" (v15). Daniel speaks of "the abomination that makes desolate" set up in the temple (Dan 9:27; 11:31; 12:11). An "abomination" (Hebrew shiqqutz) was something idolatrous and detestable to God. Daniel's words were fulfilled first in 167 BC, when the Greek king Antiochus IV Epiphanes set up an altar to Zeus on the temple's altar and sacrificed a pig (1 Macc 1:54). Jesus, speaking two centuries later, says it will happen again.
  • What it refers to. Luke's version reads, "when you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies, then know that its desolation has come near" (Luke 21:20). Many therefore link it to the Roman siege, with its legion standards bearing images of eagles and the emperor, which Jews counted as idols. Josephus also describes Zealots in AD 68 committing murder in the holy place and installing a false high priest, and Roman soldiers sacrificing to their standards in the temple court after it burned. Futurist interpreters connect it instead, or as well, to a future "man of lawlessness" who "takes his seat in the temple of God, proclaiming himself to be God" (2 Thess 2:3–4; Rev 13). "Let the reader understand" is an aside to the reader of Matthew, urging careful thought about Daniel.
  • "Let those who are in Judea flee to the mountains" (v16). The church historian Eusebius records that Christians in Jerusalem, warned by a prophecy, left the city before the siege and settled in Pella, a town east of the Jordan in the hills. Many see that as obedience to this command.
  • Housetop, field, pregnant women, winter, Sabbath (vv17–20). Flat roofs had outside stairs, so a man could flee without going indoors. Winter rains turned roads to mud and swelled the Jordan, and on the Sabbath travel was limited and gates might be shut. Matthew alone mentions the Sabbath, which fits his Jewish-Christian readers.
  • "Great tribulation, such as has not been from the beginning of the world until now, no, and never will be" (v21). The words echo Daniel 12:1. Josephus, an eyewitness, describes the siege of Jerusalem in horrific terms: famine so severe that people ate leather, over a million deaths by his count (likely too high), and thousands crucified around the walls. Futurists argue that "and never will be" points to the very end. Others read it as prophetic language for unspeakable suffering, as in Joel 2:2.
  • "False christs and false prophets will arise and perform great signs and wonders" (v24). Not every miracle comes from God (Deut 13:1–3; 2 Thess 2:9). "If possible, even the elect" suggests it is not possible: God keeps his own.
  • "Look, he is in the wilderness… in the inner rooms" (v26). Several would-be deliverers in the first century led crowds into the wilderness, the place where Israel expected a new exodus. Others claimed secret knowledge. Jesus says his coming will not be a rumor that needs checking. It will be like lightning that "shines as far as the west" (v27), seen by everyone.
  • "Wherever the corpse is, there the vultures will gather" (v28). This seems to have been a proverb. The KJV has "eagles," and the Greek word (aetos) can mean either, but carrion birds are meant. The point may be that judgment comes as surely as vultures find a carcass, or that his coming will be as unmistakable as circling birds.
  • Commentators: Guzik holds a futurist view, reading the abomination as an idol set up in a future temple's holy place, linked to Daniel, 2 Thessalonians 2 and Revelation 13. He grants the flight to Pella as a real foreshadowing but argues that a tribulation worse than all before or after must still be future. JFB, in its notes on Mark 13, takes the opposite view: the abomination "was intended to point to the Roman ensigns," supported by Luke 21:20, and it records that "the Christians fled to Pella." JFB adds, though, that the language "rises and swells beyond all temporal and partial vengeance," so it also looks beyond AD 70. Henry: "If God opens a door of escape, we ought to make our escape, otherwise we do not trust God, but tempt him."

The Coming of the Son of Man (vv29–31)

  • "The sun will be darkened, and the moon will not give its light, and the stars will fall from heaven" (v29). This is the language of the prophets. Isaiah used almost the same words for the fall of Babylon (Isa 13:10) and of Edom (Isa 34:4), and Ezekiel for Egypt (Ezek 32:7–8). Joel uses it for the day of the LORD (Joel 2:10, 31). Such language can describe the collapse of a world order, with lights going out over a nation in judgment. It can also describe a real shaking of creation at the end (Heb 12:26–27; 2 Pet 3:10; Rev 6:12–14). Interpreters who see AD 70 here read it the first way. Those who see the second coming read it the second, or both.
  • "Immediately after the tribulation of those days" (v29). "Immediately" is a key word in the debate. If the tribulation was AD 70, either these verses also speak of that judgment in picture language, or "immediately" places Christ's coming right after the tribulation from God's view of time, in which "one day is as a thousand years" (2 Pet 3:8).
  • "The sign of the Son of Man… the Son of Man coming on the clouds of heaven with power and great glory" (v30). Jesus quotes Daniel 7:13–14, where "one like a son of man" comes with the clouds to the Ancient of Days and receives an everlasting kingdom. In Daniel he comes to God to be enthroned, so some read this as Jesus' vindication, seen in his resurrection, ascension and judgment on Jerusalem (26:64). Most Christians, with the creeds, read it as his visible return to earth (Acts 1:11; 1 Thess 4:16; Rev 1:7). What "the sign" is, is not said. It may simply be the Son of Man himself appearing.
  • "All the tribes of the earth will mourn" (v30). This echoes Zechariah 12:10–14, where "they look on me, on him whom they have pierced," and the tribes of Israel mourn. "Earth" (gē) can also mean "land," which is part of why some read it of Israel and AD 70. Revelation 1:7 joins Daniel 7 and Zechariah 12 the same way.
  • "He will send out his angels with a loud trumpet call, and they will gather his elect from the four winds" (v31). The trumpet announced festivals and gatherings in Israel, and Isaiah promised that "a great trumpet will be blown" to gather God's scattered people (Isa 27:13). Paul uses the same picture (1 Thess 4:16–17; 1 Cor 15:52). Some who read the passage as AD 70 take "angels" (angeloi, "messengers") as preachers gathering the elect through the gospel.
  • Commentators: Guzik reads the darkened sun and falling stars as real cosmic signs and judges the AD 70 reading of these verses a stretch. He notes that the Greek for "sign" can carry the sense of a raised banner, a rallying signal. JFB (on Mark) argues that such imagery is used in Scripture for national judgments and so does not prove a reference only to the final day, though that day brings its fullest fulfillment. Henry plainly reads it of Christ's return: "Then they shall see the Son of man coming in the clouds."

The Lesson of the Fig Tree (vv32–35)

  • "As soon as its branch becomes tender and puts out its leaves, you know that summer is near" (v32). The fig loses its leaves in winter and leafs late in spring, so new leaves mean summer is close.
  • "When you see all these things, you know that he is near, at the very gates" (v33). The Greek can mean "he is near" or "it is near." "All these things" most naturally means the events of vv4–28.
  • "This generation will not pass away until all these things take place" (v34). This is the hardest verse in the chapter. In Matthew "this generation" always means the people living in Jesus' day (11:16; 12:41–45; 16:4; 17:17; 23:36, just two verses before this chapter). Many therefore conclude that "all these things" means the events leading to and including AD 70, which happened within forty years, while the day and hour of Christ's return (v36) is a different matter. Others take "this generation" as the generation alive when the final signs begin, or as "this kind of people" (unbelieving Israel, or humanity) that will continue until the end. Some critics say Jesus was simply wrong, but that ignores how the chapter separates "all these things" from "that day."
  • "Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away" (v35). Isaiah says, "The grass withers, the flower fades, but the word of our God will stand forever" (Isa 40:8). Jesus says the same of his own words, putting them on the level of God's. He had said earlier that not one letter of the law would pass away (5:18).
  • Commentators: Guzik takes "this generation" as the generation that sees the end-time signs, since the disciples did not see his return. He mentions the reading of genea as "race" (the Jewish people will survive until he returns), but notes that France considers it a poor translation. JFB (on Mark) says the prophecy was met in the destruction under Titus, or, if the phrase is pressed further, in the later scattering of the Jews under Hadrian.

No One Knows That Day and Hour (vv36–51)

  • "But concerning that day and hour no one knows" (v36). "But" marks a turn. The events of "these things" have signs and a time frame. "That day," the day of the Lord's return, has none. Paul says it will come "like a thief in the night" (1 Thess 5:2). This is the strongest warning in Scripture against setting dates for Christ's return, a practice that has failed again and again.
  • "Not even the angels of heaven, nor the Son, but the Father only" (v36). Some manuscripts of Matthew lack "nor the Son," which is why the KJV omits it here. The words are secure in Mark 13:32 and are found in the earliest manuscripts of Matthew, so the ESV includes them. How can the Son not know? Christians have usually explained it this way: in becoming man, the Son willingly set aside the independent use of some divine privileges and lived as a man dependent on the Father (Phil 2:6–8; Luke 2:52). As God he is all-knowing; in his earthly ministry he spoke what the Father gave him (John 12:49).
  • "As were the days of Noah" (vv37–39). People were "eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage." None of these things is evil. The point is that life went on as normal, and no one took Noah's warning seriously, "until the flood came and swept them all away." Peter calls Noah "a herald of righteousness" (2 Pet 2:5), so they had been warned. Christ's return will come in the middle of ordinary life.
  • "One will be taken and one left" (vv40–41). Two men work side by side in the field; two women grind grain together at a hand mill, a daily job done in pairs. Their outward lives look identical, but their destinies are not. Many, including those who hold to a rapture of the church (1 Thess 4:17), read "taken" as taken to be with the Lord. Others note that in Noah's day the wicked were "swept away" and Noah was left, and read "taken" as taken in judgment. The word for "taken" (paralambanō) is used elsewhere in Matthew of taking someone to be with oneself (1:20; 17:1). Either way the point is the sudden separation.
  • "Stay awake, for you do not know on what day your Lord is coming" (v42). "Stay awake" (grēgoreō) is the same word Jesus will use in Gethsemane, where the disciples fall asleep (26:38–41).
  • "If the master of the house had known in what part of the night the thief was coming" (v43). The Greek for "broken into" is literally "dug through," since many walls were mud brick. No thief sends notice.
  • "Who then is the faithful and wise servant…?" (v45). The parable is about a head servant placed over the household to give out food on time. It fits church leaders who feed God's people (1 Pet 5:2), and every disciple given a trust. "Blessed is that servant whom his master will find so doing when he comes" (v46). Readiness means doing the work given.
  • "My master is delayed" (v48). His mistake is not denying the master will return, but assuming there is no hurry. He abuses his fellow servants and lives for himself.
  • "Will cut him in pieces and put him with the hypocrites" (v51). "Cut in pieces" (dichotomeō) literally means to cut in two, a punishment known in the ancient world (compare 1 Sam 15:33; Heb 11:37). Some take it as a figure for severe punishment or for being cut off. Matthew says "with the hypocrites," the word of chapter 23. "Weeping and gnashing of teeth" is his recurring phrase for final judgment.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Our Lord's coming will be happy to those that shall be found ready, but very dreadful to those that are not," and he adds the sobering line, "We know we have but a little time to live." Guzik reads "one taken" as believers being caught up to the Lord, following France's observation that the verb means being taken to be with someone. He calls "there is no hurry" the most dangerous lie a servant can believe. On "cut in pieces," he cites F. F. Bruce's view that it may be a figure for severe flogging. JFB, on Mark, sets out the main explanations of how the Son could not know the day, including that he "was not at that time in possession of the knowledge referred to" as part of what he came to reveal.

Pointing to Christ

Jesus speaks in this chapter as the Lord of history. He foretells the fall of the temple forty years ahead, names the dangers his people will face, and promises that his words will outlast heaven and earth. He also speaks as the Son of Man of Daniel's vision, who will come on the clouds with power and gather his people from the ends of the earth. The days before his return will bring false christs, suffering and cold love, but they will not bring a hidden or uncertain Christ. When he comes, everyone will see. Until then he gives his servants work to do and a promise to hold: "the one who endures to the end will be saved." Within three days the King who said all this was crucified, bearing judgment so that his people need not fear the day.

Reflection

  • Guard Against Deception (v.4) — Jesus' first warning about the end is not persecution but deception — many will come claiming to speak for Him. How do you test what you're taught against Scripture rather than accepting it on personality or confidence alone?
  • Love Growing Cold (v.12) — As lawlessness increases, Jesus warns that the love of many will grow cold. Is your love for God and others staying warm, or slowly cooling as the world around you grows harder?
  • Endurance to the End (v.13) — Salvation is tied not to a single decision but to a lifetime of faithful endurance. What does enduring faithfully look like in your circumstances right now?
  • A Mission to Complete (v.14) — The gospel must reach all nations before the end comes. Are you actively participating in that mission, or simply waiting on the sidelines?
  • An Unknowable Hour (v.36) — Jesus says even He, in His human knowledge, does not know the day or hour of His return. Does the unknown timing create urgency in how you live, or does it breed complacency?
  • Found Faithfully Doing (v.46) — The blessed servant is the one the master finds actively doing his assigned work when he returns, not idly waiting. What has God assigned you to do, and are you doing it right now?
