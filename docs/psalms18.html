1For the choirmaster. Of David the servant of the LORD, who sang this song to the LORD on the day the LORD had delivered him from the hand of all his enemies and from the hand of Saul. He said: I love You, O LORD, my strength.

2The LORD is my rock, my fortress, and my deliverer. My God is my rock, in whom I take refuge, my shield, and the horn of my salvation, my stronghold.

3I will call upon the LORD, who is worthy to be praised; so shall I be saved from my enemies.

4The cords of death encompassed me; the torrents of chaos overwhelmed me.

5The cords of Sheol entangled me; the snares of death confronted me.

6In my distress I called upon the LORD; I cried to my God for help. From His temple He heard my voice, and my cry for His help reached His ears.

7Then the earth shook and quaked, and the foundations of the mountains trembled; they were shaken because He burned with anger.

8Smoke rose from His nostrils, and consuming fire came from His mouth; glowing coals blazed forth.

9He parted the heavens and came down with dark clouds beneath His feet.

10He mounted a cherub and flew; He soared on the wings of the wind.

11He made darkness His hiding place, and storm clouds a canopy around Him.

12From the brightness of His presence His clouds advanced—hailstones and coals of fire.

13The LORD thundered from heaven; the voice of the Most High resounded—hailstones and coals of fire.

14He shot His arrows and scattered the foes; He hurled lightning and routed them.

15The channels of the sea appeared, and the foundations of the world were exposed, at Your rebuke, O LORD, at the blast of the breath of Your nostrils.

16He reached down from on high and took hold of me; He drew me out of deep waters.

17He rescued me from my powerful enemy, from foes too mighty for me.

18They confronted me in my day of calamity, but the LORD was my support.

19He brought me out into the open; He rescued me because He delighted in me.

20The LORD has rewarded me according to my righteousness; He has repaid me according to the cleanness of my hands.

21For I have kept the ways of the LORD and have not wickedly departed from my God.

22For all His ordinances are before me; I have not disregarded His statutes.

23And I have been blameless before Him and kept myself from iniquity.

24So the LORD has repaid me according to my righteousness, according to the cleanness of my hands in His sight.

25To the faithful You show Yourself faithful, to the blameless You show Yourself blameless;

26to the pure You show Yourself pure, but to the crooked You show Yourself shrewd.

27For You save an afflicted people, but You humble those with haughty eyes.

28For You, O LORD, light my lamp; my God lights up my darkness.

29For in You I can charge an army, and with my God I can scale a wall.

30As for God, His way is perfect; the word of the LORD is flawless. He is a shield to all who take refuge in Him.

31For who is God besides the LORD? And who is the Rock except our God?

32It is God who arms me with strength and makes my way clear.

33He makes my feet like those of a deer and stations me upon the heights.

34He trains my hands for battle; my arms can bend a bow of bronze.

35You have given me Your shield of salvation; Your right hand upholds me, and Your gentleness exalts me.

36You broaden the path beneath me so that my ankles do not give way.

37I pursued my enemies and overtook them; I did not turn back until they were consumed.

38I crushed them so they could not rise; they have fallen under my feet.

39You have armed me with strength for battle; You have subdued my foes beneath me.

40You have made my enemies retreat before me; I destroyed those who hated me.

41They cried for help, but there was no one to save them—to the LORD, but He did not answer.

42I ground them as dust in the face of the wind; I trampled them like mud in the streets.

43You have delivered me from the strife of the people; You have made me the head of nations; a people I had not known shall serve me.

44When they hear me, they obey me; foreigners cower before me.

45Foreigners lose heart and come trembling from their strongholds.

46The LORD lives, and blessed be my Rock! And may the God of my salvation be exalted—

47the God who avenges me and subdues nations beneath me,

48who delivers me from my enemies. You exalt me above my foes; You rescue me from violent men.

49Therefore I will praise You, O LORD, among the nations; I will sing praises to Your name.

50Great salvation He brings to His king. He shows loving devotion to His anointed, to David and his descendants forever.

Summary

A lengthy royal psalm of thanksgiving, closely paralleled in 2 Samuel 22, celebrating God's deliverance of David from Saul and other enemies. David opens with an outpouring of love for the LORD as his rock, fortress, and deliverer, describing how he cried out in the grip of death and was heard from God's temple as the earth itself shook (vv. 1-7). What follows is a dramatic theophany of smoke, darkness, thunder, and lightning as God comes down to rescue him because he delights in him (vv. 8-19), and David affirms that God rewards him according to his righteousness and deals with each person according to their own character (vv. 20-27). God arms him for battle, sets him above his enemies, and makes the nations submit to him (vv. 28-45). The psalm closes in praise of God, the living Rock, who grants great victories to his king and shows steadfast love to David and his descendants forever (vv. 46-50).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Royal Thanksgiving / Theophany Psalm
Attributed Author: David — "To the chief Musician, A Psalm of David, the servant of the LORD, who spake the words of this song in the day that the LORD delivered him from the hand of all his enemies, and from the hand of Saul"
Key Themes: Comprehensive deliverance, theophany (God appearing in storm), God as rock/fortress/deliverer, divine warrior, gentleness that makes great, Messianic kingship
Historical Context: This is the longest psalm in Book I and one of the most dramatic. It also appears as 2 Samuel 22 (with minor variations), placed near the end of David's life as a summary of God's faithfulness throughout. The superscription dates it to after David's deliverance from Saul and all enemies — a career-spanning thanksgiving. The theophany section (vv.7-15) is among the most spectacular divine-warrior passages in Scripture — God bends the heavens, rides on a cherub, shakes the earth, and floods the sky with fire to rescue one man. Paul quotes v.49 in Romans 15:9 as prophecy of Gentile worship.
The LORD Is My Rock (vv.1-3): I will love thee, O LORD, my strength, and what follows is a stack of titles rather than a sentence, my rock, and my fortress, and my deliverer, my God, my strength, my buckler, the horn of my salvation, and my high tower. Seven of them, all defensive, and most of them geological or architectural. This psalm appears almost word for word as 2 Samuel 22, which makes it one of only a handful of texts preserved twice in the Old Testament.
The Sorrows of Death Compassed Me (vv.4-6): The sorrows of death compassed me, and the floods of ungodly men made me afraid. The danger is put in water and rope imagery, the sorrows of hell compassed me about, the snares of death prevented me. Then the one action taken, in my distress I called upon the LORD, and the answer is located, he heard my voice out of his temple, and my cry came before him, even into his ears.
He Bowed the Heavens (vv.7-15): Nine verses of theophany, and it is the longest sustained storm description in the psalter. Then the earth shook and trembled, the foundations also of the hills moved. The imagery is drawn from every element at once, smoke out of his nostrils, coals of fire, darkness under his feet, a cherub as a vehicle, thick clouds as a pavilion, hail and coals of fire, arrows and lightnings. And the last verse strips the world back to bedrock, then the channels of waters were seen, and the foundations of the world were discovered. All of this is the answer to one man calling from distress.
He Drew Me Out of Many Waters (vv.16-19): He sent from above, he took me, he drew me out of many waters. After nine verses of cosmic disturbance the rescue itself is four verbs and one man being lifted. And the reason given is unexpected in a passage this violent, he delivered me, because he delighted in me. The last verse names where he was put, he brought me forth also into a large place, which is the opposite of the snares and floods of verse 5.
I Have Kept the Ways of the LORD (vv.20-24): The LORD rewarded me according to my righteousness, according to the cleanness of my hands hath he recompensed me. The claim is uncomfortable to modern readers and the psalm makes it without qualification, I was also upright before him, and I kept myself from mine iniquity. What it is claiming is loyalty in a specific conflict rather than sinlessness, which is how the same claim works in Psalm 7.
With the Merciful Thou Wilt Shew Thyself Merciful (vv.25-29): With the merciful thou wilt shew thyself merciful, with an upright man thou wilt shew thyself upright, with the pure thou wilt shew thyself pure, and with the froward thou wilt shew thyself froward. Four clauses on one principle: God is met in the manner he is approached. Then two images of scale, thou wilt save the afflicted people, but wilt bring down high looks, and one of personal capability, by my God have I leaped over a wall.
He Teacheth My Hands to War (vv.30-42): Thirteen verses on equipment and training, and the striking thing is how much of it is instruction rather than protection, he teacheth my hands to war, so that a bow of steel is broken by mine arms. The physical detail is specific, he maketh my feet like hinds' feet, thou hast enlarged my steps under me, that my feet did not slip, thou hast girded me with strength unto the battle. And the outcome is reported without decoration, I have pursued mine enemies, and overtaken them, neither did I turn again till they were consumed.
A People Whom I Have Not Known (vv.43-45): Thou hast made me the head of the heathen, a people whom I have not known shall serve me. The horizon widens from the battlefield to an empire, and the submission is described as coming before contact, as soon as they hear of me, they shall obey me. Paul quotes verse 49 from the next section in Romans 15:9 as evidence that the Gentiles were always in view.
The LORD Liveth (vv.46-50): The LORD liveth, and blessed be my rock, and let the God of my salvation be exalted. The psalm returns to the titles it opened with, which closes a frame across fifty verses. And the last verse names the reach of the promise beyond the singer, great deliverance giveth he to his king, and sheweth mercy to his anointed, to David, and to his seed for evermore.

Deep Dive

Setting: The longest title in the first book of Psalms tells us exactly where this song comes from: David sang it "on the day when the LORD delivered him from the hand of all his enemies, and from the hand of Saul." It looks back over a lifetime, from the years as a fugitive hunted by Saul to the wars that made Israel secure (2 Sam 8). The same song, with small differences, appears in 2 Samuel 22, placed near the end of David's story as one of his last words. Here it was handed "to the choirmaster" for Israel's worship. David calls himself "the servant of the LORD," a title given to Moses and Joshua (Deut 34:5; Josh 24:29). Psalms 16 and 17 were prayers for protection from deadly enemies. Psalm 18 is the answer, the great thanksgiving of a man who was rescued. It is the Old Testament's fullest picture of God coming down to save one person, and it ends looking far beyond David, to the king and his offspring "forever."

I Love You, O LORD (vv1–3)

  • "I love you, O LORD, my strength" (v1). The verb is unusual. It is racham, normally "to have compassion," used of God's tender mercy toward his people (103:13) and of a mother toward her child (Isa 49:15). In this form it appears nowhere else in the Old Testament, and it is missing from the 2 Samuel 22 version. It is the language of deep, tender, almost family affection, and it is rare for a psalmist to say it so directly to God. The whole song that follows explains why.
  • "My rock and my fortress and my deliverer" (v2). Nine names for God pile up in one breath: rock, fortress, deliverer, God, rock, shield, horn, stronghold, and "my strength" from v1. Most are military pictures drawn from David's own life. The first "rock" (sela) is a crag or cliff, like the rocks of the wilderness of En-gedi where David hid from Saul (1 Sam 23:25, 28; 24:2). The second (tsur) is a great solid rock, an image used of God from Moses' song onward (Deut 32:4, 15, 18). "Fortress" (metsudah) is the same word as the "stronghold" David holed up in (1 Sam 22:4–5; 24:22). What he once found in caves and cliffs, he found finally in God.
  • "The horn of my salvation" (v2). A horn is the weapon and pride of a strong animal like a wild ox (Deut 33:17), so it means power that wins. Zechariah, father of John the Baptist, took the phrase for Jesus: God "has raised up a horn of salvation for us in the house of his servant David" (Luke 1:69).
  • "I call upon the LORD… and I am saved" (v3). This is the whole psalm in miniature: call, and be saved. Verses 4–19 tell the story at length.
  • Commentators: Henry: "The poetry is very fine… but the piety far exceeds the poetry," and he takes "I will love thee" as "the scope and contents of the whole." JFB: "I will love thee—with most tender affection," and he explains the horn as "a frequent emblem of power or strength efficiently exercised." Guzik, following Boice, notes that this is a word normally used of the stronger's compassion for the weaker, which makes it striking on David's lips toward God, and passes on Maclaren's description of the list of titles as a "loving accumulation of dear names."

The Cords of Death (vv4–6)

  • "The cords of death encompassed me" (v4). Death is pictured as a hunter with ropes, snares and nets, and as a flood. "Torrents of destruction" is literally "torrents of Belial," worthlessness or ruin, the word that later became a name for Satan (2 Cor 6:15). "Sheol" (v5) is the realm of the dead (6:5; 16:10). David is not describing a bad week. He was a breath away from death many times (1 Sam 20:3, "there is but a step between me and death").
  • "In my distress I called upon the LORD" (v6). "Distress" (tsar) means a narrow, tight place. It will be answered in v19 by "a broad place." One cry goes up from a man in a tight spot, and all of heaven moves.
  • "From his temple he heard my voice" (v6). Solomon's temple did not yet exist. This is God's heavenly temple, his throne room (11:4). The cry of one hunted man reached the King's ears.
  • Commentators: JFB says "sorrows" (KJV) is literally "bands as of a net," and that "prevented" (KJV, v5) means death "encountered me, crossed my path." David was in danger, not in death's power. Guzik notes the temple must mean heaven, since Jerusalem's temple had not been built.

He Bowed the Heavens and Came Down (vv7–15)

  • The theophany. A theophany is an appearance of God. These verses describe God coming to rescue David in the language of earthquake, fire, storm and flood. No battle in Samuel is described like this, and most readers take it as poetry: this is what was really happening, unseen, every time God saved David. The images come from Israel's great memories of God's coming: Sinai shaking under fire, smoke and thick cloud (Exod 19:16–18), the Red Sea parting before "the blast of your nostrils" (Exod 15:8), and the hailstones that fell on Israel's enemies in Joshua's day (Josh 10:11).
  • "Smoke went up from his nostrils, and devouring fire from his mouth" (v8). In Hebrew, anger is "heat of the nose," so this is a vivid picture of God's anger. It is not anger at David. It is anger at those trying to destroy the one he loves.
  • "He bowed the heavens and came down" (v9). God tilts the sky down like a tent roof and steps down through it. The prophets use the same language: "Oh that you would rend the heavens and come down" (Isa 64:1).
  • "He rode on a cherub and flew" (v10). Cherubim are the mighty angelic beings who guard God's holiness (Gen 3:24). Golden cherubim stretched their wings over the ark, where God was "enthroned on the cherubim" (1 Sam 4:4). Canaanite poems praised Baal as the "rider of the clouds," the storm god. The psalm takes that language and gives it to its rightful owner. It is the LORD who rides the storm (68:4; 104:3).
  • "He made darkness his covering" (v11). God hides as he comes. Thick darkness surrounded him at Sinai (Deut 4:11; 5:22). He is near enough to save, and still too holy to be looked at.
  • "Hailstones and coals of fire" (vv12–13). Hail and fire together recall the seventh plague on Egypt (Exod 9:23–24). The last line of v13 repeats v12, and the 2 Samuel version lacks it. Some scholars think a scribe copied it twice. Nothing in the meaning depends on it.
  • "He sent out his arrows and scattered them" (v14). Lightning bolts are God's arrows (77:17; Hab 3:11). The enemies are scattered and routed in panic, as at the Red Sea (Exod 14:24).
  • "The channels of the sea were seen" (v15). The sea floor is laid bare, as when Israel crossed on dry ground. What God did for a nation at the Exodus, he did for one man in his trouble.
  • Commentators: JFB says God's coming is "described in figures drawn from His appearance on Sinai," and that the cherub and wind "may represent all rational and irrational agencies of God's providence." He says the language of v15 does not describe "any special physical changes" but "the utter subversion of the order of nature. Before such a God none can stand." Guzik takes the scene as poetry built on the Exodus, Sinai and the conquest, but allows that during David's unrecorded fugitive years God may have saved him in ways close to this. Both agree that judgment on David's enemies is the same act as rescue for David.

He Drew Me Out of Many Waters (vv16–19)

  • "He sent from on high, he took me" (v16). After the thunder, a single hand. The God who shook mountains reaches down and takes hold of one man.
  • "He drew me out of many waters" (v16). The verb mashah, "draw out," is rare. It is the word Pharaoh's daughter used when she named Moses: "Because I drew him out of the water" (Exod 2:10). David, the new servant of the LORD, is drawn out as Moses was. "Many waters" is the Bible's picture of chaos and overwhelming trouble (32:6; 124:4–5).
  • "My strong enemy… they were too mighty for me" (v17). The singular "strong enemy" is probably Saul, named in the title. David admits plainly that he could not win this on his own. That admission is what makes the rest of the psalm thanksgiving rather than boasting.
  • "They confronted me in the day of my calamity" (v18). Enemies strike when a man is down. "But the LORD was my support," literally "my staff," something to lean on.
  • "He brought me out into a broad place" (v19). The narrow place of v6 has become open country. Room to breathe and move is a favorite psalm picture of freedom (4:1; 31:8; 118:5).
  • "Because he delighted in me" (v19). Here is the root of the rescue. God saved David because he took pleasure in him. That delight was there before David did anything. The LORD chose him when he was a boy tending sheep, looking not at outward appearance but at the heart (1 Sam 16:7). At Jesus' baptism the Father said, "with you I am well pleased" (Matt 3:17), and believers are loved "in the Beloved" (Eph 1:6).
  • Commentators: JFB says God, "as seated on a throne, directing these terrible scenes," reached down to "His humble worshipper," and that "many waters" means calamities. Guzik sees two sides to God's delight in v19: his free choice of David, and his pleasure in a life that followed him. Henry reads "the servant of the Lord" in the title as a higher honour than being king.

According to My Righteousness (vv20–24)

  • "The LORD dealt with me according to my righteousness" (v20). This section can make a reader flinch, especially knowing about Bathsheba and Uriah. But David is not claiming to be sinless or to have earned God's love. He has just said God rescued him "because he delighted in me." He is claiming that in the matters he was accused of, above all his conduct toward Saul, his hands were clean. Twice he could have killed Saul and would not touch "the LORD's anointed" (1 Sam 24:6; 26:9–11).
  • "I have kept the ways of the LORD… all his rules were before me" (vv21–22). David describes the direction of his life: God's word kept in front of him, not thrown behind his back. This is covenant faithfulness, the loyal life of Psalm 1, not perfection.
  • "I was blameless before him" (v23). "Blameless" (tamim) means whole, complete, undivided, the word used of Noah (Gen 6:9) and Abraham (Gen 17:1). It describes integrity, a heart not split between God and something else, rather than a spotless record. "I kept myself from my guilt" suggests David knew exactly which sins tempted him most and guarded against them.
  • Before and after Bathsheba. If 2 Samuel is right to place this song late in David's life, he sang it after his great sins and after Psalm 51. That suggests he sang it as a forgiven man, whose overall path God had kept. Believers in Christ can speak this way only because they have been given a righteousness not their own (Phil 3:9). The psalm will speak of God making David's "way blameless" (v32).
  • Commentators: JFB says the claims refer to David's conduct "during all the trials to which he was subjected," and that "the perfection does not relate to degree." He suggests "mine iniquity" may be the temptation to kill Saul (1 Sam 24:6), and adds that the psalm cannot mean all David's conduct "is evident from Ps 51." Guzik also rules out a claim of sinless perfection, pointing to David's compromises among the Philistines (1 Sam 27; 29–30). He passes on Spurgeon's distinction: humble before God, David could still speak of clean hands before his slanderers. Guzik uses "I kept myself" to urge believers to guard against the particular sins they know they are drawn to (2 Tim 2:21).

With the Merciful You Show Yourself Merciful (vv25–30)

  • "With the merciful you show yourself merciful" (v25). "Merciful" is chasid, someone loyal in covenant love (chesed). God meets people as they come to him. The loyal find him loyal, the blameless find him whole-hearted, the pure find him pure. Jesus put it this way: "with the measure you use it will be measured to you" (Matt 7:2).
  • "With the crooked you make yourself seem tortuous" (v26). This does not mean God becomes crooked. The verb comes from a root meaning "twisted," the same root as Rachel's "wrestlings" when she named Naphtali (Gen 30:8). To the person who twists and wrestles against him, God's ways feel like a maze, and he outmaneuvers them. Pharaoh found this out. So did Saul.
  • "You save a humble people, but the haughty eyes you bring down" (v27). Hannah sang the same thing at the start of the books of Samuel: "The LORD… brings low and he exalts" (1 Sam 2:7). Mary sang it again: "he has… exalted those of humble estate" (Luke 1:52).
  • "It is you who light my lamp" (v28). A lamp kept burning in a house means life goes on there. David himself was called "the lamp of Israel" (2 Sam 21:17), and God later promised David a "lamp" in Jerusalem, a line of descendants on the throne (1 Kgs 11:36).
  • "By you I can run against a troop… leap over a wall" (v29). A raiding party and a city wall: the two obstacles of a soldier's life. Some think of the capture of Jerusalem, a city everyone said could not be taken (2 Sam 5:6–8).
  • "This God—his way is perfect" (v30). "Perfect" is tamim, the same word as "blameless" in v23. David's integrity was partial and borrowed. God's is complete. "The word of the LORD proves true," literally "is refined," tested in the fire like silver and found pure (12:6). Agur quotes this line almost word for word (Prov 30:5).
  • Commentators: JFB says God renders to men "according to their deeds in a penal, not vindictive, sense" (Lev 26:23–24), and that "tried" means "as metals are tried by fire and proved genuine." Henry calls God "a finishing God": "he is not only perfect himself, but his way is perfect." Guzik, following Boice, notes that the Hebrew of v26 is hard to translate, and passes on Spurgeon's point that faith may face harder battles but also receives more strength.

Who Is a Rock, Except Our God? (vv31–45)

  • "Who is God, but the LORD? And who is a rock, except our God?" (v31). These words echo Hannah's song, "there is no rock like our God" (1 Sam 2:2), and Moses' song, "their rock is not as our Rock" (Deut 32:31). Hannah's song opens the books of Samuel and this song closes them (2 Sam 22). Both speak of God raising the humble, of a "horn," and of God's "king" and "anointed" (1 Sam 2:10; Ps 18:50). The whole history of David is framed by these two songs.
  • "The God who equipped me with strength" (v32). Literally "girded me." Loose robes had to be tied up with a belt before running or fighting. God fastens strength around David like a soldier's belt (also v39).
  • "Feet like the feet of a deer… secure on the heights" (v33). A deer (or hind) moves over rocky cliffs without slipping. Habakkuk borrows the line (Hab 3:19). Holding the heights gave a fighter the advantage.
  • "My arms can bend a bow of bronze" (v34). A bow made entirely of bronze would not flex. This is probably a composite bow strengthened with metal, a weapon that took real strength to draw. The point is that God trained the hands and gave the strength.
  • "Your gentleness made me great" (v35). The noun (anavah) comes from the root for being lowly or humble. It is the humility of a great king who stoops down to lift someone. The 2 Samuel version may read "your answering." David's greatness did not come from his strength but from God stooping to him. Isaiah says the same: God dwells on high "and also with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit" (Isa 57:15).
  • "I pursued my enemies and overtook them" (vv37–42). The tone turns fierce and martial, and it can trouble a modern reader. This is a king fighting the wars God gave him to secure Israel against nations that attacked it (2 Sam 5:17–25; 8:1–14). Even here the credit goes upward: "you equipped me… you made those who rise against me sink under me" (v39). "You made my enemies turn their backs to me" (v40) is literally "you gave me their neck," the ancient gesture of a victor's foot on a defeated enemy's neck (Josh 10:24).
  • "They cried to the LORD, but he did not answer them" (v41). A sobering line. When pressed, even enemies called on Israel's God, but too late and not in faith. Saul himself, near the end, "inquired of the LORD" and got no answer (1 Sam 28:6). Proverbs 1:28 warns of the same.
  • "You made me the head of the nations" (v43). Under David, Israel's neighbors, Philistia, Moab, Aram, Edom and Ammon, became subject or allied (2 Sam 8; 10). "People whom I had not known served me," like Hiram king of Tyre, who sent cedar and craftsmen (2 Sam 5:11).
  • "Foreigners came cringing to me" (v44). The verb (kachash) means to lie or feign. It pictures defeated peoples flattering a conqueror, offering obedience they did not mean. David knows that outward submission is not true allegiance. The psalm's last lines will look for something better.
  • Commentators: Henry reads "thy gentleness hath made me great" as God's discipline and teaching: "The good lessons David learned in his affliction prepared him for the dignity and power," and "the lessening of him helped very much to increase his greatness." He adds, "In all this David was a type of Christ… made victorious" over the powers of darkness. Guzik passes on Kidner's comment that God's gentleness was the true source of David's greatness, and Kidner's suggestion that the bow was wood reinforced with metal. JFB explains girding as "an expressive figure for describing the gift of strength."

The LORD Lives (vv46–50)

  • "The LORD lives, and blessed be my rock" (v46). The gods of the nations were carved and dead. Israel's God lives, so he can hear, act and keep his promises. The psalm began "The LORD is my rock" (v2) and ends "blessed be my rock."
  • "The God who gave me vengeance" (v47). David did not take vengeance on Saul himself. He left it to God (1 Sam 24:12; 26:10), and God settled the account. Paul says the same to believers: "Never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God" (Rom 12:19).
  • "I will praise you, O LORD, among the nations" (v49). David means to sing Israel's God to foreign peoples. Paul quotes this verse in Romans 15:9 as proof that the Gentiles were always meant to glorify God for his mercy. He strings it together with three other Old Testament texts (Rom 15:10–12) to show that Christ came to gather Jews and Gentiles into one worshipping people. Those foreigners who came "cringing" in v44 are replaced by nations singing for joy.
  • "Great salvation he brings to his king… to his anointed, to David and his offspring forever" (v50). "Anointed" is mashiach, Messiah. The last word of the psalm reaches back to God's promise in 2 Samuel 7:12–16 that David's throne would be established forever. No king in David's line fulfilled that "forever" until the true Son of David came.
  • Commentators: Henry: "The gods of the heathen were dead gods. The best friends we have among men are dying friends. But God lives… and, because he lives, they shall live also." Guzik passes on Maclaren's observation about the psalm's opening and closing "rock," and links v50 directly to the covenant with David in 2 Samuel 7. On v49 he notes that Paul hears in it Christ himself praising God among the Gentiles, while Theodoret pointed out that David's own psalms are still sung among the nations, so the verse is true of both.

Pointing to Christ

Paul read this psalm as the voice of Christ (Rom 15:9), and it fits him better than it fit David. The cords of death did more than threaten him. They bound him. He called out from the cross, and the earth shook and the sky went dark (Matt 27:45, 51). God reached down and drew him up out of the many waters of death on the third day, because he delighted in him. His righteousness needed no qualification, and his hands were truly clean. Now God has made him "head of the nations," and people he had not known come to serve him, not cringing and feigning, but singing. The "great salvation" promised to David's offspring forever belongs to him, and through him to everyone who calls on the LORD and is saved.

Reflection

  • No Single Image Captures God (v.2) — David piles up metaphor after metaphor for God: rock, fortress, deliverer, shield, horn, tower. Which of these pictures of God do you need most in your current situation?
  • Crying Out From Real Distress (v.6) — David calls to God from genuine anguish, and his voice reaches God's ears. Do you bring your distress to God directly, trusting that He hears?
  • Rescued Because God Delights in You (v.19) — David attributes his deliverance not to his own effort but to God's pleasure in him. Do you see God's care for you as an obligation He owes, or as something He delights to give?
  • God Matches Your Posture Toward Him (v.25) — David describes God as merciful to the merciful and faithful to the faithful. Is how you approach God shaping the kind of relationship you experience with Him?
  • Greatness Through Gentleness (v.35) — David credits God's gentleness, not his own strength, for making him great. Are you willing to receive God's gentle care rather than relying only on your own toughness?
  • Praise as the Final Word (v.46) — After recounting every rescue and victory, David ends by exalting God as his living Rock. Does your story, told honestly, end in praise?
