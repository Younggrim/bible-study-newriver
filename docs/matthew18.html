1At that time the disciples came to Jesus and asked, “Who then is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven?”

2Jesus invited a little child to stand among them.

3“Truly I tell you,” He said, “unless you change and become like little children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven.

4Therefore, whoever humbles himself like this little child is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven.

5And whoever welcomes a little child like this in My name welcomes Me.

6But if anyone causes one of these little ones who believe in Me to stumble, it would be better for him to have a large millstone hung around his neck and to be drowned in the depths of the sea.

7Woe to the world for the causes of sin. These stumbling blocks must come, but woe to the man through whom they come!

8If your hand or your foot causes you to sin, cut it off and throw it away. It is better for you to enter life crippled or lame than to have two hands and two feet and be thrown into the eternal fire.

9And if your eye causes you to sin, gouge it out and throw it away. It is better for you to enter life with one eye than to have two eyes and be thrown into the fire of hell.

10See that you do not look down on any of these little ones. For I tell you that their angels in heaven always see the face of My Father in heaven.

12What do you think? If a man has a hundred sheep and one of them goes astray, will he not leave the ninety-nine on the hills and go out to search for the one that is lost?

13And if he finds it, truly I tell you, he rejoices more over that one sheep than over the ninety-nine that did not go astray.

14In the same way, your Father in heaven is not willing that any of these little ones should perish.

15If your brother sins against you, go and confront him privately. If he listens to you, you have won your brother over.

16But if he will not listen, take one or two others along, so that ‘every matter may be established by the testimony of two or three witnesses.’

17If he refuses to listen to them, tell it to the church. And if he refuses to listen even to the church, regard him as you would a pagan or a tax collector.

18Truly I tell you, whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth will be loosed in heaven.

19Again, I tell you truly that if two of you on the earth agree about anything you ask for, it will be done for you by My Father in heaven.

20For where two or three gather together in My name, there am I with them.”

21Then Peter came to Jesus and asked, “Lord, how many times shall I forgive my brother who sins against me? Up to seven times?”

22Jesus answered, “I tell you, not just seven times, but seventy-seven times!

23Because of this, the kingdom of heaven is like a king who wanted to settle accounts with his servants.

24As he began the settlements, a debtor owing ten thousand talents was brought to him.

25Since the man was unable to pay, the master ordered that he be sold to pay his debt, along with his wife and children and everything he owned.

26Then the servant fell on his knees before him. ‘Have patience with me,’ he begged, ‘and I will pay back everything.’

27His master had compassion on him, forgave his debt, and released him.

28But when that servant went out, he found one of his fellow servants who owed him a hundred denarii. He grabbed him and began to choke him, saying, ‘Pay back what you owe me!’

29So his fellow servant fell down and begged him, ‘Have patience with me, and I will pay you back.’

30But he refused. Instead, he went and had the man thrown into prison until he could pay his debt.

31When his fellow servants saw what had happened, they were greatly distressed, and they went and recounted all of this to their master.

32Then the master summoned him and said, ‘You wicked servant! I forgave all your debt because you begged me.

33Shouldn’t you have had mercy on your fellow servant, just as I had on you?’

34In anger his master turned him over to the jailers to be tortured, until he should repay all that he owed.

35That is how My heavenly Father will treat each of you unless you forgive your brother from your heart.”

Summary

When the disciples ask who is greatest in the kingdom of heaven, Jesus calls a child to himself and says that only those who humble themselves like children will enter the kingdom at all, and that welcoming such a child is welcoming him (vv. 1-5). He warns severely against causing one of these little ones to sin, saying it would be better to be drowned than to cause such stumbling, and urges radical measures to avoid sin (vv. 6-9). Through the parable of the lost sheep, he shows that the Father is unwilling that any of these little ones should perish (vv. 10-14). Jesus then lays out a process for confronting a sinning brother — privately, then with witnesses, then before the church — and promises his presence wherever two or three gather in his name (vv. 15-20). When Peter asks how many times he must forgive, Jesus answers not seven times but seventy times seven, and illustrates the point with the parable of the unforgiving servant, who is forgiven a massive debt yet refuses to forgive a much smaller one and is handed over to be punished (vv. 21-35).

Authorship & Background

Author: Matthew (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 18 is Matthew's fourth major discourse — the "Community Discourse" or "Church Discourse." It addresses how citizens of the kingdom relate to one another: humility (vv.1-5), protecting the vulnerable (vv.6-14), church discipline (vv.15-20), and radical forgiveness (vv.21-35).
Classification: Gospel — Narrative and Discourse
Key Themes: a child called into the middle of the room to answer a question about greatness, the most violent language in the whole Gospel aimed at anyone who causes one of these little ones to offend, a shepherd's arithmetic applied to a single lost sheep out of ninety-nine, an explicit three-step process for confronting sin within the community that aims at restoration rather than punishment, Peter's generous offer of seven times answered with seventy times seven, not a larger number but the abolition of counting, and a parable pairing an unpayable debt of ten thousand talents against a manageable debt of a hundred pence to explain exactly why
Who Is the Greatest (vv.1-14): The question is asked directly, who is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven? and the answer is a child called into the middle of the room, with a demand attached, except ye be converted, and become as little children, ye shall not enter. Then the warnings, and they are the most violent language in Matthew, aimed at whoever causes one of these little ones to offend: a millstone, and then hand, foot and eye. The section ends on the lost sheep, and Matthew's version is about a shepherd's arithmetic, doth he not leave the ninety and nine, and go into the mountains, and seek that which is gone astray? with the conclusion, it is not the will of your Father that one of these little ones should perish.
Church Discipline (vv.15-17): Jesus gives the only explicit process for addressing sin within the community of believers — private confrontation first, then witnesses, then the church. The goal at every stage is restoration, not punishment.
Where Two or Three Are Gathered (vv.18-22): The binding and loosing granted to Peter in chapter 16 is granted here to the community, and the context is the discipline procedure immediately before it rather than prayer in general. The promise about two or three gathered together in my name is part of the same paragraph. Then Peter's question, and he offers a generous number expecting approval, till seven times? The answer is seventy times seven, which is not a larger limit but the abolition of counting, and the parable that follows exists to explain why.
The Parable of the Unforgiving Servant (vv.23-35): Ten thousand talents was an astronomical sum — roughly 200,000 years of wages. It represents an unpayable debt. One hundred pence (denarii) was about 100 days' wages — a real but manageable debt. The contrast is the point: God has forgiven us an infinite debt; how can we refuse to forgive the small debts others owe us?

⚑ Report an issue or ideaⓘ How this site is made

Setting: Capernaum, late in Jesus' Galilean ministry, around autumn of AD 29. Jesus has just spoken plainly a second time about his coming death (17:22–23) and paid the temple tax for himself and Peter (17:24–27). Mark says that on the road the disciples had been arguing about which of them was the greatest (Mark 9:33–34). Chapter 18 is the fourth of the five great teaching blocks in Matthew (after chs 5–7, 10 and 13), and it ends with Matthew's usual formula, "when Jesus had finished these sayings" (19:1). It is often called the sermon on life in the church. Its themes run in order: humility, not causing others to fall, seeking the one who strays, restoring a brother who sins, and forgiving without limit.

Who Is the Greatest? (vv1–6)

  • "Who is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven?" (v1). The disciples have just heard that Jesus will be killed, and their next question is about rank. Some think Peter's prominence, or the three chosen for the mountain (17:1), set off the rivalry. They still picture the kingdom as a court with positions to hand out (20:20–21).
  • "Calling to him a child, he put him in the midst of them" (v2). Jesus answers with an object lesson. In the ancient world a child had no status, no rights and no power. Children were loved, but they were not admired or looked up to, and no one thought of them as models.
  • "Unless you turn and become like children, you will never enter" (v3). Jesus changes the question. They asked who would be greatest in the kingdom, and he says that without a change of heart they will not enter it at all. "Turn" (strephō) is the language of conversion, of facing a new direction. The point is not that children are innocent, which no parent believes, but that they are small, dependent and unconcerned with rank.
  • "Whoever humbles himself like this child is the greatest" (v4). Humility here is not thinking badly of oneself but accepting a low place before God, as a child accepts that it cannot provide for itself. Jesus himself is the model: he "humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death" (Phil 2:8).
  • "Whoever receives one such child in my name receives me" (v5). "Such" widens the circle from literal children to all who have become small in this way. Welcoming the unimportant believer is welcoming Christ (10:40–42; 25:40).
  • "One of these little ones who believe in me" (v6). "Little ones" in this chapter means believers, especially the vulnerable and the young in faith. "Causes… to sin" is skandalizō, to set a trap or stumbling block that makes someone fall away. The noun skandalon first meant the stick in a trap that springs it.
  • "A great millstone fastened around his neck" (v6). Literally a "donkey's millstone," the large upper stone turned by a donkey, as opposed to the small hand mill women used at home. Drowning was a Roman and Greek punishment that Jews regarded with horror. Jesus is saying that a quick and terrible death would be better than what awaits the one who wrecks a believer's faith.
  • Commentators: Guzik notes that Jesus did not answer by naming Peter as greatest, which he thinks tells against later claims of Petrine supremacy. He stresses that the child is held up for humility and lack of concern for status, not for innocence, and mentions a late, weakly supported tradition that the child grew up to be Ignatius of Antioch. Henry draws the lesson from the child set "in the midst" that greatness in Christ's kingdom is measured by humility. JFB gives no comment on vv1–9 in Matthew and sends readers to its notes on Mark 9.

Temptations to Sin (vv7–9)

  • "Woe to the world for temptations to sin!" (v7). "Temptations to sin" is the plural of skandalon, stumbling blocks. "Woe" here is both grief and warning.
  • "It is necessary that temptations come, but woe to the one by whom the temptation comes" (v7). In a fallen world stumbling blocks are certain. That does not excuse the one who sets them. Scripture holds both together: God's purposes stand, and people are still accountable for their choices (26:24; Acts 2:23).
  • "If your hand or your foot causes you to sin, cut it off" (v8). Jesus said much the same in the Sermon on the Mount about lust (5:29–30). Here he turns from causing others to fall to the things that make us fall. This is deliberate overstatement, a common Jewish teaching style. The church has never taken it as a command to maim the body. Origen of Alexandria reportedly castrated himself in his youth after reading 19:12 too literally, and later regretted it. The point is that nothing, however precious, is worth keeping if it is dragging someone toward ruin.
  • "Better… to enter life crippled… than… thrown into the eternal fire" (vv8–9). "Life" means eternal life in the kingdom. "The hell of fire" is literally "the Gehenna of fire." Gehenna was the Valley of Hinnom south of Jerusalem, where children had once been burned as offerings to Molech (2 Kgs 23:10; Jer 7:31). By Jesus' day its name had become the word for final judgment.
  • Commentators: Henry writes, "The outward occasions of sin must be avoided." Guzik points out that cutting off a limb would not actually solve the problem, since sin lives in the heart, so Jesus is calling for a radical inward change and not a surgical one. He finds comfort in v7 as well: God takes the harm done to his little ones seriously and will answer for them.

The Parable of the Lost Sheep (vv10–14)

  • "See that you do not despise one of these little ones" (v10). "Despise" is kataphroneō, to look down on or treat as not worth bothering about. The disciples, arguing about greatness, were in danger of exactly that.
  • "Their angels always see the face of my Father" (v10). In royal courts, only the most trusted servants could "see the king's face" (2 Kgs 25:19; Esth 1:14). The angels who serve these little ones have that kind of access. Jewish tradition already spoke of angels watching over nations and people (Dan 10:13, 20–21), and Hebrews calls angels "ministering spirits sent out to serve" those who will inherit salvation (Heb 1:14). Christians have long drawn the idea of guardian angels from this verse. Scripture does not say whether each believer has one assigned angel, and the verse's point is simpler: if heaven pays such attention to these little ones, no disciple should look down on them. When Peter was freed from prison, the believers said "It is his angel!" (Acts 12:15), which shows the idea was current.
  • Verse 11 is missing. The ESV moves from v10 to v12 and gives v11 in a footnote: "For the Son of Man came to save the lost." The earliest manuscripts of Matthew lack it, and most scholars think scribes borrowed it from Luke 19:10, where it is secure. Its truth is not in doubt, only its place here.
  • "If a man has a hundred sheep" (v12). A flock of a hundred was a fair size for a family in Judea. Shepherds counted their sheep at night, so one missing would be noticed. Leaving the ninety-nine "on the mountains" sounds reckless, but they would be left together on the hillside pasture, likely with another herdsman, while the owner searched.
  • Two uses of one parable. Luke tells this story to Pharisees grumbling about Jesus eating with sinners, and there the sheep is a lost sinner and the parable ends with joy in heaven over one who repents (Luke 15:3–7). Matthew places it in teaching to disciples, and here the sheep is a believer who "has gone astray." Jesus likely used the parable more than once. The shift in words matches: in Luke the sheep is "lost," in Matthew it "goes astray" (planaō), the word Jesus uses for being led astray by false teachers (24:4–5, 11).
  • "He rejoices over it more than over the ninety-nine" (v13). Note "if he finds it." The search is not guaranteed to succeed, but the shepherd does not count the cost of trying.
  • "It is not the will of my Father… that one of these little ones should perish" (v14). The closing line brings the parable back to v10. The Father's heart is set on keeping every one of his little ones, so the church must share that care for the one who wanders, and not shrug it off.
  • Commentators: JFB calls v10 "a difficult verse," and thinks it shows the angels have a special, familiar access to the Father because of their charge over these believers. JFB's text still includes v11, which it calls "a golden saying, once and again repeated in different forms." Guzik accepts that angels serve believers (Heb 1:14) but does not limit this to one guardian angel for each person. He also compares this parable with Luke 15, noting that the two use it for different purposes. Henry: "A father takes care of all his children, but is particularly tender of the little ones."

If Your Brother Sins Against You (vv15–20)

  • "If your brother sins against you" (v15). Some important early manuscripts lack "against you," which would make the instruction apply to any sin a fellow believer commits. The ESV keeps the words with a footnote. Peter's question in v21 about a brother sinning "against me" suggests the personal sense fits the context.
  • "Go and tell him his fault, between you and him alone" (v15). The first step is private, which protects the brother's reputation and cuts off gossip. "Tell him his fault" is elenchō, to show someone his wrong so he can see it. Leviticus 19:17–18 commands the same: "you shall reason frankly with your neighbor," right before "you shall love your neighbor as yourself." The goal is put plainly: "you have gained your brother." The word "gain" (kerdainō) is a merchant's word for profit. A restored brother is the prize, not a won argument.
  • "Take one or two others along with you" (v16). Jesus quotes Deuteronomy 19:15, the law that no one could be convicted on a single witness. The extra witnesses protect the accused as much as the accuser. They can tell whether the charge is true, and they add weight to the appeal.
  • "Tell it to the church" (v17). The Greek word is ekklēsia, used only twice in the Gospels, here and in 16:18. In 16:18 it meant the whole church Jesus would build. Here it means a local gathering of believers. Jesus is preparing his followers for life together after he is gone.
  • "Let him be to you as a Gentile and a tax collector" (v17). A devout Jew would not share a table with either. So the person who refuses to listen to the whole church is to be treated as outside the fellowship. Paul applies the same principle (1 Cor 5:1–13; 2 Thess 3:14–15). There is a gentle irony here too: Jesus is known for eating with tax collectors and sinners (9:10–11), and Matthew was one. Treating someone as an outsider does not mean hating him; it means calling him back as one would call an unbeliever, to repentance. The goal throughout, as Paul says, is restoration (2 Cor 2:6–8; Gal 6:1).
  • "Whatever you bind on earth shall be bound in heaven" (v18). In 16:19 Jesus gave Peter the keys and said this to him in the singular. Here the "you" is plural and the words are given to the disciples, and so to the church. "Bind" and "loose" were rabbinic terms for forbidding and permitting, and also for declaring someone under discipline or free from it. The Greek verb forms are future perfects ("shall have been bound"), which many read as meaning the church's right decisions line up with what heaven has already decided, rather than heaven simply rubber-stamping the church. John 20:23 is a close parallel.
  • "If two of you agree on earth about anything they ask" (v19). "Agree" is symphōneō, from which comes "symphony": to sound together. In context this is about the hard work of discipline: when two or three believers seek God together over a matter like this, the Father hears. It is often used more widely as a promise about united prayer, which is fair as long as it is not turned into a formula that guarantees any request.
  • "Where two or three are gathered in my name, there am I among them" (v20). "Two or three" echoes the witnesses of v16. A later Jewish saying in the Mishnah says that when two sit together over the words of the law, God's presence (the Shekinah) rests among them (m. Avot 3:2). Jesus puts himself in that place. He speaks as God present with his people, which fits the Gospel's frame: he is "Immanuel… God with us" (1:23) and promises "I am with you always" (28:20).
  • Commentators: Henry sums up the first step: "to go to the offender privately, state the matter kindly, and show him his conduct." JFB sets out the four steps in order and insists that the binding and loosing of v18, "whatever it means, it means nothing peculiar to Peter," since it is now given to all the Twelve. Guzik adds that not every offense has to be raised, because love also covers and bears with others, and stresses that the aim at every step is restoration, not winning. He reads "agree" as a picture of instruments in tune with one another, which fits the lexicon's "to sound together" or "be in harmony." His list of what is not essential to a true gathering (numbers, rank, building, form) and what is (gathering around Jesus' name) is a helpful application of v20.

The Parable of the Unforgiving Servant (vv21–35)

  • "As many as seven times?" (v21). Some rabbis taught that a person should forgive the same offense three times, drawing on Amos 1–2 ("for three transgressions… and for four"). Peter more than doubles that and adds one, choosing seven, the number of completeness. He thinks he is being generous.
  • "Seventy-seven times" (v22). The Greek is hebdomēkontakis hepta, which can mean "seventy times seven" (KJV, NKJV) or "seventy-seven times" (ESV, NIV). The same phrase appears in the Greek Old Testament of Genesis 4:24, where Lamech boasts that if Cain is avenged sevenfold, "Lamech seventy-sevenfold." Jesus takes the old song of unlimited revenge and turns it into unlimited forgiveness. Whether 77 or 490, the point is the same: forgiveness that keeps count has missed the point.
  • "A king who wished to settle accounts with his servants" (v23). These "servants" are probably high officials or governors, men who collected revenues on the king's behalf, as in many ancient courts.
  • "Ten thousand talents" (v24). A talent was the largest unit of money, about 6,000 denarii, roughly twenty years' wages for a laborer. Ten thousand (myrioi) was the largest number in Greek counting, the source of English "myriad." So the debt is the biggest number with the biggest unit, about 60 million days' wages. For comparison, the historian Josephus says the yearly tribute from all of Galilee and Perea under Herod Antipas was 200 talents. The debt is deliberately absurd. It could never be paid.
  • "Ordered him to be sold, with his wife and children" (v25). Selling a debtor and his family into slavery was legal in the ancient world (2 Kgs 4:1; Neh 5:5). It would not have come close to covering the debt. A slave might fetch a talent at most. It was punishment more than repayment.
  • "Have patience with me, and I will pay you everything" (v26). The servant's promise is impossible. The master knows it.
  • "Out of pity for him, the master… released him and forgave him the debt" (v27). "Pity" is splanchnizomai, a deep, gut-level compassion, the word used of Jesus' compassion for the crowds (9:36; 14:14). The master gives far more than was asked. The servant asked for time; he receives a cancelled debt.
  • "A hundred denarii" (v28). A denarius was a day's wage, so this was about a hundred days' work: real money, but tiny beside what he had just been forgiven, about one six-hundred-thousandth of it. "Seizing him, he began to choke him" describes grabbing someone by the throat. The forgiven man walks out and becomes a thug.
  • "Have patience with me, and I will pay you" (v29). The fellow servant falls down and uses almost the same words the first servant used. Readers hear the echo; the first servant does not. And this time the promise could actually be kept.
  • "Put him in prison until he should pay the debt" (v30). Debtor's prison meant the family had to find the money. He chose to keep his fellow servant from earning anything at all.
  • "His fellow servants… were greatly distressed" (v31). The rest of the household sees what he cannot.
  • "You wicked servant! I forgave you all that debt… should not you have had mercy… as I had mercy on you?" (vv32–33). This is the heart of the parable. Mercy received is meant to become mercy given. The master's question needs no answer.
  • "Delivered him to the jailers, until he should pay all his debt" (v34). The word is literally "torturers" (basanistai), and such treatment of prisoners was common in the ancient world. Since the debt can never be paid, the sentence is without end.
  • "So also my heavenly Father will do… if you do not forgive your brother from your heart" (v35). This echoes the Lord's Prayer and its comment: "if you do not forgive others their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses" (6:12, 14–15). Jesus is not saying forgiveness is earned by forgiving others. The servant's debt was cancelled freely, before he did anything. He is saying that a person who truly receives God's mercy will be changed by it, and one who refuses all mercy to others shows he never let God's mercy reach his heart. "From your heart" rules out words without the will behind them, though forgiving is often a decision made before the feelings catch up.
  • Commentators: JFB reckons the debt at "above a million and a half sterling" in Victorian money if Attic talents are meant, and reads "seventy times seven" as forgiveness offered "so long as it shall be needed and sought." On v35 it says the Father deals with each person "in this spirit, or on this principle." Henry: "We do not forgive our offending brother aright, if we do not forgive from the heart." Guzik makes a striking point: believers have an even greater reason to forgive than God has, because God has never needed forgiving and they always do. He distinguishes forgiveness, which one person can give, from reconciliation, which takes two, and notes that forgiving does not cancel all earthly consequences (Rom 13). The Tyndale notes read the jailers as a picture of final judgment.

Pointing to Christ

Every part of this chapter rests on who Jesus is. He is the one who "humbled himself" to the lowest place, and so the child becomes the measure of greatness. He is the shepherd who goes after the one that wanders, and the one who promises to be among two or three who gather in his name. Above all he is the reason the great debt can be cancelled. The king in the parable simply absorbs a loss no one could ever repay, and at the cross Jesus paid that cost himself. Those who have been forgiven ten thousand talents in him are set free to forgive a hundred denarii, again and again.

Reflection

  • Greatness Through Humility (v.4) — Jesus overturns the disciples' status-seeking by naming the one who humbles himself like a child as the greatest in the kingdom. Where is your desire for recognition competing with a call to humility?
  • The Weight of Influence (v.6) — Jesus gives a severe warning to anyone who causes a vulnerable believer to stumble. As a father, leader, or mentor, are you using your influence to point people toward Christ or away from Him?
  • Pursuit of the Wanderer (vv.12-14) — The shepherd leaves the ninety-nine to search for the one that strayed, showing the Father's heart for the lost. Do you view people who have drifted from faith with judgment, or with pursuit?
  • Direct, Private Restoration (v.15) — Jesus' path for addressing sin between believers is private and aimed at winning back a brother, not public exposure or silent avoidance. Is there a conflict you're avoiding or gossiping about instead of addressing directly?
  • Forgiveness Without Limit (v.22) — Jesus tells Peter to forgive not seven times but seventy times seven — forgiveness with no ceiling. Is there someone you've mentally written off after being wronged one time too many?
  • Forgiven Much, Forgiving Little (vv.32-33) — A servant forgiven an enormous debt turns around and refuses to forgive a small one owed to him. Is there someone you're withholding forgiveness from, even though God has forgiven you far more?
