1Then the word of the LORD came to me, saying,

2“Son of man, prophesy against the shepherds of Israel. Prophesy and tell them that this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Woe to the shepherds of Israel, who only feed themselves! Should not the shepherds feed their flock?

3You eat the fat, wear the wool, and butcher the fattened sheep, but you do not feed the flock.

4You have not strengthened the weak, healed the sick, bound up the injured, brought back the strays, or searched for the lost. Instead, you have ruled them with violence and cruelty.

5They were scattered for lack of a shepherd, and when they were scattered they became food for all the wild beasts.

6My flock went astray on all the mountains and every high hill. They were scattered over the face of all the earth, with no one to search for them or seek them out.’

7Therefore, you shepherds, hear the word of the LORD:

8‘As surely as I live, declares the Lord GOD, because My flock lacks a shepherd and has become prey and food for every wild beast, and because My shepherds did not search for My flock but fed themselves instead,

9therefore, you shepherds, hear the word of the LORD!’

10This is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Behold, I am against the shepherds, and I will demand from them My flock and remove them from tending the flock, so that they can no longer feed themselves. For I will deliver My flock from their mouths, and it will no longer be food for them.’

11For this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Behold, I Myself will search for My flock and seek them out.

12As a shepherd looks for his scattered sheep when he is among the flock, so I will look for My flock. I will rescue them from all the places to which they were scattered on a day of clouds and darkness.

13I will bring them out from the peoples, gather them from the countries, and bring them into their own land. I will feed them on the mountains of Israel, in the ravines, and in all the settlements of the land.

14I will feed them in good pasture, and the lofty mountains of Israel will be their grazing land. There they will lie down in a good grazing land; they will feed in rich pasture on the mountains of Israel.

15I will tend My flock and make them lie down, declares the Lord GOD.

16I will seek the lost, bring back the strays, bind up the broken, and strengthen the weak; but the sleek and strong I will destroy. I will shepherd them with justice.’

17This is what the Lord GOD says to you, My flock: ‘I will judge between one sheep and another, between the rams and the goats.

18Is it not enough for you to feed on the good pasture? Must you also trample the rest of the pasture with your feet? Is it not enough for you to drink the clear waters? Must you also muddy the rest with your feet?

19Why must My flock feed on what your feet have trampled, and drink what your feet have muddied?’

20Therefore this is what the Lord GOD says to them: ‘Behold, I Myself will judge between the fat sheep and the lean sheep.

21Since you shove with flank and shoulder, butting all the weak ones with your horns until you have scattered them abroad,

22I will save My flock, and they will no longer be prey. I will judge between one sheep and another.

23I will appoint over them one shepherd, My servant David, and he will feed them. He will feed them and be their shepherd.

24I, the LORD, will be their God, and My servant David will be a prince among them. I, the LORD, have spoken.

25I will make with them a covenant of peace and rid the land of wild animals, so that they may dwell securely in the wilderness and sleep in the forest.

26I will make them and the places around My hill a blessing. I will send down showers in season—showers of blessing.

27The trees of the field will give their fruit, and the land will yield its produce; My flock will be secure in their land. Then they will know that I am the LORD, when I have broken the bars of their yoke and delivered them from the hands that enslaved them.

28They will no longer be prey for the nations, and the beasts of the earth will not consume them. They will dwell securely, and no one will frighten them.

29And I will raise up for them a garden of renown, and they will no longer be victims of famine in the land or bear the scorn of the nations.

30Then they will know that I, the LORD their God, am with them, and that they, the house of Israel, are My people,’ declares the Lord GOD.

31‘You are My flock, the sheep of My pasture, My people, and I am your God,’ declares the Lord GOD.”

1And the word of the LORD came unto me, saying,

9Therefore, O ye shepherds, hear the word of the LORD;

14I will feed them in a good pasture, and upon the high mountains of Israel shall their fold be: there shall they lie in a good fold, and in a fat pasture shall they feed upon the mountains of Israel.

Summary

God pronounces judgment on the shepherds of Israel — its leaders — who have fed themselves rather than the flock, neglecting the weak, sick, injured, and scattered sheep (vv. 1-10). Because the shepherds have failed, God declares He will personally search for His sheep, gather them, and feed and heal them Himself (vv. 11-16). He also promises to judge between the fat and lean sheep, protecting the weak from being pushed aside by the strong (vv. 17-22), and to set up one shepherd over them, His servant David, under a covenant of peace that brings security and abundant blessing (vv. 23-31).

Authorship & Background

Author: Ezekiel son of Buzi (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — Against False Shepherds + The Divine Shepherd + Messianic Promise
Key Themes: Failed leadership indicted, God as personal Shepherd, gathering the scattered, the messianic "David," covenant of peace, blessings restored
Historical Context: After chapters of judgment (including oracles against nations, chs.25-32), chapter 34 begins the restoration section of Ezekiel (chs.34-48). The "shepherds" (Israel's kings, priests, prophets, elders) failed: they fed themselves, didn't heal the sick, didn't bind the broken, didn't seek the lost (vv.2-4). The sheep were scattered among nations — the exile was partially the shepherds' fault. God's response: (1) I will judge the shepherds, (2) I myself will shepherd My flock, (3) I will set up "my servant David" (the Messiah) as one shepherd. Jesus identifies Himself as this shepherd in John 10.
Woe to the Shepherds Who Feed Themselves (vv.1-6): Shepherd was the ordinary ancient title for a king, so this is an oracle against Judah's rulers and everyone hearing it knew that. The charge is stated as a reversal of function, woe be to the shepherds of Israel that do feed themselves, should not the shepherds feed the flocks. What follows is a job description read backwards: the diseased ye have not strengthened, neither have ye healed that which was sick, neither have ye bound up that which was broken, neither have ye brought again that which was driven away, neither have ye sought that which was lost. And the manner is named as well as the omissions, but with force and with cruelty have ye ruled them.
God Against the Shepherds (vv.7-10): Behold, I am against the shepherds, and I will require my flock at their hand, and cause them to cease from feeding the flock. The removal is total, neither shall the shepherds feed themselves any more, and I will deliver my flock from their mouth, that they may not be meat for them. What is being described is not reform of the office but its confiscation.
God Himself Will Shepherd (vv.11-16): I will both search my sheep, and seek them out, and the actions listed are the omissions of verse 4 performed one by one: I will seek that which was lost, and bring again that which was driven away, and will bind up that which was broken, and will strengthen that which was sick. The conditions are named too, delivered out of all places where they have been scattered in the cloudy and dark day, fed upon the mountains of Israel, lying in a good fold. Both Luke 15 and John 10 draw on this passage directly. The last clause keeps it from being only comfort, but I will destroy the fat and the strong, I will feed them with judgment.
Judgment Between Sheep (vv.17-22): The oppression in this section is not by rulers, which is why it needs its own place: it is by other members of the flock, and I judge between cattle and cattle. The questions put are about waste rather than about consumption, is it a small thing unto you to have eaten up the good pasture, but ye must tread down with your feet the residue of your pastures, and to have drunk of the deep waters, but ye must foul the residue with your feet. Then the physical picture, ye push with side and with shoulder, and push all the diseased with your horns, therefore will I save my flock, and they shall no more be a prey.
My Servant David, One Shepherd (vv.23-24): I will set up one shepherd over them, and he shall feed them, even my servant David. David had been dead about four hundred years when this was said, so the name is being used dynastically, and the arrangement is stated in two halves that are held together everywhere in this book, and I the LORD will be their God, and my servant David a prince among them. Jesus takes up the whole chapter when he calls himself the good shepherd, and the contrast he draws with the hireling is the contrast this chapter opened with.
A Covenant of Peace, and Showers of Blessing (vv.25-31): I will make with them a covenant of peace, and the safety described is concrete rather than spiritual, the evil beasts caused to cease out of the land, and they shall dwell safely in the wilderness, and sleep in the woods. The agricultural promise contains a phrase that has passed into ordinary speech, I will cause the shower to come down in his season, there shall be showers of blessing. The chapter ends by stating the relationship twice, once from each side, thus shall they know that I the LORD their God am with them, and that they, even the house of Israel, are my people, and then in the second person, ye my flock, the flock of my pasture, are men, and I am your God.

Setting: Babylonia, soon after the news of Jerusalem's fall reached the exiles in early 585 BC (33:21). The city was in ruins, the temple burned, the king blinded and in chains, and the people scattered. Chapter 33 reopened Ezekiel's mouth, and with chapter 34 he begins his messages of restoration (chs 34–48). He starts by asking why the disaster happened and who was to blame. His answer uses one of the oldest pictures in the ancient world. Kings across the Near East called themselves shepherds of their people; the law code of Hammurabi opens with the king as a shepherd, and Israel's ideal king, David, had been a shepherd boy (2 Sam 5:2; Ps 78:70–72). Jeremiah had already used the picture (Jer 23:1–6). Ezekiel builds a whole chapter on it: the bad shepherds, the God who will shepherd his own flock, the bullies within the flock, and a coming shepherd from David's line.

Prophecy Against the Shepherds of Israel (vv1–10)

  • "Prophesy against the shepherds of Israel" (v2). The "shepherds" are Israel's rulers: the kings above all, and with them the princes and officials who governed. Priests and prophets had their own charges (22:25–28), but this chapter is about those who held power. Peter later uses the same picture for elders in the church, who must shepherd "not domineering over those in your charge" (1 Pet 5:2–3).
  • "Ah, shepherds of Israel who have been feeding yourselves!" (v2). "Ah" is hoy, the cry of a mourner at a funeral, used by the prophets as "woe." The question that follows is the whole charge in one line: "Should not shepherds feed the sheep?" A shepherd exists for the flock, not the flock for the shepherd.
  • "You eat the fat, you clothe yourselves with the wool, you slaughter the fat ones" (v3). A shepherd could fairly live from his flock: milk, wool, the occasional animal (1 Cor 9:7). These shepherds took everything and gave nothing back. The Hebrew consonants for "fat" can also be read as "milk," which is how the Greek translation takes it, and some modern versions follow that. Either way the rulers took the best for themselves. The kings of Judah's last years taxed heavily and built for their own glory (Jer 22:13–17).
  • Five failures (v4). "The weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed, the injured you have not bound up, the strayed you have not brought back, the lost you have not sought." Each is what a real shepherd does every day. The list will be reversed point by point when God himself takes over (v16).
  • "With force and harshness you have ruled them" (v4). "Harshness" (perek) is the word used of Pharaoh's slave masters, who "ruthlessly made the people of Israel work as slaves" (Exod 1:13–14). The law forbade Israelites to rule fellow Israelites that way (Lev 25:43, 46). Judah's own kings had become like Egypt to their people.
  • "So they were scattered, because there was no shepherd" (v5). The rulers were there, but in every way that mattered there was "no shepherd." The scattering is the exile. Jesus saw the same thing in Galilee: the crowds were "like sheep without a shepherd" (Matt 9:36; compare Num 27:17).
  • "My sheep… my sheep… my sheep" (vv5–8). Again and again in these verses God calls them "my sheep." The kings had treated the people as their property. God reminds them whose flock it is.
  • "I am against the shepherds, and I will require my sheep at their hand" (v10). "Require" is the language of accounting, as with the watchman in 33:6. The shepherds will be dismissed, and the sheep rescued "from their mouths." By the end of the picture the shepherds have become the wild beasts.
  • Commentators: Henry reads the shepherds as "rulers both in church and state" and says they "fed themselves, contrived every thing to gratify and indulge their own appetite." JFB limits them to civil rulers, who "sought in their government their own selfish ends," and notes the "milk" reading of v3. Guzik applies the passage to church leaders: it is right for a congregation to support its pastor, but a leader serves the flock, chiefly by feeding it God's Word, and must never lead by force or manipulation. Citing Block, he notes that "my flock" is a term of affection, not only ownership, and that leaders must never treat the church as their own.

The Lord God Will Seek Them Out (vv11–24)

  • "Behold, I, I myself will search for my sheep" (v11). The doubled "I" is emphatic. When the shepherds fail, God does not send a better committee. He comes himself.
  • "On a day of clouds and thick darkness" (v12). This is the language of the day of the LORD (Joel 2:2; Zeph 1:15), which for Judah was the fall of Jerusalem. The judgment day has come and gone, and now the shepherd goes out after the scattered flock.
  • "I will bring them out from the peoples… and will bring them into their own land" (v13). The return from exile is the first fulfilment, beginning in 538 BC (Ezra 1). The language of gathering "from the countries" reaches further than that partial return, and the prophets use it for God's final gathering of his people.
  • "There they shall lie down in good grazing land" (v14). This is Psalm 23 written large: green pastures, lying down, rest. "I myself will make them lie down" (v15). Sheep only lie down when they feel safe and fed.
  • "I will seek the lost, and I will bring back the strayed, and I will bind up the injured, and I will strengthen the weak" (v16). This reverses the shepherds' failures in v4 nearly word for word. Jesus echoes it when he says "the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost" (Luke 19:10) and tells of the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine (Luke 15:4–7).
  • "And the fat and the strong I will destroy. I will feed them in justice" (v16). The good shepherd is not soft. Some sheep have grown fat by pushing others aside, and the next verses explain who they are.
  • "I judge between sheep and sheep, between rams and male goats" (v17). Now God turns to the flock itself. Not all the blame lay with the kings. Within the people, the powerful had abused the weak. Shepherds in Israel often grazed sheep and goats together, and the rams and male goats were the strong leaders of the herd. This is not the same image as Jesus' separating sheep from goats at the judgment (Matt 25:32–33), though the idea of God sorting his flock is similar.
  • "Must you muddy the rest of the water with your feet?" (vv18–19). It was not enough for the strong to eat the best grass and drink the clean water. They trampled what was left and fouled the stream, so the weak got only leftovers. The picture is of the rich exploiting the poor, which the prophets condemned again and again (22:29; Amos 4:1).
  • "You push with side and shoulder, and thrust at all the weak with your horns" (v21). Anyone who has watched a feeding trough knows the scene. The "fat sheep and the lean sheep" (v20) are the well-fed bullies and their thin victims.
  • "I will set up over them one shepherd, my servant David" (v23). David had been dead for four hundred years. This is a new David from his line, the king promised in 2 Samuel 7:12–16 and by the prophets (Isa 11:1–5; Jer 23:5–6; 30:9; Hos 3:5). "One shepherd" also means one king over a reunited people, no longer split into Israel and Judah, which chapter 37 will act out (37:22–24).
  • "I, the LORD, will be their God, and my servant David shall be prince among them" (v24). Ezekiel's word here is nasi, "prince" or "chief," not melek, "king." He uses nasi often for Israel's rulers, and in chapters 44–48 for a ruler who serves under God. The title keeps the coming David firmly under the LORD and close to his people. In 37:24 Ezekiel also calls him "king."
  • Who is "David"? Most Christian readers, from the early church on, have seen here the Messiah, Jesus, the Son of David, who calls himself "the good shepherd" (John 10:11–16) and speaks of "one flock, one shepherd" (John 10:16). Some, especially many dispensational interpreters, think it means David himself raised from the dead to rule under the Messiah in a future kingdom. The striking thing is that God says "I myself will be the shepherd" (v15) and also "I will set up… my servant David" as shepherd (v23). The New Testament holds those together in one person.
  • Commentators: Henry, on God's search: "Wherever they are the eye of God will find them out." He identifies "David" as Christ, "one after God's own heart, set as his King upon the holy hill of Zion." JFB agrees, calling him "the antitypical David, Messiah," and reads the rams and he-goats (v17) as "the rich and ungodly of the people" who oppressed their poorer brothers. Guzik notes the emphatic I myself and, citing Alexander, says Jesus clearly had this chapter in mind in John 10. Unlike Henry and JFB, Guzik leans toward a resurrected David ruling under the Messiah, while noting that many take it as Jesus himself. He also warns that mature believers can harm weaker ones by grabbing resources and attention for themselves.

The Lord's Covenant of Peace (vv25–31)

  • "I will make with them a covenant of peace" (v25). "Peace" is shalom, which means much more than the end of war: wholeness, safety, things as they should be. Ezekiel uses the phrase again in 37:26, and Isaiah uses it of God's unbreakable love (Isa 54:10).
  • "Banish wild beasts from the land, so that they may dwell securely in the wilderness and sleep in the woods" (v25). The blessings that follow come almost straight from Leviticus 26:4–13, the blessings promised for covenant faithfulness: rain in season, fruitful land, safety, freedom from slavery, God among his people. Israel had experienced the curses of Leviticus 26 instead: wild beasts, drought, sword and exile (5:17; 14:15). Now God promises the blessings, and the curses reversed.
  • "I will make them and the places all around my hill a blessing" (v26). "My hill" is Zion, the temple mount. God's people will not only be blessed but become a blessing, the old promise to Abraham (Gen 12:2–3).
  • "Showers in their season; they shall be showers of blessing" (v26). In Israel's climate everything depended on the autumn rains that softened the ground for plowing and the spring rains that filled out the grain (Deut 11:14). Rain at the right time was life.
  • "When I break the bars of their yoke" (v27). The yoke was the wooden frame on an ox's neck, held by bars and straps. God used the same picture when he brought Israel out of Egypt: "I have broken the bars of your yoke and made you walk erect" (Lev 26:13). The return from Babylon will be a new exodus.
  • "None shall make them afraid" (v28). This is a phrase from the prophets' pictures of peace (Mic 4:4; Zeph 3:13): each family safe under its own vine and fig tree.
  • "A renowned place for planting" (v29). Literally "a planting for a name," a land famous for its crops, so that Israel will no longer be mocked by the nations for its hunger (36:30). Older commentators, reading the KJV's "plant of renown," often took it as a title of the Messiah, the "Branch" of Isaiah 11:1. Most modern translations take it as fertile land, which fits the context of harvest and hunger.
  • "The LORD their God with them" (v30). This is the goal of every covenant in the Bible: "I will be their God, and they shall be my people," with God himself among them (Exod 29:45; Rev 21:3).
  • "You are my sheep, human sheep of my pasture, and I am your God" (v31). The Hebrew adds adam, "human," "mankind." The ESV keeps it as "human sheep." It drops the metaphor for a moment to make sure no one misses the point: the sheep in this parable are people, and God is truly their God.
  • Commentators: Henry: "The covenant of grace is a covenant of peace," and he reads the showers both as rain and as the blessing of the gospel. JFB reads "my hill" as Zion, with Israel to be a source of blessing to the nations, and follows the older reading of v29 as "Messiah, the 'Rod' and 'Branch,'" while noting the alternative "a plantation for a name." Guzik explains shalom as wholeness and harmony with God, others and creation. He links "showers of blessing" to the Spirit poured out (Isa 44:3; Acts 3:19–20) and expects a literal fulfilment in a future renewed creation (Isa 11:6–9).

Pointing to Christ

Ezekiel's promise that God himself would come looking for his lost sheep, and that he would set one shepherd from David's line over them, meets in Jesus. He stands up in the temple courts and says, "I am the good shepherd" (John 10:11), knowing his hearers had Ezekiel 34 in their ears. Unlike the shepherds who fed on the flock, he "lays down his life for the sheep." Unlike the hired hand, he does not run when the wolf comes. He goes after the one that strayed, binds up the broken, and will one day gather "one flock" under "one shepherd" (John 10:16), "the great shepherd of the sheep," raised from the dead "by the blood of the eternal covenant" (Heb 13:20).

Reflection

  • The Self-Feeding Shepherd (v.2) — God's charge against Israel's leaders is precise: they fed themselves instead of the flock they were responsible for. If you're in any position of leadership over others, are you serving the people in your care, or quietly extracting from them?
  • The Job Description of a Real Shepherd (v.4) — God lists exactly what the failed shepherds neglected: strengthening the weak, healing the sick, binding the broken, seeking the lost. Which of these is most missing from how you actually care for the people God has put under your influence?
  • When Human Shepherds Fail, God Steps In (v.11) — God announces that He Himself will search for His sheep because the appointed shepherds abandoned that responsibility. If human leaders in your life have let you down, does that shake your trust in God, or remind you that He never delegated His care for you?
  • One Shepherd, Not Many Competing Voices (v.23) — God promises to set up one shepherd, His servant David, rather than leaving the flock to a confusion of leaders. Are you trying to follow too many voices at once instead of anchoring your life to the one Shepherd?
  • Showers, Not Scarcity (v.26) — God promises showers of blessing coming down in their season, not a stingy trickle but genuine abundance at the right time. Do you actually expect God's provision to be generous, or have you settled for scarcity as the default?
