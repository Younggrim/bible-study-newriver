1James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ, To the twelve tribes of the Dispersion: Greetings.

2Consider it pure joy, my brothers, when you encounter trials of many kinds,

3because you know that the testing of your faith develops perseverance.

4Allow perseverance to finish its work, so that you may be mature and complete, not lacking anything.

5Now if any of you lacks wisdom, he should ask God, who gives generously to all without finding fault, and it will be given to him.

6But he must ask in faith, without doubting, because he who doubts is like a wave of the sea, blown and tossed by the wind.

7That man should not expect to receive anything from the Lord.

8He is a double-minded man, unstable in all his ways.

9The brother in humble circumstances should exult in his high position.

10But the one who is rich should exult in his low position, because he will pass away like a flower of the field.

11For the sun rises with scorching heat and withers the plant; its flower falls and its beauty is lost. So too, the rich man will fade away in the midst of his pursuits.

12Blessed is the man who perseveres under trial, because when he has stood the test, he will receive the crown of life that God has promised to those who love Him.

13When tempted, no one should say, “God is tempting me.” For God cannot be tempted by evil, nor does He tempt anyone.

14But each one is tempted when by his own evil desires he is lured away and enticed.

15Then after desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin; and sin, when it is full-grown, gives birth to death.

16Do not be deceived, my beloved brothers.

17Every good and perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of the heavenly lights, with whom there is no change or shifting shadow.

18He chose to give us birth through the word of truth, that we would be a kind of firstfruits of His creation.

19My beloved brothers, understand this: Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to anger,

20for man’s anger does not bring about the righteousness that God desires.

21Therefore, get rid of all moral filth and every expression of evil, and humbly accept the word planted in you, which can save your souls.

22Be doers of the word, and not hearers only. Otherwise, you are deceiving yourselves.

23For anyone who hears the word but does not carry it out is like a man who looks at his face in a mirror,

24and after observing himself goes away and immediately forgets what he looks like.

25But the one who looks intently into the perfect law of freedom, and continues to do so—not being a forgetful hearer, but an effective doer—he will be blessed in what he does.

26If anyone considers himself religious and yet does not bridle his tongue, he deceives his heart and his religion is worthless.

27Pure and undefiled religion before our God and Father is this: to care for orphans and widows in their distress, and to keep oneself from being polluted by the world.

1From James, a slave of God and the Lord Jesus Christ, to the 12 tribes dispersed abroad. Greetings!

2My brothers and sisters, consider it nothing but joy when you fall into all sorts of trials,

3because you know that the testing of your faith produces endurance.

4And let endurance have its perfect effect, so that you will be perfect and complete, not deficient in anything.

5But if anyone is deficient in wisdom, he should ask God, who gives to all generously and without reprimand, and it will be given to him.

6But he must ask in faith without doubting, for the one who doubts is like a wave of the sea, blown and tossed around by the wind.

7For that person must not suppose that he will receive anything from the Lord,

8since he is a double-minded individual, unstable in all his ways.

9Now the believer of humble means should take pride in his high position.

10But the rich person’s pride should be in his humiliation because he will pass away like a wildflower in the meadow.

11For the sun rises with its heat and dries up the meadow; the petal of the flower falls off and its beauty is lost forever. So also the rich person in the midst of his pursuits will wither away.

12Happy is the one who endures testing because when he has proven to be genuine, he will receive the crown of life that God promised to those who love him.

13Let no one say when he is tempted, “I am tempted by God,” for God cannot be tempted by evil, and he himself tempts no one.

14But each one is tempted when he is lured and enticed by his own desires.

15Then when desire conceives, it gives birth to sin, and when sin is full grown, it gives birth to death.

16Do not be led astray, my dear brothers and sisters.

17All generous giving and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or the slightest hint of change.

18By his sovereign plan he gave us birth through the message of truth, that we would be a kind of firstfruits of all he created.

19Understand this, my dear brothers and sisters! Let every person be quick to listen, slow to speak, slow to anger.

20For human anger does not accomplish God’s righteousness.

21So put away all filth and evil excess and humbly welcome the message implanted within you, which is able to save your souls.

22But be sure you live out the message and do not merely listen to it and so deceive yourselves.

23For if someone merely listens to the message and does not live it out, he is like someone who gazes at his own face in a mirror.

24For he gazes at himself and then goes out and immediately forgets what sort of person he was.

25But the one who peers into the perfect law of liberty and fixes his attention there, and does not become a forgetful listener but one who lives it out—he will be blessed in what he does.

26If someone thinks he is religious yet does not bridle his tongue, and so deceives his heart, his religion is futile.

27Pure and undefiled religion before God the Father is this: to care for orphans and widows in their adversity and to keep oneself unstained by the world.

Summary

James greets the twelve tribes of the dispersion (v. 1) and tells believers to count trials as joy, since testing their faith produces the endurance that leads to maturity (vv. 2-4). Anyone lacking wisdom should ask God in faith without doubting, and rich and poor alike should find their true standing in Christ rather than in circumstance (vv. 5-11). The one who endures trial is blessed, but temptation itself springs from a person's own desire, not from God, and leads to sin and death, while every good and perfect gift comes down from the Father of lights (vv. 12-18). Believers should be quick to listen, slow to speak, slow to anger, and receive the implanted word with humility, then live it out rather than merely hearing it (vv. 19-25). True religion controls the tongue, cares for orphans and widows, and keeps itself unstained by the world (vv. 26-27).

Authorship & Background

Author: James (v.1) — most likely James the brother of Jesus (Matt 13:55, Gal 1:19), who became the leader of the Jerusalem church (Acts 15:13, 21:18). He was not one of the twelve apostles but became a believer after the resurrection (1 Cor 15:7). Known as "James the Just" for his piety. Martyred in AD 62 according to Josephus.
Recipient: "The twelve tribes which are scattered abroad" (v.1) — Jewish Christians dispersed throughout the Roman Empire. Written approximately AD 45-49, making James possibly the earliest NT book.
Purpose: Practical wisdom for daily Christian living. James is the "Proverbs of the NT" — short, punchy, practical. He addresses trials, wisdom, the tongue, favoritism, faith and works, prayer, and more. His concern: faith that works, not just faith that talks.
Theme: Genuine faith produces visible fruit. "Be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only" (v.22). Faith without works is dead (2:26).
Classification: Epistle — General, Wisdom
Key Themes: Trials producing endurance, wisdom asked for in faith, the deceptive course from desire to death, every good gift coming down from above, and a faith measured by what it does
Greeting to the Scattered Twelve Tribes (v.1): James identifies himself only as “a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ”. Almost certainly the half-brother of Jesus and leader of the Jerusalem church, he claims no family relationship, exactly as Jude does. The addressees are “the twelve tribes which are scattered abroad”, most naturally Jewish believers dispersed outside Judea, which suits the letter's assumed familiarity with synagogue life and the law.
Count It All Joy: Trials and Endurance (vv.2-4): The letter opens on hardship with no preamble. Joy is commanded not because trials are pleasant but because of what they produce — the testing of faith works patience, and patience left to finish its work produces maturity. The logic runs forward to an outcome rather than asking anyone to enjoy the process. Verse 4's “perfect” is completeness rather than flawlessness, the sense of something having reached its intended end.
Ask for Wisdom, Without Wavering (vv.5-8): Wisdom is the thing to ask for in the middle of trials, and the offer is generous — God gives “liberally” and without reproach, meaning without making the asker feel foolish for needing it. The condition is asking in faith without wavering, and the image for wavering is a wave driven by wind. The word rendered “double minded” in v.8 appears first here in surviving Greek and describes a person divided in two directions at once, which is the letter's diagnosis of much that follows.
Poverty, Riches, and the Withering Flower (vv.9-11): The low brother is told to glory in being lifted up and the rich in being made low, a reversal James returns to repeatedly. The grass and flower burned by the scorching wind is Isaiah 40's image, and the point is not that wealth is wicked but that it is temporary and a poor foundation for identity. In a letter addressed to scattered communities where economic pressure was real, this is pastoral rather than abstract.
Temptation: Desire, Sin, Death (vv.12-18): James separates trials that come from outside from temptation that works from within, and insists God tempts no one. The sequence in vv.14-15 is deliberate and almost clinical: desire conceives, brings forth sin, and sin brings forth death. Nothing about it is sudden. Against that he sets v.17, every good and perfect gift coming down from the Father “with whom is no variableness, neither shadow of turning” — a God who does not shift, set against a desire that shifts constantly.
Swift to Hear: Doing the Word (vv.19-27): The practical turn the letter is known for. Swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath; receive the word with meekness; and then the mirror — a man who hears without doing is like someone who looks at his face and immediately forgets it. The chapter closes by defining religion God accepts in terms that sidestep ritual entirely: bridling the tongue, visiting orphans and widows in their affliction, keeping oneself unspotted from the world. James is not opposing faith to works but insisting that a faith which changes nothing has not been described accurately.

Deep Dive

Setting: The letter comes from James, almost certainly the brother of Jesus (Matt 13:55; Gal 1:19). He did not believe in Jesus during his ministry (John 7:5), but the risen Lord appeared to him (1 Cor 15:7), and he became the leading elder of the Jerusalem church (Acts 15:13–21; Gal 2:9). The Jewish historian Josephus records that he was stoned on the orders of the high priest in AD 62. The other well-known James, the son of Zebedee, was killed much earlier (Acts 12:2). Many scholars date the letter to the mid-40s, which would make it one of the earliest books of the New Testament, while others place it in the late 50s or early 60s. It reads less like a typical letter and more like a collection of short sermons in the style of Jewish wisdom books like Proverbs, and it echoes Jesus' Sermon on the Mount more than any other New Testament letter. Its readers were Jewish believers scattered outside Judea, many of them poor and under pressure from wealthy, powerful people. Chapter 1 introduces almost every theme of the letter: trials, wisdom, rich and poor, temptation, speech, anger and the call to do what God's word says.

Greeting (v1)

  • "James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ" (v1). James could have introduced himself as the Lord's brother. Instead he calls himself a "servant," doulos, a slave. The man who grew up in the same house as Jesus now calls him "Lord." Jesus is named only twice in the whole letter, here and in 2:1, both times as "the Lord Jesus Christ."
  • "To the twelve tribes in the Dispersion" (v1). The "Dispersion" (diaspora) was the usual term for Jews living outside the land of Israel, scattered since the Assyrian and Babylonian exiles. The prophets promised that God would one day gather all twelve tribes again (Ezek 37:15–28), and Jesus chose twelve apostles with that hope in view (Matt 19:28). James writes to believers in Jesus as that renewed Israel. Some think he means Jewish believers scattered after Stephen's death (Acts 8:1; 11:19). Others read it as all Christians, God's people living as exiles in the world (1 Pet 1:1). The many Jewish touches in the letter favor Jewish believers, but the message is for the whole church.
  • "Greetings" (v1). Chairein, the standard Greek letter opening, used also in the letter James helped write in Acts 15:23. It means literally "rejoice," and James plays on it at once: chairein, then charan, "joy" (v2).

Testing of Your Faith (vv2–18)

  • "Count it all joy, my brothers" (v2). "Count" means to consider, to make a judgment. James does not say trials feel joyful. He tells believers to reckon them as an occasion for joy because of what God does through them. "My brothers" (used about fifteen times in this short letter) shows the warmth beneath his blunt commands.
  • "When you meet trials of various kinds" (v2). "Meet" is the word for stumbling into something unexpectedly, like the man who "fell among robbers" (Luke 10:30). "Trials" is peirasmos, which can mean either an outward test or an inner temptation. James uses the same word family for both in this chapter (vv2, 12, 13–14), and the shift in meaning is part of his point. The same hardship that God uses to test and strengthen faith can become a temptation to sin.
  • "The testing of your faith produces steadfastness" (v3). "Testing" (dokimion) is the process of proving genuineness, like refining metal in a furnace. Peter uses the same word for faith "tested by fire" (1 Pet 1:7). "Steadfastness" (hypomonē) means remaining under a load without giving way: active endurance, not passive resignation.
  • "That you may be perfect and complete, lacking in nothing" (v4). "Perfect" (teleios) means mature, fully grown, reaching the goal. "Complete" (holoklēros) means whole in every part. This is the aim of the trials. James then makes a link with the next verse: "lacking in nothing" (v4), "if any of you lacks wisdom" (v5). Both use the same verb.
  • "If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God" (v5). In the Bible, wisdom is not cleverness but skill in living God's way, especially in hard situations (Prov 2:6). The person in the middle of a trial needs to know how to go through it well. James will describe this wisdom "from above" in 3:13–18.
  • "Who gives generously to all without reproach" (v5). "Generously" is haplōs, which also means simply, with a single heart, without hidden motives. The lexicon gives "simply, sincerely, graciously." It sets up a contrast with the "double-minded" man in v8. "Without reproach" means God does not scold the asker or throw past failures back in his face.
  • "Let him ask in faith, with no doubting" (v6). "Doubting" (diakrinomai) here means not honest questions but being divided within oneself, wavering between trusting God and trusting something else. Jesus used the same word of the faith that moves mountains (Matt 21:21; Mark 11:23).
  • "Like a wave of the sea that is driven and tossed by the wind" (v6). A wave has no direction of its own. It is pushed one way and then another. Jews were not a seafaring people and tended to see the sea as restless and threatening, so the picture would have been vivid.
  • "He is a double-minded man, unstable in all his ways" (v8). "Double-minded" is dipsychos, literally "two-souled." It appears here for the first time in any surviving Greek writing, so James may have coined it, and it returns in 4:8. It describes someone with divided loyalty, one soul turned to God and the other to the world. "Unstable" means restless, unsettled. The word is used again in 3:8 and 3:16 ("disorder").
  • "Let the lowly brother boast in his exaltation" (v9). "Lowly" means poor and of low social standing, like most of James's readers (2:5). Their "exaltation" is what God has given them: they are children of God and heirs of the kingdom. Boasting is usually bad in Scripture, but boasting in what God has done is right (Jer 9:23–24; 1 Cor 1:31).
  • "And the rich in his humiliation" (v10). Two main readings. Some think the rich man is a believer who should boast that in Christ he has been brought low, learning that his money counts for nothing before God. Others think he is an unbeliever, and the "boast" is bitter irony: let him boast, if he can, in his coming ruin. The unbelieving rich in 2:6–7 and 5:1–6 make the second view attractive, but the word "brother" in v9 may carry over to v10, supporting the first. Either way, wealth is not security.
  • "Like a flower of the grass he will pass away" (vv10–11). This is drawn from Isaiah 40:6–8: "All flesh is grass… The grass withers, the flower fades, but the word of our God will stand forever." "Scorching heat" may be the hot east wind, the sirocco, which could wither a Palestinian hillside of spring wildflowers in a day. "Fade away in the midst of his pursuits" is the sharpest point: death finds the rich man busy, on his way to his next deal (compare 4:13–14).
  • "Blessed is the man who remains steadfast under trial" (v12). James returns to the trials of vv2–4. "Blessed" begins like Psalm 1 and the Beatitudes (Matt 5:3–12). "When he has stood the test" uses dokimos, "approved," a metal that has passed the furnace.
  • "The crown of life" (v12). The "crown" (stephanos) was the victor's wreath at the games, or a festive garland. "Crown of life" means the crown that is life, eternal life itself. Jesus promises the same to the suffering church in Smyrna (Rev 2:10). It is promised "to those who love him." Endurance is the fruit of love, not a grim effort to earn something.
  • "Let no one say when he is tempted, 'I am being tempted by God'" (v13). Now peirazō clearly means temptation to sin. James answers an excuse as old as Adam: "The woman whom you gave to be with me" (Gen 3:12). Some Jewish teachers of the time spoke of God creating the evil inclination in humans, which could become a way of blaming God. James uses a teacher's technique called diatribe, quoting an imagined objection in order to answer it.
  • "God cannot be tempted with evil, and he himself tempts no one" (v13). God does test his people (Gen 22:1, where the Greek Old Testament uses this same verb), but he never entices anyone to do wrong. "Cannot be tempted" is a single word, apeirastos, found only here in the New Testament. Evil has nothing in God to appeal to.
  • "Lured and enticed by his own desire" (v14). Two hunting and fishing words. "Lured" (exelkō) means dragged out, like a fish pulled from its hiding place, and "enticed" (deleazō) means baited, as with bait on a hook or in a trap. The bait works only because of "his own desire." The source is inside the person, not in God.
  • "Desire when it has conceived gives birth to sin, and sin when it is fully grown brings forth death" (v15). A grim family tree: desire conceives, sin is born, sin grows up and gives birth to death. The verb for "brings forth" (apokyeō) is a birth word, used in the New Testament only here and in v18.
  • "Do not be deceived, my beloved brothers" (v16). This sentence turns the argument. Do not believe that God is the source of evil. The truth is the opposite.
  • "Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above" (v17). In Greek these words fall into a line of poetry with a regular rhythm, so James may be quoting a well-known saying. Evil comes from below, from desire. Every good comes from above.
  • "The Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shadow due to change" (v17). God made the sun, moon and stars (Gen 1:14–18). Those lights shift, wax and wane, and cast moving shadows as they travel. "Variation" and "shadow due to change" sound like the language of ancient astronomy. Their Maker never changes (Mal 3:6). He will never become a giver of evil.
  • "Of his own will he brought us forth by the word of truth" (v18). The same birth word as in v15, now used of God. Desire gives birth to sin and death. God gives birth to new life. "Of his own will" means it was God's free choice, not something believers caused. "The word of truth" is the gospel (Eph 1:13; Col 1:5). Many hear new birth here, as in John 3:3 and 1 Peter 1:23. A few scholars think James means God's creation of humanity, but the phrase "word of truth" points to the gospel.
  • "A kind of firstfruits of his creatures" (v18). The firstfruits were the first part of the harvest, offered to God as a pledge of the rest (Lev 23:9–14). Believers are the beginning of God's new creation, a sign that he will renew all he has made (Rom 8:19–23).
  • Commentators: Henry: "We should not pray so much for the removal of an affliction as for wisdom to make a right use of it," and of God's giving, "Ask when you will, and as often as you will, you will meet with no upbraidings." JFB says the trials are "not in the limited sense of allurements to sin" but tests that "purify the Christian character," and on v15 warns, "Nip sin in the bud of lust." Guzik reads hypomonē as "remaining under" a load and, following Hiebert, takes dipsychos as "two-souled," which the lexicon confirms. He also says the deepest motive for resisting temptation is love for God, not fear of being caught. JFB reads "Father of lights" mainly of spiritual lights, while Guzik and the Tyndale notes take it of the heavenly bodies; the second fits better with the astronomy language that follows.

Hearing and Doing the Word (vv19–27)

  • "Quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger" (v19). Three short commands, each of which James will develop later: speech (3:1–12) and anger and quarrels (4:1–3). Proverbs says much the same (Prov 10:19; 15:1; 17:27). The setting here is hearing God's word. A person who is busy talking or angry cannot listen.
  • "The anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God" (v20). Some believers may have thought their anger served God's cause, perhaps anger at the wealthy oppressors. James says human anger never accomplishes the right living God wants. Jesus' righteous anger is not in view here. James means the ordinary anger of offended pride.
  • "Put away all filthiness and rampant wickedness" (v21). "Put away" is the word for taking off clothes (Eph 4:22; Col 3:8; 1 Pet 2:1). "Filthiness" (rhyparia) is from the same root as the "shabby" clothes of the poor man in 2:2. Moral dirt is a garment to strip off. "Rampant wickedness" is literally "abundance of evil," the overflow of a bad heart.
  • "Receive with meekness the implanted word, which is able to save your souls" (v21). "Meekness" is gentle, teachable humility, the opposite of anger. "Implanted" (emphytos) means rooted, grown into. James is likely thinking of the new covenant promise: "I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts" (Jer 31:33). The word has already been planted (v18). Now it must be welcomed and allowed to grow. "Souls" here means the whole person.
  • "Be doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving yourselves" (v22). Most early Christians could not read and owned no Scriptures, so they heard God's word read aloud in worship. Hearing could easily become a substitute for obeying. Jesus ended the Sermon on the Mount with the same warning: the wise man hears and does, the foolish man hears and does not (Matt 7:24–27). The self-deception is thinking that hearing is enough.
  • "Like a man who looks intently at his natural face in a mirror" (v23). "Natural face" is literally "the face of his birth," the face he was born with. Ancient mirrors were polished bronze and gave a dim image (1 Cor 13:12), but James's point is not the mirror's quality. It is the man's carelessness. He sees, walks away and "at once forgets what he was like" (v24).
  • "The one who looks into the perfect law, the law of liberty" (v25). "Looks into" is parakyptō, to stoop down and peer in. It is the word used of Peter and the beloved disciple bending to look into the empty tomb (John 20:5, 11) and of angels longing "to look into" the gospel (1 Pet 1:12). The contrast is between a quick glance and a careful, bent-over gaze.
  • "The perfect law, the law of liberty" (v25). This is God's law as Jesus taught it and as it is written on the heart in the new covenant. It is "perfect" (Ps 19:7, "The law of the LORD is perfect, reviving the soul") and it gives freedom (Ps 119:45, "I shall walk in a wide place, for I have sought your precepts"). James will call it "the royal law" in 2:8 and returns to "the law of liberty" in 2:12. It frees because it comes with a new heart that wants to obey.
  • "And perseveres… he will be blessed in his doing" (v25). "Perseveres" means stays beside it, keeps looking. The blessing is not for knowing but for doing, like Jesus' "blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and keep it" (Luke 11:28).
  • "If anyone thinks he is religious and does not bridle his tongue" (v26). "Religious" (thrēskos) and "religion" (thrēskeia) refer to the outward practice of religion: worship, rituals and observances. The lexicon defines it as "religion in its external aspect." James is not against religious practice, but outward devotion with an unbridled tongue is "worthless." "Bridle" is the horse-and-bit picture he will develop in 3:2–3.
  • "To visit orphans and widows in their affliction" (v27). Orphans and widows had no male protector or provider and were the most vulnerable people in the ancient world. God calls himself their defender (Deut 10:18; Ps 68:5), and the prophets measured Israel's faithfulness by how it treated them (Isa 1:17). "Visit" means to look after, care for, the word Jesus used: "I was sick and you visited me" (Matt 25:36).
  • "And to keep oneself unstained from the world" (v27). "The world" in James is human life organized without God and against him (4:4). Pure religion has two sides together: compassion that reaches out, and holiness that keeps clean. James is not giving a full definition of religion, as if belief did not matter. He is saying what genuine faith looks like when it shows.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Bare hearers are self-deceivers," and of the mirror, "that which is truly the word of God is no flattering glass." On the tongue he writes, "There is no strength nor power in that religion which will not enable a man to bridle his tongue." On v19 JFB passes on a rabbinic saying: "Two ears are given to us… but only one tongue." Henry takes the implanted word as something to "receive… as the stock does the graft," while JFB says the word is "engrafted by the Holy Spirit." Guzik notes that the verb in v25 implies careful scrutiny, which parakyptō supports, and says the New Testament rarely uses the word "religious" in a positive sense.

Pointing to Christ

James mentions Jesus by name only twice in this letter, but he is everywhere in it. The perfectly mature man "lacking in nothing" (v4) is the one who endured every trial and was tempted in every way without sin (Heb 4:15). The Father who gives "every perfect gift" gave his greatest gift in his Son, and the "word of truth" that brings believers to new birth is the good news about him. James's demand to be doers and not hearers only sounds like his brother's teaching, because it is: Jesus himself is the Word who did all that the Father said. The crown of life promised to those who endure is the life of the risen Lord whom James once doubted and then served as a slave.

Reflection

  • Trials Produce Endurance (vv.2-4) — James says testing your faith isn't cause for despair but for joy, because trials build the kind of endurance that leads to spiritual maturity. What trial are you facing right now that God might be using to grow you rather than punish you?
  • Ask God for Wisdom (vv.5-8) — Anyone lacking wisdom is told to ask God, who gives generously without finding fault, but must ask in settled faith rather than wavering doubt. What decision are you facing that you haven't actually brought to God in prayer?
  • The Reversal of Rich and Poor (vv.9-11) — The believer in humble circumstances is told to take pride in his high standing before God, while the rich are reminded their wealth will fade like a wildflower. Where does your sense of worth come from — your circumstances, or your standing before God?
  • Temptation Comes From Within (vv.13-15) — God never tempts anyone; sin starts with our own desire, which conceives and grows into sin, and sin, left unchecked, leads to death. Where are you tempted to blame outside circumstances for a pull that actually starts in your own heart?
  • Quick to Listen, Slow to Speak (v.19) — James gives a simple filter for relationships and conflict: be quick to hear, slow to speak, and slow to anger. Which of those three comes hardest to you?
  • Doers of the Word (vv.22-25) — Hearing truth without acting on it is like glancing in a mirror and immediately forgetting what you saw; real blessing comes to those who put the word into practice. What's one truth you've heard recently that you still haven't acted on?
