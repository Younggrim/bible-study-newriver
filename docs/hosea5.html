Summary

God pronounces judgment on Israel's priests, people, and royal house, calling them a snare and a net that has trapped the nation in rebellion (vv. 1-2). Their spirit of prostitution keeps them from knowing the LORD, and Israel's arrogance testifies against them as both Israel and Judah stumble in guilt (vv. 3-5). When they finally seek the LORD with their flocks and herds it will be too late, since he has withdrawn from them because of their unfaithfulness (vv. 6-7). Alarms sound across Ephraim and Judah as God, like a moth or dry rot, slowly consumes them, and Ephraim's turn to Assyria for help proves powerless to heal their wound (vv. 8-13). God declares he will be like a lion tearing his prey and will then withdraw to his place until the people acknowledge their guilt and earnestly seek his face in their distress (vv. 14-15).

Authorship & Background

Author: Hosea son of Beeri. Prophesied to the northern kingdom (Israel) approximately 755-715 BC. Contemporary with Isaiah and Micah. Key theme: God's unfailing love for unfaithful Israel, illustrated through Hosea's marriage to Gomer (a prostitute). Despite Israel's spiritual adultery, God says: "I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely" (14:4).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — Judgment on Both Israel and Judah
Key Themes: God as a lion and a moth, failed alliances with Assyria, pride as the root of destruction, God withdrawing until repentance, seeking God in distress
Historical Context: Chapter 5 expands the indictment to include both Israel (Ephraim) and Judah. The priests, the royal house, and the people are all guilty. God has become like a "moth" to Ephraim (slow, invisible decay) and will become like a "lion" (sudden, devastating judgment). The chapter references the Syro-Ephraimite crisis (approximately 735 BC), when Ephraim sought help from Assyria rather than God (v.13) — a political alliance that would ultimately destroy them. The chapter ends with God "returning to His place" — withdrawing divine presence until Israel genuinely repents.
Triple Indictment: Priests, Israel, Royal House (vv.1-7): Three groups are summoned at once, hear ye this, O priests, and hearken, ye house of Israel, and give ye ear, O house of the king. No class is exempt, and the images are of trapping, a snare on Mizpah and a net spread upon Tabor, both of them high places used for worship. The reason judgment is certain is a matter of character, not circumstance: they will not frame their doings to turn unto their God, for the spirit of whoredoms is in the midst of them. Their sacrifices cannot reach Him, they shall not find him, because the offering is not the problem.
The Alarm: War is Coming (vv.8-9): Blow ye the cornet in Gibeah, and the trumpet in Ramah. The towns named are on the border between the two kingdoms, and the alarm is being raised across it, which fixes the setting: this is the Syro-Ephraimite war of around 735 BC, when Israel and Syria attacked Judah and Judah called Assyria in. Ephraim shall be desolate in the day of rebuke, and the desolation is announced as settled.
Judah Also Condemned (v.10): The princes of Judah were like them that remove the bound. Moving a boundary stone is the quiet theft the law singles out in Deuteronomy 19 and 27, a crime committed at night against a neighbor who cannot prove it. Judah has just been warned not to follow Israel, and this is the charge that lands against it, not idolatry but the deliberate shifting of a line.
God as Moth and Lion (vv.11-14): Two images of how judgment arrives, and the pairing is the point. I will be unto Ephraim as a moth, and to the house of Judah as rottenness, decay so slow it is only noticed when the garment tears. Between them sits the diagnosis, when Ephraim saw his sickness he went to Assyria, treating the symptom with the empire that would destroy him. Then the second image, I will be unto Ephraim as a lion, and there shall be none to deliver. The moth is what they have not noticed, the lion is what they will not survive.
God Withdraws Until Repentance (v.15): I will go and return to my place, till they acknowledge their offence, and seek my face. The most severe thing in the chapter is not an act but a withdrawal, and it is stated with a condition attached and an expectation of the outcome, in their affliction they will seek me early. Absence is the instrument here, and it is temporary by design.

Setting: Hosea is preaching to the northern kingdom of Israel, which he often calls Ephraim after its largest tribe, during its final decades before the Assyrian conquest of 722 BC. Chapter 4 brought God's lawsuit against the people and especially the priests: there is "no knowledge of God in the land." Chapter 5 widens the charge to the king's court and brings Judah, the southern kingdom, into the picture. The second half of the chapter (vv8–15) likely belongs to the crisis of 734–732 BC, the "Syro-Ephraimite war." Israel and Syria (Aram) attacked Judah to force it into an alliance against Assyria. Judah's king Ahaz appealed to Assyria for help (2 Kings 16:5–9; Isa 7). Assyria crushed Syria, took large parts of Israel, and made Judah a vassal. Both kingdoms looked to Assyria for rescue instead of to God, and that is the chapter's final complaint. The ESV treats the whole chapter as one section.

Punishment Coming for Israel and Judah (vv1–15)

  • "Hear this, O priests! Pay attention, O house of Israel! Give ear, O house of the king!" (v1). Three groups are summoned: the religious leaders, the people (or perhaps the elders who represent them), and the royal court. "The judgment is for you" can mean either "the verdict is against you" or "justice was your responsibility." Both are true: those charged with justice are now on trial.
  • "A snare at Mizpah and a net spread upon Tabor" (v1). Snares and nets were the hunter's tools for birds and animals. The leaders, who should have protected the people, have been trapping them. Mizpah ("watchtower") was the name of several towns. This is likely the one in Gilead east of the Jordan, or the one in Benjamin where Samuel judged Israel (1 Sam 7:16). Mount Tabor stands alone above the Jezreel Valley. Both were probably sites of idolatrous shrines. The Tyndale notes admit the exact sins at these places are unknown.
  • "The revolters have gone deep into slaughter" (v2). The Hebrew is difficult. Many scholars read it as "they have made deep the pit of Shittim," continuing the hunting picture (a pit dug to trap game). Shittim was where Israel first fell into Baal worship in the wilderness (Num 25:1–3), which would suit Hosea's theme. The ESV reads "slaughter," possibly of sacrifices. JFB says their "so-called sacrifices were butcheries rather than sacrifices." "But I will discipline all of them": the word musar means correction or training, not only punishment. God's aim is to change them.
  • "I know Ephraim, and Israel is not hidden from me" (v3). The tragedy of Hosea's day is that Israel does not know God (4:1, 6). But God knows Israel completely. Nothing done at Mizpah, Tabor or anywhere else escapes him. "Israel is defiled" uses the word for ritual uncleanness that barred a person from worship (Lev 11–15). Their idolatry has made them unfit to approach God.
  • "Their deeds do not permit them to return to their God" (v4). This is one of the most sobering lines in Hosea. Sin is not just something they do; it has become a prison. Habits of sin shape a person until turning back seems impossible. "The spirit of whoredom is within them" echoes 4:12. Jeremiah asks the same question: "Can the Ethiopian change his skin or the leopard his spots? Then also you can do good who are accustomed to do evil" (Jer 13:23). Only God's grace can break such a hold, which is why the book's final hope rests on what God does (14:4).
  • "The pride of Israel testifies to his face" (v5). The phrase is the same as in Amos 8:7, "the pride of Jacob." Here it is Israel's arrogance, and it is the witness against them in court. Their own pride proves the case. The verse repeats in 7:10. "Judah also shall stumble with them": the south is not immune. This is the first time Hosea puts Judah under the same judgment (contrast 1:7; 4:15).
  • "With their flocks and herds they shall go to seek the LORD, but they will not find him" (v6). They will try the religious remedy: lots of sacrifices. But God is not bought with livestock (6:6; Amos 5:21–22; Mic 6:6–8). "He has withdrawn from them": the verb means to pull off or slip away, as a shoe is taken off (Deut 25:9–10). It recalls Amos 8:12, people running to and fro seeking God's word and not finding it.
  • "They have dealt faithlessly with the LORD; for they have borne alien children" (v7). "Faithlessly" (bagad) is the word for betraying a spouse (Mal 2:14). "Alien children" may continue the marriage picture of 1:2 and 2:4, children born of unfaithfulness, a new generation raised as strangers to God's covenant. JFB: they are "regarded by God as strangers, not His."
  • "Now the new moon shall devour them with their fields" (v7). The new moon festival, one of the feasts they had corrupted (2:11), will become the occasion of their ruin. Some take it as a time marker: within a month disaster will come. JFB takes "a month" as a very short time before judgment falls.
  • "Blow the horn in Gibeah, the trumpet in Ramah" (v8). Here the scene shifts to war. The horn (shofar, a ram's horn) and trumpet sounded the alarm. Gibeah (Saul's hometown), Ramah (Samuel's home) and Beth-aven (Bethel renamed, as in 4:15) were towns of Benjamin along the road north from Jerusalem toward Bethel. Benjamin lay on the border between Judah and Israel. Many scholars, following Albrecht Alt, read vv8–15 as the moment in the Syro-Ephraimite war when Judah struck back and pushed north into Benjamin. "We follow you, O Benjamin!" is literally "after you, Benjamin," an old battle cry (Judg 5:14).
  • "Ephraim shall become a desolation in the day of punishment" (v9). This is no longer a conditional warning. "I make known what is sure": it will certainly happen. JFB: "that which shall surely be." Assyria annexed much of Israel's territory in 733 BC, and the rest fell in 722.
  • "The princes of Judah have become like those who move the landmark" (v10). Boundary stones marked the family land each Israelite household received when the land was divided (Josh 13–19). Moving them was theft of a neighbour's inheritance and was cursed (Deut 19:14; 27:17; Prov 22:28). If vv8–15 belong to the war, Judah's leaders may be seizing border territory in Benjamin from Israel. Guzik, following Leon Wood, also sees a spiritual sense: they were moving the lines God set between true and false worship. "I will pour out my wrath like water" pictures an overwhelming flood.
  • "Ephraim is oppressed, crushed in judgment" (v11). The words "oppressed" and "crushed" are the ones Deuteronomy 28:33 uses for the covenant curse: "you shall be only oppressed and crushed continually." "Because he was determined to go after filth": the last word (tsav) is uncertain. The ESV reads "filth" (perhaps meaning idols), the KJV "the commandment," which JFB takes as Jeroboam's order to worship the calves. Some scholars read it as "worthless things" or a mocking sound like gibberish (Isa 28:10, where the same syllable appears). The point is the same: Israel chose to follow something worthless.
  • "But I am like a moth to Ephraim, and like dry rot to the house of Judah" (v12). This is one of the most startling images of God in Scripture. The moth eats through cloth silently (Isa 50:9; Matt 6:19), and rot eats wood from the inside. God's judgment can work unseen, slowly hollowing a nation out before anyone notices. Henry's outline: God "will be as a moth to them in secret judgments" and as a lion "in public judgments" (v14).
  • "When Ephraim saw his sickness, and Judah his wound" (v13). Both kingdoms realized something was wrong. But instead of turning to God, "Ephraim went to Assyria, and sent to the great king." Israel's kings did this more than once. Menahem paid a huge tribute to Assyria's king Pul (Tiglath-pileser III) to keep his throne (2 Kings 15:19–20), and Hoshea first submitted, then rebelled (2 Kings 17:3–4). Judah's Ahaz sent the temple's silver and gold to Tiglath-pileser, saying "I am your servant and your son" (2 Kings 16:7–8).
  • "The great king" (v13). The KJV reads "King Jareb," as if it were a name. No Assyrian king was named Jareb. The Hebrew melek yareb probably means "a contentious king" or "warrior king," or, slightly redivided, malki rab, "the great king," a known Assyrian royal title (2 Kings 18:19). The ESV follows that reading. JFB suggests "their defender." "He is not able to cure you or heal your wound": the one they ran to would become their destroyer.
  • "I will be like a lion to Ephraim" (v14). The quiet moth becomes a roaring lion. Assyria will be the lion's paws, but God says, "I, even I, will tear." He is the real power behind history. Amos 3:8 had said "The lion has roared." "No one shall rescue": certainly not Assyria. The irony is bitter: they sought help from the very animal that would tear them.
  • "I will return again to my place, until they acknowledge their guilt and seek my face" (v15). The lion goes back to its den. God withdraws, as v6 said, but now the purpose is stated. He waits "until" they own their guilt. "Acknowledge their guilt" (the verb may mean "bear their guilt" or "admit it") is the first step of repentance. "Earnestly seek me" is from a root meaning "dawn" (shachar). The KJV renders "seek me early." It pictures someone up at first light, eager to find God. "In their distress": sometimes it takes affliction to bring people to that point (Ps 119:67, 71). This verse leads directly into the call of 6:1, "Come, let us return to the LORD."
  • Commentators: Guzik stresses that the real tragedy was not that Israel sinned but that it would not accept God's correction, and on v6 describes people who keep moving on just as God draws near, running from him while imagining they are seeking him. On v13 he notes, citing Wood, that "Jareb" is not a known Assyrian name and probably means "warrior," and he calls it folly to seek rescue from the coming conqueror. JFB says the "cornet" was a curved animal horn used by shepherds and the "trumpet" a straight one used in war, and reads the moth and rot as "a slowly but surely consuming" judgment. Henry's outline divides God's dealings into "secret judgments" (the moth) and "public judgments" (the lion), and calls v15 the "intimation of a right course at last." All three read v15 as hope, not just threat: the goal of judgment is to bring people back.

Pointing to Christ

Israel's leaders were a snare; their deeds would not let them return; their remedies, sacrifices and treaties, could not heal their wound. The wound was beyond any king's power. Isaiah, preaching at the same time, saw a servant who would carry it: "with his wounds we are healed" (Isa 53:5). And the lion who tears is also the one who heals. Hosea will say so in the next breath: "he has torn us, that he may heal us" (6:1). In Revelation the "Lion of the tribe of Judah" turns out to be "a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain" (Rev 5:5–6). In Christ, God did not merely withdraw to his place to wait. He came to find those who could not return on their own.

Reflection

  • Pride That Testifies Against Us (v.5) — Israel's own pride is described as testifying openly against them, visible to everyone but themselves. Where might pride be evident to others in your life even though you can't quite see it yourself?
  • Ritual Without Presence (v.6) — The people bring their offerings to seek the Lord but do not find Him, because the ritual isn't matched by genuine repentance. Is your own spiritual activity — church attendance, giving, service — reaching toward God authentically, or has it become performance?
  • Slow Decay Before Sudden Collapse (vv.12, 14) — God describes acting first like a moth, a slow and quiet decay, before acting like a lion in sudden judgment. Is there a slow spiritual decline in your own life right now — fading passion, cooling prayer — that you're ignoring because it hasn't become a crisis yet?
  • Wrong Solutions for a Spiritual Problem (v.13) — Ephraim's wound comes from God, yet they seek healing through a political alliance with Assyria instead of returning to Him. When a struggle in your life is fundamentally spiritual, are you looking for solutions everywhere except back to God?
  • Affliction That Leads to Seeking (v.15) — God says He will withdraw until the people, in their distress, earnestly seek His face. If you're currently in a season of hardship, could it be the very thing prompting you to seek God more earnestly than before?
