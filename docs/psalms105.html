Summary

A historical psalm of praise urging Israel to give thanks, seek the LORD, and remember his wonders, grounded in the everlasting covenant he swore to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob to give them the land of Canaan (vv. 1-11). It traces God's faithful care through the patriarchs' wanderings, Joseph's rise from slavery and prison to power in Egypt, and Israel's growth into a great nation there (vv. 12-25). The psalm recounts the plagues sent through Moses and Aaron, the exodus with plunder from Egypt, and God's provision of cloud, fire, quail, manna, and water in the wilderness (vv. 26-41). It closes by tying it all to God's remembered promise to Abraham, as he gave Israel the land of the nations so they would keep his statutes and laws (vv. 42-45).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Historical Psalm / Hymn of Praise
Attributed Author: Anonymous (portions appear in 1 Chr 16:8-22, attributed to David for the ark's arrival in Jerusalem)
Key Themes: God's faithfulness to His covenant, Israel's history as God's story, the Abrahamic covenant, Joseph's suffering and exaltation, the Exodus plagues, providence through history, remembering God's works
Historical Context: Psalm 105 is a sweeping narrative of Israel's history told as praise — from Abraham's covenant through the Exodus to the Promised Land. It is paired with Psalm 106 (which tells the same history emphasizing Israel's failures). Together they show: God was faithful (105) even when Israel was not (106). Verses 1-15 appear in 1 Chronicles 16:8-22 as part of the song David composed when the ark was brought to Jerusalem. This psalm emphasizes God as the active agent throughout — He remembered, he sent, he brought out. The purpose of Israel's rescue: "that they might observe his statutes" (v.45).
Make Known His Deeds Among the People (vv.1-6): O give thanks unto the LORD; call upon his name: make known his deeds among the people. These verses stand almost word for word in 1 Chronicles 16:8-22, sung when David brought the ark to Jerusalem, which is the ground for associating the psalm with him though it carries no superscription. The instruction is to talk, talk ye of all his wondrous works, and then to remember, remember his marvellous works that he hath done. The audience is named by descent, O ye seed of Abraham his servant, which sets up the whole psalm as a family record.
He Hath Remembered His Covenant for Ever (vv.7-11): He hath remembered his covenant for ever, the word which he commanded to a thousand generations. The psalm's thesis is here and it is about God's memory, not Israel's. The covenant is traced through three generations, made with Abraham, sworn to Isaac, confirmed unto Jacob, and its content is reduced to one clause, unto thee will I give the land of Canaan, the lot of your inheritance. Everything that follows is the working out of that sentence.
Touch Not Mine Anointed (vv.12-15): When they were but a few men in number; yea, very few, and strangers in it. The patriarchs are described by their weakness, which is the psalm's way of making the protection remarkable. He suffered no man to do them wrong: yea, he reproved kings for their sakes. The quoted warning, touch not mine anointed, and do my prophets no harm, applies both titles to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, none of whom was a king or held prophetic office in the later sense; the psalm is using the words for men God had claimed.
He Sent a Man Before Them, Even Joseph (vv.16-22): Moreover he called for a famine upon the land: he brake the whole staff of bread. The famine is God's doing, stated without hedging, and so is what follows, he sent a man before them, even Joseph, who was sold for a servant. The psalm passes over the brothers entirely and reads the sale as a sending. Whose feet they hurt with fetters: he was laid in iron. Then the release, and the reason given is the word rather than the dream, until the time that his word came: the word of the LORD tried him. Senators in verse 22 renders a word for elders; KJV reached for a Roman title.
Wonders in the Land of Ham (vv.23-36): Israel also came into Egypt; and Jacob sojourned in the land of Ham. Fourteen verses on Egypt, and the hardest line is the one about the Egyptians' change of mind, he turned their heart to hate his people, which credits God with the hostility as directly as verse 16 credited him with the famine. The psalm states this and does not explain it, and it is the same difficulty Exodus raises when it hardens Pharaoh's heart. The plagues are then listed out of the order Exodus gives them, darkness first and the death of the firstborn last, with hail, locusts and flies between; the psalm is arranging for effect rather than reporting a sequence, and the count is seven rather than ten.
He Brought Them Forth with Silver and Gold (vv.37-41): He brought them forth also with silver and gold: and there was not one feeble person among their tribes. The departure is described as a success without a casualty, and Egypt's relief is noted with something close to humor, Egypt was glad when they departed. The wilderness provision is given in three lines and no complaint appears in any of them, he spread a cloud for a covering, the people asked, and he brought quails, he opened the rock, and the waters gushed out. Anyone who knows Exodus and Numbers will notice what has been left out; Psalm 106 supplies it.
That They Might Observe His Statutes (vv.42-45): For he remembered his holy promise, and Abraham his servant. The psalm returns to its thesis and closes the circle, and the entry into the land is stated as a handover, he gave them the lands of the heathen. The last verse gives the purpose of the whole history, that they might observe his statutes, and keep his laws, so the gift was for obedience rather than for comfort. Praise ye the LORD. Read alone this psalm is a record of unbroken faithfulness on one side only, and that is deliberate: the psalter puts the other side immediately after it.

Deep Dive

Setting: Psalm 105 has no title, but its first fifteen verses appear almost word for word in 1 Chronicles 16:8–22, as part of the song David gave the Levites when the ark of the covenant was brought into Jerusalem (around 1000 BC). Many readers take David as the author. Others think the Chronicler drew on a psalm already in use, or that the full psalm was expanded later. Either way, it was sung at one of Israel's great worship moments. The psalm retells Israel's history from Abraham to the conquest of Canaan, and its theme is a single sentence: "He remembers his covenant forever" (v8). It is the first of a pair. Psalm 105 tells the story as God's faithfulness, leaving out every failure. Psalm 106 tells the same story again as Israel's unfaithfulness. Read together, they show a faithful God and a faithless people, and the faithful God wins. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement through Israel's story.

Make Known His Deeds Among the Peoples (vv1–6)

  • "Oh give thanks to the LORD; call upon his name" (v1). The psalm opens with a burst of commands, about ten in six verses: give thanks, call, make known, sing, sing praises, tell, glory, rejoice, seek, remember. "Give thanks" (hodu) also means "confess" or "acknowledge," to declare publicly what God has done. Psalms 106, 107, 118 and 136 open the same way.
  • "Make known his deeds among the peoples!" (v1). Israel's history was never meant to be private. What God did for Abraham's family was meant to be told to the nations (Isa 12:4, which echoes this line). Worship and witness belong together.
  • "Tell of all his wondrous works!" (v2). "Tell" (siach) can mean to talk about, muse on, or meditate. It is the word for turning something over in conversation and in the mind.
  • "Glory in his holy name" (v3). "Glory" here means boast. Jeremiah says, "let him who boasts boast in this, that he understands and knows me" (Jer 9:24), and Paul quotes it (1 Cor 1:31). God's people have nothing of their own to boast about, but they can boast in him.
  • "Seek the LORD and his strength; seek his presence continually!" (v4). "Seek" appears three times in vv3–4, using two different Hebrew verbs (baqash and darash). "His strength" may refer to the ark itself, which Psalm 78:61 calls God's "power" and Psalm 132:8 "the ark of your might." If so, the line fits David's ark procession exactly. "His presence" is literally "his face."
  • "Remember the wondrous works that he has done" (v5). The whole psalm is an exercise in remembering. God remembers his covenant (vv8, 42), and his people are called to remember his works. "The judgments he uttered" are his verdicts, especially his judgments on Egypt (Exod 6:6; 12:12).
  • "O offspring of Abraham, his servant, children of Jacob, his chosen ones!" (v6). The psalm speaks to Israel as a family. "Servant" was a title of honor for those God used in his plan (Gen 26:24; Num 12:7). "Chosen" points to God's choice, not Israel's merit (Deut 7:6–8). 1 Chronicles 16:13 reads "Israel his servant" instead of "Abraham his servant."
  • Commentators: Henry notes that this psalm's appearance in 1 Chronicles 16 shows that "we may, if we please, furnish ourselves out of the word of God" with words for praise, rather than needing to compose new ones. JFB says to call on God "according to His historically manifested glory," and calls seeking God "the only true mode of getting true happiness." Guzik, following Morgan, introduces Psalms 105 and 106 as a matched pair, God's faithfulness and Israel's failure, and stresses that God's people forget easily, so remembering has to be deliberate.

He Remembers His Covenant Forever (vv7–15)

  • "He is the LORD our God; his judgments are in all the earth" (v7). Israel's God is not a tribal deity. He is "our God," but his rule covers "all the earth." That is why he could judge Egypt and rebuke foreign kings.
  • "He remembers his covenant forever" (v8). When the Bible says God "remembers," it does not mean he had forgotten. It means he acts on what he promised (Gen 8:1; Exod 2:24). "For a thousand generations" is a way of saying forever (Deut 7:9). 1 Chronicles 16:15 makes it a command: "Remember his covenant forever."
  • "The covenant that he made with Abraham, his sworn promise to Isaac, which he confirmed to Jacob" (vv9–10). The promise passed from father to son to grandson: to Abraham (Gen 12:7; 15:18; 17:7–8), sworn to Isaac (Gen 26:3), confirmed to Jacob at Bethel (Gen 28:13–15; 35:12). "Sworn promise" recalls God's oath after Abraham offered Isaac: "By myself I have sworn" (Gen 22:16). Hebrews says that God, unable to swear by anyone greater, swore by himself (Heb 6:13–18).
  • "To you I will give the land of Canaan as your portion for an inheritance" (v11). The heart of the covenant here is the land. "Portion" is literally "cord" or "measuring line," the rope used to measure out a plot of land. The rest of the psalm shows how God kept this promise.
  • "When they were few in number, of little account, and sojourners in it" (v12). Abraham's family was tiny. Jacob went down to Egypt with seventy people (Gen 46:27). They owned no land in Canaan except a burial cave Abraham bought (Gen 23). They lived as "sojourners," resident foreigners without rights (Heb 11:9, 13).
  • "Wandering from nation to nation" (v13). Abraham went from Ur to Haran to Canaan, down to Egypt and back, and to Gerar among the Philistines. Isaac lived in Gerar. Jacob fled to Paddan-aram and returned.
  • "He allowed no one to oppress them; he rebuked kings on their account" (v14). God plagued Pharaoh for taking Sarah (Gen 12:17) and warned Abimelech king of Gerar in a dream: "you are a dead man" (Gen 20:3). He protected Isaac in the same place (Gen 26:11). Boice, cited by Guzik, notes that in both cases Abraham had lied. The protection came from God's faithfulness, not Abraham's.
  • "Touch not my anointed ones, do my prophets no harm!" (v15). The patriarchs were never literally anointed with oil. "Anointed" (meshichay, from the same root as "Messiah") here means set apart by God for his purpose. God called Abraham a "prophet" when he warned Abimelech (Gen 20:7). This verse is sometimes used today to forbid any criticism of church leaders, but in context it is God protecting his covenant family from foreign kings, not shielding leaders from accountability.
  • Commentators: Henry heads vv8–11 "God's covenant with the patriarchs" and vv12–15 "his care of them while they were strangers." JFB names Pharaoh and Abimelech as the kings rebuked, and reads "Jehovah" in v7 as showing that God keeps his promises. Guzik passes on Kidner's point that the covenant rests on God's own command, not on a bargain struck between equals, and Morgan's observation that "he" dominates the psalm: God is the subject of almost every verb. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, suggests the words of v15 may be a poetic summary of God's dealings, not a recorded speech, which is fair, since Genesis records no such exact words.

He Had Sent a Man Ahead of Them (vv16–25)

  • "When he summoned a famine on the land and broke all supply of bread" (v16). God "summoned" the famine as a master calls a servant. JFB puts it plainly: "God ordered the famine." "Supply of bread" is literally "staff of bread," bread as the staff that holds up life (Lev 26:26; Ezek 4:16).
  • "He had sent a man ahead of them, Joseph, who was sold as a slave" (v17). The psalm sees the whole Joseph story from God's side. His brothers sold him (Gen 37:28), but God "sent" him. Joseph himself said so: "God sent me before you to preserve life… it was not you who sent me here, but God" (Gen 45:5–8; 50:20).
  • "His feet were hurt with fetters; his neck was put in a collar of iron" (v18). Genesis does not describe Joseph's chains, so the psalm adds detail. The second line is literally "his nephesh came into iron." Nephesh usually means "soul" or "life," but it can also mean throat or neck, which is how the ESV reads it. The old Prayer Book version rendered it "the iron entered into his soul," which has passed into English as a phrase for deep suffering. Both senses are true of Joseph.
  • "Until what he had said came to pass, the word of the LORD tested him" (v19). "What he had said" may be Joseph's dreams (Gen 37:5–11) or his interpretation of the cupbearer's dream (Gen 40). "Tested" (tsaraph) is the word for refining metal in a furnace (Ps 12:6; 66:10). God's promise to Joseph did not shield him from prison. It refined him in prison, for perhaps thirteen years (Gen 37:2; 41:46).
  • "The king sent and released him; the ruler of the peoples set him free" (v20). The same Pharaoh who could have left him to die released him in a day (Gen 41:14).
  • "He made him lord of his house and ruler of all his possessions" (v21). Joseph went from the prison to second in the land (Gen 41:40–44).
  • "To bind his princes at his pleasure and to teach his elders wisdom" (v22). The man whose feet were bound now "binds" princes, a deliberate echo of v18. JFB puts it: "The soul that was once bound itself now binds others, even princes." The Greek translation reads "to instruct his princes," a different vowel reading, which fits the second line about teaching. JFB adds that "the ground of his exaltation by Pharaoh was his wisdom."
  • "Then Israel came to Egypt; Jacob sojourned in the land of Ham" (v23). "The land of Ham" is a poetic name for Egypt, since Mizraim (Egypt) was a son of Ham (Gen 10:6). Jacob went down at God's word: "Do not be afraid to go down to Egypt… I myself will go down with you" (Gen 46:3–4).
  • "And the LORD made his people very fruitful and made them stronger than their foes" (v24). This echoes Exodus 1:7 almost exactly. The family of seventy became a nation.
  • "He turned their hearts to hate his people, to deal craftily with his servants" (v25). This is a striking line. The psalm says God turned Egypt against Israel. The Bible holds together two truths without resolving all the tension: Egypt freely chose to oppress Israel and was guilty for it, and God ruled over even that choice to bring about the exodus. "Deal craftily" recalls Pharaoh's words, "Come, let us deal shrewdly with them" (Exod 1:10).
  • Commentators: Henry calls this section God "raising up Joseph to be the shepherd and stone of Israel," borrowing Jacob's blessing (Gen 49:24). JFB says on v25 that "God controls men's free acts," so no plot against his people succeeds unless he allows it. Guzik, citing Spurgeon, adds the guardrail that God is never the author of sin, even when his acts provoke wicked people. On v18 Guzik passes on Trapp's reading, that the iron entered Joseph's soul, and Meyer's point that the fetters shaped Joseph for leadership.

He Sent Moses, His Servant (vv26–36)

  • "He sent Moses, his servant, and Aaron, whom he had chosen" (v26). As God "sent" Joseph (v17), he now "sends" Moses and Aaron (Exod 3:10; 4:14–16). The deliverers, like the famine, come at God's command.
  • "They performed his signs among them and miracles in the land of Ham" (v27). The plagues were "signs," acts that pointed beyond themselves. God said they would show that "I am the LORD" (Exod 7:5) and execute "judgments on all the gods of Egypt" (Exod 12:12).
  • Eight plagues, in a new order (vv28–36). Exodus records ten plagues. The psalm lists eight, leaving out the death of the livestock (fifth) and the boils (sixth), and puts darkness, the ninth, first. Psalm 78:44–51 gives a different selection again. The psalmists were not giving a checklist but retelling the story as poetry. Here the list opens with darkness, perhaps because it was the most fearsome, and ends with the death of the firstborn, the climax.
  • "He sent darkness, and made the land dark; they did not rebel against his words" (v28). Darkness "that can be felt" covered Egypt for three days, while Israel had light (Exod 10:21–23). The Egyptian sun god Ra was helpless. "They did not rebel" most likely means Moses and Aaron obeyed God's words. The Greek translation drops the "not" and reads "they rebelled against his words," referring to the Egyptians. Hebrew manuscripts agree on the ESV's reading.
  • "He turned their waters into blood and caused their fish to die" (v29). The Nile was Egypt's lifeline and was linked to the god Hapi (Exod 7:17–21).
  • "Their land swarmed with frogs, even in the chambers of their kings" (v30). Frogs were linked to the fertility goddess Heqet. They came into Pharaoh's own bedroom (Exod 8:3). No palace was safe.
  • "He spoke, and there came swarms of flies, and gnats throughout their country" (v31). "He spoke" stresses that these were not natural accidents but responses to God's word (Exod 8:16–24).
  • "He gave them hail for rain, and fiery lightning bolts through their land" (v32). Rain is rare in Egypt, which relies on the Nile. Instead of rain they got a hailstorm with fire, "such as had never been in all the land of Egypt" (Exod 9:23–24).
  • "He struck down their vines and fig trees, and shattered the trees of their country" (v33). Exodus says the hail "struck down every plant of the field and broke every tree" (Exod 9:25). Vines and figs are named in Psalm 78:47 too.
  • "He spoke, and the locusts came, young locusts without number" (vv34–35). Whatever the hail spared, the locusts ate (Exod 10:12–15). "Young locusts" may be a second kind of locust or the wingless young.
  • "He struck down all the firstborn in their land, the firstfruits of all their strength" (v36). The firstborn son was the pride and future of a family, "the firstfruits of strength" (Gen 49:3; Deut 21:17). Pharaoh had ordered Israel's baby boys killed (Exod 1:22). Now judgment falls on Egypt's sons, while Israel's firstborn are spared under the Passover blood (Exod 12:12–13, 29).
  • Commentators: Henry groups vv23–38 as "the increase of Israel in Egypt and their deliverance out of Egypt." JFB notes that the fifth and sixth plagues are omitted, sees the darkness as partly a symbol of God's wrath, and calls the death of the firstborn the climax. Guzik, following Boice, pairs each plague with an Egyptian god it humiliated, and passes on Kidner's point that the psalm lists the plagues to praise God's power, not to trace Pharaoh's hardening. Guzik says the vines and fig trees are not in Exodus, but Exodus 9:25 does say every tree was broken, so the psalm is naming what Exodus summarizes.

He Remembered His Holy Promise (vv37–45)

  • "Then he brought out Israel with silver and gold" (v37). The Egyptians gave Israel silver and gold jewellery as they left, so that "they plundered the Egyptians" (Exod 12:35–36). God had told Abraham his descendants would come out "with great possessions" (Gen 15:14). Much of that gold later went into the tabernacle (Exod 35:22).
  • "There was none among his tribes who stumbled" (v37). A nation of slaves set out on foot, with old and young, and no one was too weak to march. Moses later said their clothes and feet did not wear out in forty years (Deut 8:4).
  • "Egypt was glad when they departed, for dread of them had fallen upon it" (v38). The Egyptians urged them to leave quickly: "We shall all be dead" (Exod 12:33). The oppressors ended up begging their slaves to go.
  • "He spread a cloud for a covering, and fire to give light by night" (v39). In Exodus the cloud mainly guided (Exod 13:21). Here it is a "covering," a shade from the desert sun, as Isaiah pictures over Zion (Isa 4:5–6).
  • "They asked, and he brought quail, and gave them bread from heaven in abundance" (v40). Exodus 16 and Numbers 11 tell how the people grumbled for food. Psalm 105 says only "they asked," and leaves the grumbling for Psalm 106:13–15. Jesus called himself "the true bread from heaven" (John 6:32–35).
  • "He opened the rock, and water gushed out; it flowed through the desert like a river" (v41). Water from the rock at Horeb (Exod 17:6) and Kadesh (Num 20:11). Paul says "the Rock was Christ" (1 Cor 10:4).
  • "For he remembered his holy promise, and Abraham, his servant" (v42). The psalm returns to its key word. Everything from the famine to the manna happened because God remembered what he had told Abraham (v8). This verse brackets the whole story.
  • "So he brought his people out with joy, his chosen ones with singing" (v43). The Song of the Sea (Exod 15) and Miriam's dancing (Exod 15:20–21) are the joy and singing.
  • "He gave them the lands of the nations, and they took possession of the fruit of the peoples' toil" (v44). Israel moved into cities they did not build and vineyards they did not plant (Deut 6:10–11; Josh 24:13). The land promise of v11 is kept.
  • "That they might keep his statutes and observe his laws" (v45). This is the purpose of the whole story. God did not rescue Israel so they could do as they pleased, but so they could live as his people under his good law, a model nation (Deut 4:5–8). Grace comes first, then obedience. The Ten Commandments begin the same way: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt" (Exod 20:2). "Praise the LORD!" closes the psalm, as at the end of Psalm 104.
  • Commentators: Henry heads this section "the care he took of them in the wilderness and their settlement in Canaan," and says in singing the psalm "we must give to God the glory of his wisdom and power." JFB says no tribe was "weak or unfit to march," and reads v45 as the purpose of every blessing listed. Guzik notes, with Maclaren, that the psalm leaves out Israel's sins, which Psalm 106 tells in full, and makes v45 his key application: deliverance is for obedience. Kidner, cited by Guzik, links it to Romans 8:4, the law's requirement fulfilled in those who walk by the Spirit.

Pointing to Christ

Psalm 105 is the story of a God who keeps his word through famine, slavery, prison and plague, and it centres on two men he "sent": Joseph, sold by his brothers and raised from prison to save many lives, and Moses, who led a nation of slaves out with singing. Both point to Jesus. He was sent by the Father, sold by one of his own for silver, bound and condemned, and raised to the right hand of the King, so that through him many would be saved (Gen 50:20; Acts 2:23–24, 36). He is the greater Moses who leads a new exodus out of slavery to sin (Luke 9:31, where "departure" is literally "exodus"), the Passover Lamb whose blood spares the firstborn (1 Cor 5:7), the bread from heaven and the rock from which living water flows. And he is the fulfilment of the promise God "remembered," for "all the promises of God find their Yes in him" (2 Cor 1:20), including the promise to Abraham that in his offspring all nations would be blessed (Gal 3:8, 16).

Reflection

  • Retelling What God Has Done (vv.1-2) — The psalm opens with a call to make God's deeds known and to sing about His wondrous works, treating storytelling as an act of worship. Do you actively retell what God has done in your life, or let those stories go untold?
  • Provision Sent Ahead of the Crisis (v.17) — Before the famine ever arrived, God had already sent Joseph into Egypt to prepare the way. Can you look back and see how God positioned help before you even knew you'd need it?
  • Tested in the Gap Between Promise and Fulfillment (v.19) — Joseph carried a God-given dream through years in a pit and a prison before it was fulfilled. If you're waiting for a promise you believe God has given you, can you trust that the waiting itself is doing something in you?
  • A God Who Remembers His Covenant (v.42) — Generations passed between the promise made to Abraham and its fulfillment in the Exodus, yet God had not forgotten. What long-standing promise from God are you tempted to believe He's overlooked?
  • Freedom With a Purpose (v.45) — Israel's deliverance is described as aimed at obedience, not freedom for its own sake. Where has God given you freedom that you've used for yourself instead of for serving Him?
