Summary

A lament in which David appeals for vindication from a place of integrity. He asks God to hear his righteous cause, having tested his heart at night and found no wickedness or deceit in it (vv. 1-3), and affirms that he has kept himself from violent paths and held to God's ways (vv. 4-5). He calls on God, who answers those who seek him, to guard him like the pupil of an eye and hide him under the shadow of his wings from the wicked who surround him like predators (vv. 6-9). His enemies are arrogant, close their hearts to pity, and hunt him down like a lion stalking prey (vv. 10-12). David asks God to rise up and confront them, since their reward is only in this life, while he looks forward to seeing God's face in righteousness and being satisfied when he awakens in his likeness (vv. 13-15).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Individual Lament / Prayer for Vindication
Attributed Author: David — "A Prayer of David"
Key Themes: Innocence before God, divine testing and approval, enemies like lions, apple of God's eye, shadow of God's wings, satisfaction in God's presence vs. worldly satisfaction
Historical Context: Labeled simply "A Prayer" — one of only five psalms so designated (17, 86, 90, 102, 142). David protests his innocence (vv.1-5) in language similar to Psalm 7, appeals for protection using intimate imagery (apple of the eye, shadow of wings), and contrasts two ultimate satisfactions: the worldly person satisfied with "this life" (v.14) vs. David satisfied with God's face at resurrection (v.15). The enemies are described as predatory lions (v.12), suggesting a period when David was hunted. The final verse (v.15) is one of the most remarkable statements of afterlife hope in the Psalter.
Hear the Right, O LORD (vv.1-5): Hear the right, O LORD, attend unto my cry, give ear unto my prayer, that goeth not out of feigned lips. The claim is not general innocence but honesty in this particular petition. Then the test invited, thou hast proved mine heart, thou hast visited me in the night, thou hast tried me, and shalt find nothing. And the ground of the claim is stated as restraint rather than achievement, concerning the works of men, by the word of thy lips I have kept me from the paths of the destroyer.
Keep Me as the Apple of the Eye (vv.6-9): I have called upon thee, for thou wilt hear me, O God, incline thine ear unto me. Then two images that have both passed into ordinary English, keep me as the apple of the eye, hide me under the shadow of thy wings. The first is the pupil, the most instinctively protected part of the body, and the second is a bird sheltering its young, which is the same figure Jesus uses of Jerusalem in Matthew 23:37.
Like a Lion Greedy of His Prey (vv.10-12): They are inclosed in their own fat, with their mouth they speak proudly. The description moves from arrogance to hunting, they have now compassed us in our steps, they have set their eyes bowing down to the earth. And the comparison is doubled to cover both danger and patience, like as a lion that is greedy of his prey, and as it were a young lion lurking in secret places.
Deliver My Soul from the Wicked (vv.13-14): Arise, O LORD, disappoint him, cast him down, deliver my soul from the wicked. Then a description of the opponents by what they have, men of the world, which have their portion in this life, and the phrase is doing careful work: their portion is real and it is bounded. And the last clause notes that it extends beyond them, they leave the rest of their substance to their babes, so the psalm concedes their prosperity is durable and still calls it a portion in this life.
I Shall Be Satisfied, When I Awake (v.15): As for me, I will behold thy face in righteousness, I shall be satisfied, when I awake, with thy likeness. One verse set against the previous one, and every term in it is the counterpart of something there: their portion against his satisfaction, this life against waking, and possessions against a face. Whether awake means morning or something further is left open by the Hebrew, and the psalm does not press it.

Setting: The title reads "A Prayer of David." Only five psalms carry that label (17, 86, 90, 102, 142), and petition fills this one from start to finish. No event is named, but most commentators place it in the years when Saul hunted David through the wilderness. David is innocent of the charges against him, surrounded by men who want him dead, and he takes his case to the only court that can clear him. Psalm 16 sang of God as the psalmist's "portion," his counsellor in the night, and the one at his right hand, ending with joy in God's presence. Psalm 17 picks up the same words under pressure: God tests him "by night" (v3), saves by his "right hand" (v7), the wicked have their "portion in this life" (v14), and the psalm ends again with God's face (v15). It is the prayer of a man who believes Psalm 16 and is now being shot at.

Hear a Just Cause (vv1–2)

  • "Hear a just cause, O LORD" (v1). Literally "hear righteousness" (tsedeq). David is not asking God to hear a good man. He is asking God to hear a case, the way a judge hears a plaintiff. The language of the law court runs through the whole psalm: a cause, a verdict, the judge's eyes, a test of evidence.
  • "Attend to my cry! Give ear to my prayer" (v1). Three verbs of hearing in one line, the same pile-up as 5:1–2. A "cry" (rinnah) is a loud, ringing call, often of joy, here of distress. This is not a calm, composed prayer.
  • "From lips free of deceit" (v1). Literally "not from lips of deceit." David is not putting on a show for God or bending the facts to win. That matters because his enemies' main weapon is their mouths (v10). The godly man pleads with honest lips, as 15:2 required of anyone who would dwell with God.
  • "From your presence let my vindication come!" (v2). "Vindication" is mishpat, a judgment or verdict. David wants the ruling to come from God's own presence, from his throne. Men have already passed their sentence on him, and he appeals over their heads. He had said the same aloud to Saul at the cave in En-gedi: "May the LORD judge between me and you" (1 Sam 24:12, 15).
  • "Let your eyes behold the right!" (v2). The word is mesharim, what is level, straight or fair. A just judge sees things as they really are. For the falsely accused, God's all-seeing eye is not a threat but a comfort.
  • Commentators: Henry reads the opening as an appeal from Saul's prejudice to "the court of heaven," and says, "Sincerity dreads no scrutiny, no, not that of God himself." He adds that "God's omniscience is as much the joy of the upright as it is the terror of hypocrites." Guzik cautions that people often think their cause is just when it is not, and urges an honest look at one's own case before claiming God's verdict. JFB glosses "sentence" (KJV for v2) as an "acquitting judgment" and "presence" as God's tribunal.

You Have Tried My Heart (vv3–5)

  • "You have tried my heart, you have visited me by night" (v3). Three testing words: bachan (to examine, as metal is assayed), paqad (to visit, inspect, call to account) and tsaraph (to refine by fire, used of smelting silver in 12:6). Night is when the heart lies exposed, when the day's defenses come down. Psalm 16:7 said, "in the night also my heart instructs me." Here, in the night, God has gone through David's heart with a lamp.
  • "You will find nothing" (v3). This is not a claim to be sinless. David knew his sins well (Ps 32; 51). He means God will find nothing of what he is accused of: no plot against Saul, no treason. Psalm 7:3–5 makes the same kind of oath on the same kind of charge, and twice David proved it by sparing Saul's life when he could have killed him (1 Sam 24; 26).
  • "I have purposed that my mouth will not transgress" (v3). Not "I hope" or "I'll try," but a settled decision. James says the person who never stumbles in what he says is perfect (Jas 3:2). David does not claim that. He claims the resolve.
  • "By the word of your lips I have avoided the ways of the violent" (v4). The Hebrew of this verse is compressed and hard to translate, but the sense is clear. Over against "the works of man," the ordinary ways people treat each other, David has steered by God's word. "The violent" (parits) means a robber or breaker-in. God's lips (v4) stand against deceitful lips (v1). Psalm 119:9 and 11 say the same: a young man keeps his way pure by guarding it according to God's word.
  • "My steps have held fast to your paths; my feet have not slipped" (v5). The ESV takes this as a statement, completing David's testimony. The KJV reads it as a request: "Hold up my goings in thy paths." The Hebrew form can be read either way. Both readings are true together: David has kept God's paths, and he knows he will only keep them if God holds him.
  • Commentators: Henry says God had tried David's heart both through his own conscience, "God's deputy in the soul," and through providence, especially the chances he was given to kill Saul. On v3 he notes David "does not say, 'My mouth never shall transgress' (for in many things we all offend)," but that he has purposed it. Guzik passes on Boice's list of questions for self-examination before praying, with his point that unconfessed sin is a great barrier to prayer. JFB notes that v5 "may be read as an assertion," as the ESV does.

Wondrously Show Your Steadfast Love (vv6–7)

  • "I call upon you, for you will answer me, O God" (v6). The "I" is emphatic. David's confidence does not rest on the strength of his case but on the character of the one he calls. Henry notes the plea of habit too: this is a man who prayed before trouble came.
  • "Wondrously show your steadfast love" (v7). The verb (palah) means to set apart, to make distinct or wonderful. It is the word used when God "made a distinction" between Israel and Egypt in the plagues (Exod 8:22; 9:4) and when David says, "the LORD has set apart the godly for himself" (4:3). David is asking for more than ordinary kindness. He wants God's covenant love (chesed) shown in a way that stands out, that everyone can see is God's doing.
  • "O Savior of those who seek refuge" (v7). The line is literally "Saviour of refuge-seekers from those who rise up, by your right hand." "Seek refuge" is the familiar chasah (2:12; 11:1; 16:1). God's right hand is his strength in action (Exod 15:6, 12). Because the Hebrew word order is loose, the KJV margin reads it as those who rise up against God's right hand. Either way, the enemies of God's people end up facing God's own strength.
  • Commentators: Henry takes "show thy marvellous loving-kindness" to mean both "distinguishing favours," "put me not off with common mercies," and "wonderful favours" that make people marvel. JFB agrees: "Show—set apart as special and eminent," citing Exodus 8 and Psalm 4:3. Guzik calls this the first appearance of "lovingkindness" in the Psalms. That is true of the NKJV he comments on, but the Hebrew word chesed has already appeared several times (5:7; 6:4; 13:5), where the NKJV translates it "mercy."

The Apple of Your Eye (vv8–9)

  • "Keep me as the apple of your eye" (v8). The Hebrew is ishon bat-ayin, "the pupil, daughter of the eye." Ishon is often explained as "little man," from the tiny reflection of oneself seen in another person's pupil, though the lexicon gives simply "pupil." It is the most sensitive, most reflexively guarded part of the body: the brow shields it, the lid snaps shut, the hand flies up. Moses used the same picture for how God kept Israel in the wilderness: "he kept him as the apple of his eye" (Deut 32:10), and Zechariah says that whoever touches God's people "touches the apple of his eye" (Zech 2:8).
  • "Hide me in the shadow of your wings" (v8). Moses' song puts the two images side by side: the apple of the eye, then the eagle spreading its wings over its young (Deut 32:10–11). Boaz blessed Ruth as one who had come to take refuge under the God of Israel's wings (Ruth 2:12), and the picture returns again and again (36:7; 57:1; 61:4; 91:4). Some, Henry among them, hear an echo of the cherubim whose wings overshadowed the ark in the Most Holy Place. Jesus took the image for himself as he wept over Jerusalem: "How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings" (Matt 23:37).
  • "My deadly enemies who surround me" (v9). Literally "enemies against my soul (nefesh)," enemies who want his life. "Surround" is the language of a hunt or a siege. These are not imaginary fears. Saul had three thousand chosen men out looking for David (1 Sam 24:2).
  • Commentators: JFB: "Similar figures, denoting the preciousness of God's people in His sight," citing Deuteronomy 32 and Matthew 23:37. Henry: "If we keep God's law as the apple of our eye (Prov. 7:2), we may expect that God will so keep us," and he prays to be hidden under God's wings "where I may be both safe and warm." Guzik walks through how the body protects the eye, and passes on Maclaren's point that fears turned into prayers are already half conquered.

Like a Lion Eager to Tear (vv10–12)

  • "They close their hearts to pity" (v10). Literally "their fat they have closed up." In the Bible, fat is a picture of prosperous, insensitive self-satisfaction: "Jeshurun grew fat, and kicked" (Deut 32:15; also Ps 73:7; 119:70). The ESV interprets the picture: a heart wrapped in so much comfort that nothing gets through, not pity, not conscience, not God.
  • "With their mouths they speak arrogantly" (v10). Here is the contrast with v1. David brought lips without deceit to God. His enemies bring proud mouths against God's people. Psalms 10 and 12 described the same kind of talk.
  • "They have now surrounded our steps" (v11). The shift to "our" fits David on the run with his band of men (1 Sam 22:2; 23:13). "Set their eyes to cast us to the ground" pictures a hunter's or predator's eyes fixed low, tracking every footprint. David's steps held to God's paths (v5); their eyes are fixed on his steps.
  • "He is like a lion eager to tear" (v12). The plural enemies narrow to one, probably Saul himself. A young lion "lurking in ambush" waits in cover for prey to come close. David had once fought lions and bears protecting his father's sheep (1 Sam 17:34–37). Now he is the hunted sheep, and he knows he needs someone else to stand between him and the lion.
  • Commentators: JFB: "enclosed … fat—are become proud in prosperity, and insolent to God," and in v11, "They pursue us as beasts tracking their prey." Henry pictures them wrapping themselves "in their own honour, and power, and plenty," and applies the lurking lion to Saul hunting David "on the rocks of the wild goats" (1 Sam 24:2). Guzik notes that the shepherd who once killed a lion now asks God to deal with this one, wanting God's hand rather than his own to win.

Arise, O LORD! (vv13–14)

  • "Arise, O LORD! Confront him, subdue him!" (v13). "Arise" is the battle cry of the ark going out before Israel (Num 10:35), already heard in 3:7, 7:6, 9:19 and 10:12. "Confront him" is literally "go before his face": meet the lion head-on before it springs. "Subdue him" means make him bow down, bring him to his knees.
  • "Deliver my soul from the wicked by your sword" (v13). The ESV, with most modern versions, reads "by your sword … by your hand" (v14), God's own power doing the rescue. The KJV reads "the wicked, which is thy sword … men which are thy hand," meaning the wicked are tools in God's hand, as Assyria was the rod of his anger (Isa 10:5). The Hebrew allows both. The ESV reading fits the parallel lines best, but the KJV reading is also a biblical truth: even David's enemies cannot move one inch outside God's control.
  • "Men of the world whose portion is in this life" (v14). The word for "world" (cheled) means duration, the passing span of a lifetime (39:5; 89:47). These are people whose whole share, their whole "portion," is here and now. Psalm 16:5 said, "The LORD is my chosen portion." The difference between David and his enemies is not that one has more than the other. It is what each has chosen as his portion. Jesus told of a rich man who "received your good things" in his lifetime (Luke 16:25) and of a farmer who stored up treasure but was "not rich toward God" (Luke 12:21).
  • "You fill their womb with treasure" (v14). The rest of the verse is notoriously hard. "Womb" (beten) can mean belly, and "treasure" (tsaphun) means something stored up or hidden. The ESV and KJV read it as a description of the worldly man's prosperity: full stomachs, many children, an estate to leave behind. It sounds like envy at first, but it is really a sober observation. God gives good things even to people who ignore him (Matt 5:45), and those things are all they will ever have. The NIV reads it as a prayer of judgment: may the punishment God has stored up fill their bellies and pass on to their children. A third reading takes "your treasured ones" as God's people, whom he fills. The ESV's reading is the most widely followed.
  • Commentators: Henry follows the KJV: the wicked are "thy sword," a sword God "can manage as he pleases… and which he will sheathe when he has done his work with it." His summary: "There is no flying from God's hand but by flying to it." JFB, unusually, supplies "with" before sword and hand, reading them as God's power, the way the ESV does. On v14 he says the wicked "appear, by fulness of bread and large families, to be prosperous," but that this is "transient." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's comment that God may give such people the husks they crave, as swine are given husks.

I Shall Behold Your Face (v15)

  • "As for me" (v15). The pronoun is emphatic and the turn is sudden. After all the noise of lions, swords, fat hearts and full barns, David looks up. Guzik passes on Kidner's image of the verse soaring straight up out of the lowlands of v14.
  • "I shall behold your face in righteousness" (v15). The wicked have their portion in this life. David's portion is God's face, the same hope as 11:7, "the upright shall behold his face." "In righteousness" may mean "as one vindicated," with the just verdict he asked for in v2 finally given. It may also mean that only the righteous can see God at all (24:3–4; Matt 5:8). The two belong together.
  • "When I awake" (v15). There are two main readings. Some take it as waking after a night spent in prayer and testing (v3), rising to find God present and the answer given. Others, including most older Christian commentators, hear the awakening from the sleep of death, as in Daniel 12:2 and Isaiah 26:19. The Old Testament's hope beyond death is still quiet here, but Psalm 16:10–11 has just said God will not abandon his holy one to the grave and will show him the path of life. Read together, the second reading fits well, and the first is not lost: every morning's waking to God is a small rehearsal of the last one.
  • "I shall be satisfied with your likeness" (v15). "Likeness" (temunah) means form, visible shape. It is the word used when God says Moses "beholds the form of the LORD" (Num 12:8), and when Israel is told it saw "no form" at Sinai, which is why no idol could picture him (Deut 4:12, 15). David hopes to see what even Israel at Sinai did not. "Satisfied" repeats v14: the worldly are "satisfied with children," and the believer will be satisfied with God himself. John says, "we know that when he appears we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is" (1 John 3:2).
  • Commentators: Henry calls the hope of v15 David's comfort in "his future happiness" and reads the psalm's prayer for preservation as a picture of Christ, kept by God through his humiliation "to the glories and joys of his exalted state." Guzik takes "when I awake" as waking into the life to come, where heaven proves more real than this world, and links "likeness" to Romans 8:29, being conformed to the image of God's Son. JFB contrasts the wicked's passing prosperity with the psalmist's "better portion in a joyful union with God hereafter."

Pointing to Christ

Henry notes that some read this psalm as a picture of Christ, "who was perfectly innocent, and yet was hated and persecuted." David's claim of innocence was true only of the charges against him. Jesus could say it without limit: tested by night in Gethsemane, found with no deceit in his mouth (1 Pet 2:22), surrounded by enemies who closed their hearts to pity. He did not take vengeance into his own hands but "continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23), and the Father gave his verdict on the third day. Because he was raised, those who take refuge under his wings share David's hope: their portion is not in this life, and when they awake they will see his face and be satisfied.

Reflection

  • Character Tested in Private (v.3) — David invites God to test him even at night, when no one else is watching. Does your private conduct match the character you display publicly?
  • Guarded Like the Apple of God's Eye (v.8) — David asks to be kept as carefully as the pupil of an eye, the most instinctively protected part of the body. Do you believe God's protection over you is this immediate and instinctive?
  • Enemies Like Predatory Lions (v.12) — David describes the danger surrounding him in vivid, threatening terms, yet still brings it to God. Are you honest with God about how dangerous or overwhelming a situation truly feels?
  • Two Different Satisfactions (v.14) — David contrasts those satisfied by present wealth and comfort with his own longing for something beyond this life. What are you ultimately seeking to satisfy you, this life's rewards or God Himself?
  • Satisfied by Becoming Like God (v.15) — David's deepest hope is to awaken in God's likeness, not simply to escape his troubles. Is being conformed to God's character the satisfaction you are pursuing, or are you settling for lesser goals?
