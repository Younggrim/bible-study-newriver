1Oh, that my head were a spring of water, and my eyes a fountain of tears! I would weep day and night over the slain daughter of my people.

2If only I had a traveler’s lodge in the wilderness, I would abandon my people and depart from them, for they are all adulterers, a crowd of faithless people.

3“They bend their tongues like bows; lies prevail over truth in the land. For they proceed from evil to evil, and they do not take Me into account,” declares the LORD.

4“Let everyone guard against his neighbor; do not trust any brother, for every brother deals craftily, and every friend spreads slander.

5Each one betrays his friend; no one tells the truth. They have taught their tongues to lie; they wear themselves out committing iniquity.

6You dwell in the midst of deception; in their deceit they refuse to know Me,” declares the LORD.

7Therefore this is what the LORD of Hosts says: “Behold, I will refine them and test them, for what else can I do because of the daughter of My people?

8Their tongues are deadly arrows; they speak deception. With his mouth a man speaks peace to his neighbor, but in his heart he sets a trap for him.

9Should I not punish them for these things? declares the LORD. Should I not avenge Myself on such a nation as this?”

10I will take up a weeping and wailing for the mountains, a dirge over the wilderness pasture, for they have been scorched so no one passes through, and the lowing of cattle is not heard. Both the birds of the air and the beasts have fled; they have gone away.

11“And I will make Jerusalem a heap of rubble, a haunt for jackals; and I will make the cities of Judah a desolation, without inhabitant.”

12Who is the man wise enough to understand this? To whom has the mouth of the LORD spoken, that he may explain it? Why is the land destroyed and scorched like a desert, so no one can pass through it?

13And the LORD answered, “It is because they have forsaken My law, which I set before them; they have not walked in it or obeyed My voice.

14Instead, they have followed the stubbornness of their hearts and gone after the Baals, as their fathers taught them.”

15Therefore this is what the LORD of Hosts, the God of Israel, says: “Behold, I will feed this people wormwood and give them poisoned water to drink.

16I will scatter them among the nations that neither they nor their fathers have known, and I will send a sword after them until I have finished them off.”

17This is what the LORD of Hosts says: “Take note, and summon the wailing women; send for the most skillful among them.

18Let them come quickly and take up a lament over us, that our eyes may overflow with tears, and our eyelids may gush with water.

19For the sound of wailing is heard from Zion: ‘How devastated we are! How great is our shame! For we have abandoned the land because our dwellings have been torn down.’”

20Now, O women, hear the word of the LORD. Open your ears to the word of His mouth. Teach your daughters to wail, and one another to lament.

21For death has climbed in through our windows; it has entered our fortresses to cut off the children from the streets, the young men from the town squares.

22Declare that this is what the LORD says: “The corpses of men will fall like dung upon the open field, like newly cut grain behind the reaper, with no one to gather it.”

23This is what the LORD says: “Let not the wise man boast in his wisdom, nor the strong man in his strength, nor the wealthy man in his riches.

24But let him who boasts boast in this, that he understands and knows Me, that I am the LORD, who exercises loving devotion, justice and righteousness on the earth—for I delight in these things,” declares the LORD.

25“Behold, the days are coming,” declares the LORD, “when I will punish all who are circumcised only in the flesh—

26Egypt, Judah, Edom, Ammon, Moab, and all the inhabitants of the desert who clip the hair of their temples. For all these nations are uncircumcised, and the whole house of Israel is uncircumcised in heart.”

Summary

Jeremiah wishes his head were a spring and his eyes a fountain so he could weep continually for his people, who are about to be destroyed for their corruption (vv. 1-2). He describes a society where neighbors deceive one another, no one speaks truthfully, and everyone's tongue is like a deadly weapon (vv. 3-8). God announces coming devastation severe enough that professional mourners must be summoned to teach the daughters wailing, because death has entered every home (vv. 17-21). The chapter closes with a call not to boast in wisdom, strength, or wealth, but instead to boast only in truly knowing and understanding God, who delights in steadfast love, justice, and righteousness (vv. 23-24).

Authorship & Background

Author: Jeremiah son of Hilkiah (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Lament and Oracle
Key Themes: The weeping prophet, pervasive deception, tongues as deadly weapons, coming desolation, the call for mourning women, true vs. false glory, circumcision of flesh vs. heart
Historical Context: This chapter gives Jeremiah his nickname: "the weeping prophet." He wishes his head were a water source and his eyes a fountain — he can't cry enough for what's coming. The chapter's diagnosis: society-wide deception (vv.2-6). No one can trust any neighbor — "every brother is a supplanter" (v.4, wordplay: "every brother is a Jacob/deceiver"). The famous conclusion (vv.23-24) is quoted by Paul (1 Cor 1:31; 2 Cor 10:17): don't boast in wisdom, might, or riches — boast in knowing GOD.
A Fountain of Tears, and a Lodge in the Wilderness (vv.1-2): Oh that my head were waters, and mine eyes a fountain of tears, that I might weep day and night for the slain of the daughter of my people. That verse is the reason he is called the weeping prophet. The verse immediately after it is quoted far less often and belongs with it, oh that I had in the wilderness a lodging place of wayfaring men, that I might leave my people, and go from them. Grief for them and the wish to be away from them, in consecutive verses, with neither canceling the other. The book records both without apologizing for the second.
Take Heed of Thy Neighbour (vv.3-6): They bend their tongues like their bow for lies. What is described here is not the collapse of national religion but the collapse of ordinary trust, and the advice given is correspondingly bleak, take ye heed every one of his neighbour, and trust ye not in any brother, for every brother will utterly supplant, and every neighbour will walk with slanders. The verb in the next line is the one that matters, they have taught their tongues to speak lies, so this is a trained skill and an inherited one, and weary themselves to commit iniquity.
I Will Melt Them (vv.7-9): Behold, I will melt them, and try them, for how shall I do for the daughter of my people. The middle clause is a question about method, not a threat, and it reads as genuine difficulty. Then the speech again, their tongue is as an arrow shot out, one speaketh peaceably to his neighbour with his mouth, but in heart he layeth his wait. And the chapter's refrain, shall I not visit them for these things.
A Lamentation for the Pastures (vv.10-11): For the mountains will I take up a weeping and wailing, and for the habitations of the wilderness a lamentation, because they are burned up, so that none can pass through them. The loss is measured by a sound that has stopped, neither can men hear the voice of the cattle, both the fowl of the heavens and the beast are fled. And the city with them, I will make Jerusalem heaps, and a den of dragons.
Why Is the Land Desolate (vv.12-16): Who is the wise man, that may understand this, and who is he to whom the mouth of the LORD hath spoken, that he may declare it, for what the land perisheth. The question is asked of the wise and then answered by God instead, because they have forsaken my law which I set before them, and have walked after the imagination of their own heart. The sentence uses two bitter substances for what is normally hospitality, I will feed them with wormwood, and give them water of gall to drink, and ends with the scattering, I will scatter them also among the heathen.
Call for the Mourning Women (vv.17-22): Call for the mourning women, that they may come, and let them make haste, and take up a wailing for us. Professional mourners were a recognized trade, and sending for them says that the grief has outrun what a family can produce on its own. The reason is then given in a sentence that makes the danger domestic, for death is come up into our windows, and is entered into our palaces, to cut off the children from without, and the young men from the streets. And the closing image is agricultural and careless, they shall fall as the handful after the harvestman, and none shall gather them.
Let Not the Wise Man Glory (vv.23-24): Let not the wise man glory in his wisdom, neither let the mighty man glory in his might, let not the rich man glory in his riches. Three of the four things anyone in that world could be proud of are named and removed, and the fourth is substituted, but let him that glorieth glory in this, that he understandeth and knoweth me. And what is to be known is specified rather than left abstract, that I am the LORD which exercise lovingkindness, judgment, and righteousness, in the earth, for in these things I delight. Paul quotes this passage twice, at 1 Corinthians 1 and 2 Corinthians 10.
Circumcised and Yet Uncircumcised (vv.25-26): I will punish all them which are circumcised with the uncircumcised, and then the list, which is the argument: Egypt, and Judah, and Edom, and the children of Ammon, and Moab, and all that are in the utmost corners. Judah is placed in a list of neighboring nations that also practised circumcision, which shows the mark to be shared and therefore not by itself a distinction. The conclusion is stated in a sentence that only works because of the paradox in it, for all these nations are uncircumcised, and all the house of Israel are uncircumcised in the heart.

Map & Geography

  • Jerusalem (v.11): Capital of Judah; the holy city where the Temple stood.
  • Zion (v.19): The hill on which Jerusalem/the Temple stood; often used poetically for God's dwelling.
  • Egypt (v.26): Land to the southwest; place of Israel's bondage and exodus.
  • Edom (v.26): Land south of the Dead Sea; descendants of Esau.
  • Moab (v.26): Land east of the Dead Sea; often in conflict with Israel.

Setting: Jerusalem, early in the reign of Jehoiakim (609–598 BC), continuing the poems that follow Jeremiah's temple sermon (chs 7–10). Chapter 8 ended with Jeremiah broken by his people's wound and asking, "Is there no balm in Gilead?" Chapter 9 picks up mid-thought. In the Hebrew Bible its first verse is the last verse of chapter 8. Jeremiah wishes he could weep without stopping, then wishes he could run away. God explains why he must judge a nation where no one can trust even a brother. The professional mourners are called, and death climbs in through the windows. Then, near the end, comes one of the great sayings of the Old Testament: let no one boast in wisdom, might or riches, but in knowing the LORD.

Jeremiah Grieves for His People (vv1–26)

  • "Oh that my head were waters, and my eyes a fountain of tears" (v1). The ESV section began at 8:18 and runs through this whole chapter. This verse is 8:23 in Hebrew (ESV footnote), so it belongs with the cry about the balm of Gilead. Jeremiah's tears are not enough. He wishes his head were a spring that never ran dry, so he could weep "day and night for the slain of the daughter of my people." The slain are not yet dead, but he mourns them ahead of time. This verse and 13:17 and 14:17 are why he is called "the weeping prophet." Lamentations, traditionally linked with him, carries the same grief (Lam 1:16; 2:11).
  • "Oh that I had in the desert a travelers' lodging place" (v2). A lodging place was a bare shelter on a desert road, a walled yard where caravans stopped for the night. It was lonely and uncomfortable, and Jeremiah would rather be there than in Jerusalem. David once wished for the same: "Oh, that I had wings like a dove! I would fly away and be at rest" (Ps 55:6–7). The prophet who weeps for his people in v1 wants to escape them in v2. Both feelings are honest, and God does not scold him for either.
  • "They are all adulterers, a company of treacherous men" (v2). Adultery here is both literal (5:7–8) and spiritual (3:8–9). "Treacherous" is the same word used for faithless Judah in chapter 3.
  • "They bend their tongue like a bow" (v3). An archer bent his bow by standing on it to string it. Their tongues are strung and aimed, and lies are the arrows (v8; Ps 64:3). "Falsehood and not truth has grown strong in the land." The KJV's "they are not valiant for the truth" is a famous rendering. The Hebrew says that lies, not truth, have become powerful.
  • "They proceed from evil to evil, and they do not know me" (v3). Sin leads to more sin. The root problem is that they do not know God. To "know" God in Hebrew means more than knowing facts about him. It means a personal, loyal relationship that shows in how one lives (22:15–16; Hos 4:1).
  • "Put no trust in any brother, for every brother is a deceiver" (v4). In Hebrew, kol ach ʿaqov yaʿqov: literally, "every brother will surely Jacob." Jacob's name sounds like the word "heel" and "supplant," because he grabbed his brother's heel at birth and later cheated Esau out of his birthright and blessing (Gen 25:26; 27:36). The whole "house of Jacob" lives up to its ancestor's worst side. Micah describes the same breakdown: "a man's enemies are the men of his own house" (Mic 7:5–6), words Jesus quoted (Matt 10:35–36).
  • "Every neighbor goes about as a slanderer" (v4). The slanderer was the talebearer the law forbade (Lev 19:16), carrying gossip from house to house.
  • "They have taught their tongue to speak lies; they weary themselves committing iniquity" (v5). Lying has become a trained skill. And sin is hard work. They exhaust themselves doing wrong.
  • "Heaping oppression upon oppression, and deceit upon deceit" (v6). The Hebrew is very compressed, literally "your dwelling is in the midst of deceit." Jeremiah lives surrounded by it. "They refuse to know me." Not knowing God is a choice, not an accident.
  • "I will refine them and test them, for what else can I do, because of my people?" (v7). The refining picture of 6:27–30. Fire melts the ore to separate silver from dross. "Because of my people" can also be read "because of the daughter of my people," which would make the line tender: what else can a father do for his child?
  • "With his mouth each speaks peace to his neighbor, but in his heart he plans an ambush for him" (v8). "Peace" is shalom, the ordinary greeting. They greet each other with a blessing while planning to destroy each other, like Joab greeting Amasa as he stabbed him (2 Sam 20:9–10) or Judas greeting Jesus with a kiss.
  • "Shall I not punish them for these things?" (v9). The refrain from 5:9 and 5:29, a third time.
  • "I will take up weeping and wailing for the mountains" (v10). The Hebrew says "I," and it is not clear whether God or Jeremiah speaks. The Greek Septuagint reads "Take up," a command. If God speaks, he mourns his own judgment. Either way the grief is for the land itself: hills and pastures where no shepherd passes, no cattle low, and even the birds have fled. In 4:25 Jeremiah saw the same in vision.
  • "I will make Jerusalem a heap of ruins, a lair of jackals" (v11). Jackals haunted ruins and abandoned places (Isa 13:22; Lam 5:18). The KJV's "dragons" comes from an older understanding of the Hebrew tannim. It means jackals.
  • "Who is the man so wise that he can understand this?" (v12). The question looks ahead to v23. The "wise" of 8:8–9 have no answer. God gives the answer himself.
  • "Because they have forsaken my law… and have stubbornly followed their own hearts and have gone after the Baals, as their fathers taught them" (vv13–14). Three steps: they left God's law, followed their own hearts, and served the Baals. "As their fathers taught them" shows how idolatry spread: parents passed it on, as the families baked cakes together for the queen of heaven (7:18).
  • "I will feed this people with bitter food, and give them poisonous water to drink" (v15). "Bitter food" is literally "wormwood," a bitter plant. The "poisonous water" is the water of rosh from 8:14. Deuteronomy 29:18 warned against "a root bearing poisonous and bitter fruit" in Israel, the very same two words. The idolatry they planted will become their food. Revelation 8:11 uses wormwood for bitter judgment.
  • "I will scatter them among the nations… and I will send the sword after them" (v16). The exile, as Leviticus 26:33 and Deuteronomy 28:64 had warned. "Until I have consumed them" stands beside the earlier promise "I will not make a full end" (5:18). The sword follows the rebels, but a remnant will survive.
  • "Call for the mourning women… send for the skillful women" (v17). Professional mourners were part of every funeral in the ancient Near East, and the custom continues in parts of the Middle East today. Women trained in the art of lament were hired to wail, sing dirges and lead the family in grief (2 Chr 35:25; Amos 5:16; Mark 5:38). Their "skill" is the same word used for wisdom. These are the "wise women" of grief, set beside the false wise men of 8:8.
  • "That our eyes may run down with tears" (v18). The mourners are to help the people weep, the tears Jeremiah wished for in v1.
  • "How we are ruined! We are utterly shamed, because we have left the land" (v19). The dirge is sung as if the exile has already happened. Their homes have been thrown down.
  • "Teach to your daughters a lament, and each to her neighbor a dirge" (v20). So many will die that there will not be enough professional mourners. Every woman will need to learn the songs of grief and teach them to the next generation. The fathers taught Baal worship (v14). Now the mothers must teach lament.
  • "Death has come up into our windows; it has entered our palaces" (v21). Death is pictured as a thief or a raider climbing in through the windows when the doors are barred. Some scholars note that Canaanite stories pictured the death god Mot entering houses, and a Ugaritic poem speaks of not putting a window in Baal's palace, perhaps to keep death out. Whether or not Jeremiah had that in mind, the point is plain: walls and locks cannot keep death out. "Cutting off the children from the streets and the young men from the squares." The places full of life (Zech 8:5) are emptied.
  • "Like sheaves after the reaper, and none shall gather them" (v22). A reaper cut grain with a sickle and left the stalks lying in a row behind him, to be bundled later. Here the bodies lie like that, but no one comes to gather them.
  • "Let not the wise man boast in his wisdom, let not the mighty man boast in his might, let not the rich man boast in his riches" (v23). Three things people trust: brains, strength and money. In Judah they were the counselors and scribes, the soldiers and their fortified cities, and the wealthy who grew "fat and sleek" (5:27–28). All three will fail in the day of judgment. "Boast" is halal, to praise, glory or take pride in, the same root as "hallelujah."
  • "But let him who boasts boast in this, that he understands and knows me" (v24). The one thing worth boasting in is knowing God. That is the cure for the people who "do not know me" (vv3, 6).
  • "I am the LORD who practices steadfast love, justice, and righteousness in the earth. For in these things I delight" (v24). Three words describe God's character. Chesed is loyal, covenant love. Mishpat is justice, setting things right. Tsedaqah is righteousness, doing what is right in relationships. These are exactly what Judah lacked: deceit instead of loyalty, oppression instead of justice. To know God is to love what he loves. Paul quotes this verse twice, "Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord" (1 Cor 1:31; 2 Cor 10:17), against people proud of their wisdom or their spiritual achievements.
  • "I will punish all those who are circumcised merely in the flesh" (v25). Circumcision was Israel's covenant sign, and Judah trusted it as it trusted the temple. But other peoples practiced circumcision too.
  • "Egypt, Judah, Edom, the sons of Ammon, Moab, and all who dwell in the desert who cut the corners of their hair" (v26). Egyptian priests and many neighboring peoples were circumcised. The desert tribes who "cut the corners of their hair" were Arab peoples who shaved the hair at their temples, a practice Israel was forbidden (Lev 19:27). Herodotus also mentions it. Judah is placed in the middle of the list, between Egypt and Edom, as one more nation. That is the sting.
  • "All these nations are uncircumcised, and all the house of Israel are uncircumcised in heart" (v26). The outward mark means nothing without the inward reality (4:4; Deut 10:16). Paul builds his argument in Romans 2:25–29 on this: "circumcision is a matter of the heart."
  • Commentators: Henry sums up v1: "Jeremiah wept much, and yet wished he could weep more." JFB agrees that the verse "is more fitly joined to the last chapter," and identifies the lodging place of v2 as a caravanserai in the desert. On v3 Henry, using the KJV, says, "Those that will be faithful to the truth must be valiant for it." JFB explains that "deceiver" in v4 means to "trip up by the heel," and Guzik notes (citing Harrison) the play on Jacob's name. On v5 Henry observes, "They are wearied with their sinful pursuits and yet not weary of them." JFB says the KJV's "dragons" in v11 are jackals, and describes the mourning women who wailed and beat their breasts with hair let loose. On v21 Henry says simply, "No palaces can keep out death," while JFB pictures soldiers climbing through windows when the doors are shut. Guzik cites Kidner's contrast between the fading glories of v23 and the lasting glory of v24. On v26 Henry presses the point to his own readers: "That only is circumcision, and that baptism, which is of the heart." JFB adds that Egypt heads the list to shame Judah, which trusted Egypt.

Pointing to Christ

Jeremiah wished for a fountain of tears for his people. Jesus wept at the tomb of his friend and over the city that would kill him (John 11:35; Luke 19:41). In a land where "every brother is a Jacob," he was the brother who did not deceive, "and no deceit was found in his mouth" (1 Pet 2:22). Jeremiah said to boast only in knowing the LORD who practices steadfast love, justice and righteousness. Paul says Christ "became to us wisdom from God, righteousness and sanctification and redemption," so that "the one who boasts" may "boast in the Lord" (1 Cor 1:30–31). And the circumcision of the heart that Judah never had is given in him: "in him also you were circumcised with a circumcision made without hands" (Col 2:11).

Reflection

  • The Weeping Prophet (v.1) — Jeremiah wishes his head were a spring of water so he could weep enough for what's coming on his people. Do you let the brokenness around you move you to genuine grief, or do you stay detached?
  • A Tongue Trained to Deceive (v.5) — The people have taught their tongues to speak lies and wearied themselves committing iniquity. Are the words you speak daily training you toward truth or toward deception?
  • Three False Sources of Glory (v.23) — God tells the wise, the mighty, and the rich not to boast in their wisdom, strength, or wealth. Which of these are you quietly leaning on for your sense of worth instead of God?
  • Knowing God as the Only Real Boast (v.24) — God says the one who boasts should boast that he understands and knows God, who delights in lovingkindness, justice, and righteousness. Is your relationship with God more head-knowledge or genuine, personal knowing?
  • Circumcised in Flesh, Not in Heart (v.26) — God lists Judah among the nations who are circumcised outwardly yet uncircumcised in heart. Does your faith show up more in outward markers than in an actual changed heart?
