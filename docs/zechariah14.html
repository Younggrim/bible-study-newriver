1Behold, a day of the LORD is coming when your plunder will be divided in your presence.

2For I will gather all the nations for battle against Jerusalem, and the city will be captured, the houses looted, and the women ravished. Half of the city will go into exile, but the rest of the people will not be removed from the city.

3Then the LORD will go out to fight against those nations, as He fights in the day of battle.

4On that day His feet will stand on the Mount of Olives, east of Jerusalem, and the Mount of Olives will be split in two from east to west, forming a great valley, with half the mountain moving to the north and half to the south.

5You will flee by My mountain valley, for it will extend to Azal. You will flee as you fled from the earthquake in the days of Uzziah king of Judah. Then the LORD my God will come, and all the holy ones with Him.

6On that day there will be no light, no cold or frost.

7It will be a unique day known only to the LORD, without day or night; but when evening comes, there will be light.

8And on that day living water will flow out from Jerusalem, half of it toward the Eastern Sea and the other half toward the Western Sea, in summer and winter alike.

9On that day the LORD will become King over all the earth—the LORD alone, and His name alone.

10All the land from Geba to Rimmon south of Jerusalem will be turned into a plain, but Jerusalem will be raised up and will remain in her place, from the Benjamin Gate to the site of the First Gate to the Corner Gate, and from the Tower of Hananel to the royal winepresses.

11People will live there, and never again will there be an utter destruction. So Jerusalem will dwell securely.

12And this will be the plague with which the LORD strikes all the peoples who have warred against Jerusalem: Their flesh will rot while they stand on their feet, their eyes will rot in their sockets, and their tongues will rot in their mouths.

13On that day a great panic from the LORD will come upon them, so that each will seize the hand of another, and the hand of one will rise against the other.

14Judah will also fight at Jerusalem, and the wealth of all the surrounding nations will be collected—gold, silver, and apparel in great abundance.

15And a similar plague will strike the horses and mules, camels and donkeys, and all the animals in those camps.

16Then all the survivors from the nations that came against Jerusalem will go up year after year to worship the King, the LORD of Hosts, and to celebrate the Feast of Tabernacles.

17And should any of the families of the earth not go up to Jerusalem to worship the King, the LORD of Hosts, then the rain will not fall on them.

18And if the people of Egypt will not go up and enter in, then the rain will not fall on them; this will be the plague with which the LORD strikes the nations who do not go up to celebrate the Feast of Tabernacles.

19This will be the punishment of Egypt and of all the nations that do not go up to celebrate the Feast of Tabernacles.

20On that day, HOLY TO THE LORD will be inscribed on the bells of the horses, and the cooking pots in the house of the LORD will be like the sprinkling bowls before the altar.

21Indeed, every pot in Jerusalem and Judah will be holy to the LORD of Hosts, and all who sacrifice will come and take some pots and cook in them. And on that day there will no longer be a Canaanite in the house of the LORD of Hosts.

Summary

The Day of the Lord brings a final siege of Jerusalem in which the city is plundered and half its people taken captive, but then the Lord himself goes out to fight for the city, his feet splitting the Mount of Olives in two and opening a valley of escape (vv. 1-5). That day will be unlike any other, ending in light at evening, with living water flowing from Jerusalem toward both seas as the Lord becomes king over all the earth (vv. 6-9). Jerusalem will be raised up and secure, while the attacking nations are struck with a horrifying plague and thrown into panic against each other, their wealth left for Judah to gather (vv. 10-15). Survivors from every nation will then go up yearly to worship the King and keep the Feast of Tabernacles, with drought or plague striking any nation that refuses (vv. 16-19). Finally, everything in Jerusalem, down to the bells on the horses and the ordinary cooking pots, will be made holy to the Lord (vv. 20-21).

Authorship & Background

Author: Zechariah son of Berechiah. Prophesied 520-480 BC. Contemporary with Haggai. Key theme: Messianic hope and the coming kingdom. The most Messianic of the Minor Prophets. Famous passages: the Branch (3:8, 6:12), "Not by might... but by my spirit" (4:6), the triumphal entry (9:9), "they shall look upon me whom they have pierced" (12:10), the shepherd struck (13:7), and the LORD's return to the Mount of Olives (14:4).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — The Day of the LORD: The Return to the Mount of Olives and Universal Kingdom
Key Themes: The final siege of Jerusalem, the LORD's physical return to earth, the Mount of Olives split, living waters flowing from Jerusalem, the LORD as King over all the earth, universal holiness, the nations worshipping God
Historical Context: Zechariah 14 is the grand finale — not only of this book but of the entire prophetic vision of the Old Testament. It describes "the day of the LORD" in its ultimate fulfillment: all nations gathered against Jerusalem, the city taken, and then — the LORD Himself comes. His feet touch the Mount of Olives (the same mountain from which Jesus ascended in Acts 1:11-12). The mountain splits. Living waters flow. And the LORD becomes "king over all the earth."
The Nations Gathered and the City Taken (vv.1-2): Behold, the day of the LORD cometh, and the chapter begins with the city losing. I will gather all nations against Jerusalem to battle, and the city shall be taken, and the houses rifled, and the women ravished, and half of the city shall go forth into captivity. Nothing is softened, and the gathering is credited to God rather than to the nations' ambition. Then the clause the rest of the chapter grows from, and the residue of the people shall not be cut off from the city. Half taken, and a remainder left.
The LORD Goes Forth and His Feet on the Mount of Olives (vv.3-5): Then shall the LORD go forth, and fight against those nations, as when he fought in the day of battle. And his feet shall stand in that day upon the mount of Olives, which is before Jerusalem on the east, and the mount of Olives shall cleave in the midst thereof. The location is specific, the same ridge from which Acts 1 records the ascension and the promise of a return in like manner. The mountain splits east and west and makes a valley to escape through, and the escape is compared to a remembered earthquake in the days of Uzziah, and the LORD my God shall come, and all the saints with thee.
A Day Neither Day Nor Night, and Living Waters (vv.6-8): It shall come to pass in that day, that the light shall not be clear, nor dark, but it shall be one day which shall be known to the LORD, not day, nor night, but at evening time it shall be light. The ordinary division of time stops working, and the light arrives at the hour it normally leaves. Then the water, and it shall be in that day, that living waters shall go out from Jerusalem, half of them toward the former sea, and half toward the hinder sea, in summer and in winter shall it be. Jerusalem sits on a ridge with no river, and the two seas named are the Dead Sea and the Mediterranean.
The LORD King Over All the Earth (v.9): And the LORD shall be king over all the earth, in that day shall there be one LORD, and his name one. The whole book has been arguing toward this sentence, and it is put in the plainest terms available. The last clause takes up the confession of Deuteronomy 6, the Shema, and treats it as something that becomes visible rather than something recited.
The Land Made Plain and Jerusalem Safely Inhabited (vv.10-11): All the land shall be turned as a plain, and the city lifted up and inhabited in her place, described by its own landmarks, from Benjamin's gate to the place of the first gate, from the tower of Hananel unto the king's winepresses. The topography around Jerusalem is flattened and the city alone is raised, which reverses the ordinary geography of a hill town in a range of hills. Then the sentence that answers chapter 2's vision of a city without walls, and there shall be no more utter destruction, but Jerusalem shall be safely inhabited.
The Plague on the Enemies (vv.12-15): This shall be the plague wherewith the LORD will smite all the people that have fought against Jerusalem, their flesh shall consume away while they stand upon their feet. What follows is not battle but collapse from within, a great tumult from the LORD among them, and they shall lay hold every one on the hand of his neighbor, and his hand shall rise up against the hand of his neighbor. The army destroys itself, the wealth is collected, and the plague is extended to the animals in the camp, the horse, the mule, the camel and the ass.
The Nations Keep the Feast of Tabernacles (vv.16-19): Every one that is left of all the nations shall even go up from year to year to worship the King, the LORD of hosts, and to keep the feast of tabernacles. Of the three annual feasts this is the one that required living in booths, remembering a nation with no houses and no land, and it is the one the nations are given to keep. The sanction is rain, which is the ordinary business of Baal in the earlier prophets, upon them shall be no rain, and Egypt is named specifically, a country whose water came from a river rather than the sky.
Holiness on the Bells of the Horses (vv.20-21): In that day shall there be upon the bells of the horses, HOLINESS unto the LORD. The engraving from the high priest's turban, the plate of gold in Exodus 39, ends up on harness fittings. And the pots in the LORD's house shall be like the bowls before the altar, so the ordinary cooking pot is levelled up to the sacred vessel rather than the sacred vessel brought down. Every pot in Jerusalem becomes usable for sacrifice, and the last line of the book removes the one thing that would make that impossible, there shall be no more the Canaanite in the house of the LORD of hosts.

Deep Dive

Setting: This is the last chapter of Zechariah and the end of the second "oracle" (12:1). Like chapter 12, it begins with all nations gathered against Jerusalem. This time the picture is darker at the start and brighter at the end. The city falls and half its people go into exile. Then the LORD himself comes out to fight, stands on the Mount of Olives, splits it in two, and becomes "king over all the earth." Living water flows from Jerusalem, the nations come up every year to worship, and holiness reaches even horse bells and cooking pots. Zechariah wrote to a small, struggling community in a minor Persian province, probably in the early 400s BC. The chapter told them that the story of Jerusalem would end not with their weakness but with God's kingdom filling the world. Christians read it in different ways. Many premillennial interpreters take it literally, of Christ's return to the Mount of Olives and an earthly reign from Jerusalem. Many amillennial and Reformed interpreters read it as a prophetic picture of the gospel age and the new creation, using the images of Israel's worship to describe a world made holy. Most agree that its center is the coming of the LORD as king. In synagogues this chapter is read on the first day of the Feast of Booths, the feast it describes in v16. The ESV gives the whole chapter one heading.

The Coming Day of the LORD (vv1–21)

  • "Behold, a day is coming for the LORD" (v1). The "day of the LORD" is the prophets' phrase for the time when God steps into history to judge and to save (Joel 2:1–2, 31; Amos 5:18–20; Mal 4:5). It is "for the LORD": his day, not man's.
  • "The spoil taken from you will be divided in your midst" (v1). The enemy is so secure that it divides the plunder inside the captured city. The chapter begins with total humiliation, and it will end with the reverse (v14).
  • "I will gather all the nations against Jerusalem to battle, and the city shall be taken" (v2). God himself gathers the nations, as he gathered Babylon against Jerusalem in 586 BC. The horrors of ancient conquest are named without softening: houses plundered, women raped. Joel 3:2 and Revelation 16:14–16 also picture the nations gathered for a final battle. "Half of the city shall go out into exile, but the rest of the people shall not be cut off": even in disaster a remnant remains, echoing the third left alive in 13:8.
  • Which siege? Some interpreters see the Roman destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, when the city was taken and its people killed or enslaved. Others point out that in AD 70 no divine rescue followed, and so they look to a final assault at the end of the age. Others again read the whole as a symbolic picture of the world's hostility to God's people.
  • "Then the LORD will go out and fight against those nations as when he fights on a day of battle" (v3). The LORD is the warrior, as at the Red Sea: "The LORD will fight for you, and you have only to be silent" (Exod 14:14; 15:3).
  • "On that day his feet shall stand on the Mount of Olives that lies before Jerusalem on the east" (v4). The Mount of Olives is the ridge just east of the city across the Kidron Valley, about 300 feet higher than the temple mount. Ezekiel saw God's glory leave the temple and stop "on the mountain that is on the east side of the city" (Ezek 11:23), and later return from the east (Ezek 43:1–5). Here the LORD's own feet stand there. In the Gospels, Jesus rode down this mountain into the city (Luke 19:37), wept over Jerusalem from it, gave his teaching about the end on it (Matt 24:3), prayed at its foot in Gethsemane, and ascended from it. The angels told the disciples he would "come in the same way as you saw him go into heaven," and Luke notes that they then returned "from the mount called Olivet" (Acts 1:11–12).
  • "The Mount of Olives shall be split in two from east to west by a very wide valley" (v4). When God comes, the earth shakes (Judg 5:4–5; Mic 1:3–4; Nah 1:5). The mountain splits and opens a road. A geological fault does run through the area, which some note with interest, but the point is God's coming, not geology.
  • "You shall flee to the valley of my mountains, for the valley of the mountains shall reach to Azal" (v5). The new valley becomes an escape route for the people. Azal is unknown. It may be a place east of the city (Beth-ezel in Micah 1:11 is sometimes suggested). Some scholars read the word as 'etsel, "beside it," meaning the valley reaches right up to the city.
  • "As you fled from the earthquake in the days of Uzziah king of Judah" (v5). This earthquake, around 760 BC, was so remembered that Amos dated his prophecy from it: "two years before the earthquake" (Amos 1:1). Archaeologists have found signs of a major earthquake in that period at Hazor and other sites. Two and a half centuries later, Zechariah's hearers still knew of it.
  • "Then the LORD my God will come, and all the holy ones with him" (v5). The "holy ones" are angels, God's heavenly army (Deut 33:2; Ps 89:5–7), and in the New Testament they are joined by his people. Paul speaks of "the coming of our Lord Jesus with all his saints" (1 Thess 3:13), and Jude quotes Enoch: "the Lord comes with ten thousands of his holy ones" (Jude 14).
  • "On that day there shall be no light, cold, or frost" (v6). The Hebrew is difficult. The traditional Hebrew text can be read "there shall be no light; the splendid ones (the stars) shall shrink" or "congeal." The ESV follows a reading supported by the ancient translations: neither cold nor frost. Either way the ordinary order of nature is suspended, as the prophets describe the day of the LORD (Joel 2:10, 31; Isa 13:10).
  • "A unique day, which is known to the LORD, neither day nor night, but at evening time there shall be light" (v7). "Unique" is literally "one day," a day unlike any other. Jesus said of his return, "concerning that day and hour no one knows… but the Father only" (Matt 24:36). When evening should bring darkness, it brings light. Isaiah promised, "the LORD will be your everlasting light" (Isa 60:19–20), and in the new Jerusalem "there will be no night there" (Rev 21:23–25; 22:5).
  • "Living waters shall flow out from Jerusalem, half of them to the eastern sea and half of them to the western sea" (v8). Jerusalem has no river, only the Gihon spring. Here living, running water flows from it in both directions: east to the Dead Sea and west to the Mediterranean. Ezekiel saw a river from the temple that made even the Dead Sea fresh (Ezek 47:1–12), and Joel saw "a fountain… from the house of the LORD" (Joel 3:18). "It shall continue in summer as in winter": most streams in Israel dried up in the summer. This one never fails. It follows the fountain opened in 13:1.
  • "The LORD will be king over all the earth" (v9). The center of the chapter. The psalms sang "The LORD reigns" (Ps 93:1; 97:1; 99:1). Now his reign is acknowledged everywhere. "On that day the LORD will be one and his name one." This echoes the Shema, Israel's great confession: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deut 6:4). What Israel confessed, the whole world will acknowledge. No other gods will be named (13:2).
  • "The whole land shall be turned into a plain from Geba to Rimmon south of Jerusalem" (v10). Geba lay about six miles north-east of Jerusalem, the traditional northern edge of Judah (2 Kgs 23:8). Rimmon (En-rimmon) lay about thirty-five miles south-west, near Beersheba (Neh 11:29). The hill country of Judah is leveled into a plain.
  • "But Jerusalem shall remain aloft on its site" (v10). While the land around it is lowered, Jerusalem is raised high. Isaiah and Micah promised that "the mountain of the house of the LORD shall be established as the highest of the mountains" (Isa 2:2; Mic 4:1). The landmarks trace the city's boundaries: the Gate of Benjamin on the north (Jer 37:13), the Corner Gate on the north-west (2 Kgs 14:13), the Tower of Hananel on the north wall (Neh 3:1; Jer 31:38) and the king's winepresses, probably in the royal gardens to the south. The whole city is restored.
  • "There shall never again be a decree of utter destruction" (v11). The word cherem means something devoted to destruction, put under God's ban, as Jericho was (Josh 6:17). Jerusalem had come under that curse in its fall (Isa 43:28; Mal 4:6). Now it never will again. Revelation uses the same idea of the new Jerusalem: "No longer will there be anything accursed" (Rev 22:3). "Jerusalem shall dwell in security."
  • "Their flesh will rot while they are still standing on their feet" (v12). The judgment on the attackers is terrible and sudden. It recalls the night the angel of the LORD struck down Sennacherib's army outside Jerusalem (2 Kgs 19:35). The picture is meant to show that fighting against God destroys a person from within. Some modern readers have compared it to the effects of nuclear weapons. The text simply calls it "the plague with which the LORD will strike."
  • "A great panic from the LORD shall fall on them, so that each will seize the hand of another" (v13). In panic, enemies turn their weapons on each other, as with Gideon's Midianites (Judg 7:22) and the armies Jehoshaphat faced (2 Chr 20:23).
  • "Even Judah will fight at Jerusalem. And the wealth of all the surrounding nations shall be collected" (v14). The plunder of v1 is reversed. The spoil taken from Jerusalem returns many times over, "gold, silver, and garments in great abundance." Haggai promised that "the treasures of all nations shall come in" to God's house (Hag 2:7).
  • "A plague like this plague shall fall on the horses, the mules, the camels, the donkeys" (v15). The enemy's whole war machine, including its pack animals, is struck.
  • "Everyone who survives of all the nations… shall go up year after year to worship the King, the LORD of hosts" (v16). The most surprising turn in the chapter. The nations who came to destroy Jerusalem now come to worship. This is the hope of 8:20–23 and Isaiah 2:2–4 fulfilled. "Go up" is the regular phrase for pilgrimage to Jerusalem, which sat on high ground (Ps 122:1–4).
  • "To keep the Feast of Booths" (v16). The Feast of Booths, or Tabernacles (Sukkot), came in the autumn after the harvest. For seven days Israel lived in shelters made of branches to remember God's care in the wilderness (Lev 23:33–43; Deut 16:13–15). It was the most joyful of the feasts, a thanksgiving for harvest. It was also the feast at which Solomon dedicated the first temple (1 Kgs 8:2) and at which the returned exiles first rebuilt the altar (Ezra 3:4). Why this feast? It celebrated God dwelling among a pilgrim people and gave thanks for the ingathering, which fits the ingathering of the nations.
  • "If any of the families of the earth do not go up… there will be no rain on them" (v17). The Feast of Booths was also when Israel prayed for the autumn rains. Later Jewish practice included a water-pouring ceremony at the altar each day of the feast. To refuse the King is to cut oneself off from the source of life (10:1).
  • "If the family of Egypt does not go up… there shall be the plague" (v18). Egypt depended on the Nile, not rain, so it might think itself safe. It is warned that it will not escape. The Hebrew of v18 is compressed, and the ESV supplies the sense. "This shall be the punishment to Egypt" (v19): the word chatta't means both "sin" and the "penalty for sin."
  • "There shall be inscribed on the bells of the horses, 'Holy to the LORD'" (v20). These words were engraved on the gold plate on the high priest's turban (Exod 28:36–38). Now they are on the bells of horses, ordinary animals and once instruments of war (9:10). What was once reserved for the holiest person in Israel is now on everyday things.
  • "The pots in the house of the LORD shall be as the bowls before the altar" (v20). The common cooking pots in the temple's kitchens will be as holy as the sacred bowls that held sacrificial blood.
  • "Every pot in Jerusalem and Judah shall be holy to the LORD of hosts" (v21). Holiness spreads from the temple to every kitchen in the land. The line between sacred and common disappears, not because the holy has become common but because everything has become holy. Worshipers can use any pot to cook their sacrificial meals. Israel was called to be "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exod 19:6), and here that calling is complete.
  • "There shall no longer be a trader in the house of the LORD of hosts" (v21). The Hebrew is kena'ani, "Canaanite." Because the Canaanites and Phoenicians were known as merchants, the word came to mean "trader" (Prov 31:24; Zeph 1:11), which the ESV follows. If "Canaanite" is meant, it points to the unclean and ungodly, now excluded. If "trader," it points to those who made money off worship. Jesus drove the traders out of the temple and said, "do not make my Father's house a house of trade" (John 2:16). Either way the book ends with God's house purified.
  • Commentators: Henry admits that "what that day is that is here meant is uncertain," and reads much of the chapter as gospel times. He says the LORD's feet on the Mount of Olives were in a sense "literally fulfilled when our Lord Jesus was often upon this mountain," and takes the splitting of the mountain as the removal of the barrier between Jews and Gentiles. On v7 he writes that deliverance often comes "when our hopes are quite spent with waiting all day to no purpose," and on v16, "The life of a good Christian is a constant feast of tabernacles." JFB reads the siege and the return literally and future, places Azal near a gate east of the city, and calls the living waters "neither dried up by heat, nor frozen by cold; ever flowing." Guzik also takes the chapter literally of Christ's return and millennial reign, and argues that AD 70 cannot be meant because no divine rescue followed. He sees the "Holy to the LORD" inscription as God making ordinary things holy, not the holy ordinary. The Tyndale notes point out that the Feast of Booths was a harvest thanksgiving and that "Canaanite" may stand for ethnic boundaries removed or for trade in God's house.

Pointing to Christ

At the Feast of Booths, whose daily ceremonies by then included pouring out water at the altar, Jesus stood up on the last and greatest day and cried out, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink. Whoever believes in me… 'Out of his heart will flow rivers of living water'" (John 7:37–38). John explains that he was speaking of the Spirit. The living water of Zechariah 14 flows from Jesus. He came down the Mount of Olives as a humble king, prayed at its foot in agony, and rose from it to heaven, and the same Jesus will come again. He drove the traders from his Father's house, and he is making a people in whom everything, down to the cooking pots, is "Holy to the LORD." When the nations come up to worship "the King, the LORD of hosts," they will be worshiping him, for "at the name of Jesus every knee should bow" (Phil 2:10). Zechariah ends where the whole Bible ends: "the throne of God and of the Lamb will be in it… night will be no more… and they will reign forever and ever" (Rev 22:3–5).

Reflection

  • A Physical, Visible Return (v.4) — The Lord's feet will literally stand on the Mount of Olives, the same mountain from which He ascended. Does the promise of Christ's bodily, visible return actually shape how you live day to day, or does it feel distant and abstract?
  • Living Waters in Every Season (v.8) — Living waters flow out from Jerusalem continuously, in both summer and winter, without interruption. Are you staying close enough to God to be refreshed by Him in every season of your life, not just the easy ones?
  • The Lord as King Over All the Earth (v.9) — The chapter declares that the Lord will be king over the whole earth, with every rival authority ultimately subject to Him. When the world around you feels chaotic or uncertain, does this settled outcome shape how you respond?
  • Worship as a Choice With Consequences (vv.16-17) — Nations are pictured as choosing whether to come worship the King, with real consequences for refusing. Are you worshiping God out of genuine willingness, or just going through the motions?
  • Holiness Extended to All of Life (v.20) — Even ordinary items like the bells on horses will bear the inscription "Holy to the Lord," erasing any line between sacred and secular. Is there an area of your everyday life — work, routines, hobbies — you've been treating as separate from your faith rather than part of your worship?
