1At the end of every seven years you must cancel debts.

2This is the manner of remission: Every creditor shall cancel what he has loaned to his neighbor. He is not to collect anything from his neighbor or brother, because the LORD’s time of release has been proclaimed.

3You may collect something from a foreigner, but you must forgive whatever your brother owes you.

4There will be no poor among you, however, because the LORD will surely bless you in the land that the LORD your God is giving you to possess as an inheritance,

5if only you obey the LORD your God and are careful to follow all these commandments I am giving you today.

6When the LORD your God blesses you as He has promised, you will lend to many nations but borrow from none; you will rule over many nations but be ruled by none.

7If there is a poor man among your brothers within any of the gates in the land that the LORD your God is giving you, then you are not to harden your heart or shut your hand from your poor brother.

8Instead, you are to open your hand to him and freely loan him whatever he needs.

9Be careful not to harbor this wicked thought in your heart: “The seventh year, the year of release, is near,” so that you look upon your poor brother begrudgingly and give him nothing. He will cry out to the LORD against you, and you will be guilty of sin.

10Give generously to him, and do not let your heart be grieved when you do so. And because of this the LORD your God will bless you in all your work and in everything to which you put your hand.

11For there will never cease to be poor in the land; that is why I am commanding you to open wide your hand to your brother and to the poor and needy in your land.

12If a fellow Hebrew, a man or a woman, is sold to you and serves you six years, then in the seventh year you must set him free.

13And when you release him, do not send him away empty-handed.

14You are to furnish him liberally from your flock, your threshing floor, and your winepress. You shall give to him as the LORD your God has blessed you.

15Remember that you were slaves in the land of Egypt, and the LORD your God redeemed you; that is why I am giving you this command today.

16But if your servant says to you, ‘I do not want to leave you,’ because he loves you and your household and is well off with you,

17then take an awl and pierce it through his ear into the door, and he will become your servant for life. And treat your maidservant the same way.

18Do not regard it as a hardship to set your servant free, because his six years of service were worth twice the wages of a hired hand. And the LORD your God will bless you in all you do.

19You must set apart to the LORD your God every firstborn male produced by your herds and flocks. You are not to put the firstborn of your oxen to work, nor are you to shear the firstborn of your flock.

20Each year you and your household are to eat it before the LORD your God in the place the LORD will choose.

21But if an animal has a defect, is lame or blind, or has any serious flaw, you must not sacrifice it to the LORD your God.

22Eat it within your gates; both the ceremonially unclean and clean may eat it as they would a gazelle or a deer.

23But you must not eat the blood; pour it on the ground like water.

Summary

Moses institutes a sabbatical year in which every seventh year, all debts owed by fellow Israelites must be canceled, though this need not apply to foreigners (vv. 1-6). He commands generosity toward the poor, warning against a hard heart or a stingy eye that withholds help simply because the year of release is near, since the poor will always be present in the land (vv. 7-11). Hebrew servants who have served six years must be freed in the seventh, sent away with generous provisions rather than empty-handed, though a servant who loves his master may choose to stay permanently (vv. 12-18). The chapter closes with instructions to dedicate every firstborn male animal to the LORD, eating it before Him each year unless it has a defect (vv. 19-23).

Authorship & Background

Author: Moses. Deuteronomy is the fifth and final book of the Pentateuch, written at the end of the 40 years of wilderness wandering (approximately 1406 BC). Hebrew title: "Devarim" — "Words/Things." Greek title: "Deuteronomion" — "Second Law" (a repetition/expansion of the Law for the new generation). The book consists of Moses' farewell speeches to Israel on the plains of Moab, just before they enter the Promised Land. Moses will not enter with them — these are his final words to the nation he has led for 40 years.
Historical Context: Chapter 15 addresses economic justice within the covenant community through three related laws: (1) the sabbatical year release of debts (vv.1-6), (2) generosity to the poor (vv.7-11), and (3) the release of Hebrew servants in the seventh year (vv.12-18), with a brief section on firstborn animals (vv.19-23). The sabbatical year (every seventh year) was already established in Exodus 23:10-11 for agricultural rest; here Moses extends the principle to debts. Every seven years, debts between Israelites are released — canceled. This prevented permanent poverty and the accumulation of crushing debt. The law anticipates human selfishness: as the seventh year approaches, lenders might refuse to lend (v.9). Moses calls this "a thought in thy wicked heart" and commands generosity regardless. The servant laws parallel Exodus 21:2-6 but add significant detail: the freed servant must not go empty-handed but be furnished liberally (vv.13-14). The motivation is always the same: "remember that thou wast a bondman in the land of Egypt, and the LORD thy God redeemed thee" (v.15). Israel's treatment of the poor and of servants must reflect God's treatment of them. The willing bond-servant (vv.16-17) — who chooses to stay out of love — has his ear pierced to the doorpost, becoming a servant forever. This is a beautiful picture of voluntary, love-motivated service — a type of Christ's willing servanthood (Psalm 40:6-8; Philippians 2:7).
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: a debt release every seven years described as belonging to the LORD rather than merely to the calendar, an ideal of no poor among you made explicitly conditional on obedience rather than promised outright, a warning against the wicked thought that calculates the release year before deciding whether to lend, a Hebrew servant sent away in the seventh year not empty-handed but liberally furnished from flock, floor and winepress, and a firstling animal that belongs to God unless blemished, in which case it becomes ordinary food rather than an acceptable offering
The Year of Release — Debt Cancellation (vv.1-6): "At the end of every seven years thou shalt make a release" (v.1). The manner: "Every creditor that lendeth ought unto his neighbour shall release it" (v.2). He shall not demand repayment from his brother "because it is called the LORD's release" (v.2). This applies to fellow Israelites; foreigners may still be required to repay (v.3). The ideal: "there shall be no poor among you" (v.4) — conditional on obedience: "Only if thou carefully hearken unto the voice of the LORD thy God" (v.5). If Israel obeys fully, God will bless so abundantly that poverty disappears. Promise: "thou shalt lend unto many nations, but thou shalt not borrow; and thou shalt reign over many nations, but they shall not reign over thee" (v.6).
Generosity to the Poor (vv.7-11): "If there be among you a poor man of one of thy brethren... thou shalt not harden thine heart, nor shut thine hand from thy poor brother" (v.7). Instead: "thou shalt open thine hand wide unto him, and shalt surely lend him sufficient for his need" (v.8). Warning against calculating selfishness: "Beware that there be not a thought in thy wicked heart, saying, The seventh year, the year of release, is at hand" (v.9) — refusing to lend because the debt will soon be canceled. If the poor man cries to the LORD, "it be sin unto thee" (v.9). Command: "Thou shalt surely give him, and thine heart shall not be grieved when thou givest" (v.10). Promise: "the LORD thy God shall bless thee in all thy works" (v.10). Reality acknowledged: "For the poor shall never cease out of the land" (v.11) — not contradicting v.4 but recognizing that Israel will not perfectly obey. Therefore: "Thou shalt open thine hand wide unto thy brother, to thy poor, and to thy needy" (v.11).
Release of Hebrew Servants (vv.12-18): "If thy brother, an Hebrew man, or an Hebrew woman, be sold unto thee, and serve thee six years; then in the seventh year thou shalt let him go free" (v.12). Critical addition: "when thou sendest him out free from thee, thou shalt not let him go away empty" (v.13). "Thou shalt furnish him liberally out of thy flock, and out of thy floor, and out of thy winepress" (v.14). Give from what God has blessed you with. Motivation: "thou shalt remember that thou wast a bondman in the land of Egypt, and the LORD thy God redeemed thee" (v.15). You were slaves; God freed you generously. Do the same. "If he say unto thee, I will not go away from thee; because he loveth thee and thine house, because he is well with thee" (v.16). The servant chooses to stay — motivated by love, not compulsion. "Then thou shalt take an aul, and thrust it through his ear unto the door, and he shall be thy servant for ever" (v.17). The pierced ear signifies permanent, voluntary devotion. "And also unto thy maidservant thou shalt do likewise" (v.17). The master should not resent freeing servants: "he hath been worth a double hired servant to thee" (v.18) — six years of service at half the cost of hired labor.
Firstborn Animals (vv.19-23): "All the firstling males that come of thy herd and of thy flock thou shalt sanctify unto the LORD thy God" (v.19). They must not be worked or sheared — they belong to God. Eaten before the LORD yearly at the chosen place (v.20). If blemished (lame, blind, any defect) — not sacrificed to God but eaten locally as common food (vv.21- 22). "Only thou shalt not eat the blood thereof" (v.23).

Deep Dive

Setting: The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life, in the law section of his second sermon (chapters 12–26). Chapter 14 ended with the third-year tithe that fed "the Levite… the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow." Chapter 15 carries that concern for the poor into a seven-year rhythm. Every seventh year debts between Israelites are released, Hebrew servants go free after six years of work, and the people are commanded to keep their hands open to the needy. The firstborn animals at the end of the chapter belong with the yearly festivals that follow in chapter 16. Behind all of it stands one memory: "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the LORD your God redeemed you" (v15). A people set free by God must not build a society where their own brothers and sisters are trapped for good.

The Sabbatical Year (vv1–23)

  • "At the end of every seven years you shall grant a release" (v1). The word is shemittah, from a verb meaning "to let drop" or "let fall." The same verb is used in Exodus 23:10–11 for letting the land lie fallow every seventh year. Just as the land rests, so does debt. This was a fixed national calendar, not seven years from each loan, so a loan made in the sixth year would come up for release almost at once (v9). Deuteronomy 31:10 says the law was to be read aloud to all Israel "at the end of every seven years, at the set time in the year of release."
  • "Every creditor shall release what he has lent to his neighbor" (v2). Was the debt cancelled or only paused for a year? Readers have long differed. The BDB lexicon defines shemittah as a "letting drop of exactions," a remitting that may be temporary, and JFB reads it as a one-year pause on collection, since no crops were harvested in the fallow year. Matthew Henry gives both views and thinks full cancellation "seems more probable." Guzik and many modern scholars agree with him, since vv9–10 make most sense if lenders stood to lose the money. Either way, it was a costly mercy. "Because the LORD's release has been proclaimed." It is the LORD's release. He owns the land and the people, and he sets the terms.
  • "Of a foreigner you may exact it" (v3). The nokri was a foreigner, usually a trader passing through on business, not the resident ger whom Israel was told to love (10:19). Commercial loans to foreign merchants were a different thing from relief loans to a struggling neighbor. The release was a family law for the covenant people, who were bound together as "brothers."
  • "But there will be no poor among you" (v4). This is an ideal, tied to a condition: "if only you will strictly obey" (v5). With God's blessing on the land and a people who lived his way, poverty would have no chance to take root.
  • "You shall lend to many nations, but you shall not borrow" (v6). In the ancient world, being in debt to another nation was a step toward being ruled by it. An obedient Israel would be a lender, not a debtor, and so free. Deuteronomy 28:12–13 and 28:43–44 lay out the two possible futures.
  • "If among you, one of your brothers should become poor" (v7). Moses moves straight from the ideal to the real. Drought, illness, a bad harvest or a death in the family could ruin anyone in a farming society. The poor person is still "your brother."
  • "You shall not harden your heart or shut your hand… but you shall open your hand" (vv7–8). Heart and hand go together. A hard heart shows up as a closed fist. "Lend him sufficient for his need, whatever it may be." The measure is the poor person's need, not the lender's convenience. Elsewhere the law forbids charging interest on these loans to fellow Israelites (23:19–20; Exod 22:25).
  • "Take care lest there be an unworthy thought in your heart" (v9). "Unworthy" is the word beliyya'al, the same word used in 13:13 for "worthless fellows" who lead towns into idolatry. A calculating thought, "The seventh year… is near," is put in the same category. Moses knows exactly how a careful lender would reason: why lend now if the debt will be wiped out next year?
  • "Your eye look grudgingly on your poor brother" (v9). Literally "your eye be evil against your brother." In Hebrew idiom an "evil eye" is a stingy, envious eye, and a "good eye" is a generous one (Prov 22:9; 28:22). Jesus uses the same idiom in Matthew 20:15, where the ESV footnote gives the literal "is your eye bad because I am good?", and in Matthew 6:23: "if your eye is bad, your whole body will be full of darkness."
  • "He cry to the LORD against you, and you be guilty of sin" (v9). The poor person who is refused has an advocate. God hears that cry, as he heard Israel's cry in Egypt (Exod 3:7; 22:23). Refusing to lend becomes a sin against God, not just a lost opportunity.
  • "Your heart shall not be grudging when you give to him" (v10). God cares about how one gives, not only whether one gives. Paul says the same: "God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Cor 9:7).
  • "For there will never cease to be poor in the land" (v11). This looks like a contradiction of v4, but the two fit. Verse 4 describes what obedience would make possible, and v11 admits what Israel would actually be like. Because the poor will always be there, the open hand will always be needed. Jesus echoed this verse at Bethany: "you always have the poor with you, and whenever you want, you can do good for them" (Mark 14:7). He was not shrugging off the poor. He was quoting a verse whose point is "therefore open wide your hand."
  • Was the release ever kept? Evidence is thin. Under King Zedekiah, Judah's leaders released their Hebrew slaves and then took them back, and Jeremiah condemned them for it (Jer 34:8–22). After the exile, the returned community pledged to observe the seventh year and forgo debts (Neh 10:31). By the first century BC, lenders were refusing to lend as the seventh year approached, exactly the attitude v9 warns against. The rabbi Hillel then created a legal device called the prosbul, which let lenders transfer debts to a court so they could still be collected.
  • "If your brother, a Hebrew man or a Hebrew woman, is sold to you" (v12). This was not chattel slavery of the kind practiced in the Americas, which was based on kidnapping, a crime that carried the death penalty in Israel (24:7; Exod 21:16). Here a poor Israelite, crushed by debt, sold his or her labor for a set term. It was a form of bonded service, a last resort to avoid starvation. "Six years, and in the seventh year you shall let him go free." This repeats Exodus 21:2–6, and Deuteronomy adds two things: it includes women equally, and it requires a generous parting gift.
  • "You shall not let him go empty-handed. You shall furnish him liberally" (vv13–14). "Furnish liberally" is literally "put a necklace on him," that is, load him up. From the flock, the threshing floor and the winepress: animals, grain and wine, enough to start again rather than fall straight back into debt. "As the LORD your God has blessed you, you shall give to him." The measure of the gift is God's blessing on the master.
  • "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the LORD your God redeemed you" (v15). This is the heart of the chapter. When Israel left Egypt, the Egyptians loaded them with silver, gold and clothing (Exod 12:35–36). They were not sent out empty. Now they must do for their own servants what God did for them.
  • "But if he says to you, 'I will not go out from you,' because he loves you and your household" (v16). Some servants found a better life in a good household than they could make on their own. The law allowed them to choose to stay. The choice had to come from the servant, from love, not from pressure.
  • "Take an awl, and put it through his ear into the door, and he shall be your slave forever" (v17). The ear was pierced against the doorpost of the house, marking him as a permanent member of that household. Exodus 21:6 adds that it was done "to God," perhaps before judges or at the sanctuary, so it was a witnessed, legal act. The ear may have been chosen because it is the organ of hearing and obeying. "Forever" likely means for life. Leviticus 25:39–41 adds that Hebrew servants went free at the Jubilee, and how the two laws fit together is debated. "To your female slave you shall do the same." Women had the same rights.
  • "At half the cost of a hired worker he has served you six years" (v18). The Hebrew is literally "double the hire of a hired worker." It may mean the servant worked day and night, unlike a hired man, or that he was worth twice what a hired man cost. Isaiah speaks of "three years, like the years of a hired worker" (Isa 16:14), and the Code of Hammurabi limited debt service to three years, so six years may be exactly double the usual term. Either way, the master has already gained a great deal and should not resent the release.
  • "All the firstborn males… you shall dedicate to the LORD your God" (v19). The firstborn belonged to God because he spared Israel's firstborn when he struck Egypt's (Exod 13:2, 11–15). "You shall do no work with the firstborn of your herd, nor shear the firstborn of your flock." No profit was to be taken from what belonged to God.
  • "You shall eat it, you and your household, before the LORD your God year by year" (v20). As with the tithe (14:23), the firstborn became a family feast at the central sanctuary. Numbers 18:17–18 gives the meat of the firstborn to the priests. Interpreters reconcile the two in different ways: perhaps the priests' share and the family meal both came from the same offering, or Deuteronomy speaks of the family's part of the celebration.
  • "But if it has any blemish… you shall not sacrifice it" (v21). Only the best could be offered to God. Malachi later rebuked priests who offered blind and lame animals: "Present that to your governor; will he accept you?" (Mal 1:8). A blemished firstborn was not wasted. It was eaten at home as ordinary meat, "as though it were a gazelle or a deer" (v22; see 12:15).
  • "Only you shall not eat its blood; you shall pour it out on the ground like water" (v23). The blood rule from 12:16 and 12:23–25 is repeated. Even ordinary meals honor God as the giver of life.
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry sees the release as a picture of the gospel: "This year of release typified the grace of the gospel, in which is proclaimed the acceptable year of the Lord." On v7: "If the hand be shut, it is a sign the heart is hardened." On v11, the poor are always with us, "so that a charitable disposition may soon find a charitable occasion." And on v15: "Let them not send their servants out empty, for they were not sent empty out of the house of bondage." JFB holds that the release was "not by an absolute discharge of the debt, but by passing over that year without exacting payment." It explains that the servant who stayed "voluntarily sold themselves to their employer," and reads v18 as "double wages." JFB says nothing on vv5–6 or on the blemished firstborn. Guzik favors full cancellation and admits there is little evidence Israel ever kept these laws fully. He reconciles v4 and v11 as the promise of opportunity versus the reality of disobedience. He sees the willing servant with the pierced ear as a picture of Christ and of every believer who chooses to serve the Master out of love. He connects it to Psalm 40:6 ("you have given me an open ear"), though the Hebrew word there means "dug" or "opened," not pierced, so the link is a picture, not a direct reference.

Pointing to Christ

When the Greek Old Testament translated "release" in this chapter, it used the word aphesis. That is the word Jesus read in the synagogue at Nazareth: "to proclaim liberty to the captives… to set at liberty those who are oppressed, to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor" (Luke 4:18–19), and it is the New Testament's usual word for the "forgiveness" of sins (Eph 1:7). Jesus is the LORD's release in person. He cancels a debt no one could pay (Col 2:13–14) and sends his people out not empty-handed but loaded with grace. And he is the servant who chose to stay: "I delight to do your will, O my God" (Ps 40:8). He took "the form of a servant" (Phil 2:7) out of love for his Father and for the household he came to save.

Reflection

  • Built-In Economic Reset (v.1) — Every seven years, debts between Israelites were simply canceled, preventing permanent bondage to debt. Do you see financial hardship as something a community should periodically relieve, or purely as each person's private burden to solve alone?
  • An Open Hand Starts With an Open Heart (v.7) — Moses connects a hardened heart directly to a closed hand toward the poor. Is there someone in need right now where your heart has quietly hardened before your hand ever had the chance to close?
  • Guarding Against Calculated Stinginess (v.9) — God calls out the person who avoids lending simply because the debt-release year is approaching, naming it a wicked thought even though it's financially sensible. Where have you let a reasonable-sounding excuse justify withholding generosity?
  • Generosity Rooted in Memory (v.15) — Israel is told to give freely because they remember being slaves whom God redeemed. What has God freed you from, and how does remembering that shape the way you treat people who are still struggling?
  • Service Motivated by Love (vv.16-17) — The servant who stays does so not from obligation but because he loves his master and household. Is your service to God and others driven by genuine love, or by a sense of duty you're just fulfilling?
