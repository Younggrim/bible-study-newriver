Summary

Israel complains that they have fasted faithfully and God hasn't noticed, but God answers that their fasting really serves their own interests while being paired with exploiting workers and stirring up conflict (vv. 1-4). He describes the fasting he actually wants: freeing the oppressed, sharing food with the hungry, providing shelter for the homeless, and clothing the naked, rather than putting on a show of humility (vv. 6-7). If they do this, he promises their light will break forth like the dawn, their healing will come quickly, and God will answer when they call (vv. 8-9). The chapter closes by connecting this same kind of practical, others-focused obedience to honoring the Sabbath, promising that those who do so will be called repairers of broken walls and restorers of streets to live in (vv. 12-14).

Authorship & Background

Author: Isaiah son of Amoz (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — True and False Worship
Key Themes: False fasting (self-serving religion), true fasting (justice and mercy), the conditions for answered prayer, light breaking forth, Sabbath delight, repairing the breach
Historical Context: The returned exiles (or the pre-exilic community — the principle is timeless) practice religious disciplines (fasting, prayer) but see no results. They accuse God of ignoring them (v.3). God's response: your fast is wrong. You fast while oppressing workers (v.3), fighting (v.4), and pursuing self-interest. True fasting (vv.6-7) is justice-oriented: free the oppressed, feed the hungry, house the homeless. This chapter inspired many social justice movements throughout church history.
Cry Aloud, Spare Not (v.1): Cry aloud, spare not, lift up thy voice like a trumpet, and shew my people their transgression, and the house of Jacob their sins. One verse of instruction, and the striking thing about it is the audience: my people and the house of Jacob, a community that is fasting and inquiring and keeping a calendar. The chapter is addressed to religious people in good standing.
Wherefore Have We Fasted (vv.2-3a): Yet they seek me daily, and delight to know my ways, they ask of me the ordinances of justice, they take delight in approaching to God. The description is complimentary and it is meant to be, which is what makes the complaint that follows worth answering. Then the grievance is quoted in their own words, wherefore have we fasted, say they, and thou seest not, wherefore have we afflicted our soul, and thou takest no knowledge. They have done the work and want to know why nothing happened.
Ye Fast for Strife (vv.3b-5): Behold, in the day of your fast ye find pleasure, and exact all your labours. The answer identifies what was happening on the fast days, and it is industrial relations: work extracted from employees while the employer fasted. Behold, ye fast for strife and debate, and to smite with the fist of wickedness. Then the theater of it is described and dismissed, is it such a fast that I have chosen, a day for a man to afflict his soul, is it to bow down his head as a bulrush. The bulrush is exact: a reed bends convincingly and springs straight back.
Is Not This the Fast (vv.6-7): Is not this the fast that I have chosen, to loose the bands of wickedness, to undo the heavy burdens, and to let the oppressed go free, and that ye break every yoke. Four clauses about releasing people, and then four about supplying them, is it not to deal thy bread to the hungry, and that thou bring the poor that are cast out to thy house, when thou seest the naked, that thou cover him. And the last clause closes the escape route, and that thou hide not thyself from thine own flesh, that is, from your own relatives, which is where such duty is most easily avoided.
Then Shall Thy Light Break Forth (vv.8-12): Then shall thy light break forth as the morning, and thine health shall spring forth speedily. The promises are conditional on the previous section and they are stated in the same order: light, healing, an answer to prayer, and guidance. Then the condition is restated in the middle, if thou draw out thy soul to the hungry, and satisfy the afflicted soul. And the images that follow are all about water in a dry place, thou shalt be like a watered garden, and like a spring of water, whose waters fail not. The last verse gives a title that would have meant a great deal to a community living in rubble, thou shalt be called, The repairer of the breach, the restorer of paths to dwell in.
If Thou Turn Away Thy Foot (vv.13-14): If thou turn away thy foot from the sabbath, from doing thy pleasure on my holy day, and call the sabbath a delight. The word delight is the operative one, since the chapter has just spent five verses on a fast that was misery for the fasters and worse for their workers. Sabbath is being offered as the opposite of that, not as a further austerity. And the promise attached is inheritance, then shalt thou delight thyself in the LORD, and I will cause thee to ride upon the high places of the earth, and feed thee with the heritage of Jacob thy father.

Deep Dive

Setting: Chapters 56–58 look at the community of God's people as it worshiped: Sabbaths kept, sacrifices offered, fasts held. Chapter 57 exposed open idolatry. Chapter 58 exposes something harder to see, religion that is real in form and busy in practice but empty at the heart. The Law commanded only one yearly fast, on the Day of Atonement, when Israel was to "afflict yourselves" (Lev 16:29–31). After Jerusalem fell, extra fasts grew up to mourn the disaster (Zech 7:3–5; 8:19). Whether this chapter addresses Judah before the exile or the returned community after it, the people are fasting, praying and puzzled. They want to know why God does not answer. God tells them. The whole chapter is one ESV section, and it is one of the Bible's clearest statements of what God wants from worshipers.

True and False Fasting (vv1–14)

  • "Cry aloud; do not hold back; lift up your voice like a trumpet" (v1). "Cry aloud" is literally "call with the throat," at full volume. JFB: "with full voice, not merely from the lips." The trumpet is the shofar, the ram's horn blown to warn of attack (Hos 8:1; Joel 2:1) or to call an assembly. The prophet must sound the alarm, and the danger is inside the camp: "declare to my people their transgression."
  • "Yet they seek me daily and delight to know my ways" (v2). This is what makes the chapter so unsettling. These are not atheists or idolaters. They come to worship every day. They enjoy religious instruction. "They ask of me righteous judgments" means they want God to rule in their favor. "They delight to draw near to God." The irony is in the words "as if": "as if they were a nation that did righteousness." It looked right from outside.
  • "'Why have we fasted, and you see it not?'" (v3). They feel cheated. "Humbled ourselves" is the same verb as the Day of Atonement command to "afflict yourselves" (Lev 16:29). They did what the rules said, so why has God not responded? Jesus tells of a Pharisee who prayed, "I fast twice a week," and went home not justified (Luke 18:12–14).
  • "In the day of your fast you seek your own pleasure, and oppress all your workers" (v3). "Pleasure" (chephets) can mean "business" or "affairs." JFB mentions this reading. They kept business going on fast days, and pushed their laborers hard while they themselves went without food. The Law warned against oppressing hired workers and holding back their wages (Deut 24:14–15). James says the wages withheld from workers "are crying out" to the Lord (Jas 5:4).
  • "You fast only to quarrel and to fight and to hit with a wicked fist" (v4). Hungry people get irritable. Their fasting made them short-tempered and violent. "Fasting like yours this day will not make your voice to be heard on high." The problem is not that God cannot hear. It is that he will not listen to prayers from hands that strike others. Isaiah said it on the first page of the book: "When you spread out your hands, I will hide my eyes… your hands are full of blood" (1:15).
  • "Is it to bow down his head like a reed, and to spread sackcloth and ashes under him?" (v5). A reed bends in the wind and then springs back up. Bowing the head was the posture of grief, but with no change inside it was like a reed nodding in a breeze. Sackcloth (rough goat-hair cloth) and ashes were real signs of mourning and repentance (Jonah 3:5–8; Dan 9:3). God does not mock the signs. He mocks signs with nothing behind them. Jesus said to fast in secret, without the gloomy face, for the Father "who sees in secret" (Matt 6:16–18).
  • "Is not this the fast that I choose: to loose the bonds of wickedness, to undo the straps of the yoke" (v6). God answers his own question with four actions that break chains. A yoke was the wooden beam fastened on oxen with straps. Here it pictures oppression: unjust debts, forced labor, people held in slavery. "The oppressed" is literally "the crushed" or "broken." JFB cites Jerome's reading, people broken by poverty and bankrupt. Jeremiah records a time when Jerusalem's leaders freed their Hebrew slaves in a covenant ceremony, then took them back (Jer 34:8–17).
  • Jesus and "let the oppressed go free" (v6). In the synagogue at Nazareth, Jesus read from Isaiah 61:1–2, and Luke's quotation includes a line from this verse: "to set at liberty those who are oppressed" (Luke 4:18). Jesus joined the two passages together to describe his own mission. The fast God chooses is the work Jesus came to do.
  • "Is it not to share your bread with the hungry and bring the homeless poor into your house" (v7). Now the actions turn from breaking chains to giving: bread, shelter, clothing. The food a person goes without on a fast day should go to someone who has none. "When you see the naked, to cover him." Jesus' parable of the sheep and the goats uses almost this list: "I was hungry and you gave me food… I was a stranger and you welcomed me, I was naked and you clothed me" (Matt 25:35–36). James: "religion that is pure and undefiled… is this: to visit orphans and widows in their affliction" (Jas 1:27).
  • "Not to hide yourself from your own flesh" (v7). "Your own flesh" means your own relatives and, more broadly, fellow Israelites, fellow human beings. It is easy to look away from need when it is close and personal. John: "If anyone has the world's goods and sees his brother in need, yet closes his heart against him, how does God's love abide in him?" (1 John 3:17).
  • "Then shall your light break forth like the dawn" (v8). The promises begin. Light after darkness, like the sun coming up. "Your healing shall spring up speedily." "Healing" is the word for new flesh growing over a wound, as a bandaged injury closes. "Your righteousness shall go before you; the glory of the LORD shall be your rear guard." This is the new-exodus picture of 52:12, where God went before and behind his people. The pillar of cloud and fire both led Israel and guarded them from behind (Exod 13:21; 14:19–20).
  • "Then you shall call, and the LORD will answer; you shall cry, and he will say, 'Here I am'" (v9). This answers their complaint in v3. They said God did not see. Now he answers immediately. "Here I am" (hinneni) is what a servant says when called. God said it to his people in 52:6. Here he promises to say it whenever they call.
  • "If you take away the yoke from your midst, the pointing of the finger, and speaking wickedness" (v9). Three things to stop: oppression, contempt and harmful speech. "Pointing of the finger" was a gesture of scorn or accusation (Prov 6:13). JFB notes that the Romans used the middle finger as an insult. The sins of the tongue are listed beside the sins of the hand.
  • "If you pour yourself out for the hungry" (v10). Literally "if you give your soul (nephesh) to the hungry." The same word is in the next line: "satisfy the desire (nephesh) of the afflicted." It is not just handing over food. It is giving yourself. "Then shall your light rise in the darkness and your gloom be as the noonday." Gloom becomes the brightest time of day.
  • "The LORD will guide you continually and satisfy your desire in scorched places" (v11). "Desire" is nephesh a third time. Those who satisfy the hungry nephesh will have their own nephesh satisfied. God's reward matches the giving. "Make your bones strong." Bones stand for the whole body's strength (Prov 3:8).
  • "You shall be like a watered garden, like a spring of water, whose waters do not fail" (v11). In a dry land, many streams run only in the rainy season and then vanish. "Do not fail" is literally "do not lie," do not deceive the thirsty traveler who comes looking (Jer 15:18). JFB notes this. This spring never disappoints. Jesus: "Whoever believes in me… 'Out of his heart will flow rivers of living water'" (John 7:38).
  • "Your ancient ruins shall be rebuilt" (v12). Jerusalem lay in ruins after 586 BC. "The foundations of many generations" means foundations laid long ago and left in rubble. "You shall be called the repairer of the breach, the restorer of streets to dwell in." A "breach" is a gap in a city wall where enemies broke through. Nehemiah rebuilt Jerusalem's walls and called the people to "build the wall of Jerusalem, that we may no longer suffer derision" (Neh 2:17). Notice: those who repair broken lives in vv6–7 get to repair broken walls. In Ezekiel, God searched for a man to "stand in the breach before me for the land" and found none (Ezek 22:30).
  • "If you turn back your foot from the Sabbath, from doing your pleasure on my holy day" (v13). The picture is of someone walking over holy ground, trampling it. "Your pleasure" is the same word as in v3, chephets, one's own business. On fast days they kept up their own business. On the Sabbath they did the same.
  • "Call the Sabbath a delight and the holy day of the LORD honorable" (v13). The Sabbath was never meant to be a burden. Jewish tradition still calls the Sabbath evening gathering oneg Shabbat, "Sabbath delight," from this verse. Henry: "We must call it a delight, not a task and a burden." "Not going your own ways, or seeking your own pleasure, or talking idly." "Talking idly" is literally "speaking a word," probably meaning ordinary business talk or chatter. The day is God's, so a person sets aside his own agenda.
  • "Then you shall take delight in the LORD" (v14). "Delight" in v13 and "take delight" in v14 are the same Hebrew root, ʿanag. Those who make the Sabbath a delight will find their delight in God himself. Psalm 37:4: "Delight yourself in the LORD, and he will give you the desires of your heart."
  • "I will make you ride on the heights of the earth" (v14). This is borrowed from the Song of Moses: "He made him ride on the high places of the land, and he ate the produce of the field" (Deut 32:13). It pictures victory, safety and plenty. "I will feed you with the heritage of Jacob your father." The land promised to Jacob (Gen 28:13–15). "For the mouth of the LORD has spoken" puts God's own seal on the promise (1:20; 40:5).
  • Do Christians keep the Sabbath? Christians have long differed. Many Reformed and Presbyterian believers hold that the Sabbath principle continues, moved to the Lord's Day, Sunday. Others, including many Baptists and evangelicals, read Colossians 2:16–17 and Hebrews 4:9–10 to mean the Sabbath's rest is fulfilled in Christ, while keeping a weekly day of worship and rest as wise and good. Seventh-day Adventists keep Saturday. All agree with Isaiah's main point here: a holy day should be a delight in God, not a burden or a ritual done to earn something.
  • Commentators: Henry outlines the chapter as a charge against "the sinners in Zion," whose "plausible profession of religion" was spoiled by the sins of their hands, with instructions on how "to keep fasts aright." Guzik says the problem is not fasting itself but fasting cut off from a sincere heart, and he cites Matthew 23:23 to show the answer is not to stop but to do it rightly. He divides vv6–10 into stopping harm (sins of commission) and starting to do good (sins of omission). JFB notes that "oppressed" in v6 is literally "the broken," and that "health" in v8 pictures a bandage on a healing wound. On the Sabbath Henry writes, "The more pleasure we take in serving God the more pleasure we shall find in it," and "Those that honour God and his sabbath he will thus honour." Guzik holds that Christians are not bound by the Sabbath law (Col 2:16–17) while still valuing regular rest.

Pointing to Christ

Jesus fulfilled this chapter twice over. He read it in Nazareth as his job description: "to set at liberty those who are oppressed" (Luke 4:18). And he lived the fast God chooses. He fed the hungry, touched the outcast, and gave himself, his nephesh, for others, pouring out his soul to death (53:12). The light breaking out like the dawn and the spring whose waters never fail are pictures the New Testament gives to Christ (Luke 1:78–79; John 7:37–38). He is also the Lord of the Sabbath (Mark 2:28), who offers the rest that the day pointed to: "Come to me… and I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28).

Reflection

  • "Why Have We Fasted, and You Don't See?" (v.3) — Israel complains that God ignores their fasting while they continue pursuing their own pleasure and oppressing their workers on those same days. Is there a spiritual discipline in your life that's more about getting God to notice you than about actually changing anything?
  • True Fasting Defined (vv.6-7) — God redefines the fast He wants: loosing the bonds of wickedness, sharing bread with the hungry, and bringing the homeless poor into your house. What would it look like this week to treat justice and mercy toward someone in need as an act of worship?
  • Light Breaking Forth (v.8) — God promises that when His people practice true fasting, their light will break forth like the dawn and their healing will spring up quickly. Could the breakthrough you're praying for be waiting on the other side of obedience you've been putting off?
  • Repairer of the Breach (v.12) — Those who rebuild what's broken and restore streets to live in will be called repairers of the breach and restorers of ruined places. Is God calling you to repair something broken — in a family, a friendship, a community — rather than just critique it?
  • Sabbath as Delight (v.13) — God calls His people to call the Sabbath a delight, not a burden, honoring it instead of going their own way. Do you treat rest as something to feel guilty about, or as something God intended you to genuinely enjoy?
