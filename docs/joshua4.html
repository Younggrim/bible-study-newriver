1When the whole nation had finished crossing the Jordan, the LORD said to Joshua,

2“Choose twelve men from among the people, one from each tribe,

3and command them: ‘Take up for yourselves twelve stones from the middle of the Jordan where the priests were standing, carry them with you, and set them down in the place where you spend the night.’”

4So Joshua summoned the twelve men he had appointed from the Israelites, one from each tribe,

5and said to them, “Cross over before the ark of the LORD your God into the middle of the Jordan. Each of you is to take a stone upon his shoulder, according to the number of the tribes of Israel,

6to serve as a sign among you. In the future, when your children ask, ‘What do these stones mean to you?’

Summary

After all Israel has crossed the Jordan, the LORD tells Joshua to select twelve men, one from each tribe, to carry twelve stones from the riverbed where the priests stood and set them up as a memorial (vv. 1-9). The purpose is so that when future generations ask what the stones mean, their parents can tell them how the LORD dried up the Jordan just as He had the Red Sea (vv. 6-7, 21-24). The Reubenites, Gadites, and half-tribe of Manasseh cross armed for battle ahead of the rest, about forty thousand strong, and the LORD exalts Joshua in the sight of all Israel so the people fear him as they had feared Moses (vv. 12-14). Once the priests step out of the riverbed with the ark, the Jordan's waters return to their place, and the people camp at Gilgal on the tenth day of the first month (vv. 15-20).

Authorship & Background

Author: Joshua, with possible later editorial additions. Joshua was Moses' assistant and successor, who led Israel into the Promised Land. The book covers approximately 25 years (1406-1381 BC) — from the crossing of the Jordan to Joshua's death. Hebrew title: 'Yehoshua' — 'YHWH saves' (the same name as Jesus in Greek). The book demonstrates God's faithfulness to His land promise to Abraham (Genesis 15:18-21) and shows that victory comes through faith and obedience, not human strength.
Historical Context: Chapter 4 records the establishment of a memorial — twelve stones taken from the middle of the Jordan and set up at Gilgal. This chapter is about remembering. God commands Israel to create a physical, permanent reminder of what He has done so that future generations will know and fear Him. The key question is "What mean these stones?" (v.6,21) — when children ask, parents must tell the story. God understands that human memory is short and that faith must be transmitted from generation to generation through deliberate teaching. The twelve stones (one per tribe) represent the unity of all Israel in this miracle. The date is significant: the tenth day of the first month (v.19) — the same day the Passover lamb was selected in Egypt (Exodus 12:3). Israel enters Canaan on the anniversary of their redemption's beginning. The chapter also records the fulfillment of God's promise to magnify Joshua (3:7): "On that day the LORD magnified Joshua in the sight of all Israel" (v.14).
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: Twelve stones taken from the exact spot where the priests' feet stood, a question children are expected to ask built into the memorial's whole purpose, two monuments raised, one in the riverbed and one at Gilgal, so the miracle is remembered from two directions, waters returning to their place the instant the priests' feet reach dry land, and a stated dual purpose, witness to the nations and fear of the LORD in Israel forever
The Twelve Stones Commanded (vv.1-7): After all the people have crossed, God commands Joshua to select twelve men (one per tribe) to take twelve stones from the exact spot where the priests stood in the Jordan (vv.1-3). Each man carries a stone on his shoulder (v.5). The purpose: "that this may be a sign among you, that when your children ask their fathers in time to come, saying, What mean ye by these stones?" (v.6). The answer: the waters of Jordan were cut off before the ark — "these stones shall be for a memorial unto the children of Israel for ever" (v.7).
The Stones Set Up (vv.8-9): The people obey exactly as commanded (v.8). Joshua also sets up twelve stones in the Jordan where the priests stood (v.9) — "and they are there unto this day." Two memorials: one in the river (visible at low water) and one at Gilgal (always visible).
The Crossing Completed (vv.10-13): The priests stand in the Jordan until everything is finished (v.10). The people cross in haste (v.10). After all have crossed, the ark and priests come up (v.11). The Transjordan tribes (Reuben, Gad, half-Manasseh) cross armed as promised — about 40,000 warriors (vv.12-13).
Joshua Exalted (v.14): "On that day the LORD magnified Joshua in the sight of all Israel; and they feared him, as they feared Moses, all the days of his life." The promise of 3:7 is fulfilled. Joshua's authority is now established by divine miracle.
The Waters Return (vv.15-18): God commands the priests to come up out of the Jordan (vv.15-17). The moment the priests' feet touch dry land, "the waters of Jordan returned unto their place, and flowed over all his banks, as they did before" (v.18). The miracle was precisely controlled — it lasted exactly as long as needed and not a moment longer.
The Memorial at Gilgal (vv.19-24): Israel camps at Gilgal on the tenth day of the first month (v.19). Joshua sets up the twelve stones at Gilgal (v.20). He instructs the people: when your children ask "What mean these stones?" tell them "Israel came over this Jordan on dry land" (vv.21-22). The explicit parallel: "as the LORD your God did to the Red sea" (v.23). The dual purpose: "That all the people of the earth might know the hand of the LORD, that it is mighty: that ye might fear the LORD your God for ever" (v.24) — witness to the nations and worship from Israel.

Setting: The same day as chapter 3: the tenth day of the first month (v19), in spring, in the season of the barley harvest. The Jordan has stopped flowing far upstream at Adam, the priests are standing with the ark in the middle of the riverbed, and the whole nation has just walked across on dry ground. Chapter 4 tells the crossing a second time from another angle. It is less about how Israel got across than about how Israel would remember it. The chapter is built around two piles of twelve stones and two scenes of children asking questions, and it finishes on the west bank at Gilgal, Israel's first camp in the promised land.

Twelve Memorial Stones from the Jordan (vv1–24)

  • "When all the nation had finished passing over" (v1). "Finished" or "completed" (tamam) runs through these chapters (3:17; 4:1, 10, 11). Nobody was left behind on the east bank. The whole people, all twelve tribes, is now in the land.
  • "Take twelve men from the people, from each tribe a man" (v2). These are the men chosen in 3:12. Levi had no land inheritance and Joseph counted as two tribes (Ephraim and Manasseh), so "twelve tribes" stayed twelve. The two and a half tribes settled east of the Jordan send a man too. The memorial belongs to all Israel, not just those who will live west of the river.
  • "From the very place where the priests' feet stood firmly" (v3). The stones come from the riverbed itself, the spot that should have been under several feet of rushing water. Each one is a piece of the miracle carried out of it.
  • "Take up each of you a stone upon his shoulder" (v5). These were not pebbles. A stone a man carries on his shoulder could weigh as much as 100 pounds.
  • "That this may be a sign among you" (v6). Ancient peoples often set up standing stones or heaps to mark a covenant or a meeting with God: Jacob at Bethel (Gen 28:18–22), Jacob and Laban (Gen 31:45–52), Moses at Sinai with twelve pillars for twelve tribes (Exod 24:4). Later Samuel sets up a stone and calls it Ebenezer, "Till now the LORD has helped us" (1 Sam 7:12).
  • "When your children ask in time to come, 'What do those stones mean to you?'" (v6). The memorial was designed to provoke a question. Moses gave the same pattern for the Passover meal (Exod 12:26–27; 13:14) and for the law itself: "When your son asks you in time to come…" (Deut 6:20–25). Faith in Israel was passed on at home, by children asking and parents telling the story. The stones did not explain themselves. Someone had to tell what God had done.
  • "The waters of the Jordan were cut off before the ark" (v7). The answer is about the ark, the sign of God's presence. The point is not that Israel was clever or brave, but that God went first and the river gave way.
  • "A memorial forever" (v7). The Hebrew zikkaron is the same word used of the Passover: "This day shall be for you a memorial day" (Exod 12:14). Both events were to be remembered by every generation, not just those who were there.
  • "The people of Israel did just as Joshua commanded" (v8). The writer stresses obedience throughout: "just as the LORD told Joshua" (v8), "according to all that Moses had commanded Joshua" (v10), "as Moses had told them" (v12). The new generation is doing what the old one would not.
  • "Joshua set up twelve stones in the midst of the Jordan" (v9). Read plainly, this is a second pile, set up in the riverbed where the priests stood. When the water returned it would have been hidden, and visible only when the river ran low. Some translations (the NIV's main text, for example) read the verse as describing the same twelve stones, but the Hebrew most naturally says Joshua "set up twelve stones" in the river, and the ancient Greek translation even says "other twelve stones." Most interpreters see two memorials: one on the bank for all to see, and one under the water where the miracle happened.
  • "They are there to this day" (v9). This phrase appears often in Joshua (5:9; 7:26; 8:28–29). It shows the account was written down, or at least finished, some time after the events, when the stones could still be pointed out. Whether that was decades or centuries later, the text does not say.
  • "The people passed over in haste" (v10). Not panic, but urgency. The priests were standing in the riverbed holding the ark, and a nation of hundreds of thousands, with children and animals, needed to be across in a single day. The hurry is the kind of detail an eyewitness remembers.
  • "The ark of the LORD and the priests passed over before the people" (v11). The ark comes out last, and then takes its place at the head of the people again.
  • "About 40,000 ready for war" (v13). These are the fighting men from Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh, keeping the promise made to Moses (Num 32:20–22) and repeated to Joshua (1:16–18). The census just before had counted over 110,000 men of military age in those tribes (Num 26), so the rest stayed behind to protect the towns and families. Some scholars think the word for "thousand" ('eleph) can also mean a military unit or clan, which would make the numbers in these books smaller. Others read it plainly. The text's point is the same either way: the eastern tribes kept their word. "Before the LORD" marks this as holy war. They were marching under God's command, not just Joshua's.
  • "To the plains of Jericho" (v13). The plain around Jericho was an oasis in the Jordan valley, watered by the spring at the city and famous for its palm groves.
  • "On that day the LORD exalted Joshua" (v14). This fulfills 3:7 word for word. "They stood in awe of him" is the language of reverent respect, the same verb used of fearing God (v24). Israel honored Joshua "all the days of his life," and Judges 2:7 says the people served the LORD all the days of Joshua and of the elders who outlived him.
  • "The ark of the testimony" (v16). The only place in Joshua where the ark is called this. "The testimony" is the two stone tablets inside it (Exod 25:16, 21), God's covenant written in stone. The ark that held back the river also held the law Israel had sworn to keep.
  • "The waters of the Jordan returned to their place and overflowed all its banks, as before" (v18). The moment the priests' feet touched the west bank, the river came back in flood. The timing works both ways: the water stopped when their feet went in and returned when their feet came out. There was no going back to the east now.
  • "The tenth day of the first month" (v19). This is the very day each household was to choose its Passover lamb (Exod 12:3). Forty years earlier, almost to the day, Israel had left Egypt (Exod 12:41; Num 33:3). Four days later they will keep their first Passover in the land (5:10).
  • "They encamped at Gilgal" (v19). The name is explained in 5:9 by a play on the Hebrew galal, "to roll," and is often taken to mean "circle," perhaps a circle of stones. Its exact site is unknown, somewhere between the Jordan and Jericho. Gilgal became Israel's base camp for the conquest (9:6; 10:6), and later a holy place where Samuel judged and Saul was made king (1 Sam 7:16; 11:14–15). Later prophets condemned the idolatry that took root there (Hos 4:15; Amos 4:4).
  • "Israel passed over this Jordan on dry ground" (v22). The children's question comes a second time. Now the answer is not about the ark (v7) but about Israel's experience: we walked across dry.
  • "As the LORD your God did to the Red Sea" (v23). Joshua ties the two crossings together. The older generation saw the Red Sea, the younger saw the Jordan, and both now share one story. Psalm 114:3 sings them as one event: "The sea looked and fled; Jordan turned back."
  • "So that all the peoples of the earth may know… that you may fear the LORD your God forever" (v24). Two purposes, outward and inward. The miracle was a witness to the nations, and the next chapter shows the kings of Canaan already losing heart (5:1). And it was meant to shape Israel's own worship, not for a day but "forever."
  • Commentators: Henry: "The works of the Lord are so worthy of remembrance, and the heart of man is so prone to forget them, that various methods are needful to refresh our memories." He notes that every tribe, not just the Levites, had a share in the memorial, and that "it is the duty of parents to tell their children betimes of the words and works of God." Guzik makes the same point in his own way: memorials are for people who were not there, they help most in hard times, and their purpose is to build faith for what God will do next, not to keep us looking back. He reads v10's "haste" as the sort of detail an eyewitness would record. JFB reads v9 as a second set of stones and notes that "unto this day" could be at least twenty years later, or a phrase added much later by an editor. It also notes that the tenth day was when the Passover lamb was set apart.

Pointing to Christ

Israel's memorial was stones taken from the place of death, where the river should have swallowed them, and set up on the far side as a witness that God had brought his people through. The church also has memorials Jesus himself gave, the bread and the cup, with the same purpose: "Do this in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19). They point back to the place where Christ stood in the deep waters, and they are kept until he comes (1 Cor 11:26). As with the stones at Gilgal, the meaning has to be told to each new generation, and the story told is not of what we did but of what God did for us.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes the importance of memorial and teaching. God knows that one generation's experience does not automatically transfer to the next — it must be deliberately taught. The stones are "sermon starters" — they provoke the question that opens the door to testimony. He also notes the date (tenth of the first month) connecting the Jordan crossing to the Passover lamb selection.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Building Memorials to Remember (vv.6-7) — God has Israel set up twelve stones specifically so that children who ask what these stones mean will hear the story of what He did. What have you done to make sure the next generation hears your story of God's faithfulness?
  • A Lasting Reminder (v.9) — Joshua also sets a second set of stones in the riverbed itself, a marker that remains long after the moment has passed. What lasting reminders of God's work have you built into your own life or home?
  • God Confirms His Chosen Leader (v.14) — Through this miracle, the Lord exalts Joshua in the eyes of all Israel just as He had promised. Are you willing to let God establish your standing rather than seeking to prove yourself?
  • Precisely Controlled Power (v.18) — The instant the priests step onto dry ground, the Jordan rushes back to full flood, showing God's total control over the miracle's timing. Does your view of God allow for this kind of exact, purposeful control over your circumstances?
  • Witness and Worship Together (v.24) — God's purpose in the crossing is twofold: that all peoples would know His power, and that Israel would fear Him forever. Does your life point others toward God even as it deepens your own reverence for Him?
