Summary

The oracles against the surrounding nations begin as God turns His attention from Jerusalem to four neighboring peoples who rejoiced over Judah's fall: Ammon (vv. 1-7), Moab (vv. 8-11), Edom (vv. 12-14), and Philistia (vv. 15-17). Each receives a specific charge and a specific sentence of judgment. Their sin is not merely idolatry but the gloating, mocking satisfaction they took in watching God's people suffer. In judging them, God shows He still defends His own people even while disciplining them.

Authorship & Background

Author: Ezekiel son of Buzi (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Oracles Against the Nations (Judgment on Gentiles)
Key Themes: God judges those who rejoice over Israel's fall, divine justice for gloating enemies, Ammon's "Aha!," Moab's contempt, Edom's vengeance, Philistia's spite, God vindicates His name among all peoples
Historical Context: Chapters 25-32 form a distinct unit: oracles against foreign nations. This is a common prophetic pattern (cf. Isaiah 13-23; Jeremiah 46-51; Amos 1-2). The placement is deliberate — between the judgment on Jerusalem (chs. 1-24) and the restoration promises (chs. 33-48). Message: God judges His own people first, but He also judges those who exploit their suffering.
Ammon, and the Word Aha (vv.1-7): The seven foreign oracles begin, and the charge in every one of the four in this chapter is something the neighbors said while Jerusalem burned. Ammon's is quoted: because thou saidst, Aha, against my sanctuary, when it was profaned, and against the land of Israel, when it was desolate, and against the house of Judah, when they went into captivity. The sentence is dispossession by desert tribes rather than by an empire, I will deliver thee to the men of the east, and they shall set their palaces in thee, with Rabbah reduced to a stable for camels. And each oracle ends the same way, ye shall know that I am the LORD.
Moab, and Judah Treated as One Nation Among Many (vv.8-11): Moab's offense is not an act but an assessment, and it is the most theological charge in the chapter: because Moab and Seir do say, Behold, the house of Judah is like unto all the heathen. That is, the fall of Jerusalem proved there was nothing to the claim that this people's God was different. It is exactly the argument the Assyrian officers made at Hezekiah's wall in 2 Chronicles 32, and the reply here is the same, the sentence is passed so that they shall know that I am the LORD.
Edom, and Vengeance Against a Brother (vv.12-14): Because that Edom hath dealt against the house of Judah by taking vengeance, and hath greatly offended. What aggravates it is family: Edom is Esau's line, so this is a brother settling old accounts at the worst possible moment. Edom's conduct at the fall of Jerusalem is the whole subject of Obadiah and the sting in Psalm 137, and Ezekiel comes back to it at chapter 35. The sentence here is unusual in this section for naming the agent, I will lay my vengeance upon Edom by the hand of my people Israel.
Philistia, and the Old Hatred (vv.15-17): Because the Philistines have dealt by revenge, and have taken vengeance with a despiteful heart, to destroy it for the old hatred. The phrase is worth pausing on, because the hatred it names had by this date been running for something like six hundred years, since the period of the judges, and it outlasted every political arrangement in between. The sentence reaches the specialized troops and the coastal remnant, I will cut off the Cherethites, and destroy the remnant of the sea coast.

Setting: Babylonia, around 587–586 BC. Ezekiel has just learned that the siege of Jerusalem has begun (24:1–2), and he has been told he will say no more about the city until a survivor brings word of its fall (24:26–27). In the meantime he turns to the nations around Judah. Chapters 25–32 are a collection of oracles against seven nations: Ammon, Moab, Edom and Philistia in this chapter, then Tyre, Sidon and finally Egypt. The Tyndale notes observe that they run roughly clockwise around Judah, from east to south to west to north, ending with Egypt. Isaiah (13–23), Jeremiah (46–51) and Amos (1–2) contain similar collections. Placing them here, between the fall of Jerusalem and the hope of chapters 33–48, says something important: God's judgment on his own people does not mean their enemies have won. Notably, Babylon is missing from Ezekiel's list. It is God's instrument of judgment in this period, and Ezekiel, like Jeremiah, does not condemn it here.

Prophecy Against Ammon (vv1–7)

  • "Set your face toward the Ammonites" (v2). The Ammonites lived east of the Jordan, on the edge of the desert, with their capital at Rabbah, the site of modern Amman in Jordan. They were distant relatives of Israel, descended from Lot (Gen 19:38), and long-standing enemies. Saul fought them (1 Sam 11), David took Rabbah (2 Sam 12:26–31), and in Ezekiel's day they were plotting against Judah's governor (Jer 40:14). Nebuchadnezzar had stood at a fork in the road deciding whether to attack Rabbah or Jerusalem, and chose Jerusalem (21:18–22).
  • "Because you said, 'Aha!' over my sanctuary when it was profaned" (v3). "Aha!" (he'ach) is a cry of glee at someone else's ruin (compare 26:2; 36:2; Ps 35:21). Ammon rejoiced at three things: the temple defiled, the land laid waste and the people taken into exile. The first is listed first for a reason. Ammon's gloating over "my sanctuary" was an insult to the LORD himself. In the ancient world, the fall of a temple was taken to show the defeat of its god.
  • When did this happen? The temple was burned in August 586 BC. This oracle may have been given just after, or it may look ahead to an event Ezekiel knew was certain. Some scholars think the oracles in chapter 25 were spoken after the news of 33:21 arrived and were placed here to keep the oracles against the nations together.
  • "I am handing you over to the people of the East" (v4). "People of the East" (literally "sons of the East") were the desert tribes beyond Ammon, nomads with their camels and flocks (Judg 6:3; Job 1:3). The settled kingdom would be overrun by herders. "They shall set their encampments among you" and "I will make Rabbah a pasture for camels" (v5). A capital city would become grazing ground. Henry and JFB note that Arab tribes did in time overrun this region. Josephus, the first-century Jewish historian, records that Nebuchadnezzar campaigned against Ammon and Moab five years after Jerusalem fell, in 582 BC (Antiquities 10.9.7).
  • "Because you have clapped your hands and stamped your feet and rejoiced with all the malice within your soul" (v6). This describes a celebration. Ammon did not just quietly approve. It danced. "With all the malice" is literally "with all the contempt in your soul." Proverbs warns, "Do not rejoice when your enemy falls… lest the LORD see it and be displeased" (Prov 24:17–18).
  • "I will cut you off from the peoples and will make you perish out of the countries" (v7). Ammon did disappear as a nation. After the Persian period it was absorbed into the Arab and Nabatean peoples, and by the time of Christ there was no Ammonite kingdom. The city of Rabbah was rebuilt by the Greek rulers of Egypt as Philadelphia and is now Amman, but the people of Ammon are gone. (Jeremiah 49:6 still holds out a promise to "restore the fortunes" of Ammon, which many see fulfilled when Gentiles from every nation are brought into God's people.)
  • "Then you will know that I am the LORD" (vv5, 7). The phrase that runs through Ezekiel's warnings to Israel is now said to the nations. God's aim, even in judgment, is that he be known.
  • Commentators: Henry opens the section: "Judgment began at the house of God… but it must not end there." He notes that Ammon had the least reason to gloat, since it had narrowly escaped being besieged first (21:20), and that "their calamities have been their neighbours' entertainments." JFB notes that the fall of the sanctuary was seen as "the triumph of heathenism over the rival claims of Jehovah," which is why God takes it so personally. Guzik, citing Feinberg, links the chapter with 1 Peter 4:17: if judgment begins with God's household, the nations cannot expect to escape. He also observes that Ezekiel's list leaves out Babylon, since it was God's instrument at that time.

Prophecy Against Moab and Seir (vv8–11)

  • "Because Moab and Seir said, 'Behold, the house of Judah is like all the other nations'" (v8). Moab lay east of the Dead Sea, south of Ammon. Like Ammon it was descended from Lot (Gen 19:37). Seir is the mountain country of Edom, south of the Dead Sea, and is mentioned here briefly before Edom gets its own oracle. Their taunt was theological. Israel claimed to be a people set apart by the one true God. Moab now said: look, they are no different from anyone else. Their God could not protect them. Their claim to be special was empty.
  • The irony. Judah had in fact tried to be "like the nations," worshiping their gods and copying their ways. The exiles had even said, "Let us be like the nations… and worship wood and stone" (20:32). God had answered that it would never happen. The Tyndale notes point out that Moab's taunt was false for that reason: whatever Judah had done, God's covenant with it still stood. Henry observes that "when those that profess religion walk unworthy of their profession they encourage the enemies of religion to hope that it will in time sink."
  • The Mesha Stone. An inscription found in 1868 at Dhiban in Jordan, from around 840 BC, records Mesha king of Moab boasting that his god Chemosh had driven Israel out of his land and that "Israel has perished forever." Moab had been saying this sort of thing for a long time (compare 2 Kings 3:4–27).
  • "I will lay open the flank of Moab… Beth-jeshimoth, Baal-meon, and Kiriathaim" (v9). "Flank" is literally "shoulder," the slope of the Moabite plateau. These three towns guarded Moab's northern frontier and were called "the glory of the country." All three had been given to the tribe of Reuben when Israel entered the land (Num 32:37–38; Josh 13:17–20), and Moab later took them back. The Mesha Stone mentions Mesha rebuilding Baal-meon and Kiriathaim. Moab's proudest towns, once taken from Israel, would be thrown open to invaders.
  • "I will give it along with the Ammonites to the people of the East as a possession" (v10). The same fate as Ammon. Moab, too, vanished as a nation. Jeremiah's long oracle against Moab (Jer 48) covers similar ground and ends with a similar promise of restoration "in the latter days" (Jer 48:47). Ruth the Moabite, great-grandmother of David and ancestor of Jesus (Ruth 4:17; Matt 1:5), is a reminder that God's purposes for Moab were not only judgment.
  • Commentators: JFB notes that Moab, Seir and Ammon were neighboring lands "stretching in one line from Gilead on the north to the Red Sea," so they naturally acted together against Judah. JFB reads the taunt as saying it is "of no use to them to serve Jehovah." Henry says that "those who judge only by outward appearance are ready to conclude that the people of God have lost all their privileges when they have lost their worldly prosperity." Guzik, citing Block, adds that the taunt was "a direct assault on Yahweh himself," since God had given Israel its special status.

Prophecy Against Edom (vv12–14)

  • "Because Edom acted revengefully against the house of Judah" (v12). Edom, south and southeast of the Dead Sea, descended from Esau, Jacob's twin brother. The bitterness went back to the brothers' rivalry (Gen 27:41). Edom refused Israel passage in the wilderness (Num 20:14–21), was ruled by David and later rebelled (2 Kings 8:20). "Acted revengefully… taking vengeance" uses the same Hebrew root twice. JFB renders it "revenging with revengement," a vengeance that went beyond hatred to settled, deep-brooding spite.
  • What did Edom do? The details come from other texts. Psalm 137:7 remembers Edomites shouting, "Lay it bare, lay it bare, down to its foundations!" as Jerusalem fell. Obadiah condemns Edom for standing aloof, gloating, looting and handing over fugitives at the crossroads (Obad 10–14). Lamentations 4:21–22 addresses "daughter of Edom" directly. Ammon and Moab gloated. Edom joined in. Archaeology shows Edomites moving into southern Judah after the exile, and the region later became known as Idumea.
  • "From Teman even to Dedan they shall fall by the sword" (v13). Teman was a region in Edom, famous for its wise men (Jer 49:7; Job 2:11). Dedan was an oasis in northern Arabia to the southeast. Together they mark the whole land, as "from Dan to Beersheba" did for Israel. Edom will be desolate from end to end, "man and beast."
  • "I will lay my vengeance upon Edom by the hand of my people Israel" (v14). Unusually, Israel itself is named as God's instrument. Most commentators see this fulfilled in the second century BC. Judas Maccabeus defeated the Idumeans (1 Macc 5:3, 65), and around 125 BC the Jewish ruler John Hyrcanus conquered them and forced them to be circumcised and live under Jewish law. JFB notes the irony that, by Christ's time, an Idumean family, the Herods, ruled the Jews. Edom had been absorbed into Israel.
  • "They shall know my vengeance" (v14). "Vengeance" in Scripture is not a private grudge. It is the just repayment of wrong by the rightful judge. Edom took vengeance for itself, and now it meets God's. Paul draws on this principle: "Never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God, for it is written, 'Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord'" (Rom 12:19, quoting Deut 32:35).
  • Commentators: Henry notes that the Edomites "had of old been tributaries to the Jews, according to the sentence that the elder should serve the younger," and took their chance for revenge. JFB traces the grudge back to Jacob's taking of Isaac's blessing and links the fulfilment to Judas Maccabeus and John Hyrcanus. Guzik agrees and cites Taylor that what Edom actually did cannot be known in detail, though it plainly sided with Babylon. Edom gets a second, longer oracle in chapter 35.

Prophecy Against Philistia (vv15–17)

  • "Because the Philistines acted revengefully and took vengeance with malice of soul" (v15). The Philistines lived on the coastal plain southwest of Judah in five main cities: Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Ekron and Gath. Unlike the others in this chapter, they were not related to Israel. Most scholars think they came from the Aegean as part of the "Sea Peoples" around 1200 BC (Amos 9:7 says from Caphtor, probably Crete). They fought Israel from the days of Samson, Saul and David onward, and as recently as the reign of Ahaz they had taken towns in Judah (2 Chron 28:18).
  • "To destroy in never-ending enmity" (v15). "Never-ending" is literally "everlasting." Their hatred was ancient and unbroken. This is the same "perpetual enmity" charged against Edom in 35:5.
  • "I will cut off the Cherethites" (v16). The Cherethites were a group among the Philistines, probably from Crete (1 Sam 30:14; Zeph 2:5). David's bodyguard included Cherethites and Pelethites (2 Sam 8:18; 15:18). In Hebrew there is a wordplay: hikratti 'et-keretim, "I will cut off the cutters," since karat means "to cut off." "The rest of the seacoast" means whatever was left of the Philistine cities.
  • "I will execute great vengeance on them with wrathful rebukes" (v17). Babylon took the Philistine cities in its campaigns of 604 BC and after. Ashkelon was destroyed in 604 BC, and archaeologists have found the burned ruins. Babylonian ration lists from the same years name the sons of Ashkelon's king among the captives held in Babylon. The Philistines never recovered as a separate people.
  • Commentators: JFB notes the "play on similar sounds in the Hebrew," which he renders "I will slay the slayers," and observes that the Cherethites excelled in archery. Henry draws the chapter's lesson for Israel: God's anger with Judah's enemies is "an encouragement to Israel to believe that though he had dealt thus severely with them yet he had not cast them off." Guzik ends on the purpose that runs through every oracle here: that the nations, too, would know that the LORD is God.

Pointing to Christ

Every nation in this chapter is judged for what it did to God's people when they were down: laughing, taunting, taking revenge. Behind it is the promise to Abraham, "him who dishonors you I will curse" (Gen 12:3). Jesus takes up the same principle when he tells the nations that what they did "to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me" (Matt 25:40), and when he asks Saul, "why are you persecuting me?" (Acts 9:4). He so binds himself to his people that their injuries become his. Yet Jesus also does what none of these nations did. When his enemies mocked and struck him, he did not take revenge, but "continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23). And the gospel goes out to the very peoples condemned here. A Moabite woman was already in his family line, and Paul speaks of a gospel in which Gentiles "who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ" (Eph 2:13).

Reflection

  • Mocking Another's Pain (v.3) — Ammon's single word of gloating over the Temple's fall is enough to draw God's specific judgment. Do you quietly enjoy watching someone you resent go through a hard season, without realizing what that reaction reveals about your own heart?
  • Denying What Makes God's People Different (v.8) — Moab's sin is treating Judah as no different from any other nation, dismissing the reality of her covenant relationship with God. Are you tempted to view faith communities as interchangeable, missing what actually sets a relationship with God apart?
  • Betrayal Cuts Deepest From Family (v.12) — Edom, Israel's own blood relative through Esau, takes vengeance on their kin instead of showing solidarity in their suffering. How do you treat fellow believers who are struggling: with the loyalty of family, or the opportunism of a stranger?
  • Judgment Aimed at Knowledge, Not Just Punishment (v.17) — Even this judgment on pagan nations ends with the purpose that they would know God is the Lord. When you face consequences for wrongdoing, do you walk away merely punished, or actually knowing God better?
