1Woe to you, O destroyer never destroyed, O traitor never betrayed! When you have finished destroying, you will be destroyed. When you have finished betraying, you will be betrayed.

2O LORD, be gracious to us! We wait for You. Be our strength every morning and our salvation in time of trouble.

3The peoples flee the thunder of Your voice; the nations scatter when You rise.

4Your spoil, O nations, is gathered as by locusts; like a swarm of locusts men sweep over it.

5The LORD is exalted, for He dwells on high; He has filled Zion with justice and righteousness.

6He will be the sure foundation for your times, a storehouse of salvation, wisdom, and knowledge. The fear of the LORD is Zion’s treasure.

7Behold, their valiant ones cry aloud in the streets; the envoys of peace weep bitterly.

8The highways are deserted; travel has ceased. The treaty has been broken, the witnesses are despised, and human life is disregarded.

9The land mourns and languishes; Lebanon is ashamed and decayed. Sharon is like a desert; Bashan and Carmel shake off their leaves.

10“Now I will arise,” says the LORD. “Now I will lift Myself up. Now I will be exalted.

11You conceive chaff; you give birth to stubble. Your breath is a fire that will consume you.

12The peoples will be burned to ashes, like thorns cut down and set ablaze.

13You who are far off, hear what I have done; you who are near, acknowledge My might.”

14The sinners in Zion are afraid; trembling grips the ungodly: “Who of us can dwell with a consuming fire? Who of us can dwell with everlasting flames?”

15He who walks righteously and speaks with sincerity, who refuses gain from extortion, whose hand never takes a bribe, who stops his ears against murderous plots and shuts his eyes tightly against evil—

16he will dwell on the heights; the mountain fortress will be his refuge; his food will be provided and his water assured.

17Your eyes will see the King in His beauty and behold a land that stretches afar.

18Your mind will ponder the former terror: “Where is he who tallies? Where is he who weighs? Where is he who counts the towers?”

19You will no longer see the insolent, a people whose speech is unintelligible, who stammer in a language you cannot understand.

20Look upon Zion, the city of our appointed feasts. Your eyes will see Jerusalem, a peaceful pasture, a tent that does not wander; its tent pegs will not be pulled up, nor will any of its cords be broken.

21But there the Majestic One, our LORD, will be for us a place of rivers and wide canals, where no galley with oars will row, and no majestic vessel will pass.

22For the LORD is our Judge, the LORD is our lawgiver, the LORD is our King. It is He who will save us.

23Your ropes are slack; they cannot secure the mast or spread the sail. Then an abundance of spoils will be divided, and even the lame will carry off plunder.

24And no resident of Zion will say, “I am sick.” The people who dwell there will be forgiven of iniquity.

Summary

Isaiah pronounces judgment on a treacherous destroyer, understood as Assyria, warning that the one who betrays others without being betrayed will itself be betrayed once its destruction is complete (v. 1). He prays for God's grace and strength each morning in this time of trouble (v. 2), describing how nations scatter and plunder is seized the instant the Lord rises in power (vv. 3-4). The Lord is exalted, filling Zion with justice and becoming his people's stability, wisdom, and treasure (vv. 5-6), even as the land mourns around them — roads abandoned, agreements broken, Lebanon, Sharon, and Carmel withering (vv. 7-9). God announces he will now act, and his enemies' plans will burn like stubble, consumed by their own hostility (vv. 10-12). Both near and far are called to witness his power, and the sinners in Zion tremble, asking who can survive his consuming presence (vv. 13-14). The answer: those who live honestly, refuse bribes and violence, and turn from evil will dwell securely, provided for, beholding the king in his beauty (vv. 15-17). Invasion and terror will become a distant memory; Jerusalem will stand as a peaceful, unshakable dwelling, with the Lord himself as its defense, like broad rivers no enemy ship can sail (vv. 18-21). He is Zion's judge, lawgiver, and king who saves it, and its people, no longer sick, will be forgiven their sin (vv. 22-24).

Authorship & Background

Author: Isaiah son of Amoz (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Woe Oracle — Sixth Woe (chs. 28-33)
Key Themes: The destroyer destroyed, prayer for God to rise up, sinners trembling in Zion, the character of those who survive, seeing the King in His beauty, the land of broad rivers, God as judge-lawgiver-king-savior
Historical Context: The final woe of the series (chs. 28-33) is directed at the "treacherous dealer" — Assyria, which broke treaties with smaller nations after extorting tribute from them. Sennacherib demanded tribute, received it, then attacked anyway (2 Kings 18:14-17). The chapter moves from lament to prayer to vision — ending with one of Isaiah's most beautiful depictions of the future Zion: a city of peace with broad rivers, where God Himself is judge, lawgiver, king, and savior (v.22).
Woe to Thee That Spoilest (v.1): Woe to thee that spoilest, and thou wast not spoiled, and dealest treacherously, and they dealt not treacherously with thee. One verse, and it is addressed to a power that has broken faith without having been wronged first, which is what Assyria did to Hezekiah after the tribute of 2 Kings 18 was paid. The sentence is stated as a turn-taking, when thou shalt cease to spoil, thou shalt be spoiled, and when thou shalt make an end to deal treacherously, they shall deal treacherously with thee.
O LORD, Be Gracious unto Us (vv.2-6): The oracle becomes a prayer in the first person plural, O LORD, be gracious unto us, we have waited for thee, be thou their arm every morning, our salvation also in the time of trouble. Then the enemy dispersing and the spoil being collected in a picture drawn from insects, as the running to and fro of locusts shall he run upon them. And the section ends on a list of what the LORD supplies, and it is arranged as a treasury, wisdom and knowledge, the stability of thy times, and the fear of the LORD is his treasure. Stability listed as an asset, which for a small state under siege is exactly what it was.
The Highways Lie Waste (vv.7-9): Behold, their valiant ones shall cry without, the ambassadors of peace shall weep bitterly. The diplomats are weeping because the diplomacy has failed, and the failure is described as a breach of agreement, he hath broken the covenant, he hath despised the cities, he regardeth no man. Then the effect on ordinary movement, which is the most concrete measure of a country under occupation, the highways lie waste, the wayfaring man ceaseth. And the land is named region by region, Lebanon, Sharon, Bashan and Carmel, all of them proverbial for fertility and all of them listed as failing.
Now Will I Rise (vv.10-13): Now will I rise, saith the LORD, now will I be exalted. The enemy's effort is dismissed as producing nothing usable, ye shall conceive chaff, ye shall bring forth stubble. Then the fire, and the people shall be as the burnings of lime, as thorns cut up shall they be burned in the fire, which is material that burns fast and leaves nothing. And the audience is widened at the end, hear, ye that are far off, what I have done, and ye that are near, behold my might.
Who Shall Dwell with Everlasting Burnings (vv.14-16): The sinners in Zion are afraid, fearfulness hath surprised the hypocrites, and they ask the question the section turns on, who among us shall dwell with the devouring fire, who among us shall dwell with everlasting burnings. The answer is unusual in this book for being a list of ordinary conduct rather than of belief: he that walketh righteously, and speaketh uprightly, he that despiseth the gain of oppressions, that shaketh his hands from holding of bribes, that stoppeth his ears from hearing of blood, and shutteth his eyes from seeing evil. Four of the six are things a person declines to do. And the promise attached is domestic, bread shall be given him, his waters shall be sure.
The King in His Beauty (vv.17-24): Thine eyes shall see the king in his beauty, they shall behold the land that is very far off. Then a backward glance at the occupation which is one of the sharpest things in the book, thine heart shall meditate terror, where is the scribe, where is the receiver, where is he that counted the towers. The men remembered from the worst years are the tax assessors and the surveyors, not the soldiers. And the language is named as the thing that made them frightening, thou shalt not see a fierce people, of a stammering tongue, that thou canst not understand. The city is then described as permanent camping, a tabernacle that shall not be taken down, not one of the stakes thereof shall ever be removed. And the last verse settles two things at once, and the inhabitant shall not say, I am sick, for the people that dwell therein shall be forgiven.

Deep Dive

Setting: This is the sixth and last of the "woes" in chapters 28–33, and the most hopeful. The crisis is Sennacherib's invasion of Judah in 701 BC. Second Kings 18:13–17 fills in the background: when the Assyrians took Judah's fortified cities, Hezekiah stripped the temple and palace to pay a huge tribute to buy peace, and Sennacherib took the money and sent his army against Jerusalem anyway. Chapter 33 speaks into that betrayal. It moves quickly between voices, like a temple liturgy: a woe on the destroyer, a prayer from God's people, a lament over the ruined land, God's answer, a searching question about who can live near a holy God, and a closing vision of the King in his beauty and Zion at peace.

O LORD, Be Gracious to Us (vv1–24)

  • "Ah, you destroyer, who yourself have not been destroyed" (v1). "Ah" translates hoy, the same "woe" that opened 28:1, 29:1, 29:15, 30:1 and 31:1. The first five woes fell on Judah and its leaders. This last one falls on the enemy. Assyria is not named, but the Tyndale notes say it was surely in Isaiah's mind, while the principle applies to every power that preys on God's people.
  • "You traitor, whom none has betrayed!" (v1). Assyria had not been wronged by Judah in a way that justified this, and it broke its own deal by taking Hezekiah's tribute and attacking anyway. "When you have finished betraying, they will betray you." The destroyer will be destroyed and the betrayer betrayed. Sennacherib was in fact murdered by two of his own sons while worshiping in his god's temple (37:38). Jesus states the same principle: "with the measure you use it will be measured to you" (Matt 7:2).
  • "O LORD, be gracious to us; we wait for you" (v2). Suddenly the people speak, and they pray. This is the response God had been waiting for: "the LORD waits to be gracious to you… blessed are all those who wait for him" (30:18). After chapters of running to Egypt, Judah finally waits on God. "Be our arm every morning." The "arm" is strength in action (Exod 15:16; Isa 40:10). They ask for it daily, not once for all.
  • "At the tumultuous noise peoples flee" (v3). The prayer turns to confidence. The "noise" is God's thunder (29:6; 30:30). When he rises, the nations scatter.
  • "Your spoil is gathered as the caterpillar gathers; as locusts leap, it is leapt upon" (v4). Locusts stripping a field leave nothing behind. That is how Jerusalem's people will strip the abandoned Assyrian camp after the army is struck down (37:36). The people who had paid tribute will collect plunder.
  • "The LORD is exalted, for he dwells on high" (v5). Not Sennacherib, whose inscriptions call him "the great king, the mighty king, king of the universe," but the LORD. "He will fill Zion with justice and righteousness" is the same pair as 32:1, 16.
  • "He will be the stability of your times" (v6). "Stability" is 'emunah, faithfulness, firmness. In a time when every human alliance shifted, God is the one fixed point. "The fear of the LORD is Zion's treasure." Hezekiah had just emptied the treasury to pay Assyria (2 Kings 18:15–16). Isaiah says Zion's real treasure was never in the treasury. It is reverence for God, which is also "the beginning of wisdom" (Prov 9:10). Many readers take "his treasure" as God's own treasure, the reverence of his people, which is grammatically possible.
  • "Behold, their heroes cry in the streets; the envoys of peace weep bitterly" (v7). Now a lament over the present. "Heroes" is a rare word, 'er'ellam, found only here. Its meaning is uncertain: "valiant ones" (KJV), "heroes" (ESV), or possibly a word related to "Ariel" (29:1). The "envoys of peace" are the officials who negotiated and paid the tribute, now weeping because the deal has been betrayed. Compare the three officials who came to Hezekiah "with their clothes torn" (36:22).
  • "The highways lie waste… covenants are broken; cities are despised; there is no regard for man" (v8). Trade and travel stop when an army is in the land (Judg 5:6). Sennacherib broke his agreement, despised the cities he had sworn to spare, and showed no respect for human life.
  • "Lebanon is confounded… Sharon is like a desert, and Bashan and Carmel shake off their leaves" (v9). These are four of the most fertile regions of the land, the cedar mountains of the north, the coastal plain of Sharon, the high pastures of Bashan east of Galilee, and the wooded ridge of Carmel. The whole land seems to wilt in mourning. Isaiah will reverse every one of these names in 35:2: "the glory of Lebanon… the majesty of Carmel and Sharon."
  • "'Now I will arise,' says the LORD, 'now I will lift myself up; now I will be exalted'" (v10). Three times "now." God has waited, his people have prayed, and the moment has come. This answers the prayer of v2 and the waiting of 30:18.
  • "You conceive chaff; you give birth to stubble; your breath is a fire that will consume you" (v11). Assyria's plans are like a pregnancy that produces nothing but dry straw. Its own fury, its "breath," is the fire that burns it up. The Tyndale notes point out the irony: Assyria made other nations into chaff (17:13; 29:5), and now it gets the same treatment.
  • "Burned to lime" (v12). Lime was made by burning limestone or bones at high heat until only white powder was left (Amos 2:1). Thorns burn fast and leave nothing.
  • "Hear, you who are far off… and you who are near" (v13). The whole world, near and far, is invited to recognize what God has done. Paul borrows the phrase for Gentiles and Jews brought together in Christ (Eph 2:17).
  • "The sinners in Zion are afraid… 'Who among us can dwell with the consuming fire?'" (v14). Here is the surprise. The fire that burned Assyria does not stop at the border. It also exposes the godless inside Zion. "Consuming fire" recalls Sinai, where God's glory looked "like a devouring fire on the top of the mountain" (Exod 24:17), and Moses' warning, "the LORD your God is a consuming fire" (Deut 4:24). Hebrews 12:29 repeats it for the church. The question is not who can survive hell, but who can live near God himself. Henry: "God himself is this devouring fire."
  • The answer (vv15–16). It is the same kind of entrance question found in Psalms 15 and 24 ("Who shall ascend the hill of the LORD?"). The person who can dwell with God is described in six phrases: he walks righteously, speaks uprightly, despises gain from oppression, shakes his hands so a bribe won't stick to them, stops his ears from plans of bloodshed, and shuts his eyes to evil. Each one is the opposite of what Jerusalem's leaders were doing (1:23; 5:23). Hands, ears and eyes: the whole person turns away from evil.
  • Is this salvation by good works? No. The Tyndale notes observe that a godly life is the evidence that someone truly knows God (Jas 2:14–18; 1 John 1:5–6). The chapter ends with forgiveness (v24), and the whole Bible makes clear that no one stands before the fire on his own record. But Isaiah will not let anyone claim to belong to God while living like the godless.
  • "He will dwell on the heights… his bread will be given him; his water will be sure" (v16). In a city under siege, bread and water were the first things to run out. The person who dwells with God will be kept, like Elijah fed by ravens (1 Kings 17:6).
  • "Your eyes will behold the king in his beauty" (v17). This is one of the most beloved lines in Isaiah. In the immediate sense it may mean Hezekiah, no longer in sackcloth (37:1) but in royal robes, with the siege lifted. But the vision grows past him: "a land that stretches afar," literally "a land of distances," no longer hemmed in by enemy armies. And v22 says the real King is the LORD himself. The Tyndale notes take the human king as God's representative under the Great King.
  • "Your heart will muse on the terror: 'Where is he who counted…?'" (v18). Looking back, the people will remember the fear as a past thing. The officials who "counted" (the scribe who recorded the tribute), "weighed the tribute" (the official who weighed the silver), and "counted the towers" (the officer who surveyed the walls for attack) are all gone. Paul echoes the form of this verse in 1 Corinthians 1:20: "Where is the one who is wise? Where is the scribe?"
  • "The people of an obscure speech that you cannot comprehend" (v19). Assyrian was a Semitic language related to Hebrew but not understood by ordinary people (36:11). Isaiah had warned that God would speak to his people "by people of strange lips" (28:11). Now those foreign voices will be heard no more.
  • "Behold Zion, the city of our appointed feasts!… an immovable tent" (v20). "Appointed feasts" are the pilgrim festivals, Passover, Pentecost and Tabernacles. During a siege no one could come up to them. Zion is pictured as a tent, but one whose stakes are never pulled up, a deliberate contrast with Israel's wandering years in the wilderness. "Untroubled habitation" uses sha'anan, the word for the false security of the women in 32:9, now a true security.
  • "The LORD in majesty will be for us a place of broad rivers and streams, where no galley with oars can go" (v21). Jerusalem had no river, unlike Nineveh on the Tigris, Babylon on the Euphrates or Thebes on the Nile. Great rivers brought wealth and defense, but also enemy warships. God himself will be Jerusalem's river: all the blessing, and no route for an enemy fleet. Compare Psalm 46:4: "There is a river whose streams make glad the city of God."
  • "The LORD is our judge; the LORD is our lawgiver; the LORD is our king; he will save us" (v22). The three roles cover all of government. Israel looked to other nations for kings, to Egypt for help and to their own advisers for law. God claims all three, and all three end in "he will save us." JFB sees a hint of the Trinity in the threefold "LORD." That is a devotional reading the verse does not require. The plain point is that God alone is enough.
  • "Your cords hang loose; they cannot hold the mast firm" (v23). The ship picture from v21 continues, and it is most naturally read as the enemy pictured as a ship whose rigging has failed. Some take "your" as Zion's ship, weak and unseaworthy, which God saves anyway. Either way the result follows: "even the lame will take the prey." The battle is so completely won by God that even those who could not fight walk in to collect the spoil. JFB suggests the plunder included the very tribute Hezekiah had paid.
  • "No inhabitant will say, 'I am sick'; the people who dwell there will be forgiven their iniquity" (v24). The chapter's last word goes to the root. Sickness and sin are linked, not because every illness is caused by a particular sin, but because a world without sin will be a world without sickness (Rev 21:4). The deepest rescue is not from Assyria. It is forgiveness. Hezekiah himself was soon to be deathly sick and healed (ch 38).
  • Commentators: Henry sees the prayer of v2 as the turning point, "Men are cruel; be thou gracious," and he lands the chapter on its last verse: "Sin is the sickness of the soul," and "when God pardons the sin he heals the disease." On v21 he writes, "If God himself be the river, it must needs be inaccessible to the enemy." JFB connects v8 directly to Sennacherib taking Hezekiah's money and attacking anyway, and he reads the King of v17 as the Messiah rather than Hezekiah, and the far land as heaven itself. Henry instead applies v17 first to Hezekiah appearing in public again after the siege. Guzik holds both: Hezekiah in the immediate sense, Jesus in the ultimate one. He adds that seeing the King face to face is the great joy of heaven (1 Cor 13:12), and that the scars Jesus kept are part of his beauty. Guzik also notes that 'er'ellam in v7 occurs only here and its meaning is uncertain.

Pointing to Christ

The question of v14 is the question every person must face: who can live with the consuming fire? Isaiah's answer describes a person whose hands, ears, eyes and words are clean, and only one person has ever fully matched it. Jesus is the righteous one who can stand in God's presence, and he brings forgiven sinners in with him (Heb 10:19–22). The "king in his beauty" whom Hezekiah faintly pictured is the King John saw, whose face will be seen by his servants (Rev 22:3–4). In the city he rules, the river flows from God's throne (Rev 22:1), the lame share the spoil of a victory they did not win, and no one says "I am sick," because every inhabitant has been forgiven.

Reflection

  • New Strength Needed Each Morning (v.2) — The prayer asks God to be His people's strength every single morning, not just once for all time. Are you relying on a past experience of God's help, or seeking fresh strength from Him today?
  • Who Can Dwell with the Fire? (v.14) — Sinners in Zion tremble and ask who can survive in the presence of a holy, consuming God. What does your own answer to that question reveal about how seriously you take God's holiness?
  • Seeing the King in His Beauty (v.17) — The promise held out to the righteous is not merely escape from judgment but actually seeing the King in His beauty. Does the hope of seeing Christ face to face shape your daily obedience, or is fear of punishment your main motivator?
  • One God for Every Need (v.22) — The LORD is named as judge, lawgiver, king, and savior all at once, one Person covering every role His people need. Are you looking to different places for guidance, authority, and rescue instead of trusting God to be all of it?
