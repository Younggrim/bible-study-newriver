Summary

God shows Amos three visions of judgment — a swarm of locusts and a consuming fire, both of which Amos intercedes against and God relents (vv. 1-6) — followed by a plumb line held against a wall, symbolizing that God will no longer overlook Israel's crookedness (vv. 7-9). Amaziah, the priest of Bethel, reports Amos to King Jeroboam and orders him to flee back to Judah and never prophesy at Bethel again, the king's sanctuary (vv. 10-13). Amos responds that he was no professional prophet but a shepherd and fig grower whom the LORD took and commanded to prophesy to Israel, then pronounces judgment on Amaziah and his family (vv. 14-17).

Authorship & Background

Author: Amos, a shepherd from Tekoa in Judah who prophesied to the northern kingdom (approximately 760-750 BC). Key theme: social justice — God judges nations (including Israel) for oppressing the poor. "Let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream" (5:24).
Classification: Prophetic Visions and Narrative — Locusts, Fire, the Plumbline, and Amaziah's Opposition
Key Themes: God's mercy delaying judgment (twice), the plumbline of absolute standard, confrontation with religious establishment, the calling of a layman-prophet, the cost of speaking truth
Historical Context: Chapter 7 shifts from oracles to visions and narrative. Three visions show God's judgment: locusts (v.1), fire (v.4), and a plumbline (v.7). Twice Amos intercedes successfully and God relents (vv.1-6). But the third time — the plumbline — God declares "I will not again pass by them" (v.8). The standard has been measured; Israel fails. Then comes the dramatic confrontation with Amaziah, the priest of Bethel, who tells Amos to go home. Amos's response is one of the most powerful prophet-defenses in Scripture: "I was no prophet... but the LORD took me."
First Vision: Locusts, and God Repents (vv.1-3): The book turns from oracles to visions, and the first two follow an identical pattern. Locusts are formed to devour the late growth, Amos intercedes -- cease, I beseech thee, by whom shall Jacob arise, for he is small -- and the LORD repents of it. The intercession works, and the ground of it is Jacob's weakness rather than any merit.
Second Vision: Fire, and God Repents (vv.4-6): A fire that devours the great deep and begins to eat up a part. The same plea in the same words, and the same answer: this also shall not be. Two successful intercessions establish that the prophet's prayer is genuinely effective, which is what makes the third vision land as hard as it does.
Third Vision: The Plumbline (vv.7-9): The third vision has no intercession recorded, because the measurement is already taken. A plumbline is a builder's tool for testing whether a wall is true, and the verdict is I will not again pass by them. Amos does not plead this time, and the text does not say why. The high places of Isaac and the sanctuaries of Israel are named, and the house of Jeroboam is threatened with the sword.
Amaziah: Go, Flee Thou to Judah (vv.10-13): The only narrative in the book, and it interrupts the visions. Amaziah, priest of Bethel, reports Amos to the king and then tells him to leave: flee thou away into the land of Judah, prophesy not again at Bethel, for it is the king's chapel. The phrase gives the whole problem away -- a sanctuary described as belonging to the king rather than to God, and a prophet judged on jurisdiction rather than truth.
I Was No Prophet: The LORD Took Me (vv.14-17): Amos's answer refuses the professional category entirely: I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son, but a herdman and a gatherer of sycomore fruit, and the LORD took me as I followed the flock. His authority is a calling rather than a credential, which is exactly what Amaziah cannot argue with. The reply then turns on Amaziah personally, and it is the most specific judgment in the book.

Reflection

  • Intercession Can Delay Judgment (vv.2-3) — Amos prays over the vision of locusts and God relents, showing that intercession genuinely moves God's response. Who in your life or community would benefit from you actually praying for them rather than just worrying about them?
  • The Plumb Line Reveals the True Standard (v.7) — After two visions of mercy, the third shows God measuring Israel against an unbending standard they fail. What would it look like to honestly measure your life against God's standard instead of comparing yourself to people around you?
  • A Point Where God Says No More (v.8) — God declares He will no longer pass over Israel's sin, showing that patience is real but not infinite. Is there something you've been putting off making right, assuming there will always be more time?
  • Religious Leaders Can Resist God's Word (v.12) — Amaziah, a priest, is the one who tries to silence Amos and send him away. Where might you be more resistant to correction from within your own faith community than from outside it?
  • God Qualifies the Called, Not the Credentialed (v.14) — Amos defends his ministry by pointing out he was simply a herdsman, not a trained prophet, when God sent him. What has God put in front of you that you've hesitated to step into because you don't feel qualified enough?
