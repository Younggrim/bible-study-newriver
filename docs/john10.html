1“Truly, truly, I tell you, whoever does not enter the sheepfold by the gate, but climbs in some other way, is a thief and a robber.

2But the one who enters by the gate is the shepherd of the sheep.

3The gatekeeper opens the gate for him, and the sheep listen for his voice. He calls his own sheep by name and leads them out.

4When he has brought out all his own, he goes on ahead of them, and his sheep follow him because they know his voice.

5But they will never follow a stranger; in fact, they will flee from him because they do not recognize his voice.”

6Jesus spoke to them using this illustration, but they did not understand what He was telling them.

7So He said to them again, “Truly, truly, I tell you, I am the gate for the sheep.

8All who came before Me were thieves and robbers, but the sheep did not listen to them.

9I am the gate. If anyone enters through Me, he will be saved. He will come in and go out and find pasture.

10The thief comes only to steal and kill and destroy. I have come that they may have life, and have it in all its fullness.

11I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down His life for the sheep.

12The hired hand is not the shepherd, and the sheep are not his own. When he sees the wolf coming, he abandons the sheep and runs away. Then the wolf pounces on them and scatters the flock.

13The man runs away because he is a hired servant and is unconcerned for the sheep.

14I am the good shepherd. I know My sheep and My sheep know Me,

15just as the Father knows Me and I know the Father. And I lay down My life for the sheep.

16I have other sheep that are not of this fold. I must bring them in as well, and they will listen to My voice. Then there will be one flock and one shepherd.

17The reason the Father loves Me is that I lay down My life in order to take it up again.

18No one takes it from Me, but I lay it down of My own accord. I have authority to lay it down and authority to take it up again. This charge I have received from My Father.”

19Again there was division among the Jews because of Jesus’ message.

20Many of them said, “He is demon-possessed and insane. Why would you listen to Him?”

21But others replied, “These are not the words of a man possessed by a demon. Can a demon open the eyes of the blind?”

22At that time the Feast of Dedication took place in Jerusalem. It was winter,

23and Jesus was walking in the temple courts in Solomon’s Colonnade.

24So the Jews gathered around Him and demanded, “How long will You keep us in suspense? If You are the Christ, tell us plainly.”

25“I already told you,” Jesus replied, “but you did not believe. The works I do in My Father’s name testify on My behalf.

26But because you are not My sheep, you refuse to believe.

27My sheep listen to My voice; I know them, and they follow Me.

28I give them eternal life, and they will never perish. No one can snatch them out of My hand.

29My Father who has given them to Me is greater than all. No one can snatch them out of My Father’s hand.

30I and the Father are one.”

31At this, the Jews again picked up stones to stone Him.

32But Jesus responded, “I have shown you many good works from the Father. For which of these do you stone Me?”

33“We are not stoning You for any good work,” said the Jews, “but for blasphemy, because You, who are a man, make Yourself out to be God.”

34Jesus replied, “Is it not written in your Law: ‘I have said you are gods’?

35If he called them gods to whom the word of God came—and the Scripture cannot be broken—

36then what about the One whom the Father sanctified and sent into the world? How then can you accuse Me of blasphemy for stating that I am the Son of God?

37If I am not doing the works of My Father, then do not believe Me.

39At this, they tried again to seize Him, but He escaped their grasp.

40Then Jesus went back across the Jordan to the place where John had first been baptizing, and He stayed there.

Summary

Jesus describes Himself first as the gate for the sheep and then as the good shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep, contrasting Himself with hired hands who abandon the flock when danger comes (vv. 1-13). He states that He knows His sheep and they know Him, that He has other sheep not of this fold whom He must also bring, and that He lays down His life of His own accord with authority to take it up again (vv. 14-18). At the Feast of Dedication in Jerusalem, Jesus tells the Jews that He and the Father are one, and they pick up stones to kill Him for blasphemy, believing He is making Himself God (vv. 22-33). Jesus defends His claim by appealing to Scripture and pointing to His works as evidence, then withdraws across the Jordan, where many come to believe in Him (vv. 34-42).

Authorship & Background

Author: The Apostle John (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Classification: Gospel — Narrative and Discourse
Key Themes: A common sheepfold and a shepherd known by voice, two I AM sayings, a life laid down rather than taken, other sheep not of this fold, and a charge of blasphemy answered from the Psalms
Historical Context: Chapter 10 contains Jesus' extended metaphor of the Good Shepherd — one of the most beloved passages in Scripture. It was delivered in the context of the Feast of Dedication (Hanukkah, v.22), which celebrated the rededication of the Temple after its desecration by Antiochus Epiphanes in 165 BC.
The Door of the Sheep (vv.1-10): Sheep from several flocks shared a stone enclosure overnight with one entrance, and each shepherd called his own out by name in the morning. That is the picture behind the discourse, and it explains why recognition is by voice rather than by sight. The first I AM saying of the chapter follows: I am the door. Verse 10 sets the contrast — the thief comes to steal and kill and destroy, and he came that they might have life more abundantly.
The Good Shepherd Giveth His Life (vv.11-18): The second I AM saying, and the definition attached to it is a death rather than a quality: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep. The hireling flees because the sheep are not his. Shepherd language is loaded in the Old Testament — God is Israel's shepherd in Psalm 23, and Ezekiel 34 condemns the shepherds who failed the flock and promises God will come himself. Verse 16's other sheep not of this fold widens it past Israel, and v.18 insists the life is laid down, not taken.
Division Among the Jews (vv.19-21): Three verses of reaction, and the split is between madness and evidence. Some say he has a devil; others answer that a devil does not open the eyes of the blind. The healing of chapter 9 is still doing work, which is why John placed these chapters together.
At the Feast of Dedication (vv.22-30): The setting is Hanukkah, commemorating the temple's rededication after Antiochus Epiphanes desecrated it in the 160s BC — a feast about cleansing God's house, which gives the question its edge. Asked plainly whether he is the Christ, he points to works and to the security of the sheep, and then states v.30: I and my Father are one.
The Charge of Blasphemy (vv.31-42): They take up stones and name the offense — that thou being a man makest thyself God — which is an accurate reading of v.30 rather than a misunderstanding. His answer quotes Psalm 82 and argues from the lesser to the greater, and then returns to the works: though ye believe not me, believe the works. He withdraws beyond Jordan, where many believed, and the chapter ends where the Gospel began, at John's baptizing place.

Map & Geography

  • Jerusalem / Solomon's Porch (v.23): Jesus walked in the Temple in Solomon's colonnade during the Feast of Dedication (Hanukkah, winter). This was a covered portico on the eastern side of the Temple.
  • Beyond the Jordan (v.40): After the Jews tried to stone Him, Jesus withdrew to "the place where John at first baptized" — Bethany beyond the Jordan (Perea). Many believed there.

Setting: There is no break in time between chapters 9 and 10. Jesus is still speaking to the Pharisees who have just thrown the healed blind man out of the synagogue (9:34–41). They claim to be Israel's guides, and they have driven out one of God's sheep. Jesus answers with the picture of a shepherd and his flock, one of the richest images in the Old Testament. God himself is Israel's shepherd (Ps 23; 80:1; Isa 40:11). Through Ezekiel he condemned Israel's leaders as false shepherds who fed themselves instead of the sheep, and promised to shepherd his people himself through a coming David (Ezek 34). The second half of the chapter jumps forward about two months to the Feast of Dedication (Hanukkah) in winter. There Jesus makes his clearest claim yet, "I and the Father are one," and the crowd again picks up stones. The chapter ends with Jesus withdrawing across the Jordan, his public ministry in Jerusalem nearly over.

I Am the Good Shepherd (vv1–21)

  • "He who does not enter the sheepfold by the door… is a thief and a robber" (v1). A sheepfold was an enclosure, often of stone walls topped with thorny branches, where sheep were kept overnight. In a village, several flocks might share one fold, guarded by a gatekeeper. A real shepherd comes through the gate. Someone climbing over the wall is up to no good. "Thief" (kleptēs) suggests stealth and "robber" (lēstēs) violence. Lēstēs is the word later used of Barabbas (18:40) and of revolutionaries. Coming right after chapter 9, the point is clear: the Pharisees who cast out the healed man are acting like intruders, not shepherds.
  • "To him the gatekeeper opens" (v3). Some try to identify the gatekeeper (John the Baptist, the Spirit, the Father), but in a parable not every detail needs a meaning. The point is that the true shepherd has the right to enter.
  • "The sheep hear his voice, and he calls his own sheep by name" (v3). Middle Eastern shepherds still know their animals individually and give them names. When several flocks share one fold, each shepherd calls, and only his own sheep come. "Leads them out" (exagō) is the word used of God leading Israel out of Egypt. It also fits chapter 9: the blind man has been "cast out" (ekballō, 9:34) of the synagogue, and Jesus is leading his own out.
  • "He goes before them, and the sheep follow him" (v4). Eastern shepherds walked ahead of their flocks and led them. They did not drive them from behind as Western herdsmen often did. Psalm 23 pictures the same: "He leads me beside still waters."
  • "A stranger they will not follow" (v5). The healed man has just shown this. He would not follow the Pharisees' voice (9:27–34), but he knew Jesus' voice (9:35–38).
  • "This figure of speech" (v6). The word is paroimia, a figurative saying or extended picture. John doesn't use the word "parable" (parabolē) that the other Gospels use. "They did not understand": the men who claimed to see (9:41) can't see what he means.
  • "I am the door of the sheep" (v7). This is the third of John's seven "I am" sayings with an image. In some field pens there was no gate at all, just an opening, and the shepherd lay down across it at night, so that nothing could get in or out except over him. Jesus is both the shepherd and the way in. There is only one door (14:6).
  • "All who came before me are thieves and robbers" (v8). This does not mean the prophets or John the Baptist, who pointed to Jesus. It refers to those who came claiming to save or lead God's people on their own terms. That would include false messiahs and revolutionaries, and the corrupt leaders Ezekiel condemned.
  • "He will be saved and will go in and out and find pasture" (v9). "Go in and out" is a Hebrew way of describing ordinary daily life lived in safety (Num 27:17; Deut 28:6; Ps 121:8). The sheep that come through Jesus are safe and also free.
  • "The thief comes only to steal and kill and destroy. I came that they may have life and have it abundantly" (v10). "Abundantly" (perisson) means more than enough, overflowing. This is not a promise of comfort or wealth. It is life in all its fullness, eternal life that begins now (17:3). The contrast is total: false leaders take life, and Jesus gives it.
  • "I am the good shepherd" (v11). "Good" is kalos, which means not only morally good but also noble, beautiful, the genuine article, the model shepherd. In Ezekiel 34:11–16 God says, "I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep," and in 34:23 he promises "one shepherd, my servant David." Jesus claims both: he is God shepherding his people, and the promised son of David.
  • "The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (v11). Shepherds did risk their lives. David fought lions and bears for his flock (1 Sam 17:34–35). But a shepherd's death usually meant disaster for the sheep. Jesus' death will be their rescue. "For" (hyper) means "on behalf of," the language of substitution (11:50–52; 15:13). This phrase, "lay down his life," appears five times in vv11–18.
  • "He who is a hired hand… sees the wolf coming and leaves the sheep and flees" (v12). A hired hand works for pay and has no stake in the flock. When danger comes, he protects himself. The wolf stands for anything that preys on God's people, including false teachers (Acts 20:29). Jesus may also be pointing to leaders who care more about their position than about people.
  • "I know my own and my own know me, just as the Father knows me and I know the Father" (vv14–15). In Scripture, "knowing" means relationship, not just information. The relationship between Jesus and his sheep is modeled on the relationship between Jesus and the Father. It is an astonishing comparison.
  • "I have other sheep that are not of this fold" (v16). The "fold" here is Israel. The "other sheep" are Gentiles, who will come in through the preaching of the apostles (11:52; 12:20–32; Acts 10). "They will listen to my voice": they will hear Jesus through his messengers. "One flock, one shepherd" echoes Ezekiel 34:23 and 37:24. The KJV reads "one fold," following Jerome's Latin Vulgate, but the Greek uses two different words: "fold" (aulē) in the first half and "flock" (poimnē) in the second. The unity is in following one shepherd, not in being inside one enclosure.
  • "I lay down my life that I may take it up again" (v17). The death and the resurrection belong together in one purpose. Elsewhere the New Testament says the Father raised Jesus (Acts 2:24; Gal 1:1), and here Jesus says he takes his life up again. Both are true, since Father and Son act together (5:19).
  • "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord" (v18). Jesus' death was not a tragic accident or a defeat. The soldiers in Gethsemane fall to the ground when he says "I am he" (18:6). He tells Pilate he would have no authority unless it were given from above (19:11). He "gave up his spirit" (19:30). "Authority" (exousia) means the right and the power. "This charge I have received from my Father": his freedom and his obedience are the same thing.
  • "He has a demon, and is insane" (v20). The listeners split again, as in 7:43 and 9:16. Some dismiss him as mad. Others reason from the evidence: "Can a demon open the eyes of the blind?" (v21). Chapter 9 hangs over the whole discussion.
  • Commentators: JFB calls the discourse "plainly… a continuation of the closing verses of the ninth chapter," and Guzik applies the "thieves and robbers" directly to the Pharisees who cast out the healed man. Henry: "Rivals with Christ are robbers of his church." Guzik describes the shepherd lying across the opening of a field pen as the "door" itself, and contrasts the stealth of a thief with the violence of a robber. JFB identifies those who "came before" as false leaders, "perverters of the people from the way of life," not the prophets. On v10 Henry writes, "Life in abundance is eternal life," and JFB says the claim to give life leaves only two options, blasphemy or "God with us." On v11 Henry calls it "the prerogative of the great Shepherd to give his life to purchase his flock." He describes hired hands as those who "love the wages more than the work," and Guzik notes that "pastor" and "shepherd" are the same word, a title that ought to be earned. On v16, JFB and Guzik both prefer "one flock" over the Vulgate and KJV's "one fold." Guzik goes further and blames the old translation for encouraging Roman Catholic claims to be the one true fold, a polemical point that not everyone accepts. Henry keeps the KJV wording but makes the same point: "One shepherd makes one fold; one Christ makes one church." JFB stresses "the absolute voluntariness" of Jesus' death in v18.

I and the Father Are One (vv22–42)

  • "The Feast of Dedication… It was winter" (v22). This is Hanukkah, celebrated in December. In 167 BC, the Syrian king Antiochus IV Epiphanes, a Greek ruler who tried to stamp out Judaism, set up an altar to Zeus in the temple and sacrificed a pig on it. Judas Maccabeus led a revolt, recaptured Jerusalem, and in 164 BC cleansed and rededicated the temple (1 Macc 4:36–59). The feast lasted eight days and was also called the Feast of Lights, because lamps were lit in homes. The later story of one day's oil lasting eight days comes from the Talmud, not from 1 Maccabees. Hanukkah celebrated God rescuing his people and a leader who delivered them. It was a festival of hopes for national liberation, and so a good moment to ask whether Jesus was the Messiah (v24).
  • "Jesus was walking in the temple, in the colonnade of Solomon" (v23). This was a covered porch along the east side of the temple's outer court, sheltered from the winter wind. It was not built by Solomon but was traditionally linked with him. The early church later met there (Acts 3:11; 5:12).
  • "How long will you keep us in suspense?" (v24). Literally, "How long will you take away our life (or breath)?", an idiom for keeping someone in suspense. "Gathered around him" (kykloō) can mean "surrounded," with a hint of threat. "If you are the Christ, tell us plainly." Jesus had avoided the title in public because people heard it in political terms (6:15). A plain "yes" would have been a pretext to hand him over to Rome as a rebel king.
  • "I told you, and you do not believe" (v25). Jesus had told them in what he said (5:17–47; 8:58) and what he did. His works speak for him.
  • "You do not believe because you are not among my sheep" (v26). He connects this back to the shepherd discourse two months earlier. The issue is not lack of evidence but lack of belonging.
  • "My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me" (v27). There are three marks of his sheep: they listen, they are known, and they follow.
  • "I give them eternal life, and they will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand" (v28). "Never" is the emphatic double negative. "Snatch" (harpazō) is the word for the wolf's attack in v12. The wolf may snatch from the hired hand, but not from this shepherd.
  • "No one is able to snatch them out of the Father's hand" (v29). The sheep are held in two hands, the Son's and the Father's. Manuscripts differ here. Several important ones read "What my Father has given me is greater than all," and others read, as the ESV does, "My Father, who has given them to me, is greater than all." Either way, the point is that the sheep are safe in the Father's keeping. These verses are central to Christian teaching on the security of believers. Reformed and Baptist readers see a promise that true believers will never finally fall away. Wesleyan and Arminian readers agree that no outside power can tear a believer away, but hold that a person may still walk away by unbelief. Both agree that the safety of the sheep rests on the shepherd's strength, not their own.
  • "I and the Father are one" (v30). "One" in Greek is neuter (hen), "one thing," not masculine (heis), "one person." Jesus does not say he and the Father are the same person, which would deny that they are distinct. Nor does he only mean they agree. In context he has just said that his hand and the Father's hand do the same work with the same power. The early church saw in this verse both the unity of God's nature and the distinction of persons. It also echoes the Shema: "The LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deut 6:4).
  • "The Jews picked up stones again to stone him" (v31). As in 8:59, they understand the claim. They try to stone him for blasphemy (Lev 24:16), and say so: "because you, being a man, make yourself God" (v33). John's readers know the truth is the reverse. He did not make himself God. He is God who became man (1:1, 14).
  • "For which of them are you going to stone me?" (v32). The question is sharp. He has healed the sick, fed the hungry and given sight to the blind. Which of those deserves death?
  • "Is it not written in your Law, 'I said, you are gods'?" (v34). This quotes Psalm 82:6. "Law" here means the Old Testament Scriptures in general (12:34; 15:25). In Psalm 82 God rebukes unjust judges and calls them "gods" (Hebrew elohim), probably because they exercised God's authority to judge (compare Exod 21:6; 22:8–9, where elohim may mean judges). Some scholars think the psalm addresses heavenly beings in God's council, and some Jewish tradition applied it to Israel at Sinai.
  • Jesus' argument (vv35–36). He argues from the lesser to the greater. If Scripture called human judges "gods," simply because God's word came to them, why is it blasphemy for the one "whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world" to call himself "the Son of God"? He is not saying he is a god only in the way those judges were. He is showing that his accusers' own Scriptures undo their charge, and then he points to his works. "Consecrated" (hagiazō) may be a deliberate link to Hanukkah, the feast of the temple's consecration. The true consecrated temple is standing in front of them.
  • "And Scripture cannot be broken" (v35). "Broken" (lyō) means loosed, annulled, set aside. Jesus rests an argument on one word of a minor psalm because he holds that every part of Scripture carries God's authority (Matt 5:17–18).
  • "Believe the works, that you may know and understand that the Father is in me and I am in the Father" (v38). Even if they can't accept his words, they could reason from his works. Mutual indwelling, the Father in the Son and the Son in the Father, explains how the two can be "one" and still be two (14:10–11; 17:21).
  • "Again they sought to arrest him, but he escaped from their hands" (v39). "Hands" echoes v28–29. They cannot take him from the Father's hand any more than they can snatch his sheep from his.
  • "Across the Jordan to the place where John had been baptizing at first" (v40). This is Bethany beyond the Jordan (1:28), in Perea. The Gospel has come full circle. Jesus' public ministry began here with John's testimony, and now it ends here, apart from the raising of Lazarus and his final entry into Jerusalem.
  • "John did no sign, but everything that John said about this man was true" (v41). John the Baptist did no miracles, but what mattered most about him was that his witness to Jesus proved true. "And many believed in him there" (v42). The contrast is pointed: rejection in the temple, faith in the wilderness.
  • Commentators: Henry calls Hanukkah a feast observed "as a good time" rather than a holy one, since it was not commanded in the Law, and JFB notes it was "instituted by Jude Maccabeus." Henry reads the demand to "tell us plainly" as a trap: if Jesus had simply said yes, they would have had grounds to accuse him as a rebel. On vv28–29 Henry says, "Those are safe who are in the hands of the Lord Jesus," and JFB says the believer's security is "founded upon the power of God," not on their own steadfastness. Guzik presents it as the sheep's safety in the shepherd's hand. On v30 JFB points to the neuter "one": "one thing," a shared essence, not one person. Like Guzik, JFB says the verse answers both errors: Arianism, which denies the Son is fully God, and Sabellianism, which makes Father and Son the same person. Henry reads it as "the oneness of the nature of Father and Son, that they are the same in substance." On Psalm 82, all three take "gods" as human judges acting with God's delegated authority. JFB calls them "the official representatives and commissioned agents of God," while Christ is the one "sanctified by the Father and sent into the world." On v35, JFB says Scripture "is itself unchangeable and indissoluble," and Henry: "The scripture cannot be broken, or broken in upon." Guzik notes that God's work went on beyond the Jordan despite the opposition in Jerusalem.

Pointing to Christ

In a chapter where religious leaders throw out a man who has been healed, Jesus shows what a true shepherd is. He knows each sheep by name, walks ahead of them, stands between them and the wolf, and lays down his life for them freely, with the authority to take it up again. He gathers sheep from outside Israel's fold into one flock. At the feast that celebrated a human deliverer and a rededicated temple, he claims to be the one the Father consecrated and sent, holding his sheep in a hand that is the Father's hand too. "I and the Father are one" is the claim that brings out the stones, and it is also the reason no one can snatch his sheep away. The good shepherd of Psalm 23 and Ezekiel 34 has come in person.

Reflection

  • Known by Name (vv.3-4) — The Good Shepherd calls his own sheep by name, and they follow him because they recognize his voice. How much time do you spend in Scripture and prayer, enough to actually recognize His voice when He speaks?
  • Abundant Life (v.10) — Jesus contrasts the thief's agenda of destruction with His own promise of life to the full. Is what you're currently pursuing the abundant life Jesus offers, or a counterfeit that only looks like it?
  • Laying Down His Life (v.11) — Unlike a hired hand who runs when danger comes, the Good Shepherd lays down His own life for the sheep. Where in your life — as a husband, father, or friend — are you tempted to be a hireling instead of staying when it costs you something?
  • A Wider Flock (v.16) — Jesus says He has other sheep not of this fold, pointing to a plan that reaches far beyond one nation or group. How does the global scope of the gospel shape how you see people who are different from you?
  • Held Secure (v.28) — Jesus promises that no one can snatch His sheep out of His hand. If you're in Christ, does that truth bring you real peace, or do you still carry uncertainty about where you stand with God?
  • I and My Father Are One (v.30) — This claim was so clear the crowd immediately picked up stones for blasphemy. How do you respond, in your own convictions, when someone challenges or denies the deity of Christ?
