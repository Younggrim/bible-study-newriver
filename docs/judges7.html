1Early in the morning Jerubbaal (that is, Gideon) and all the men with him camped beside the spring of Harod. And the camp of Midian was north of them in the valley near the hill of Moreh.

2Then the LORD said to Gideon, “You have too many men for Me to deliver Midian into their hands, lest Israel glorify themselves over Me, saying, ‘My own hand has saved me.’

3Now, therefore, proclaim in the hearing of the men: ‘Whoever is fearful and trembling may turn back and leave Mount Gilead.’” So twenty-two thousand of them turned back, but ten thousand remained.

4Then the LORD said to Gideon, “There are still too many men. Take them down to the water, and I will sift them for you there. If I say to you, ‘This one shall go with you,’ he shall go. But if I say, ‘This one shall not go with you,’ he shall not go.”

5So Gideon brought the men down to the water, and the LORD said to him, “Separate those who lap the water with their tongues like a dog from those who kneel to drink.”

6And the number of those who lapped the water with their hands to their mouths was three hundred men; all the others knelt to drink.

7Then the LORD said to Gideon, “With the three hundred men who lapped the water I will save you and deliver the Midianites into your hand. But all the others are to go home.”

8So Gideon sent the rest of the Israelites to their tents but kept the three hundred men, who took charge of the provisions and rams’ horns of the others. And the camp of Midian lay below him in the valley.

9That night the LORD said to Gideon, “Get up and go down against the camp, for I have delivered it into your hand.

10But if you are afraid to do so, then go down to the camp with your servant Purah

11and listen to what they are saying. Then your hands will be strengthened to attack the camp.” So he went with Purah his servant to the outposts where armed men were guarding the camp.

12Now the Midianites, Amalekites, and all the other people of the east had settled in the valley like a swarm of locusts, and their camels were as countless as the sand on the seashore.

13And as Gideon arrived, a man was telling his friend about a dream. “Behold, I had a dream,” he said, “and I saw a loaf of barley bread come tumbling into the Midianite camp. It struck the tent so hard that the tent overturned and collapsed.”

14His friend replied: “This is nothing less than the sword of Gideon son of Joash, the Israelite. God has delivered Midian and the whole camp into his hand.”

15When Gideon heard the dream and its interpretation, he bowed in worship. He returned to the camp of Israel and said, “Get up, for the LORD has delivered the camp of Midian into your hand.”

16And he divided the three hundred men into three companies and gave each man a ram’s horn in one hand and a large jar in the other, containing a torch.

17“Watch me and do as I do,” Gideon said. “When I come to the outskirts of the camp, do exactly as I do.

18When I and all who are with me blow our horns, then you are also to blow your horns from all around the camp and shout, ‘For the LORD and for Gideon!’”

19Gideon and the hundred men with him reached the outskirts of the camp at the beginning of the middle watch, just after the changing of the guard. They blew their horns and broke the jars that were in their hands.

20The three companies blew their horns and shattered their jars. Holding the torches in their left hands and the horns in their right hands, they shouted, “A sword for the LORD and for Gideon!”

21Each Israelite took his position around the camp, and the entire Midianite army fled, crying out as they ran.

22And when the three hundred rams’ horns sounded, the LORD set all the men in the camp against one another with their swords. The army fled to Beth-shittah toward Zererah as far as the border of Abel-meholah near Tabbath.

23Then the men of Israel were called out from Naphtali, Asher, and all Manasseh, and they pursued the Midianites.

24Gideon sent messengers throughout the hill country of Ephraim to say, “Come down against the Midianites and seize the waters of the Jordan ahead of them as far as Beth-barah.” So all the men of Ephraim were called out, and they captured the waters of the Jordan as far as Beth-barah.

25They also captured Oreb and Zeeb, the two princes of Midian; and they killed Oreb at the rock of Oreb and Zeeb at the winepress of Zeeb. So they pursued the Midianites and brought the heads of Oreb and Zeeb to Gideon on the other side of the Jordan.

Summary

The LORD tells Gideon his army of 32,000 is too large, since Israel might boast that its own strength saved it, so 22,000 fearful men are sent home, leaving 10,000 (vv. 1-3). Still too many, the LORD has Gideon sift the rest at the water, keeping only the 300 who lap water from their hands and sending the remaining 9,700 away (vv. 4-8). That night the LORD sends Gideon down to the enemy camp, where he overhears a Midianite soldier's dream of a barley loaf overturning a tent, interpreted as Gideon's coming victory, and Gideon worships before returning to rally his men (vv. 9-15). He arms his 300 with trumpets, torches, and empty jars, dividing them into three companies (v. 16). At his signal in the night, they smash their jars, blow their trumpets, and shout, throwing the vast Midianite camp into panic so that the enemy turns swords on each other and flees (vv. 19-22). Israel then pursues, and the men of Ephraim capture and kill the Midianite princes Oreb and Zeeb, bringing their heads to Gideon (vv. 23-25).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Samuel. Judges covers approximately 350 years (1380-1050 BC) between Joshua's death and the rise of the monarchy. Hebrew title: 'Shophetim' — 'Judges' (deliverers/ rulers raised by God). The book records Israel's repeated cycle: sin → oppression → crying out → deliverance → rest → sin again. The refrain: 'In those days there was no king in Israel: every man did that which was right in his own eyes' (17:6, 21:25). The book demonstrates humanity's need for a righteous king — ultimately fulfilled in Christ.
Historical Context: Chapter 7 contains one of the most famous military narratives in Scripture — Gideon's 300. God deliberately reduces Gideon's army from 32,000 to 300 so that Israel cannot claim the victory as their own. The reduction happens in two stages: first, 22,000 fearful men are sent home (v.3); then, at the water test, only 300 who lap water from their hands are retained (vv.5-7). The remaining 300 attack the vast Midianite camp at night with torches, trumpets, and empty jars — no conventional weapons. The result: God causes the Midianites to turn their swords on each other in panic (v.22), and the entire host flees. This chapter is the supreme Old Testament illustration of 2 Corinthians 4:7: "We have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the excellency of the power may be of God, and not of us." The jars (earthen vessels) are broken to reveal the light within — a picture of God's power displayed through human weakness.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: An army called too many rather than too few before a single battle is fought, two rounds of reduction that leave three hundred out of thirty-two thousand, a barley-cake dream overheard in the enemy's own camp before Gideon ever gives an order, an attack carried out with trumpets, empty jars and torches instead of a single drawn sword, and Midianites destroying each other in the dark while Israel's three hundred simply stand and shout
The Reduction — Too Many (vv.1-8): God's stunning declaration: "The people that are with thee are too many" (v.2). Not too few — too many! God's concern: "lest Israel vaunt themselves against me, saying, Mine own hand hath saved me" (v.2). God will not share His glory with human strength. First reduction: anyone fearful may leave — 22,000 depart, leaving 10,000 (v.3). Still too many (v.4). Second reduction: the water test. Those who lap water from their hands (remaining alert, heads up) = 300. Those who kneel to drink (vulnerable, heads down) = 9,700 sent home (vv.5-7). God will save with 300 against an army "like grasshoppers for multitude" (v.12). The ratio is approximately 450 to 1.
God's Encouragement — The Dream (vv.9-15): God knows Gideon still fears. He offers another sign: "if thou fear to go down, go... and thou shalt hear what they say" (vv.10-11). Gideon overhears a Midianite soldier recounting a dream: a barley cake tumbles into camp and flattens a tent (v.13). His companion interprets: "This is nothing else save the sword of Gideon... God hath delivered Midian" (v.14). Even the enemy knows God has decided the battle. Gideon's response: he worships (v.15). Faith is strengthened by hearing what God has already done in the enemy's camp.
The Attack — Torches, Trumpets, Jars (vv.16-22): Gideon divides 300 men into three companies (v.16). Each man carries a trumpet, an empty jar (pitcher), and a torch inside the jar (v.16). At the beginning of the middle watch (approximately 10 PM), they surround the camp (v.19). On signal: they blow trumpets, smash jars (revealing the torches), and shout "The sword of the LORD, and of Gideon!" (v.20). They stand in place — they do not attack with weapons (v.21). The result: "the LORD set every man's sword against his fellow" (v.22). The Midianites destroy themselves in panic. The host flees.
The Pursuit (vv.23-25): Israel's other tribes join the pursuit (v.23). Ephraim captures the fords of Jordan (v.24). Two Midianite princes — Oreb ("raven") and Zeeb ("wolf") — are captured and killed (v.25). Their heads are brought to Gideon.

Setting: Gideon has been called (ch 6). He tore down his father's altar to Baal, the Spirit of the LORD clothed him, he sounded the trumpet, and men came from Manasseh, Asher, Zebulun and Naphtali (6:34–35). Twice God answered his fleece. Now the armies face each other in the Jezreel Valley, the broad plain in northern Israel that cuts from the coast toward the Jordan. Chapter 7 is the heart of Gideon's story. God deliberately makes Israel's army smaller and smaller until no one could mistake who won the battle.

Gideon's Three Hundred Men (vv1–18)

  • "Jerubbaal (that is, Gideon)" (v1). The new name from 6:32 ("Let Baal contend against him") now stands first. The man who pulled down Baal's altar leads the army.
  • "The spring of Harod… the hill of Moreh" (v1). Harod sits at the foot of Mount Gilboa on the south side of the valley. Moreh is a hill a few miles north across the plain. The Tyndale notes explain that this ground guarded the pass from the Jezreel Valley down to the Jordan crossings near Beth-shan. Israel is on the high ground, with Midian spread out in the valley below. Saul will die on this same mountain against the Philistines (1 Sam 31:1).
  • A name that fits (vv1, 3). Harod comes from the Hebrew verb charad, "to tremble." Verse 3 sends home everyone "fearful and trembling" (chared). Gideon's army camps at "Trembling Spring," and the trembling are sent away.
  • "The people with you are too many for me" (v2). Israel has 32,000 men. Judges 8:10 puts Midian's force at about 135,000, so Israel is already outnumbered more than four to one. God's problem is not that Israel is too weak but that it is too strong: "lest Israel boast over me, saying, 'My own hand has saved me.'" The danger God guards against is pride, not defeat (compare Deut 8:17; 1 Sam 14:6).
  • "Whoever is fearful and trembling, let him return home" (v3). This was already the law of Israel's warfare (Deut 20:8), so that fear would not spread through the ranks. Gideon himself was fearful (6:15, 27; 7:10), so he is sending home men much like himself. Two out of three go, 22,000 men, and 10,000 remain.
  • "Mount Gilead" (v3). Gilead is normally the land east of the Jordan, which does not fit a camp at Gilboa. Some think this was a local name for a hill near Harod. Others think an early copyist confused it with Gilboa, or that the phrase is an idiom for "go home quickly." The text does not settle it.
  • "The people are still too many" (v4). Now God does the choosing: "I will test them for you there." Gideon does not have to pick.
  • Lapping and kneeling (vv5–6). 300 men scoop water to their mouths with their hands and lap it "as a dog laps." Everyone else kneels with face to the water. What did the test mean? Some see the lappers as alert men who kept their eyes up and weapons ready. Others see them as men in a hurry. The Tyndale notes see no military virtue at all: the test simply produced a small number. The text never praises the 300. The point is the number, not the men.
  • "With the 300 men who lapped I will save you" (v7). Now the odds are something like 450 to one. Gideon has nothing left to trust but God's word.
  • "Took provisions in their hands, and their trumpets" (v8). The 300 keep the supplies and the rams'-horn trumpets of the men going home, which is how they end up with 300 trumpets and 300 jars (v16). "The camp of Midian was below him in the valley."
  • "If you are afraid to go down, go down… with Purah your servant" (vv9–10). God gives the command and the promise ("I have given it into your hand") and then, kindly, makes room for Gideon's fear. He does not scold. He offers a companion and one more piece of reassurance, this time without being asked. Gideon goes, which says the fear was real.
  • "Like locusts in abundance… as the sand that is on the seashore" (v12). From the edge of the camp Gideon sees the size of it. The camels again are "without number." "Sand on the seashore" is the language God used of Israel's own future numbers (Gen 22:17). Here it describes the enemy.
  • The barley loaf dream (vv13–14). A soldier dreams that a round loaf of barley bread rolls into camp and flattens a tent. Barley was cheap bread, the food of poor farmers, and Israel under Midian had been reduced to exactly that. The tent was the nomad's home and symbol. His comrade reads it at once: "This is no other than the sword of Gideon the son of Joash." The enemy already knows Gideon's name and already fears him. In the ancient world dreams were taken seriously as messages from the gods, and in the Old Testament God sometimes speaks even through pagans' dreams (Gen 41; Dan 2). Gideon arrived in the right spot at the right minute to hear it.
  • "He worshiped" (v15). Before he gives an order, Gideon bows. The Hebrew word means to bow down low. Then the frightened man becomes the encourager: "Arise, for the LORD has given the host of Midian into your hand." He has moved from "if you are with me" to "the LORD has given."
  • Trumpets, jars and torches (vv16–18). Three companies of 100 are each given a trumpet, an empty clay jar and a torch hidden inside the jar. Normally only a few men in a large army carried trumpets and torches, so 300 of each would suggest a huge force. "Look at me, and do likewise" (v17) is simple leadership by example. The battle cry gives the credit in the right order: "For the LORD and for Gideon."
  • Commentators: All three see the shrinking army as God guarding his glory. Matthew Henry: "God provides that the praise of victory may be wholly to himself." JFB ties v3 to the law of Deuteronomy 20:8 and sees God teaching Israel dependence on him. On the drinking test they differ: Guzik reads the lappers as more alert, with their eyes on their surroundings, JFB reads them as quick men who drank like travelers on the move, and the Tyndale notes say the choice seems arbitrary and was simply a way to cut numbers. Guzik also draws the lesson that abundance can make dependence on God harder, pointing to Paul's thorn (2 Cor 12:7–10). On the dream, Henry calls the barley cake one of "the foolish things of the world" chosen "to confound the wise," and JFB notes barley was "usually the food of the poor, and of beasts." Guzik points out how Gideon's encouragement, once he had it, spread to his men.

Gideon Defeats Midian (vv19–25)

  • "At the beginning of the middle watch" (v19). Israel divided the night into three watches. The middle watch began about 10 p.m. The guards had "just set the watch," so the new sentries were barely awake to the dark, and the sleeping camp had settled in for the night.
  • Smashed jars, blazing torches, blaring trumpets (vv19–20). In a moment the hills on three sides burst with light and sound. The men held torches in the left hand and trumpets in the right, so they had no hand free for a sword. "A sword for the LORD and for Gideon!" was shouted by men who were not using swords at all. The only swords drawn that night were Midian's.
  • "Every man stood in his place around the camp" (v21). Israel did not charge. The 300 simply stood still while the camp panicked. Picture tethered camels bolting, men waking in the dark to noise on all sides, and no idea who was friend or enemy.
  • "The LORD set every man's sword against his comrade" (v22). This is the decisive act, and it is God's. The same thing happens when Jonathan attacks the Philistines (1 Sam 14:20) and when Jehoshaphat's choir sings (2 Chr 20:22–23). Israel blows trumpets, and God fights.
  • The flight east (v22). The army runs southeast down the valley toward the Jordan fords: Beth-shittah ("house of the acacia"), Zererah, and Abel-meholah, later Elisha's hometown (1 Kgs 19:16). Some Hebrew manuscripts read "Zeredah" for Zererah. They were trying to get back across the river to home ground.
  • "The men of Israel were called out" (v23). Men from Naphtali, Asher and Manasseh join the chase. JFB thinks these were many of the same men sent home the day before, who had not gone far. The 300 broke the army, and now the wider people share in the pursuit.
  • "Capture the waters… and also the Jordan" (v24). Gideon calls on Ephraim, the large tribe to the south, to seize the river crossings and trap the fleeing army. Ehud did the same against Moab (3:28), and Jephthah will do it against Ephraim itself (12:5–6). Beth-barah's location is unknown, but it was one of the fords.
  • Oreb and Zeeb (v25). The names mean "Raven" and "Wolf," fitting names for desert raiders. The Tyndale notes point out that these were commanders, not the two kings, who are still at large (8:5). The places where they died were named after them, so later Israelites could point to the rock and the winepress and remember. The "winepress of Zeeb" is a sharp echo: Gideon was first found hiding in a winepress from Midian (6:11), and now a Midianite prince dies in one. Isaiah later looks back on "the slaughter of Midian at the rock of Oreb" as the model of God's sudden rescue (Isa 10:26; see also Isa 9:4 and Ps 83:11).
  • "Across the Jordan" (v25). Gideon has already crossed the river in pursuit, which leads straight into chapter 8 and Ephraim's complaint.
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry sums up the plan as "not to fight them, but to frighten them," and calls the story one that "shines very brightly in the book of the wars of the Lord." Guzik says the battle cry was not pride but good tactics, since the Midianites already dreaded Gideon's name (v14). He also mentions Origen's old allegory that the trumpets are preaching and the torches holy living, which is an illustration, not the meaning of the text. Guzik reads Gideon's call to Ephraim as wisdom, letting many share the work once God had begun it. JFB says the jars were cheap earthenware that broke easily and kept the torches hidden until the signal, and calls Oreb and Zeeb "appropriate designations of Arab leaders."

Pointing to Christ

God shrank Gideon's army until no one could boast, and then won the battle while the 300 stood still holding lights. Paul uses the same pattern for the gospel: "we have this treasure in jars of clay, to show that the surpassing power belongs to God and not to us" (2 Cor 4:7), and God chose what is weak and low "so that no human being might boast" (1 Cor 1:27–29). The barley loaf that flattened Midian's tent was poor man's bread. Jesus fed the five thousand from five barley loaves (John 6:9) and then called himself "the bread of life." He won his greatest victory through weakness, on a cross, and the people who belong to him share the spoils of a battle they did not fight.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes that the water test was not about the superiority of the lappers — it was simply God's chosen method of selection. The point is not the quality of the 300 but the power of God working through inadequate means. He connects the broken jars to 2 Corinthians 4:7 — treasure in earthen vessels.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Reduced So God Gets the Glory (v.2) — God shrinks Gideon's army from 32,000 to 300 specifically so Israel can't claim the victory as their own doing. What in your life might God be stripping away so that His power, not yours, gets the credit?
  • Even the Enemy Confirms What God Has Decided (v.14) — Gideon overhears a Midianite soldier interpret a dream as certain defeat at God's hand before the battle even starts. Does knowing that God has already secured the outcome change how you approach today's fight?
  • Light Only Shows Through What's Broken (v.16) — The torches inside Gideon's men's jars couldn't shine until the jars were smashed. Where might your own brokenness be the very thing God wants to use to reveal His light?
  • Putting God's Name Before Your Own (v.18) — The battle cry names the LORD, and Gideon — not the reverse. Whose name tends to come first when you talk about the things you've accomplished?
  • Standing Still and Letting God Fight (v.22) — Gideon's 300 never swung a weapon; God turned the Midianites against each other. Is there a battle right now where your job is simply to obey and trust, not to force a solution?
