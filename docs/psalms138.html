Summary

In this thanksgiving psalm, David praises the LORD with his whole heart, bowing toward his temple and praising him for his steadfast love and faithfulness, since God has exalted his word above all else (vv. 1-2). He recalls how the LORD answered him and strengthened his soul when he cried out (v. 3), and anticipates kings of the earth praising the LORD once they hear his words (vv. 4-5). Though the LORD is exalted, he takes notice of the lowly while keeping the proud at a distance (v. 6). David closes confident that even amid trouble, God will preserve him and complete the work he has begun, since his steadfast love endures forever (vv. 7-8).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Individual Thanksgiving / Hymn of Praise
Attributed Author: David (superscription: "A Psalm of David")
Key Themes: Wholehearted praise, God's faithfulness to His word, answered prayer bringing boldness, God's regard for the lowly, the LORD perfecting what concerns His people
Historical Context: Psalm 138 opens the final Davidic collection in the Psalter (138-145). It is a confident psalm of thanksgiving — David praises with his whole heart, declaring that God has magnified His word above all His name (v.2). The psalm moves from personal thanksgiving to universal vision (all kings will praise God) to confident trust that God will complete what He has begun. Verse 8 contains a powerful theological declaration about God's ongoing work in the believer's life.
Before the Gods Will I Sing Praise (vv.1-3): I will praise thee with my whole heart: before the gods will I sing praise unto thee. The gods may be the gods of the nations, before whom the praise is a provocation, or the judges and powers of the earth, which the same Hebrew word can mean; either way the singing is done in front of rivals. I will worship toward thy holy temple. Then a clause that reads oddly and is textually disputed, for thou hast magnified thy word above all thy name, which some read as above all, thy name and thy word, and the Hebrew word order allows the doubt. The occasion is an answered prayer, in the day when I cried thou answeredst me.
All the Kings of the Earth Shall Praise Thee (vv.4-5): All the kings of the earth shall praise thee, O LORD, when they hear the words of thy mouth. One man's answered prayer becomes the ground for expecting kings to sing, which is a large step and the psalm takes it without argument. What they are said to respond to is words rather than power.
He Hath Respect unto the Lowly (v.6): Though the LORD be high, yet hath he respect unto the lowly: but the proud he knoweth afar off. The verse turns height into a reason for attention rather than distance, and then reverses the expectation about the proud, who are the ones held at arm's length. Knowing from afar is not ignorance; it is refusal to come close.
The LORD Will Perfect That Which Concerneth Me (vv.7-8): Though I walk in the midst of trouble, thou wilt revive me. The trouble is granted rather than denied, which is this psalm's habit, and both of its last two verses begin by conceding something. The LORD will perfect that which concerneth me, where perfect means finish. And then the last line, which is a prayer made out of an argument, forsake not the works of thine own hands, so the speaker asks God not to abandon his own workmanship.

Setting: Psalm 138 opens the last collection of David's psalms in the Psalter (Psalms 138–145). After the anonymous Songs of Ascents and the temple hymns of 135–137, David's name returns. The study notes describe the shape of this final group: two psalms of praise and trust (138–139) and a closing hymn (145) frame five laments (140–144), cries for help under attack. Psalm 138 is a thanksgiving. David has prayed in trouble, God has answered, and now he gives thanks publicly. No specific event is named. Some link it to God's promise of a lasting dynasty in 2 Samuel 7, because v2 praises God for exalting his "word" and v8 for fulfilling "his purpose for me." That is a reasonable suggestion, not something the psalm states. Some scholars think the heading means "in David's style" or "for the Davidic king," and date the psalm later. The placement right after Psalm 137 is striking. There the exiles could not sing the LORD's song among foreign captors. Here David sings it boldly "before the gods," and foresees "all the kings of the earth" giving thanks to the LORD. The psalm moves from David's own thanks (vv1–3), to the nations' praise (vv4–6), to confidence for the future (vv7–8). The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanzas as the ESV spaces them.

I Give You Thanks with My Whole Heart (vv1–3)

  • "I give you thanks, O LORD, with my whole heart" (v1). "Give thanks" ('odeka, from yadah) is public, spoken acknowledgment, the same word as the refrain of Psalm 136. "Whole heart" means undivided: not half-hearted, not distracted, not holding anything back (Ps 9:1; 86:12). In Hebrew thought the heart is not only the feelings but the mind and will too, the center of the person.
  • "Before the gods I sing your praise" (v1). "Gods" translates 'elohim, the ordinary Hebrew word for God or gods. Who are these "gods"? There are three main views. (1) The gods of the nations: David sings to the LORD in open defiance of every idol, as a witness against them. (2) Angels or heavenly beings: the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint) translates "before the angels," and 'elohim is used of heavenly beings elsewhere (Ps 8:5; 82:1, 6). (3) Earthly rulers and judges, sometimes called "gods" because they exercise God's authority (Ps 82:6; Exod 21:6 in some translations). That would fit v4, where "all the kings of the earth" praise God. The study notes take it as the gods of the nations, and that fits the flow of the Psalter after 135:5 ("our Lord is above all gods") and 136:2 ("the God of gods"). Each view makes the same point: David praises the LORD openly, in front of every rival power.
  • "I bow down toward your holy temple" (v2). Israelites far from Jerusalem prayed facing toward the place of God's presence (1 Kings 8:29–30; Dan 6:10; Jonah 2:4). In David's day there was no stone temple yet, but "temple" (heikal, palace) was also used of the tabernacle, God's tent (1 Sam 1:9; 3:3; Ps 5:7). Bowing down was the posture of worship before a king.
  • "For your steadfast love and your faithfulness" (v2). "Steadfast love" (chesed) and "faithfulness" ('emet, truth, reliability) are a pair that runs through the Old Testament. God named himself with them on Sinai: "abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Exod 34:6). John's Gospel echoes it: Jesus came "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14).
  • "For you have exalted above all things your name and your word" (v2). The Hebrew is unusual. Read literally, it says "you have made great, above all your name, your word." The ESV footnote gives that reading: "you have exalted your word above all your name." Older translations follow it (KJV: "thou hast magnified thy word above all thy name"). It would mean that God has honored his promise above every other way he has made himself known, by keeping it. JFB takes it this way: God's promise "exceeded all other manifestations of Himself as subject of praise." Others think the line means God has made his name and his word great above everything, which is how the ESV text reads it. Some scholars suggest a small change to the Hebrew to get "above the heavens." Whichever reading is right, the point stands: God has backed his word with his own name. He has proved himself by doing what he said.
  • "On the day I called, you answered me" (v3). Here is the reason for the thanks. David prayed, and God answered on the same day. The psalm does not tell us what the trouble was.
  • "My strength of soul you increased" (v3). The verb (rahav) is unusual. It usually describes pressing hard, storming or acting boldly, and in this form it means "made me bold." Maclaren, as Guzik reports, renders it "made me proud," meaning a holy confidence. God's answer was not only to change the circumstances. He strengthened David inside, so that he could face them. Paul knew the same thing: "I can do all things through him who strengthens me" (Phil 4:13; 2 Cor 12:9).
  • Commentators: Henry takes "the gods" to mean princes, judges and great men, possibly angels, and says "praising God is work which the greatest of men need not be ashamed of." He reads the temple in a Christian light: "Christ is our temple, and towards him we must look with an eye of faith." JFB lists angels, princes or idols as possible meanings and says the phrase shows "a readiness to worship the true God alone." Guzik also sets out the three views. On v2 he follows the reading "your word above all your name," and passes on Boice's point that God values his own integrity and wants to be believed, without lowering his other attributes. The lexicon supports "embolden" for the verb in v3.

All the Kings of the Earth Shall Give You Thanks (vv4–6)

  • "All the kings of the earth shall give you thanks, O LORD" (v4). David's personal thanks widens to the whole world. The verb is the same as in v1. The kings of the nations will one day do what David is doing now. Psalm 2 called on the kings of the earth to "serve the LORD with fear" (Ps 2:10–11). The study notes link the two. Psalm 72 foresaw that "all kings fall down before him" (Ps 72:11), and Isaiah pictured kings coming to the brightness of God's light (Isa 60:3).
  • "For they have heard the words of your mouth" (v4). They praise because they have heard God's word. The same word that God exalted in v2 goes out to the nations. Kings are won not by force but by hearing.
  • "And they shall sing of the ways of the LORD, for great is the glory of the LORD" (v5). The "ways" of the LORD are his acts and his character, how he deals with people. "Glory" (kavod) is literally "weight," the heaviness of God's worth and majesty. JFB notes the line can be read "when the glory shall be great," pointing to the day when God completes his saving purposes.
  • "For though the LORD is high, he regards the lowly" (v6). This is the center of the psalm. God is "high," exalted above everything (Ps 113:4–6). Yet he "regards," literally "sees," the lowly. Isaiah says the same: "I dwell in the high and holy place, and also with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit" (Isa 57:15). Hannah's song (1 Sam 2:7–8) and Mary's song (Luke 1:48, 52) celebrate it. The God whose glory the kings will sing is the God who stoops to see the humble.
  • "But the haughty he knows from afar" (v6). God sees the proud too, but at a distance. He is not fooled by them and does not draw near to them. JFB says he knows "their ways and deserts." The New Testament gathers the thought into one sentence, from Proverbs 3:34: "God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble" (James 4:6; 1 Pet 5:5).
  • Commentators: JFB calls v6 a "general principle of God's government." Henry says God knows the proud, "but disowns them and rejects them." Guzik ties v6 to Proverbs 3:34, James 4:6 and 1 Peter 5:5, and passes on Trapp's picture of the proud Pharisee and the humble tax collector in Luke 18. He also notes, with Boice, that if the kings of the earth will praise God when they hear his word, then that word must be carried to them.

Though I Walk in the Midst of Trouble (vv7–8)

  • "Though I walk in the midst of trouble, you preserve my life" (v7). David does not expect a life without trouble. He expects God's care in the middle of it. "Preserve my life" (techayyeni) is literally "you make me live" or "you revive me," the word used often in Psalm 119 ("give me life," 119:25, 37). Psalm 23 says the same: "Even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil, for you are with me" (Ps 23:4).
  • "You stretch out your hand against the wrath of my enemies, and your right hand delivers me" (v7). The "hand" and "right hand" of God are pictures of his power acting on behalf of his people, as at the Red Sea: "Your right hand, O LORD, shatters the enemy" (Exod 15:6). The enemies are angry, but God's hand is stronger.
  • "The LORD will fulfill his purpose for me" (v8). The verb (gamar) means to complete, to finish, to bring to an end. Psalm 57:2 uses the same word: "I cry out to God Most High, to God who fulfills his purpose for me." God does not start things he does not finish. The study notes observe that David sees himself as part of God's plans. Paul says the same of every believer: "he who began a good work in you will bring it to completion at the day of Jesus Christ" (Phil 1:6).
  • "Your steadfast love, O LORD, endures forever" (v8). David borrows the refrain of Psalm 136. His confidence about the future rests on the same thing as Israel's whole history: God's love never ends.
  • "Do not forsake the work of your hands" (v8). The psalm ends with a prayer, not a boast. David is confident God will complete his purpose, and still he asks God not to abandon it. Confidence and prayer belong together. "The work of your hands" can mean David himself, made by God (Ps 139:13–16; Job 10:8), or God's purposes in David's life. The prayer appeals to God's own investment: you made me; you began this; do not let it go.
  • Commentators: Henry says David's confidence rests on God's mercy, not his own strength, so that confidence turns into prayer: "Forsake not... the work of thy own hands." JFB says simply that God "will fulfil His promise." Guzik links v8 to Philippians 1:6 and to God's promise to David in 2 Samuel 7. He passes on Morgan's point that the hope rests on God's determination, not the singer's, and Spurgeon's that God's enemies can vanish at a stretch of his arm.

Pointing to Christ

Psalm 138 sings of a God who is high and still regards the lowly, who exalts his word by keeping it, and who will finish what he starts. Jesus is all of that in person. He is the Word of God made flesh, and in him every one of God's promises finds its "Yes" (John 1:14; 2 Cor 1:20). He is the high God who came down, "being found in human form, he humbled himself" (Phil 2:8), and he said, "everyone who humbles himself will be exalted" (Luke 14:11). He walked "in the midst of trouble," and on the cross he committed himself to the Father's hand (Luke 23:46). God did not forsake the work of his hands. He raised him up. Because of that, the kings of the earth will one day bow, "and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord" (Phil 2:10–11; Rev 21:24). The last words of his work on the cross, "It is finished" (John 19:30), are the guarantee that the work he began in his people will be finished too.

Reflection

  • Wholehearted Thanksgiving for Answered Prayer (vv.1-3) — David praises God with his whole heart because when he called, God answered and strengthened his soul. Can you name a specific answered prayer you haven't properly thanked God for yet?
  • A Witness Reaching Even Kings (vv.4-5) — David anticipates that even the kings of the earth will one day praise God after hearing His words. Is your life, in its own small sphere, actually pointing others toward God, or is your faith kept private and unseen?
  • Nearness Reserved for the Lowly (v.6) — Though the Lord is exalted above all, He draws near to the lowly while keeping His distance from the proud. Is pride quietly keeping you further from God than you realize?
  • Trusting God to Finish What He Started (vv.7-8) — David closes with confidence that the Lord will fulfill His purpose for him and not abandon the work of His hands. What unfinished area of your life do you need to trust God is still actively completing, not neglecting?
