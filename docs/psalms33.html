Summary

A hymn of praise calling the righteous to sing joyfully to the LORD with instruments and a new song (vv. 1-3), praising his word as right and all his work as faithful, since he loves righteousness and justice and the earth is full of his steadfast love (vv. 4-5). By his word the heavens were made and the waters gathered, so all the earth is called to stand in awe of him (vv. 6-9). The LORD frustrates the plans of nations, but his own purposes stand firm forever, and he watches all humanity from heaven, understanding every heart and deed (vv. 10-15). No king is saved by the size of his army, and a warhorse offers no guarantee of victory, but the LORD's eye watches over those who fear him and hope in his steadfast love, delivering them from death and keeping them alive in famine (vv. 16-19). The psalm closes with a request that God's steadfast love rest on the worshipers as they put their hope in him (vv. 20-22).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Hymn of Praise / Creation Psalm
Attributed Author: Anonymous (no superscription — one of the few in Book I without a Davidic attribution)
Key Themes: Singing a new song to God, creation by God's word, God's sovereign plan over nations, trust in God rather than military might, hope in God's steadfast love
Historical Context: Psalm 33 is one of the few psalms in Book I (Psalms 1-41) that lacks a superscription attributing it to David. The Septuagint (Greek Old Testament) does assign it to David, and many scholars believe it was composed as a companion piece to Psalm 32, since Psalm 32 ends with a call to "shout for joy" (v.11) and Psalm 33 opens with that same call fulfilled.
Sing unto Him a New Song (vv.1-3): Rejoice in the LORD, O ye righteous, for praise is comely for the upright. The instruction is musical and specific, praise the LORD with harp, sing unto him with the psaltery and an instrument of ten strings. And the last clause asks for competence as well as enthusiasm, play skilfully with a loud noise, which is a rare thing for a hymn to specify.
He Loveth Righteousness and Judgment (vv.4-5): For the word of the LORD is right, and all his works are done in truth. Two verses that set the psalm's subject, and the last clause is a claim about quantity that the rest of the poem will argue for, the earth is full of the goodness of the LORD.
By the Word of the LORD Were the Heavens Made (vv.6-9): By the word of the LORD were the heavens made, and all the host of them by the breath of his mouth. The creation is described as speech rather than labor, which follows Genesis 1 closely, and the sea is handled without a struggle, he gathereth the waters of the sea together as an heap. Then the conclusion drawn is a demand for attention from everybody, let all the earth fear the LORD, and the reason is put in two clauses that leave no interval, for he spake, and it was done, he commanded, and it stood fast.
The Counsel of the LORD Standeth for Ever (vv.10-12): The LORD bringeth the counsel of the heathen to nothing, he maketh the devices of the people of none effect. Against that, the counsel of the LORD standeth for ever, the thoughts of his heart to all generations. The contrast is between plans that expire and one that does not. And the beatitude that closes the section is national, blessed is the nation whose God is the LORD.
He Fashioneth Their Hearts Alike (vv.13-15): The LORD looketh from heaven, he beholdeth all the sons of men. The observation is described as comprehensive and the reason given for its accuracy is unusual, he fashioneth their hearts alike, he considereth all their works. The maker of the instrument is the one reading it.
There Is No King Saved by the Multitude of an Host (vv.16-17): There is no king saved by the multitude of an host, a mighty man is not delivered by much strength. Two verses of military skepticism, and the second names the most expensive asset of the age, an horse is a vain thing for safety, neither shall he deliver any by his great strength. This is the same argument as Psalm 20:7 and Isaiah 31:1.
The Eye of the LORD Is upon Them That Fear Him (vv.18-19): Behold, the eye of the LORD is upon them that fear him, upon them that hope in his mercy. The general observation of verses 13 to 15 is here narrowed to a particular attention, and what it is for is stated in two plain outcomes, to deliver their soul from death, and to keep them alive in famine.
Our Soul Waiteth for the LORD (vv.20-22): Our soul waiteth for the LORD, he is our help and our shield. The psalm ends in the first person plural and in the posture the psalter recommends most often. And the last verse is a petition rather than a declaration, which is a modest ending for a poem about creation and empires, let thy mercy, O LORD, be upon us, according as we hope in thee.

Deep Dive

Setting: Psalm 33 has no title in Hebrew, which is rare in the first book of Psalms. Apart from Psalms 1 and 2, which introduce the whole collection, and Psalm 10, which completes Psalm 9, it is the only untitled psalm in Psalms 1–41. The Greek translation (the Septuagint) adds "Of David," and Henry thinks David probably wrote it. Its opening picks up exactly where Psalm 32 ended. "Be glad in the LORD, and rejoice, O righteous" (32:11) becomes "Shout for joy in the LORD, O you righteous!" (33:1). A few Hebrew manuscripts even join the two psalms. Boice, cited by Guzik, says the first three verses read almost like an expansion of 32:11. The Tyndale notes suggest the link is deliberate: the forgiven sinner of Psalm 32 is now called to live a new life in the fear of the Lord. Maclaren, also cited by Guzik, notes that it seems written for public worship, a step away from the private struggles of the psalms before it. It has 22 verses, the number of letters in the Hebrew alphabet, though it is not an acrostic. It is a hymn of praise: to the God who made the world by his word (vv4–9), rules the nations by his counsel (vv10–12), sees every heart (vv13–17), and watches over those who hope in him (vv18–22).

Shout for Joy in the LORD (vv1–3)

  • "Shout for joy in the LORD, O you righteous!" (v1). "Shout for joy" (rannenu) is a ringing cry. The joy is "in the LORD," not in circumstances. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik: rejoicing in comforts is dangerous, in self foolish, in sin fatal, "but to rejoice in God is heavenly."
  • "Praise befits the upright" (v1). "Befits" (na'veh) means lovely, fitting, becoming, like a garment that suits the one wearing it. Praise looks right on God's people. Silence or complaint looks out of place.
  • "Give thanks to the LORD with the lyre; make melody to him with the harp of ten strings!" (v2). The lyre (kinnor) was David's instrument (1 Sam 16:23), a small hand-held stringed instrument. The nevel was probably larger, a harp. "Of ten strings" may describe a particular harp or a separate instrument. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, passes on older scholars' guesses about how the two were built, but no one knows exactly. JFB notes that "the instruments… do not exclude the voice." Christians have disagreed about instruments in worship. Some traditions sing without them, holding that they belonged to the temple. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, preferred unaccompanied singing but would not call instruments wrong. Most churches use them, citing psalms like this one.
  • "Sing to him a new song" (v3). This is the first "new song" in the Psalms (40:3; 96:1; 98:1; 144:9; 149:1; Isa 42:10). It does not have to mean a song never sung before. It means a song that comes out of a fresh experience of God. Boice, cited by Guzik, puts it that way. Revelation's new song celebrates the Lamb who was slain (Rev 5:9; 14:3).
  • "Play skillfully on the strings, with loud shouts" (v3). Skill and loudness together. The best musicians played for God (1 Chr 15:22; 25:7). "Loud shouts" (teru'ah) is the trumpet blast or battle cry of 27:6. Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes the call for "freshness and skill as well as fervour," three qualities that rarely come together.
  • Commentators: Henry notes that Psalm 32 ended with joy and Psalm 33 begins with it, "for all our religious exercises should both begin and end with a holy complacency and triumph in God." JFB links the new song to Psalms 40:3 and 96:1 and reads it as "fresh, adapted to the occasion." Guzik passes on Horne: there is "no instrument like the rational soul, and no melody like that of well-tuned affections."

By the Word of the LORD (vv4–9)

  • "For the word of the LORD is upright, and all his work is done in faithfulness" (v4). The first reason to praise is God's character. His word is straight and true, and his work matches his word. Kidner, cited by Guzik: "His word and His work are inseparable, for His words are never empty."
  • "He loves righteousness and justice; the earth is full of the steadfast love of the LORD" (v5). God does not merely do right. He loves it. And the whole earth is full of his chesed, his loyal, covenant love. That is a bold claim in a broken world, but the psalm insists on it. Psalm 119:64 says the same: "The earth, O LORD, is full of your steadfast love."
  • "By the word of the LORD the heavens were made" (v6). This is Genesis 1 in a sentence: "And God said… and it was so." God did not need tools or materials. He spoke. Hebrews 11:3: "the universe was created by the word of God, so that what is seen was not made out of things that are visible."
  • "And by the breath of his mouth all their host" (v6). "Breath" (ruach) is the same word as "Spirit." "All their host" means the sun, moon and stars, the armies of heaven. In Genesis 1:2, "the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters." JFB says that in "word" and "breath" "there may be an allusion to the Son (Joh 1:1) and Holy Spirit." Henry goes further: "Christ is the Word, the Spirit is the breath." The psalmist would not have spelled out the Trinity, but the New Testament shows that creation through the Word was creation through the Son: "All things were made through him" (John 1:3; Col 1:16).
  • "He gathers the waters of the sea as a heap" (v7). "Heap" (ned) is the word used for the waters of the Red Sea standing up "in a heap" (Exod 15:8) and the Jordan piling up when Israel crossed (Josh 3:13, 16). The psalm pictures creation, when God gathered the waters into one place so dry land appeared (Gen 1:9), with the language of the exodus. The ancient Greek and Aramaic versions read a very similar Hebrew word as "a wineskin" (nod), "gathers the waters as in a bottle," which also makes sense beside "storehouses." The Hebrew text has "heap."
  • "He puts the deeps in storehouses" (v7). The great ocean depths, which the nations around Israel feared as a power of chaos, are kept like grain in God's barns. The Tyndale notes point out that neighboring peoples thought the sea had divine power, but here it is stored and under control (104:9; Job 38:8–11). Maclaren, cited by Guzik, notes that the verbs are participles, so God is still doing this: he keeps holding the waters back.
  • "Let all the earth fear the LORD" (v8). The right response to a Creator like this is awe. Henry: it is dangerous "being at war with him who has the host of heaven for his armies and the depths of the sea for his magazines."
  • "For he spoke, and it came to be; he commanded, and it stood firm" (v9). Two short, strong lines. JFB notes that the literal Hebrew is simply "he said, and it was," and the KJV's added word "done" weakens it. The centurion understood this kind of authority: "Only say the word, and my servant will be healed" (Matt 8:8).
  • Commentators: Henry: "With men saying and doing are two things, but it is not so with God." He notes the same Word and Spirit made man (Gen 1:26; 2:7) and build the church, the "new creation." Guzik passes on Meyer: there is no spot on earth where the traces of God's love cannot be seen "if only the eyes and the heart are opened." He also passes on Kidner's sharp observation that nature's obedience to God reminds us of man's defiance.

The Counsel of the LORD Stands Forever (vv10–12)

  • "The LORD brings the counsel of the nations to nothing" (v10). "Counsel" ('etsah) means plans, strategy, the decisions made in royal councils. The nations make their plans, and God overturns them. Psalm 2 showed the nations plotting against the LORD and his anointed, and God laughing. Isaiah says, "Take counsel together, but it will come to nothing" (Isa 8:10). Henry gives examples: Ahithophel's advice "turned into foolishness" (2 Sam 17:14) and Haman's plot undone (Esth 7).
  • "The counsel of the LORD stands forever" (v11). The same word "counsel." Human plans collapse; God's plan stands. "Stands" echoes v9, where creation "stood firm." The same God who made the world stand by his word keeps his purposes standing through history. Isaiah: "My counsel shall stand, and I will accomplish all my purpose" (Isa 46:10). Proverbs: "Many are the plans in the mind of a man, but it is the purpose of the LORD that will stand" (Prov 19:21).
  • "Blessed is the nation whose God is the LORD" (v12). The God whose plans rule history has chosen a people for himself. "Heritage" (nachalah) is his own inheritance (28:9). This verse belongs first to Israel, chosen not for its size or goodness but because of God's love (Deut 7:6–8). Henry adds that it reaches "all others who own God for theirs… whatever nation they are of," the spiritual seed of Abraham. Peter applies the same language to the church: "a chosen race… a holy nation, a people for his own possession" (1 Pet 2:9). Clarke, cited by Guzik, underlines how much it means to have Yahweh, the eternal LORD, as one's own God.
  • Commentators: Henry: God "over-rules all the counsels of men, and makes them, contrary to their intention, serviceable to his counsels." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's image of the world's plots against God as puff-balls thrown against a granite wall. JFB: "In God's providence He thwarts men's purposes and executes His own."

The LORD Looks Down from Heaven (vv13–17)

  • "The LORD looks down from heaven; he sees all the children of man" (v13). Three verbs for God's sight in three verses: looks down, sees, looks out (vv13–14). In Psalm 14:2 God looked down to see if any were wise and found none. Here he looks to watch over and judge. Nothing escapes him. JFB: "intently."
  • "From where he sits enthroned" (v14). God's throne is the place he rules from (29:10). His seeing is not idle curiosity. It is a king surveying his kingdom.
  • "He who fashions the hearts of them all and observes all their deeds" (v15). "Fashions" (yatsar) is the potter's word, used when God "formed" Adam from the dust (Gen 2:7). The God who made every heart knows what is in it (Jer 17:10; Heb 4:13). "Of them all" is literally "together," every one without exception. JFB: "God knows men's motives."
  • "The king is not saved by his great army" (v16). If God sees and rules everything, then human strength is no real security. The great army, the mighty warrior, the war horse, the best a king could rely on, are not enough. David learned this facing Goliath (1 Sam 17:47), and the census that brought judgment came from trusting in numbers (2 Sam 24). Ecclesiastes 9:11: "the battle is not to the strong."
  • "The war horse is a false hope for salvation" (v17). Horses and chariots were the tanks of the ancient world. Egypt was famous for them, and Pharaoh's horses and chariots sank in the sea (Exod 15:1, 4). Israel's kings were told not to multiply horses (Deut 17:16), and Isaiah condemned those who "rely on horses… but do not look to the Holy One of Israel" (Isa 31:1). "False hope" is literally "a lie" (sheqer). JFB: "a lie, which deceives us." Psalm 20:7 is the summary: "Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God."
  • Commentators: Henry: God "not only beholds them, but he looks upon them; he looks narrowly upon them." He stresses that though God gave people understanding and free will, he still governs the very faculties by which they govern themselves. JFB: "Men's usual reliances in their greatest exigencies are, in themselves, useless." He points to Job 39:19–25, God's own description of the war horse.

Our Soul Waits for the LORD (vv18–22)

  • "Behold, the eye of the LORD is on those who fear him" (v18). God's eye sees everyone (v13), but it rests with special care on his people. "Behold" calls attention. This answers 32:8: "I will counsel you with my eye upon you." The Tyndale notes connect the two.
  • "On those who hope in his steadfast love" (v18). Not those who are strong, but those who hope. The contrast with vv16–17 is the point. Kings with armies are not saved. People who hope in God's chesed are.
  • "That he may deliver their soul from death and keep them alive in famine" (v19). Death and famine were the two great threats of the ancient world, the sword and hunger. God kept Elijah alive by ravens and a widow's jar of flour (1 Kings 17), and Joseph's family alive in a famine through Joseph (Gen 45:7). JFB: God saves "from the greatest earthly evil and its most painful precursor, and hence from all."
  • "Our soul waits for the LORD" (v20). Now the whole congregation speaks: "our," "we." "Waits" (chakah) means to wait with longing, to wait patiently. Isaiah 8:17: "I will wait for the LORD, who is hiding his face."
  • "He is our help and our shield" (v20). Not the army, not the horse. The same pairing appears in 115:9–11, three times over.
  • "For our heart is glad in him, because we trust in his holy name" (v21). The psalm returns to the joy it began with. The reason is trust. Joy and trust grow together.
  • "Let your steadfast love, O LORD, be upon us, even as we hope in you" (v22). The last line is a prayer, and it ties together the psalm's key words: "steadfast love" (vv5, 18) and "hope" (v18). "Hope" (yachal) is waiting with expectation. The Tyndale notes suggest a sound-play between this word and "great might" (chayil) in v17: the war horse's strength fails, but those who hope are not disappointed. "Even as we hope in you" does not mean God's love is measured out according to how much we hope, as if faith earned it. It means: let your love meet the hope we have put in you. The Te Deum, the ancient Christian hymn, closes with an echo of this verse: "O Lord, let thy mercy lighten upon us, as our trust is in thee."
  • Commentators: Henry: it is the wisdom of God's people "that they take the Lord for their God," and their happiness that he has taken them for his people. JFB on v22: "Our faith measures mercy (Mt 9:29); and if of grace, it is no more of debt (Ro 11:6)." That is, God meets faith with mercy, but the mercy is still a gift, never wages.

Pointing to Christ

The God who spoke and the heavens were made is the God who spoke in his Son, "through whom also he created the world" (Heb 1:2). When the Word became flesh, the same authority over creation was heard on earth: he spoke, and storms were still, the sick were healed and the dead got up. The nations' counsel came to nothing at the cross. Herod, Pilate, the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel gathered together against God's anointed and did "whatever your hand and your plan had predestined to take place" (Acts 4:25–28). Their plot became God's plan of salvation. The king in Psalm 33 is not saved by his great army, and the true King refused one: he could have called "more than twelve legions of angels" (Matt 26:53) but went to the cross instead. Because of him, those who hope in God's steadfast love are delivered from death, and one day will sing the new song: "Worthy are you… for you were slain, and by your blood you ransomed people for God from every tribe and language and people and nation" (Rev 5:9).

Reflection

  • A New Song of Praise (vv.1-3) — The psalm opens with a call to sing a fresh song, not stale repetition. Has your worship grown routine, and what recent evidence of God's goodness deserves a new expression of praise?
  • Creation by His Word (vv.6-9) — The heavens were made simply by God speaking, and what He commands stands firm. Does the same confidence you would place in a God who spoke galaxies into being show up in how you trust His word over your circumstances?
  • The Futility of Human Strength (vv.16-17) — No king is saved by the size of his army, and no warrior by his strength alone. What horses and chariots — money, status, ability — do you quietly lean on instead of God?
  • Watched Over by a Faithful God (vv.18-19) — God's eye rests on those who fear Him and hope in His steadfast love, ready to preserve their lives. Does knowing you are watched over change how you face present dangers or scarcity?
  • Waiting as an Act of Faith (vv.20-22) — The psalm closes with a soul that waits for the Lord as its help and shield. Are you willing to wait on God's timing rather than forcing your own solution?
