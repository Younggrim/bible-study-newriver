Summary

This collection of proverbs opens by valuing integrity over cleverness, warning that a poor person who walks with integrity is better than a fool who twists his words, and that zeal without knowledge or hasty decisions lead a person astray (vv. 1-2). A person's own folly ruins his way, yet his heart still rages against the Lord as if God were to blame (v. 3). Several proverbs contrast wealth and poverty in relationships: riches multiply friends and flattery draws favor-seekers, while the poor are shunned even by family, and a false witness or liar will not go unpunished (vv. 4-9). The chapter warns that luxury does not suit a fool and it is worse still for a servant to rule over princes, while good sense produces patience and the glory of overlooking an offense (vv. 10-11). A king's fury is likened to a lion's roar and his favor to dew on the grass, while a foolish son ruins his father and a quarrelsome wife wears a husband down like constant dripping (vv. 12-13). Houses and wealth may be inherited from parents, but a prudent wife is a gift from the Lord, and laziness leads to hunger while keeping God's commandment preserves life (vv. 14-16). Whoever is generous to the poor is effectively lending to the Lord, who will repay it, and parents are urged to discipline a son while there is still hope for him (vv. 17-18). A person's own plans may multiply, but it is the Lord's purpose that ultimately stands, and the fear of the Lord leads to life, contentment, and protection from harm (vv. 19-23). The chapter closes with warnings against laziness, folly, and disrespect toward parents, noting that correcting a scoffer benefits onlookers even if the scoffer himself resents it, and that punishment is prepared for scoffers and fools (vv. 24-29).

Authorship & Background

Author: Primarily Solomon (1:1, 10:1, 25:1), with sections by Agur (ch.30) and King Lemuel (ch.31). Compiled over several centuries. Proverbs is wisdom literature — practical instruction for godly living. Hebrew title: 'Mishlei' — 'Proverbs/Comparisons.'
Historical Context: Chapter 19 addresses poverty and wealth (vv.1, 4, 6-7, 17, 22), God's sovereignty over human plans (v.21), parenting and discipline (vv.13, 18, 26), and the fear of the LORD as life (v.23). The chapter is notable for its compassion toward the poor (v.17: lending to the poor = lending to God) and its unflinching honesty about how the world treats the poor (vv.4, 7). Verse 21 is one of the great sovereignty statements in Proverbs.
Themes in This Chapter: Poverty, Wealth, and Friendship (vv.1, 4, 6-7, 17, 22); God's Sovereignty (v.21); Discipline and Parenting (vv.13, 18); The Fear of the LORD (v.23); Patience and Passing Over Transgression (v.11)
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: integrity preferred over cleverness from the opening verse, better is the poor that walketh in his integrity, than he that is perverse in his lips, poverty's effect on friendship stated without any moralizing, wealth maketh many friends, but the poor is separated from his neighbour, the collection's boldest statement on charity, he that hath pity upon the poor lendeth unto the LORD, discipline urged while there is still hope, chasten thy son while there is hope, and a closing pair naming the fear of the LORD as tending to life while judgment waits for scorners
Poverty, Haste and the Household (vv.1-14): The block opens by preferring integrity to speech, better is the poor that walketh in his integrity, than he that is perverse in his lips. Several verses observe how poverty affects friendship without moralizing about it, wealth maketh many friends, but the poor is separated from his neighbour. Two sayings about temper are placed near each other, he that is hasty of spirit sinneth, and the discretion of a man deferreth his anger. And the block closes on domestic life with the chapter's most quoted line, a continual dropping, and beside it a house and riches are the inheritance of fathers, but a prudent wife is from the LORD.
Instruction, Kindness and the Fear of the LORD (vv.15-29): The second half is about correction and about who deserves care. He that hath pity upon the poor lendeth unto the LORD, and that which he hath given will he pay him again, which is the collection's boldest statement about charity. Then a cluster on raising children, chasten thy son while there is hope, and hear counsel, that thou mayest be wise in thy latter end. The chapter ends on the fear of the LORD tending to life, and on judgment prepared for scorners.

Deep Dive

Setting: The collection headed "The proverbs of Solomon" continues (10:1–22:16; see Proverbs 10 on how it was gathered and how to read a proverb). The ESV has no headings inside it, so the headings below simply group the sayings in order. Chapter 19 keeps returning to a few threads: the poor and how the world treats them (vv1, 4, 6–7, 17, 22), false witnesses (vv5, 9, 28), family life (a foolish son, a quarrelsome wife, a prudent wife, discipline, a son who mistreats his parents), and laziness. At its heart stands one of the book's great statements of God's rule: "Many are the plans in the mind of a man, but it is the purpose of the LORD that will stand" (v21).

Integrity, Haste and Blaming God (vv1–3)

  • "Better is a poor person who walks in his integrity than one who is crooked in speech and is a fool" (v1). A "better than" proverb. 28:6 has nearly the same saying with "rich" in the second line, and JFB thinks "fool" here implies the rich fool: "Integrity is better than riches." "Integrity" (tom) means wholeness, a life that is all of one piece. Henry: the poor person who fears God is "more honourable and happy" than the rich without wisdom. The Tyndale notes add that dishonest gain doesn't last anyway (10:2).
  • "Desire without knowledge is not good, and whoever makes haste with his feet misses his way" (v2). "Desire" is nephesh, often "soul," here meaning appetite or zeal. Enthusiasm without understanding leads to rash steps. "Misses his way" uses the verb chata, the ordinary word for "sin," whose root picture is missing a mark (Judg 20:16 uses it of slingers who "would not miss"). Paul describes Israel the same way: "they have a zeal for God, but not according to knowledge" (Rom 10:2). Henry: we sin when we do not take time "to ponder the path of [our] feet."
  • "When a man's folly brings his way to ruin, his heart rages against the LORD" (v3). "Brings to ruin" is literally "twists" or "overturns." A fool wrecks his own life and then is furious with God about it. The verb "rages" (za'aph) is used of a storm-tossed sea (Jonah 1:15 uses a related noun). Adam began this pattern: "The woman whom you gave to be with me" (Gen 3:12). The Tyndale notes: fools blame the Lord instead of owning their mistakes. Guzik passes on the comparison of a criminal blaming the judge who sentences him, and notes that taking responsibility is the first step out of folly.
  • Commentators: Guzik stresses on v1 that not all poverty comes from moral failure. JFB and the Tyndale notes both read v2 as rashness coming from ignorance. All agree that v3 describes a person who will not take responsibility.

Fair-Weather Friends and False Witnesses (vv4–9)

  • "Wealth brings many new friends, but a poor man is deserted by his friend" (v4). Not advice, just how things are. JFB says such facts are stated with "implied disapprobation." Henry: it shows "how strong is men's love of money." Compare 14:20. The prodigal son had plenty of friends until the money ran out, and then "no one gave him anything" (Luke 15:16).
  • "A false witness will not go unpunished, and he who breathes out lies will not escape" (v5). In Israel a false witness got the punishment he tried to bring on the accused (Deut 19:16–21). "Breathes out lies" pictures lying as easy as breathing. The Tyndale notes connect the ninth commandment (Exod 20:16). Human courts sometimes fail, as with Naboth (1 Kgs 21) and Jesus himself (Mark 14:56–59), but God's court does not.
  • "Many seek the favor of a generous man, and everyone is a friend to a man who gives gifts" (v6). "Seek the favor" is literally "stroke the face," the courtier's flattery. Again, an observation about selfish friendship. Henry turns it toward God, whose gifts are countless: "We are without excuse if we do not love God with all our hearts."
  • "All a poor man's brothers hate him; how much more do his friends go far from him! He pursues them with words, but does not have them" (v7). Unusually, a three-line proverb, the only one in this collection of two-line sayings. The third line is hard in Hebrew, and the old Greek translation has a different, longer text here, which suggests the line was damaged early. The ESV's sense: the poor man calls after his friends and they are gone. "Hate" in Hebrew can mean "reject" or "love less." Job knew this: "My relatives have failed me, my close friends have forgotten me" (Job 19:14). Henry and Guzik both think of Christ, deserted by his friends, who "though he was rich, yet for your sake became poor" (2 Cor 8:9).
  • "Whoever gets sense loves his own soul" (v8). "Sense" is literally "heart." Getting wisdom is the truest self-love, because it does real good to one's own life. JFB: shown by pursuing one's own best interests. The opposite is 8:36: "all who hate me love death."
  • "A false witness will not go unpunished, and he who breathes out lies will perish" (v9). Repeats v5 almost exactly, with "will perish" in place of "will not escape," saying more plainly what not escaping means. JFB notes "perish" explains "not escape." Henry: lying is "a damning, destroying sin." Repetition in Proverbs signals importance.
  • Commentators: JFB skips vv6–7 and comments on v9 only through v5. Guzik on v7 contrasts how people abandon the poor with how God pursues them. Henry and Guzik both read vv4–7 as showing how money corrupts friendship.

Luxury, Patience and the King (vv10–12)

  • "It is not fitting for a fool to live in luxury, much less for a slave to rule over princes" (v10). "Not fitting" sayings (17:7; 26:1) describe something out of place. A fool with luxury doesn't know how to use it, and a slave suddenly in power often becomes a tyrant (30:21–22; Eccl 10:5–7). JFB: each should "abide in his calling." Joseph is the exception, a slave raised to rule who ruled wisely (Gen 41). Guzik notes the gospel turns this upside down: the greatest must be servant of all (Matt 20:26).
  • "Good sense makes one slow to anger, and it is his glory to overlook an offense" (v11). "Slow to anger" is literally "long of nostrils," the same phrase God uses of himself (Exod 34:6). "Overlook" is 'abar, "pass over," the verb Micah uses of God: he "pardons iniquity and passes over transgression" (Mic 7:18). Letting an insult go is not weakness but glory, because it is God-like. Henry: true glory belongs to those who "overcome evil with good." JFB sees in this teaching that true religion is the same in both Testaments (Matt 5:39; Eph 4:32).
  • "A king's wrath is like the growling of a lion, but his favor is like dew on the grass" (v12). A young lion's growl warned of attack (20:2). Dew was vital in Israel's dry summer, when there was no rain at all for months. The same king can be terror or refreshment. Compare 16:14–15. JFB: a motive to submit to "lawful authority." Guzik points to the true Lion of Judah (Rev 5:5), whose favor is far greater and whose wrath far more serious. Hosea pictures God himself as both: a roaring lion (Hos 11:10) and the dew to Israel (Hos 14:5).
  • Commentators: Guzik and JFB agree on v11 that patience is strength, and Guzik adds that the one who has been forgiven much can overlook much. Henry reads v12 as the king's terror to enemies and favor to his own people.

Fathers, Wives and Sons (vv13–16)

  • "A foolish son is ruin to his father, and a wife's quarreling is a continual dripping of rain" (v13). "Ruin" is plural in Hebrew, "calamities," and JFB notes they are many and varied. A leaky roof in the rainy season meant a steady drip that would not stop. 27:15 repeats the picture. Guzik passes on an Arab proverb that lists three things that drive a man from home: a leaking roof, a nagging wife and bugs. Henry: our greatest griefs often come where we expect the most comfort.
  • "House and wealth are inherited from fathers, but a prudent wife is from the LORD" (v14). Property passes down by human inheritance. A wise wife is God's gift. The line echoes 18:22 and 8:35. Abraham's servant prayed for God to choose Isaac's wife, and God answered with Rebekah (Gen 24:12–27). JFB contrasts men's gifts with God's, given through his "more special providence." The Tyndale notes say no decision matters more than choosing a spouse, and a wise man seeks the Lord's help in it. Guzik notes the verse gives God the credit when a marriage goes well. It doesn't explain every unhappy marriage.
  • "Slothfulness casts into a deep sleep, and an idle person will suffer hunger" (v15). "Deep sleep" (tardemah) is the word for the heavy sleep God sent on Adam (Gen 2:21) and on Abram (Gen 15:12). Laziness drugs a person. JFB: "utter indifference." Compare 6:9–11.
  • "Whoever keeps the commandment keeps his life; he who despises his ways will die" (v16). A play on "keep": keep God's commandment and it keeps you. "Despises his ways" means not caring how one lives. JFB: treating one's moral conduct as unworthy of regard. Henry: grace does not cancel the need for obedience.
  • Commentators: Guzik reads the "dripping" of v13 three ways: a constant irritation, a slow erosion of what is valuable, and a sign of a deeper problem in the house. The Tyndale notes and Henry both put the prudent wife above all inherited wealth.

Lending to the LORD and Disciplining a Son (vv17–20)

  • "Whoever is generous to the poor lends to the LORD, and he will repay him for his deed" (v17). One of the most striking promises in Proverbs. God puts himself in the place of the borrower. JFB: pity "shown by deeds." Kindness to the poor is not lost money but a loan to the richest debtor there is. Guzik: God never stays in debt to anyone. Guzik also passes on the careful point that the repayment is not always in money. Jesus: "as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me" (Matt 25:40), and "you will be repaid at the resurrection of the just" (Luke 14:14).
  • "Discipline your son, for there is hope; do not set your heart on putting him to death" (v18). "For there is hope" can also be read "while there is hope," while the child is young and can still be shaped. The second line has been read two ways. (1) Do not, by neglecting discipline, make yourself a party to his ruin (23:13–14; the King James has "let not thy soul spare for his crying"). (2) Do not discipline in rage, as if trying to destroy him. JFB takes the second, a caution to "passionate parents" against angry discipline. Both are true. Paul balances it: "Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord" (Eph 6:4). Henry: "foolish tenderness" serves neither parent nor child.
  • "A man of great wrath will pay the penalty, for if you deliver him, you will only have to do it again" (v19). The hothead keeps getting into trouble, and rescuing him only means another rescue later. Sometimes love lets consequences teach. JFB: kind efforts are wasted on the ill-natured. Henry links it to v18: an indulged child easily becomes an angry adult.
  • "Listen to advice and accept instruction, that you may gain wisdom in the future" (v20). "In the future" is literally "in your latter end." Learning now pays off later. JFB: prepare in youth for old age.
  • Commentators: JFB reads v18 as warning against angry discipline, while the Tyndale notes and Guzik stress the danger of neglect. Guzik observes that many children are ruined by neglect, not only by harshness.

The LORD's Purpose and Steadfast Love (vv21–23)

  • "Many are the plans in the mind of a man, but it is the purpose of the LORD that will stand" (v21). The heart of the chapter. Human plans are many and changing. God's purpose is one, and it stands, a word used of God's counsel that endures forever (Ps 33:10–11; Isa 46:10). Compare 16:1, 9. This doesn't mean planning is wrong. It means plans should be held loosely: "If the Lord wills, we will live and do this or that" (Jas 4:15). Joseph's brothers planned evil, but "God meant it for good" (Gen 50:20). Henry: our purposes should "agree with God's holy will."
  • "What is desired in a man is steadfast love, and a poor man is better than a liar" (v22). "Steadfast love" is hesed, loyal, covenant kindness. The first line is debated. The ESV reads it as "what people long for in a person is loyal love." Others read "a man's desire (or greed) is his shame," since hesed in rare cases means "disgrace" (Lev 20:17). The second line is clearer: better poor and honest than a liar who promises kindness he never delivers. JFB: the poor man who wants to do good is better than the liar who talks but does not act. Henry agrees: a willing heart is better than ability without it.
  • "The fear of the LORD leads to life, and whoever has it rests satisfied; he will not be visited by harm" (v23). "Rests" is literally "spends the night." The one who fears God can sleep in peace (Ps 4:8). "Visited by harm" means visited by judgment, as JFB notes. It doesn't promise a life without trouble, but that evil will not have the last word. Guzik: the world thinks fearing God makes people miserable, and the opposite is true.
  • Commentators: Guzik on v21 says planning is right but must be done in reverence for God's will, and on v22 that kindness must never push out truthfulness. JFB and Henry both read v22 as a willing heart being better than an empty promise.

The Sluggard, the Scoffer and the Shameful Son (vv24–29)

  • "The sluggard buries his hand in the dish and will not even bring it back to his mouth" (v24). Proverbs' funniest picture. People ate from a common bowl with their hands, dipping bread in. The sluggard reaches in and then can't be bothered to lift it out. JFB notes the old "bosom" of the King James is really a wide dish, and 26:15 repeats the joke. The Tyndale notes: a humorous take on laziness. Guzik passes on the view that it fits anyone who starts a job and lacks the energy to finish.
  • "Strike a scoffer, and the simple will learn prudence; reprove a man of understanding, and he will gain knowledge" (v25). Three kinds of people. The scoffer won't learn even from a beating, but onlookers will. The simple learn by watching. The wise learn from a word. The Tyndale notes: the mocker can't be helped, but punishing him teaches others. Henry: "A gentle rebuke goes farthest with a man of understanding." 21:11 repeats the idea.
  • "He who does violence to his father and chases away his mother is a son who brings shame and reproach" (v26). In old age parents depended on their children. A son who robs his father and drives out his widowed mother leaves her defenseless. The law treated striking or cursing parents as a capital crime (Exod 21:15, 17). Jesus condemned those who used religious loopholes to avoid caring for their parents (Mark 7:9–13). JFB cites 17:21–25 and Deuteronomy 21:18–21.
  • "Cease to hear instruction, my son, and you will stray from the words of knowledge" (v27). The only "my son" in this chapter, recalling chapters 1–9. It is irony: "go ahead, stop listening, and see where you end up." Guzik passes on the sobering note that Solomon himself strayed when he stopped heeding his own wisdom (1 Kgs 11:1–8).
  • "A worthless witness mocks at justice, and the mouth of the wicked devours iniquity" (v28). "Worthless" is beliyya'al, the word used of the two scoundrels who lied about Naboth (1 Kgs 21:10, 13). Later Jewish writing turned it into a name for Satan, and Paul uses "Belial" that way (2 Cor 6:15). The wicked gulp down sin like food. JFB: "swalloweth, as something delightful." Henry: "Those are the worst of sinners, who are glad of an opportunity to sin."
  • "Condemnation is ready for scoffers, and beating for the backs of fools" (v29). The chapter ends with judgment already prepared. JFB: their punishment is "sure, fixed, and ready."
  • Commentators: Guzik passes on a neat summary of v25: the scoffer has a closed mind, the simple an empty one, the wise an open one. On v29 Henry notes that Christ himself "was not spared" when he bore others' sins, so those who refuse him cannot expect to escape.

Pointing to Christ

Proverbs 19 is honest about how the world treats the poor: friends leave, brothers turn away, and the poor man calls after them with words that go unanswered (v7). Christ entered that world as the poor one, deserted by his friends, and "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Cor 8:9). He so identified with the poor that kindness to them is kindness to him (v17; Matt 25:40). He was condemned by false witnesses (vv5, 9, 28) and bore the "beating for the backs of fools" (v29; Isa 53:5) that was ours. His is the glory of overlooking offense (v11), praying "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). And the cross itself is the clearest case of v21: men made their plans, but "the definite plan and foreknowledge of God" stood (Acts 2:23), and through it came the steadfast love (hesed) that everyone longs for (v22).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes v.17's remarkable claim: God considers Himself a debtor to those who show mercy to the poor. He notes this is extraordinary theology — the Creator owing the creature. On v.21, Guzik states: "Man proposes, God disposes. Plan freely but hold loosely, because only God's counsel stands."
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • The Glory of Overlooking an Offense (v.11) — Patience that lets an insult go without retaliating is called a person's glory, not weakness. What offense are you holding onto that it would honor God to release?
  • Generosity to the Poor Is a Loan to God (v.17) — Kindness shown to the poor is treated as a loan made directly to the LORD, who promises to repay it. Does that change how freely you give?
  • Discipline While There Is Still Hope (v.18) — Correction is meant to happen while a child's character is still forming, not once patterns have hardened. Where do you need to act now instead of putting correction off?
  • Many Plans, One Prevailing Purpose (v.21) — A person can hold many plans in their heart, but only the LORD's purpose ultimately stands. How tightly are you holding your own plans versus God's leading?
  • The Fear of the LORD Leads to Life (v.23) — Reverence for God brings life, contentment, and protection from harm. What would change in your daily choices if you took this connection seriously?
