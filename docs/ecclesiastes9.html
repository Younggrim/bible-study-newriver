1So I took all this to heart and concluded that the righteous and the wise, as well as their deeds, are in God’s hands. Man does not know what lies ahead, whether love or hate.

2It is the same for all: There is a common fate for the righteous and the wicked, for the good and the bad, for the clean and the unclean, for the one who sacrifices and the one who does not. As it is for the good, so it is for the sinner; as it is for the one who makes a vow, so it is for the one who refuses to take a vow.

3This is an evil in everything that is done under the sun: There is one fate for everyone. Furthermore, the hearts of men are full of evil and madness while they are alive, and afterward they join the dead.

4There is hope, however, for anyone who is among the living; for even a live dog is better than a dead lion.

5For the living know that they will die, but the dead know nothing. They have no further reward, because the memory of them is forgotten.

6Their love, their hate, and their envy have already vanished, and they will never again have a share in all that is done under the sun.

7Go, eat your bread with joy, and drink your wine with a cheerful heart, for God has already approved your works:

8Let your garments always be white, and never spare the oil for your head.

9Enjoy life with your beloved wife all the days of the fleeting life that God has given you under the sun—all your fleeting days. For this is your portion in life and in your labor under the sun.

10Whatever you find to do with your hands, do it with all your might, for in Sheol, where you are going, there is no work or planning or knowledge or wisdom.

11I saw something else under the sun: The race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong; neither is the bread to the wise, nor the wealth to the intelligent, nor the favor to the skillful. For time and chance happen to all.

12For surely no man knows his time: Like fish caught in a cruel net or birds trapped in a snare, so men are ensnared in an evil time that suddenly falls upon them.

13I have also seen this wisdom under the sun, and it was great to me:

14There was a small city with few men. A mighty king came against it, surrounded it, and built large siege ramps against it.

15Now a poor wise man was found in the city, and he saved the city by his wisdom. Yet no one remembered that poor man.

16And I said, “Wisdom is better than strength, but the wisdom of the poor man is despised, and his words are not heeded.”

17The calm words of the wise are heeded over the shouts of a ruler among fools.

Summary

The Preacher affirms that the righteous and the wise are in God's hand, though no one can know from human experience whether they meet with love or hatred, since the same fate — death — comes to everyone regardless of righteousness, wickedness, or ritual observance (vv. 1-3). He values even the humblest living creature over the mightiest of the dead, since the dead have no further knowledge, reward, or memory (vv. 4-6). He therefore counsels enjoying bread and wine with a joyful heart, dressing well, and living joyfully with one's spouse, since such enjoyment is the portion God gives for this life, and there is no work, planning, or wisdom left in the grave (vv. 7-10). He observes that speed, strength, and skill do not guarantee success since time and chance affect everyone, and illustrates this with a poor wise man who saved a besieged city yet was quickly forgotten (vv. 11-16). He closes by affirming that quiet wisdom outweighs a fool's shouting, though a single sinner can destroy much good (vv. 17-18).

Authorship & Background

Author: 'The Preacher' (Qoheleth), traditionally identified as Solomon. Written late in life as a reflection on life's meaning. Hebrew title: 'Qoheleth' — 'The Assembler/Preacher.'
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: One event happening to the righteous and the wicked alike, the living told to eat and drink and work because the dead cannot, time and chance overriding swiftness and strength, and a poor wise man whose city forgot him
Historical Context: Chapter 9 confronts the great equalizer: death. The righteous and the wicked, the clean and the unclean, share "one event" — they all die (vv.1-3). This observation leads not to nihilism but to the book's most passionate enjoyment passage (vv.7-10): since death is certain, live fully now — eat with joy, dress festively, love your spouse, and do your work with all your might. The urgency is palpable: "there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave, whither thou goest" (v.10). The chapter then turns to the role of "time and chance" (v.11) — outcomes are not always proportional to ability — and concludes with a parable about a poor wise man who saves a city but is forgotten (vv.13-18). The message: wisdom is superior to strength and weapons, yet it is despised and unrewarded in this world. Live faithfully regardless.
One Event unto All (vv.1-6): The righteous and the wicked, the clean and the unclean, the one who sacrifices and the one who does not — one event happeneth to them all, and he calls this an evil under the sun. Verse 4 offers the one advantage the living hold: a living dog is better than a dead lion. The dead know nothing and have no more portion in anything done under the sun, which is the premise the next section builds its counsel on.
Go Thy Way, Eat Thy Bread (vv.7-10): The most vigorous passage in the book. Eat with joy, drink with a merry heart, wear white, keep oil on your head, live joyfully with the wife you love, and whatever your hand finds to do, do it with your might. The reason given is the one from the previous section — there is no work nor device nor knowledge in the grave where thou goest. Mortality is the argument for engagement rather than for withdrawal.
Time and Chance (vv.11-12): The race is not to the swift nor the battle to the strong, and bread does not reliably go to the wise. Time and chance happeneth to them all. The birds and fishes of v.12 are taken suddenly in a net, and men are said to be taken the same way. Competence improves the odds and does not settle them, which is a harder thing to say than either fatalism or confidence.
The Poor Wise Man (vv.13-18): A small city besieged by a great king is delivered by one poor wise man, and no man remembered him. That is the whole parable, and it is offered as an example of wisdom he says he saw. The closing lines hold the tension: wisdom is better than strength, and the poor man's words are not heard. Both are said to be true at once.

Deep Dive

Setting: Chapter 8 ended with the Preacher admitting that no one, however wise, can "find out the work that is done under the sun" (8:17). Chapter 9 takes that limit and presses it as far as it goes. The righteous and the wicked are both "in the hand of God," but no one can read from events whether God's hand is for or against them (v1). The same end, death, comes to everyone (vv2–6). So he gives his warmest call yet to enjoy life and work hard while there is time (vv7–10). Then he shows how little control anyone has over outcomes (vv11–12), and closes with a parable about a poor wise man whose wisdom saved a city and was then forgotten (vv13–18). For the question of who wrote the book, see the Setting of chapter 1.

Death Comes to All (vv1–6)

  • "All this I laid to heart, examining it all" (v1). "Examining" is literally "to clear up" or "sift." JFB renders it "explore." He has been studying the puzzle of justice since 8:10, and here is what the study turned up.
  • "The righteous and the wise and their deeds are in the hand of God" (v1). This is a statement of faith, not despair. The "hand" of God in the Old Testament means his power and care (Ps 31:15, "My times are in your hand"; Deut 33:3). The righteous are not adrift. They are held.
  • "Whether it is love or hate, man does not know; both are before him" (v1). But being in God's hand does not tell a person how God regards him. Nothing in a good harvest or a bad illness labels it as God's love or God's anger. The Tyndale notes put it plainly: there is no promise that doing good will bring rewards in this life. Guzik makes the same point and draws a Christian conclusion: we do not read God's love off our circumstances, we read it at the cross (Rom 5:8). Some take "love or hate" as human feelings instead, meaning people cannot know what they will come to love or hate. JFB, on v6, reads it as the wicked's feelings toward the righteous. The ESV's wording leaves God's disposition as the most natural sense.
  • "The same event happens to the righteous and the wicked" (v2). The "event" (miqreh) is the word from 2:14–15 and 3:19, and it means death. He lists five pairs: righteous and wicked, good and evil, clean and unclean (ritually fit for worship or not), the one who sacrifices and the one who doesn't, and the one who keeps an oath and the one who avoids one. Religious and moral differences, all of them real, make no difference to the grave. JFB notes this is true "not universally; but as to death."
  • "This is an evil in all that is done under the sun" (v3). He does not call death natural or neutral. He calls it "an evil." Scripture agrees: death is an enemy (1 Cor 15:26), something that entered through sin (Gen 2:17; Rom 5:12).
  • "The hearts of the children of man are full of evil, and madness is in their hearts while they live" (v3). Because death treats everyone alike, people conclude that how they live does not matter, and they live accordingly. "Madness" (holelut) is the word for senseless folly in 1:17 and 2:12. Then "after that they go to the dead," the Hebrew ending abruptly, as death itself does.
  • "He who is joined with all the living has hope" (v4). Hope here is modest. While someone lives, things can still change. JFB pushes it further: while alive, a person can still repent and be saved. The text itself says only that life holds possibility and death does not.
  • "A living dog is better than a dead lion" (v4). In Israel the dog was not a pet but a scavenger, a byword for the lowest and most despised (1 Sam 17:43; 2 Sam 9:8). The lion was the king of beasts, a picture of strength and nobility. The proverb says the meanest life beats the grandest death.
  • "The living know that they will die, but the dead know nothing" (v5). This is bitter irony. The living's one advantage is that they know they will die. The dead do not even have that. He is speaking of the dead as seen "under the sun": they take no part in earthly life, earn no more wages ("reward" here means pay for work), and are soon forgotten (1:11; 2:16). This verse is sometimes used to teach "soul sleep," the idea that the dead are unconscious until the resurrection. Most interpreters think that reads too much into it. The Preacher is describing death from the outside, from the viewpoint of this world, not answering every question about the life to come. JFB says the dead know nothing "so far as their bodily senses and worldly affairs are concerned." Later revelation says much more (Luke 23:43; Phil 1:23; 2 Cor 5:8).
  • "Their love and their hate and their envy have already perished" (v6). The strongest human passions, the ones that drive so much of life, stop at the grave. "Forever they have no more share" means no more portion (cheleq) in anything "under the sun." The qualifier matters. He is speaking of this world.
  • Commentators: Henry calls the core puzzle "the little difference that is made between good men and bad," and observes that "commonly as to outward things, good and bad men fare much alike." Guzik passes on Kidner's point that observation alone cannot show how God is disposed toward us. JFB on the living dog: the dog is a metaphor "for the vilest persons," the lion for "the noblest."

Enjoy Life with the One You Love (vv7–10)

  • "Go, eat your bread with joy, and drink your wine with a merry heart" (v7). The sixth and warmest of the enjoyment passages (2:24; 3:12–13, 22; 5:18–20; 8:15). Earlier ones said there is "nothing better." This one is a command: "Go." Bread and wine were the staples of an ordinary meal. He is not commending feasting and drunkenness but the plain table received gladly.
  • "For God has already approved what you do" (v7). "Approved" is ratsah, "be pleased with, accept favorably." It is the word used of God accepting a sacrifice (Lev 1:4). The joy is grounded in God's favor. JFB reads it as God accepting the believer's works "as He has previously accepted thy person." Guzik is more cautious, noting a hint of wishful thinking in the Preacher's voice. Either way, he is not saying God approves whatever anyone does. In context, enjoying God's gifts is itself what God intends (2:24–25; 5:19).
  • "Let your garments be always white. Let not oil be lacking on your head" (v8). In a hot climate, white clothes reflected the sun and showed they were freshly washed. Oil on the head soothed and refreshed the skin. Both were signs of festivity; mourners wore dark sackcloth and went without oil (2 Sam 12:20; 14:2). "Always" means not saving joy only for special occasions. JFB adds that white is "typical of the spotless righteousness of Jesus Christ" (Rev 3:4–5; 19:8), which goes beyond the plain sense but fits the Bible's later use of the image.
  • "Enjoy life with the wife whom you love" (v9). Literally "see life," that is, experience it. After his long search among wealth, projects and pleasures (2:1–11), and his sour words about the woman whose heart is snares (7:26), the Preacher names marriage as one of life's great goods. Notice it is "the wife," singular, and "whom you love." JFB contrasts this "godly and true love" with the many concubines. Proverbs says the same: "Rejoice in the wife of your youth" (Prov 5:18).
  • "All the days of your vain life" (v9). "Vain" is hebel, the book's key word, a breath or vapor. Life is fleeting. That is exactly why its gifts should be enjoyed rather than postponed. "That is your portion" (cheleq) answers v6: the dead have no more portion, the living do, and this is it.
  • "Whatever your hand finds to do, do it with your might" (v10). Joy and work belong together here. The opportunity in front of a person should get full effort, not half-hearted attention. Paul says much the same: "Whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord" (Col 3:23).
  • "For there is no work or thought or knowledge or wisdom in Sheol, to which you are going" (v10). Sheol is the Old Testament name for the place of the dead. The Tyndale notes say it is not always linked to punishment. It is simply where everyone goes. The Preacher's point is practical: earthly work and planning end at death. Jesus said, "We must work the works of him who sent me while it is day; night is coming, when no one can work" (John 9:4).
  • An ancient parallel. The Old Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh (about 1800 BC) has a tavern keeper named Siduri give Gilgamesh strikingly similar advice: fill your belly, make merry day and night, wear clean clothes, wash your head, and delight the wife in your embrace. The likeness is real and has often been noted. The difference is just as real. Siduri tells Gilgamesh to give up searching because the gods kept life for themselves. The Preacher grounds joy in God's approval and gift ("that he has given you").
  • Commentators: Henry draws the lesson that "death puts a final period to all our employments and enjoyments in this world," so we should use both while we have them. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's line that no one ever served God by doing things tomorrow. JFB on v10 quotes South: "The soul's play-day is Satan's work-day," and insists this is not the voice "of a skeptic" but of one serving God.

Wisdom Better Than Folly (vv11–18)

  • "The race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong" (v11). Five things people expect to follow from ability: speed wins races, strength wins wars, wisdom earns a living, intelligence brings wealth, knowledge wins favor. Often they don't. David was no match for Goliath on paper (1 Sam 17), and Gideon's three hundred routed an army (Judg 7).
  • "But time and chance happen to them all" (v11). "Chance" is pega, "occurrence," a word found only here and in 1 Kings 5:4, where it means a misfortune. It does not mean blind luck in a modern sense, a universe without God. It means the unforeseen event that no one could plan for. "Time" is the right or wrong moment. Guzik, following Kidner, notes the two are paired because both take matters out of our hands. JFB calls chance "seemingly, really Providence," and sums it up: "Duties are ours; events, God's."
  • "Man does not know his time" (v12). In this book "time" can be the time of death, and JFB reads it so here. The fish swims into the net and the bird into the trap with no warning. Jesus uses the same picture for his return: that day will come "like a trap" (Luke 21:34–35).
  • "There was a little city with few men in it, and a great king came against it" (v14). The parable is set up as a mismatch: little city, few men, great king, great siegeworks. "Siegeworks" were the ramps and towers attackers built against the walls. No particular city is named. JFB points to the wise woman of Abel who saved her city from Joab's siege by a few words (2 Sam 20:16–22), a real example of the same kind.
  • "A poor, wise man, and he by his wisdom delivered the city. Yet no one remembered that poor man" (v15). The Hebrew can also be read "he could have delivered the city," that is, his wisdom would have saved it if anyone had listened. Most translations, including the ESV, take it as something that happened. Either way the sting is the same: the city owed him everything and forgot him. JFB compares Pharaoh's cupbearer, who "did not remember Joseph, but forgot him" (Gen 40:23).
  • "Wisdom is better than might, though the poor man's wisdom is despised" (v16). He holds two truths together. Wisdom really is stronger than force. And people rarely honor it if it comes from someone without status.
  • "The words of the wise heard in quiet are better than the shouting of a ruler among fools" (v17). Wisdom often speaks softly. Folly often shouts, and a loud voice in charge of fools can drown it out. Elijah heard God not in the wind or earthquake but in "a low whisper" (1 Kgs 19:12).
  • "Wisdom is better than weapons of war, but one sinner destroys much good" (v18). The chapter ends on a warning. Wisdom can save a city, but one foolish or wicked person can undo it all. The Tyndale notes connect this to the next verse: "Dead flies make the perfumer's ointment give off a stench" (10:1). Achan's one sin brought defeat on all Israel (Josh 7). Guzik observes that destroying is always easier than building.
  • Commentators: Henry says "God's providence often crosses the fairest and most hopeful probabilities of men's endeavour," so people must use means but not trust in them. On the parable he notes that "wisdom often makes men very useful, and yet gains them little respect." Guzik answers the forgotten poor man with the God who does not forget his people (Mal 3:16; 2 Tim 2:19). JFB reads the parable typically: the little city is the Church, the great king Satan, the poor wise man Jesus Christ. That is an allegory laid over the story, not its plain meaning, though the picture fits the gospel strikingly.

Pointing to Christ

The Preacher saw that the same event, death, comes to all, and he called it "an evil" (v3). He could say no more than that a living dog is better than a dead lion. Christ entered that shared fate himself. The righteous one died the death of the wicked, and on the third day he rose, so that for those in him death is no longer the end of all "work or thought or knowledge" but the doorway to being "with Christ, for that is far better" (Phil 1:23). The parable of the poor wise man reads, in hindsight, like a sketch of him: he "became poor" (2 Cor 8:9), he delivered his people by wisdom the world counted foolish (1 Cor 1:23–24), and he was despised and rejected (Isa 53:3). Unlike the poor man of the parable, he will not be forgotten. And the one who knows God's love not from circumstances but from the cross can do what v7 commands: eat bread with joy, because God has already approved.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik notes that vv.7-10 constitute "the most energetic and passionate of all the 'enjoyment passages'" in Ecclesiastes. Solomon moves from observation to command: Go! Eat! Drink! Work! Live! The awareness of death (vv.1-6) does not produce despair but urgency — if life is brief, then waste none of it. The parable of the poor wise man (vv.13-18) teaches that wisdom's value is intrinsic, not dependent on recognition. Do the right thing even when no one notices or remembers.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • The Same Ending for Everyone (vv.2-3) — The righteous and the wicked, the clean and the unclean, all share the same fate in the end. Does knowing that death erases every earthly advantage change what you're chasing right now?
  • An Invitation to Live Fully, Not Cautiously (vv.7-9) — The Preacher urges eating with joy, dressing for celebration, and loving your spouse, because God has already approved these ordinary pleasures. Are you holding back from joy out of guilt when God has already given you permission to receive it?
  • No Second Chances After This (v.10) — He insists that whatever your hand finds to do, you should do it with all your strength, since there is no work or planning left in the grave. What are you putting off that you'd regret leaving undone?
  • Effort Doesn't Guarantee the Outcome (v.11) — He observes that the race doesn't always go to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, since time and chance overtake everyone. Where are you assuming that hard work should automatically produce the result you want?
  • Doing Right Even When No One Remembers (vv.15-16) — A poor wise man saves a city with his wisdom, and no one remembers him for it. Would you still choose to do the wise, faithful thing even if you knew you'd never get credit?
