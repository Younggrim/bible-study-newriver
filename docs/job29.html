Summary

Job longs for the months now gone, when God's friendship lit his path and his household still surrounded him (vv. 2-6). He recalls being honored at the city gate, where young men stepped aside and elders rose in his presence (vv. 7-10), because he rescued the poor, the orphan, and the dying, defended the widow, served as eyes for the blind and feet for the lame, and broke the grip of the wicked on their prey (vv. 11-17). He had expected to die at home in peace after a long, honored life, with people hanging on his counsel like farmers waiting for rain (vv. 18-25).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown (see Chapter 1 notes for full discussion).
Historical Context: Chapters 29-31 form Job's final monologue — his closing statement before the court of heaven. These three chapters are structured brilliantly: chapter 29 looks backward (former glory), chapter 30 describes the present (current misery), and chapter 31 looks forward (oath of innocence and challenge to God). Together they constitute Job's most extended and passionate self-defense. Chapter 29 specifically is Job's "remember when" — a poignant elegy for the life he has lost. It is not self-pity but evidence: this is who I was, and these are the blessings God gave me. The contrast with chapter 30 will be devastating.
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: an elegy for a vanished life opening with when God preserved me and his candle shined upon my head, elders rising to their feet and princes falling silent the moment Job appeared at the city gate, righteousness described entirely in terms of active advocacy for the poor, the widow, the blind, the lame, directly refuting accusations Eliphaz has not yet even made, an expectation of dying peacefully in his own nest with days multiplied like the sand, and a closing image of Job living as a king among his people, one who comforted mourners rather than needing comfort himself
Job's Former Blessings (vv.1-6): Job remembers when "God preserved me" (v.2), when God's "candle shined upon my head" (v.3), when "the secret of God was upon my tabernacle" (v.4), when his children surrounded him (v.5), and when he "washed his steps with butter" (v.6). This is a portrait of total blessing: divine favor, divine guidance, divine intimacy, family joy, and material abundance. Every dimension of life was full.
Job's Former Honor (vv.7-11): When Job went to the city gate (the place of public life and legal proceedings), young men withdrew in respect (v.8), elders rose to their feet (v.8), princes stopped talking (v.9), and nobles fell silent (v.10). "When the ear heard me, then it blessed me" (v.11). Job was the most respected man in his community — not through force but through character.
Job's Former Righteousness (vv.12-17): Why was Job honored? Not for wealth but for justice. He delivered the poor (v.12), blessed the perishing (v.13), made widows sing (v.13), put on righteousness like clothing (v.14), was eyes to the blind and feet to the lame (v.15), was father to the poor (v.16), and broke the jaws of the wicked (v.17). This directly refutes Eliphaz's accusations in 22:6-9. Job did not oppress the poor — he was their champion.
Job's Former Expectations (vv.18-25): Job expected to die in peace: "I shall die in my nest, and I shall multiply my days as the sand" (v.18). He expected continued vigor (v.20), continued leadership (vv.21-25), continued influence. He lived "as a king in the army, as one that comforteth the mourners" (v.25). The cruelty of his suffering is sharpened by the beauty of what was lost.

Setting: The debate with the three friends is over, though no one announces it. After Bildad's six-verse speech (ch 25), Zophar never takes his turn. In chapter 27 Job swore an oath: "As God lives, who has taken away my right... till I die I will not put away my integrity from me" (27:2, 5). He then described the end of the wicked man in words that sound much like the friends (27:13–23), and many scholars wonder whether those verses were once Zophar's missing speech. Chapter 28 followed with a quiet poem on wisdom. People can dig mines into the dark and bring hidden gold to light, but no one can dig up wisdom or buy it. Only God knows its place, and he has told humanity, "the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom" (28:28). Now Job begins his last speech, which runs to "The words of Job are ended" (31:40). It has three movements. Chapter 29 looks back at what his life used to be. Chapter 30 turns on the words "But now" (30:1). Chapter 31 is a series of oaths of innocence ending with a demand that God answer. This chapter is the "before" picture, and almost every line answers something a friend has said about him.

Job's Summary Defense (vv1–25)

  • "And Job again took up his discourse" (v1). The same words open chapter 27. "Discourse" is the word often translated "proverb" or "parable," a formal, poetic speech. JFB and Henry both picture Job pausing after chapter 28 to give the friends room to answer. When none of them speaks, he goes on. Guzik notes that Job is no longer answering the friends point by point but laying out his whole case.
  • "Oh, that I were as in the months of old, as in the days when God watched over me" (v2). The first thing Job misses is not his wealth or even his children. It is God. "Watched over" is shamar, to keep, guard or preserve, the verb of the blessing "The LORD bless you and keep you" (Num 6:24). The Tyndale notes point out the irony. Job has used this same verb to complain that God was watching him like a guard watching a prisoner: "If I sin, you watch me" (10:14; also 13:27; 14:16). Here it means the kindly care of Psalm 121, "He who keeps you will not slumber." The same God, the same watching, now feels completely different.
  • "When his lamp shone upon my head, and by his light I walked through darkness" (v3). God's favor felt like a lamp held over him. JFB suggests the lamps carried before caravans "in nightly travels through deserts." Job had dark days, but he always had light to walk by: "it is you who light my lamp" (Ps 18:28). The image answers Bildad, who said of the wicked, "his lamp above him is put out" (18:6). Job's lamp has gone out, and he is the last person who would say he deserved it.
  • "As I was in my prime, when the friendship of God was upon my tent" (v4). The ESV footnote gives the Hebrew: "my autumn days." The lexicon explains choreph as harvest time, here a picture of maturity. In Israel autumn was not decline but the season of ripe fruit and full barns. JFB calls it "the time of the ripe fruits of my prosperity." "Friendship" is sod, which the lexicon defines as an intimate circle, close counsel, the private talk of friends. It is the word in "The friendship of the LORD is for those who fear him" (Ps 25:14). Eliphaz had sneered, "Have you listened in the council of God?" (15:8), using the same word. Job's answer is that he once knew God that closely. The lexicon also notes a proposed one-letter change, "when God sheltered my tent," which would echo Satan's complaint about the "hedge around him and his house" (1:10).
  • "When the Almighty was yet with me, when my children were all around me" (v5). "The Almighty" is Shaddai, the name of God from the patriarchs (Gen 17:1) and Job's favorite name for him; the lexicon counts about thirty uses in Job. The order matters: God's presence first, the children second. This is the chapter's only mention of his children, and its restraint is moving beside the messenger's report that the house fell "on the young people, and they are dead" (1:19). JFB has no comment on this verse.
  • "When my steps were washed with butter, and the rock poured out for me streams of oil!" (v6). This is deliberate exaggeration, the language of overflowing plenty. "Butter" is chem'ah, which the lexicon defines as curds, thick soured milk; JFB says the same. Olive trees grew well on rocky hillsides, so oil from rock is a natural picture of abundance, as JFB notes. The closest parallel is the Song of Moses, where God gave Israel "oil out of the flinty rock" and "curds from the herd" (Deut 32:13–14). Zophar had said the wicked man "will not look upon... the streams flowing with honey and curds" (20:17). Job had those streams and lost them, and he wants it known he did not lose them for being wicked.
  • "When I went out to the gate of the city, when I prepared my seat in the square" (v7). Now Job moves from home to public life. The Tyndale notes explain that the gate was where a town did its business and held court, as when Boaz gathers ten elders there to settle a legal matter (Ruth 4:1–2). The good wife's husband "is known in the gates when he sits among the elders" (Prov 31:23). Guzik passes on Elmer Smick's point that a city with a gate and square was a real urban center, so Job was a leading citizen and judge, not only a herdsman. The book never names the city.
  • "The young men saw me and withdrew, and the aged rose and stood" (v8). In an honor culture, respect is shown with the body. The young stepped aside. The old men, who were themselves owed honor ("You shall stand up before the gray head," Lev 19:32), rose when Job arrived. Henry says they would not even stay in their seats.
  • "The princes refrained from talking and laid their hand on their mouth" (v9). Even the powerful went quiet. "Their tongue stuck to the roof of their mouth" (v10) is the idiom of Psalm 137:6. JFB pictures the chiefs held in awe by his presence. There is irony here. Men laid their hands on their mouths before Job. At the end of the book, when God speaks from the whirlwind, Job himself will say, "I lay my hand on my mouth" (40:4). The silent awe others once showed him, he will show God.
  • "When the ear heard, it called me blessed" (v11). "Called me blessed" is ashar, related to the "blessed" that opens the Psalms. The lexicon gives its meaning here as to pronounce someone happy, as when the good wife's children "call her blessed" (Prov 31:28). It is also used of the ideal king: "may they call him blessed" (Ps 72:17). JFB hears the language of witnesses in a court and points to the crowd in Nazareth who "all spoke well of him" (Luke 4:22).
  • "Because I delivered the poor who cried for help, and the fatherless who had none to help him" (v12). This is the hinge of the chapter. Job's honor was not for wealth or birth but for what he did, and what he did answers Eliphaz almost line by line. Eliphaz had accused him: "you have... stripped the naked of their clothing... You have sent widows away empty, and the arms of the fatherless were crushed" (22:6, 9). The curated cross-references tie v12 directly to those verses, and so does JFB: "So far was I from sending 'widows' away empty." Job is not making a vague claim to be good. He is giving specific testimony from a public life the whole town had seen.
  • "I caused the widow's heart to sing for joy" (v13). "Him who was about to perish" is someone at the end of his rope, from hunger or a ruinous lawsuit. Job did not just give alms; he made widows sing. In the Law, the widow, the fatherless and the stranger are the people God personally defends (Deut 10:18), and a judge who cared for them was doing God's work (Ps 82:3). Job will return to this in his oaths (31:16–19).
  • "I put on righteousness, and it clothed me" (v14). The verb is labash, to put on or wear a garment. The lexicon notes it is often used for being clothed with a quality such as righteousness or strength. The second line can be read "and it put me on," as if righteousness wrapped itself around Job, just as "the Spirit of the LORD clothed Gideon" (Judg 6:34) with the same verb. Job used labash earlier for something very different: "My flesh is clothed with worms and dirt" (7:5).
  • "My justice was like a robe and a turban" (v14). The Tyndale notes call these royal images and point to the robe as a picture of righteousness elsewhere (Ps 132:9; Isa 61:10; Rev 19:8). The "robe" is me'il, which the lexicon describes as an outer garment of men of rank, and also of the high priest. It is the robe Job tore when the news of his children came (1:20). The "turban" is tsaniph, used of the high priest's clean turban (Zech 3:5) and a "royal diadem" (Isa 62:3). Justice was not something Job did now and then. It was what he wore.
  • "I was eyes to the blind and feet to the lame" (v15). The Law forbade putting "a stumbling block before the blind" (Lev 19:14). Job went further and became what the disabled lacked. Moses asked Hobab to stay with Israel and "serve as eyes for us" (Num 10:31). JFB adds that the line also fits the spiritual help a wiser person gives someone less enlightened.
  • "I was a father to the needy, and I searched out the cause of him whom I did not know" (v16). The Psalms say this of God: "Father of the fatherless and protector of widows is God" (Ps 68:5). The second line is the mark of a just judge. Job investigated the case of a stranger, someone who was not his relative or client, with nothing to gain. The Tyndale notes draw out this concern for strangers.
  • "I broke the fangs of the unrighteous and made him drop his prey from his teeth" (v17). Kindness to the weak meant toughness with the predator. The oppressor is a wild beast with a victim in its jaws, and Job forces its mouth open. The Psalms say the same of God: "you break the teeth of the wicked" (Ps 3:7). JFB: "So compassionate was Job to the oppressed, so terrible to the oppressor!" Henry draws the lesson that a good ruler needs courage as well as tenderness.
  • "Then I thought, 'I shall die in my nest'" (v18). Job expected to grow old safely and die at home. The lexicon notes the Hebrew is difficult and literally reads "with my nest," which JFB takes as with his family alive around him, "Instead of my family dying before me, as now." A nest is a picture of a secure home: "your nest is set in the rock" (Num 24:21). Henry warns that even good prosperity tempts a man to conclude, "To morrow shall be as this day," like the rich fool of Luke 12, though he hopes Job's confidence rested on God.
  • "And I shall multiply my days as the sand" (v18). The Tyndale notes link "sand" to God's promise of countless descendants to Abraham and Jacob (Gen 22:17; 32:12): to Job, long life was a sign of God's blessing. The word chol has a famous second reading. An old Jewish tradition told of a long-lived bird called the chol that is renewed, much like the phoenix of Egyptian and Greek legend. The old Greek translation reads "like the trunk of a palm tree," and the Greek word for palm, phoinix, is also the name of the phoenix. JFB thinks "Job probably refers to this," since bird and nest go together. Guzik passes on Smick's discussion and treats the phoenix reading as unlikely. The lexicon gives "sand" and mentions the phoenix only as a view some hold. Either way the point is a very long life.
  • "My roots spread out to the waters, with the dew all night on my branches" (v19). This is the tree of Psalm 1, "planted by streams of water," and of Jeremiah 17:8, which "does not fear when heat comes." Guzik passes on Adam Clarke's note that such a tree flourishes in every season. Dew was precious in a land with no summer rain (Gen 27:28; Hos 14:5). JFB notes the line answers Bildad's picture of the wicked man: "His roots dry up beneath, and his branches wither above" (18:16).
  • "My glory fresh with me, and my bow ever new in my hand" (v20). "Glory" here is honor and standing. The bow is strength, as when Jacob blessed Joseph: "his bow remained unmoved" (Gen 49:24). JFB calls it a war metaphor for strength "ever renewed." In chapter 30 the picture is reversed: "God has loosed my cord and humbled me" (30:11), the bowstring cut.
  • "Men listened to me and waited and kept silence for my counsel" (v21). The ESV begins a new paragraph here, returning to Job's public life after his private hopes (vv18–20). A few scholars move vv21–25 to follow v10, but no manuscript does, and the poem reads well as it stands: honor, hopes, then honor again as the climax.
  • "After I spoke they did not speak again, and my word dropped upon them" (v22). When Job gave counsel, there was nothing left to add. For twenty chapters the friends have answered every word he said, and after this speech they never speak again. "Dropped" pictures speech falling gently like rain, as in "May my teaching drop as the rain" (Deut 32:2). JFB: like "the genial rain" on the soil "on which it gently drops."
  • "They opened their mouths as for the spring rain" (v23). "Spring rain" is malqosh, the late rain of March and April, which the lexicon explains was vital because it filled out the crops before harvest (Deut 11:14). The lexicon also points to Proverbs 16:15: "In the light of a king's face there is life, and his favor is like the clouds that bring the spring rain." That proverb holds both this image and the next verse's "light of my face," so Job is describing himself in the language used for a good king.
  • "I smiled on them when they had no confidence, and the light of my face they did not cast down" (v24). Translations split on this verse. The ESV reads it as encouragement: when people lost heart, Job's smile lifted them, and their gloom never darkened his face. The King James, followed by Henry and JFB, reads "If I laughed on them, they believed it not": Job was so grave and respected that people could hardly believe it when he smiled, and still never took his kindness lightly. JFB calls gravity "a virtue much esteemed in the East," and Guzik, through Matthew Poole, reads it similarly. Either way, a shining face is the language of favor, as in "The LORD make his face to shine upon you" (Num 6:25).
  • "I lived like a king among his troops, like one who comforts mourners" (v25). The whole chapter has been building to this comparison: robe and turban, a seat at the gate, princes falling silent, favor like spring rain. But the picture of a king ends not with conquest but with comfort, and that is the most pointed line of all. Eliphaz admitted it: "your words have upheld him who was stumbling" (4:4), and the Tyndale notes point back to that verse. The friends came "to show him sympathy and comfort him" (2:11), and Job has called them "miserable comforters" (16:2). He was the comforter they failed to be. The Tyndale notes also link the line to Isaiah's Messiah, sent "to comfort all who mourn" (Isa 61:2), and to the "God of all comfort" (2 Cor 1:3–4).
  • Was Job boasting? Readers notice how often "I" appears here, and some find the portrait proud. Others answer that this is testimony in a trial, not bragging: he has been accused of specific crimes (22:6–9) and is giving specific evidence against them. The book sides with Job on the facts. The narrator and God both call him "blameless and upright" (1:1, 8), and God never says Job lied about his past. What God challenges is Job's claim to understand how the world is run (38:2). Guzik passes on Mason's view that Job's long delay before mentioning his good works shows humility, and Francis Andersen's point that for Job, failing to do good to anyone of any rank was a serious offense against God. And Job puts first not his wealth or honor but "the friendship of God" (v4).
  • Commentators: All three read the chapter as Job measuring the height he fell from, and all three tie vv12–17 to Eliphaz's false charges. Henry's outline is clearest: comfort at home (vv1–6), honor in his country (vv7–10), good done as a magistrate (vv11–17), and his expectation that it would last, at home (vv18–20) and abroad (vv21–25). He compares Job to Naomi: "I went out full," but brought "home again empty" (Ruth 1:21). Guzik agrees that Job misses God before anything else, and passes on Charles Spurgeon's reading of vv2–4 as four losses: God's protection, comfort, guidance in perplexity and close fellowship. JFB is most help on single images: caravan lamps (v3), "thick milk" (v6), the court (v11), the bow (v20) and the gentle rain (v22). On the phoenix (v18), JFB favors it, Guzik through Smick doubts it, and the lexicon keeps "sand." On v24, Henry and JFB follow the King James, not the ESV's reading. Henry and JFB both end by seeing Christ in Job's good ruler. JFB has no comment on v5.

Pointing to Christ

Job's portrait of himself reads like Psalm 72, the prayer for the ideal king, which uses the same verb: "may they call him blessed" (Ps 72:17). That psalm says the king "delivers the needy when he calls, the poor and him who has no helper" (Ps 72:12), almost exactly Job's words in v12. Henry saw it: "Our Lord Jesus is such a King as Job was," and JFB says Job "unconsciously foreshadows Jesus Christ." The likeness is real, but so is the difference. Job was a good man whose goodness was partial and whose kingdom reached as far as one city's gate. Jesus was eyes to the blind and feet to the lame in the plainest sense (Matt 11:5), and in Nazareth he read from the very prophecy that promises comfort to mourners and said, "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:21). Then, like Job, he lost everything, and more. He laid aside his honor (Phil 2:6–8), was mocked by those he had helped, and was dressed in a mock royal robe (Matt 27:28). Job lost his robe and never learned why. Christ gave his up on purpose, so that people with no righteousness of their own could be covered "with the robe of righteousness" (Isa 61:10). Job longed for "the days when God watched over me." Those who belong to Christ are kept, and "no one will snatch them out of my hand" (John 10:28).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik notes that Job's recollection is not mere nostalgia but legal evidence. He is building his case: this was my character, this was my conduct, and therefore the suffering I now endure cannot be explained by the retribution principle my friends promote. Guzik also observes that Job's deepest loss is not wealth or health but the "friendship of God" (v.4) — intimate communion with his Maker.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.

Reflection

  • The Deepest Loss Is Relational (v.4) — Among everything Job recalls, what he most treasures is the intimate friendship of God that once rested on his life. If God's presence disappeared but everything else stayed the same, what would you actually be mourning?
  • Honor That Was Earned, Not Demanded (vv.7-11) — The community's deep respect for Job at the city gate came from his character, not his wealth or position. Does your influence over others come more from what you own or from who you are?
  • Righteousness Expressed as Generosity to the Vulnerable (vv.12-17) — Job's reputation rested on defending the poor, caring for widows and orphans, and confronting injustice. Who are the vulnerable people in your life that your faith should be moving you to protect?
  • Expecting Blessing to Continue Forever (v.18) — Job assumed his faithful life guaranteed a peaceful death surrounded by abundance. Where have you assumed that past faithfulness entitles you to future ease?
