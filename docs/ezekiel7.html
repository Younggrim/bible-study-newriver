1And the word of the LORD came to me, saying,

2“O son of man, this is what the Lord GOD says to the land of Israel: ‘The end! The end has come upon the four corners of the land.

3The end is now upon you, and I will unleash My anger against you. I will judge you according to your ways and repay you for all your abominations.

4I will not look on you with pity, nor will I spare you, but I will punish you for your ways and for the abominations among you. Then you will know that I am the LORD.’

5This is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Disaster! An unprecedented disaster—behold, it is coming!

6The end has come! The end has come! It has roused itself against you. Behold, it has come!

7Doom has come to you, O inhabitants of the land. The time has come; the day is near; there is panic on the mountains instead of shouts of joy.

8Very soon I will pour out My wrath upon you and vent My anger against you; I will judge you according to your ways and repay you for all your abominations.

9I will not look on you with pity, nor will I spare you, but I will punish you for your ways and for the abominations among you. Then you will know that it is I, the LORD, who strikes the blow.

10Behold, the day is here! It has come! Doom has gone out, the rod has budded, arrogance has bloomed.

11Their violence has grown into a rod to punish their wickedness. None of them will remain: none of their multitude, none of their wealth, and nothing of value.

12The time has come; the day has arrived. Let the buyer not rejoice and the seller not mourn, for wrath is upon the whole multitude.

13The seller will surely not recover what he sold while both remain alive. For the vision concerning the whole multitude will not be revoked, and because of their iniquity, not one of them will preserve his life.

14They have blown the trumpet and made everything ready, but no one goes to war, for My wrath is upon the whole multitude.

15The sword is outside; plague and famine are within. Those in the country will die by the sword, and those in the city will be devoured by famine and plague.

16The survivors will escape and live in the mountains, moaning like doves of the valley, each for his own iniquity.

17Every hand will go limp, and every knee will turn to water.

18They will put on sackcloth, and terror will overwhelm them. Shame will cover all their faces, and all their heads will be shaved.

19They will throw their silver into the streets, and their gold will seem unclean. Their silver and gold cannot save them in the day of the wrath of the LORD. They cannot satisfy their appetites or fill their stomachs with wealth, for it became the stumbling block that brought their iniquity.

20His beautiful ornaments they transformed into pride and used them to fashion their vile images and detestable idols. Therefore I will make these into something unclean for them.

21And I will hand these things over as plunder to foreigners and loot to the wicked of the earth, who will defile them.

22I will turn My face away from them, and they will defile My treasured place. Violent men will enter it, and they will defile it.

23Forge the chain, for the land is full of crimes of bloodshed, and the city is full of violence.

24So I will bring the most wicked of nations to take possession of their houses. I will end the pride of the mighty, and their holy places will be profaned.

25Anguish is coming! They will seek peace, but find none.

26Disaster upon disaster will come, and rumor after rumor. Then they will seek a vision from a prophet, but instruction from the priests will perish, as will counsel from the elders.

27The king will mourn, the prince will be clothed with despair, and the hands of the people of the land will tremble. I will deal with them according to their conduct, and I will judge them by their own standards. Then they will know that I am the LORD.’”

Summary

Ezekiel proclaims that the end has come upon the land of Israel from every direction (vv. 1-4). He describes it with urgent, staccato language: the day is near, doom is dawning, and violence has grown into a rod of wickedness ready to strike (vv. 5-11). Ordinary economic life collapses, since buying and selling lose all meaning when no one will keep what they gain, and silver and gold are thrown into the streets as worthless (vv. 12-19). The chapter ends with the sanctuary itself defiled and the land handed over to foreign plunderers (vv. 20-27).

Authorship & Background

Author: Ezekiel son of Buzi (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Oracle — The End Has Come
Key Themes: The finality of judgment ("the end"), economic collapse (silver useless), violence blossoming like a rod, all normal activity suspended, the Day of the LORD
Historical Context: This chapter has the rhythm of a funeral dirge — short, percussive phrases hammering home one message: The end. Not "an" end but "the" end — the definitive, final judgment on the land of Israel. Normal economic activity ceases (buying/selling meaningless, v.12-13). Military strength fails (trumpet blown but nobody goes to battle, v.14). Money becomes worthless (silver cast into streets, v.19). The chapter likely anticipates 586 BC — when everything they knew collapsed simultaneously.
An End Upon the Four Corners of the Land (vv.1-4): The word this chapter is built on is the end, and it is repeated until it stops being an announcement and becomes a sound. An end, the end is come upon the four corners of the land. Now is the end come upon thee. What follows is judgment described as accounting, I will judge thee according to thy ways, and will recompense upon thee all thine abominations, with the withdrawal stated twice, mine eye shall not spare thee, neither will I have pity.
An Evil, an Only Evil (vv.5-9): This block says the same thing as the last one with small variations, an evil, an only evil, behold, it is come. That is how the chapter works. It does not develop an argument, it repeats one with the intervals shortening, which is a formal imitation of what it describes. The day of trouble is near, and not the sounding again of the mountains, that is, not the echo of a shout but the thing itself. And the refrain lands again, ye shall know that I am the LORD that smiteth.
The Rod Has Blossomed (vv.10-13): Behold the day, behold, it is come, the morning is gone forth, the rod hath blossomed, pride hath budded. Growing imagery used of a punishment is characteristic of this book. Then the practical consequence, and it is economic: let not the buyer rejoice, nor the seller mourn, because the seller shall not return to that which is sold. Land in Israel was inalienable in principle and reverted at the jubilee; the announcement here is that the machinery of property has stopped meaning anything, because there will be nobody to inherit.
The Trumpet Blown, and Nobody Goes (vv.14-18): They have blown the trumpet, and made ready, but none goeth to the battle, which is the most economical picture of collapse in the chapter: the alarm still works and the response does not. The sword is without and pestilence and famine within, so both leaving and staying are fatal. Those who escape to the mountains are compared to doves of the valleys, all of them mourning. The physical description that follows became standard prophetic shorthand, all hands shall be feeble, and all knees shall be weak as water, with sackcloth and baldness upon all heads.
Silver in the Streets (vv.19-22): They shall cast their silver in the streets, and their gold shall be removed, and the reason is given in terms of what money can and cannot buy, their silver and their gold shall not be able to deliver them in the day of the wrath of the LORD, they shall not satisfy their souls, neither fill their bowels. Then the aggravation, that the same metal had been the material of the idols, it is the stumblingblock of their iniquity. The section ends with the temple itself handed over, I will give it into the hands of strangers for a prey, and they shall pollute it.
The Chain, and the Collapse of Every Office (vv.23-27): Make a chain, for the land is full of bloody crimes, and the city is full of violence. Peace is specifically ruled out, they shall seek peace, and there shall be none, and rumour follows rumour and mischief follows mischief. The last verses take the society apart by function, which is the most complete such list in the prophets: the law shall perish from the priest, and counsel from the ancients, the king shall mourn, the prince shall be clothed with desolation, and the hands of the people of the land shall be troubled. Every office named is one that was supposed to hold things together.

Setting: Babylonia, around 592 BC. Ezekiel has acted out the siege of Jerusalem (ch 4), shaved his head to picture the city's fate (ch 5), and pronounced judgment on the idol shrines across the mountains of Israel (ch 6). Chapter 7 closes this first block of judgment oracles (chs 4–7) with something closer to a poem than a sermon. The lines are short, breathless and repetitive, full of exclamations: "An end!" "Behold, it comes!" "Behold, the day!" It reads like a watchman shouting from the wall as the enemy pours over the hills. The message is that the "day of the LORD," which earlier prophets had warned about (Amos 5:18–20; Joel 1:15; Zeph 1:14–18), is no longer coming. It has arrived. Within about six years Jerusalem would be besieged, and in 586 BC it would fall.

The Day of the Wrath of the Lord (vv1–27)

  • One long oracle. The ESV gives the chapter a single heading. It falls into three parts, each marked by "Thus says the Lord GOD" or "Behold": the end has come (vv2–4), disaster after disaster (vv5–9), and the day itself described in full (vv10–27). The first two end with almost the same words, and all three end with "you will know that I am the LORD" (vv4, 9, 27).
  • "An end! The end has come upon the four corners of the land" (v2). The word is qets, "end." Amos had seen a basket of summer fruit (qayits) and heard God say, "The end (qets) has come upon my people Israel" (Amos 8:2). Ezekiel takes up Amos's word for the northern kingdom's end and applies it to Judah, nearly a century and a half later. "The four corners of the land" means everywhere, the whole land from edge to edge. There will be nowhere to hide.
  • "I will judge you according to your ways" (v3). This phrase runs through the chapter (vv3, 4, 8, 9, 27). The punishment fits the crime. God is not being arbitrary. "While your abominations are in your midst" (v4) means while their idols and their sins still sit right among them. Judgment will come with the evidence in plain view.
  • "My eye will not spare you, nor will I have pity" (v4). This repeats 5:11. The time for holding back is over.
  • "Disaster after disaster! Behold, it comes" (v5). Some Hebrew manuscripts read "a disaster, a unique disaster," that is, one without parallel, which fits 5:9. Either way, catastrophe is piling on catastrophe.
  • "An end has come; the end has come; it has awakened against you" (v6). This is a wordplay that English can't keep: ha-qets heqits, "the end has awakened." As if judgment had been sleeping all these years while Israel thought itself safe, and now it is wide awake.
  • "Your doom has come to you" (v7). The word tsephirah is rare and its meaning uncertain. The KJV took it as "morning," as in the dawn of the day of judgment. Others suggest "a turn of the wheel," "the cycle has come round," or a crown or garland. The ESV's "doom" follows the sense of the context.
  • "A day of tumult, and not of joyful shouting on the mountains" (v7). The mountains were where the people had held their noisy festivals at the high places (ch 6). Now the noise on the mountains will be the noise of panic and battle.
  • "Then you will know that I am the LORD, who strikes" (v9). This is a startling new title. At the Red Sea God had said, "I am the LORD, your healer" (Exod 15:26). Now he names himself "the LORD who strikes." The same God who healed his people in the exodus will strike them in the exile. The Tyndale notes point out the contrast.
  • "Behold, the day! Behold, it comes!" (v10). "The day" means the day of the LORD, the day when God steps in to judge. Earlier prophets had warned that the people who longed for it as a day of victory over their enemies would find it was "darkness, and not light" (Amos 5:18).
  • "The rod has blossomed; pride has budded" (v10). This is a dark echo of Aaron's rod, which "sprouted and put forth buds and produced blossoms" as a sign that God had chosen him (Num 17:8). Here the blossoming rod is a rod of punishment. Interpreters split over whose rod and whose pride. Many see Babylon as the rod God uses (Isa 10:5 calls Assyria "the rod of my anger") and Nebuchadnezzar's arrogance as the pride. Others think the rod and the pride belong to Judah: their own insolence has grown and ripened, ready for judgment. Verse 11 seems to support that second view: Judah's violence has grown into "a rod of wickedness" that will strike them.
  • "None of them shall remain, nor their abundance, nor their wealth" (v11). The Hebrew of this verse is very compressed and hard to translate. The general sense is clear: everything they had, their crowds, their riches, their importance, will be swept away.
  • "Let not the buyer rejoice, nor the seller mourn" (v12). In ordinary life a buyer is glad of a bargain and a seller sorry to part with family land. But when the city falls, the buyer will lose what he bought and the seller would have lost it anyway. Normal business means nothing on the day of judgment. Jesus said the same about the days before the flood and before Sodom fell: "they were buying and selling, planting and building" (Luke 17:28).
  • "The seller shall not return to what he has sold, while they live" (v13). Israelite law said family land sold out of need went back to its family in the Year of Jubilee, every fiftieth year (Lev 25:10–13, 23–28). The exile cancels that hope for this generation. They will not live to see it. "Because of his iniquity, none can maintain his life." No one can hold on to life by persisting in sin.
  • Jeremiah's field. At about this time Jeremiah, in Jerusalem, was told by God to buy a field from his cousin, as a sign that "houses and fields and vineyards shall again be bought in this land" (Jer 32:15). The two prophets do not contradict each other. Ezekiel speaks of the present generation, who would not return. Jeremiah speaks of a future restoration, after seventy years.
  • "They have blown the trumpet… but none goes to battle" (v14). The trumpet (taqoa', a horn blast) called Israel's men to war, and in holy war God promised to be with them when it sounded (Num 10:9; Judg 7:18–22). Now it sounds and no one marches. God's wrath has drained their courage. Some may also have been away fighting or already dead.
  • "The sword is without; pestilence and famine are within" (v15). The three judgments of 5:12 and 6:11–12 again. Those outside the city walls die by the sword, and those inside die of hunger and disease. Lamentations remembers both: "the sword bereaves; in the house it is like death" (Lam 1:20).
  • "Like doves of the valleys, all of them moaning" (v16). Doves nest in the valleys, but these survivors flee up into the mountains, out of their natural place. The rock dove's call is a low, mournful moan, and the prophets used it for grief (Isa 38:14; 59:11; Nah 2:7). "Each one over his iniquity." Their grief is finally for their own sin, as in 6:9.
  • "All hands are feeble, and all knees turn to water" (v17). Literally "all knees will go water," a blunt picture of losing control of the body in sheer terror. The Greek Old Testament understood it as wetting themselves. It is a scene of complete collapse. Ezekiel repeats it in 21:7.
  • "Sackcloth… horror… shame… baldness" (v18). These are the signs of mourning: rough goat-hair cloth, and heads shaved in grief (Isa 15:2–3; Amos 8:10). Ezekiel had shaved his own head in chapter 5. Now the whole nation does.
  • "They cast their silver into the streets, and their gold is like an unclean thing" (v19). The word for "unclean thing" is niddah, the word for a woman's monthly impurity (Lev 15:19–24), something to be kept at a distance. Money that once seemed to promise security becomes useless and even disgusting. "Their silver and gold are not able to deliver them in the day of the wrath of the LORD." Zephaniah, a little earlier in Judah, said exactly the same words (Zeph 1:18). No one can eat gold during a siege. "For it was the stumbling block of their iniquity." Their wealth was what tripped them up, because they made idols from it.
  • "His beautiful ornament they used for pride, and they made their abominable images" (v20). The "beautiful ornament" is either their jewelry or the temple. If jewelry, the scene recalls the golden calf, made from the gold earrings Israel had taken from Egypt (Exod 32:2–4; compare Hos 2:8: "her silver and gold, which they used for Baal"). If the temple, it looks ahead to chapter 8, where idols stand inside God's house itself. "Therefore I make it an unclean thing to them." What they defiled, God now treats as defiled.
  • "I will give it into the hands of foreigners for prey" (v21). The Babylonians would plunder the temple and carry its gold and bronze back to Babylon (2 Kings 25:13–17; Dan 5:2–3).
  • "I will turn my face from them, and they shall profane my treasured place" (v22). To turn the face away is to withdraw favor and protection. It is the reverse of the priestly blessing, "the LORD make his face to shine upon you" (Num 6:25). "My treasured place" (tsephuni, "my hidden thing" or "my secret place") is most likely the temple, perhaps the Most Holy Place itself, where only the high priest went once a year. "Robbers shall enter and profane it." The holiest spot on earth would be trampled by soldiers. Chapters 10–11 explain how this could happen: God's glory had already left.
  • "Forge a chain!" (v23). The word (rattoq) appears only here and probably means a chain for leading captives. Assyrian and Babylonian reliefs show prisoners marched off in lines, chained neck to neck (Nah 3:10; Jer 40:1). "For the land is full of bloody crimes and the city is full of violence." Idolatry and injustice went together. Jeremiah charged King Jehoiakim with shedding innocent blood (Jer 22:17), and Manasseh before him "filled Jerusalem with innocent blood" (2 Kings 21:16; 24:4).
  • "I will bring the worst of the nations to take possession of their houses" (v24). God does not call the Babylonians good. They are "the worst of the nations," violent and cruel. Habakkuk had asked how God could use a nation more wicked than Judah (Hab 1:13), and God's answer was that Babylon too would be judged in time (Hab 2). "Their holy places shall be profaned." Either the temple courts or the high places they had made holy for themselves.
  • "They will seek peace, but there shall be none" (v25). False prophets in Jerusalem kept saying "Peace, peace," when there was no peace (Jer 6:14; 8:11; Ezek 13:10).
  • "Rumor follows rumor" (v26). News of the advancing army comes in waves. "They seek a vision from the prophet, while the law perishes from the priest and counsel from the elders." The three sources of guidance in Israel all fail together: the prophet's vision, the priest's instruction in the law (torah), and the elders' practical wisdom. There is a bitter echo here of what Jeremiah's enemies had said when they plotted against him: "the law shall not perish from the priest, nor counsel from the wise, nor the word from the prophet" (Jer 18:18). They thought they could do without Jeremiah. Now they will have none of them. Amos had warned of "a famine… of hearing the words of the LORD" (Amos 8:11–12).
  • "The king mourns, the prince is wrapped in despair" (v27). Ezekiel usually avoids calling Zedekiah "king," preferring "prince" (nasi', 12:10, 12), since many exiles still regarded the captive Jehoiachin as the true king. Here the two words may simply be poetic pairs for the royal house. "The hands of the people of the land are paralyzed by terror." From the palace to the common people, everyone is helpless. "According to their way I will do to them… and they shall know that I am the LORD." The chapter ends where it began.
  • Commentators: Guzik reads the book's refrain about knowing the LORD (vv4, 9, 27) as the hopeful heart of the chapter: the judgment has a good purpose, to bring God's people back to true knowledge of him. He sees the blossoming rod as a reversal of Aaron's rod and also mentions the view that the rod is Babylon. JFB takes that second view firmly, saying, "The 'rod' is the Chaldean Nebuchadnezzar," and reads the pride as "the insolence of the Babylonian conqueror," though v11 suits the other reading better. Henry stresses that the end, though "long in coming," is certain and reaches every corner of the land, and he reads it as a picture of the last judgment of the world. On the buyer and seller, JFB reconciles Ezekiel with Jeremiah's field: "Ezekiel is speaking of the parents, and of the present." Guzik explains niddah in v19 as the language of menstrual impurity, and takes v17 as describing loss of bladder control. JFB reads the "beautiful ornament" as the temple, "the especial glory of the Jews," and the "secret place" as the holy of holies. Guzik takes the king and prince of v27 as Jehoiachin and Zedekiah. JFB calls the chain the "symbol of the captivity."

Pointing to Christ

The day of the LORD that Ezekiel saw arrive over Jerusalem was a real day in history, and it fell exactly as he said. But the prophets also spoke of a final day for the whole world, and the New Testament keeps that warning alive (2 Pet 3:10). Ezekiel says silver and gold cannot deliver anyone on that day. Peter says the same: we were "ransomed… not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Pet 1:18–19). On the cross the day of wrath came early, and fell on one person. At noon darkness covered the land (Mark 15:33), as the prophets had said the day of the LORD would bring "darkness at noon" (Amos 8:9). God turned his face from his Son (Mark 15:34) so that he would never have to turn it from those who are in him. For them the chain is broken, and the day of the LORD becomes the day they long for.

Reflection

  • Everything Built on Sand Has an End (v.2) — Ezekiel announces that judgment is coming on every corner of the land, exposing how temporary their comforts and assumptions really were. Are you building your life on things that can end, or on the God who doesn't?
  • Sin That Blossoms Into Judgment (v.10) — The oracle pictures wickedness as a rod that has budded and is now flowering into full ripeness. What small compromise in your life is quietly budding toward consequences you haven't yet reckoned with?
  • Wealth That Can't Buy a Way Out (v.19) — When judgment arrives, silver and gold are thrown into the streets because money has no power to satisfy or rescue anyone that day. What are you investing your time and resources in that would prove worthless if it mattered most today?
  • When Every Source of Guidance Goes Dark (v.26) — Prophets lose their visions, priests lose the law, and elders lose their counsel all at once when God withdraws His presence. Are you treasuring the guidance available to you now, while it's still there to seek?
