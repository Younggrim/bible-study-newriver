1One day as Jesus was teaching the people in the temple courts and proclaiming the gospel, the chief priests and scribes, together with the elders, came up to Him.

2“Tell us,” they said, “by what authority are You doing these things, and who gave You this authority?”

3“I will also ask you a question,” Jesus replied. “Tell Me:

4John’s baptism—was it from heaven, or from men?”

5They deliberated among themselves and said, “If we say, ‘From heaven,’ He will ask, ‘Why did you not believe him?’

6But if we say, ‘From men,’ all the people will stone us, for they are convinced that John was a prophet.”

7So they answered that they did not know where it was from.

8And Jesus replied, “Neither will I tell you by what authority I am doing these things.”

9Then He proceeded to tell the people this parable: “A man planted a vineyard, rented it out to some tenants, and went away for a long time.

10At harvest time, he sent a servant to the tenants to collect his share of the fruit of the vineyard. But the tenants beat the servant and sent him away empty-handed.

11So he sent another servant, but they beat him and treated him shamefully, sending him away empty-handed.

12Then he sent a third, but they wounded him and threw him out.

13‘What shall I do?’ asked the owner of the vineyard. ‘I will send my beloved son. Perhaps they will respect him.’

14But when the tenants saw the son, they discussed it among themselves and said, ‘This is the heir. Let us kill him, and the inheritance will be ours.’

15So they threw him out of the vineyard and killed him. What then will the owner of the vineyard do to them?

16He will come and kill those tenants and give the vineyard to others.” And when the people heard this, they said, “May such a thing never happen!”

17But Jesus looked directly at them and said, “Then what is the meaning of that which is written: ‘The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone’?

18Everyone who falls on this stone will be broken to pieces, but he on whom it falls will be crushed.”

19When the scribes and chief priests realized that Jesus had spoken this parable against them, they sought to arrest Him that very hour. But they were afraid of the people.

20So they watched Him closely and sent spies who pretended to be sincere. They were hoping to catch Him in His words in order to hand Him over to the rule and authority of the governor.

21“Teacher,” they inquired, “we know that You speak and teach correctly. You show no partiality but teach the way of God in accordance with the truth.

22Is it lawful for us to pay taxes to Caesar or not?”

23But Jesus saw through their duplicity and said to them,

24“Show Me a denarius. Whose image and inscription are on it?” “Caesar’s,” they answered.

25So Jesus told them, “Give to Caesar what is Caesar’s, and to God what is God’s.”

26And they were unable to trap Him in His words before the people. And amazed at His answer, they fell silent.

27Then some of the Sadducees, who say there is no resurrection, came to question Him.

28“Teacher,” they said, “Moses wrote for us that if a man’s brother dies and leaves a wife but no children, the man is to marry his brother’s widow and raise up offspring for him.

29Now there were seven brothers. The first one married a wife but died childless.

30Then the second

31and the third married the widow, and in the same way all seven died, leaving no children.

32And last of all, the woman died.

33So then, in the resurrection, whose wife will she be? For all seven were married to her.”

34Jesus answered, “The sons of this age marry and are given in marriage.

35But those who are considered worthy to share in the age to come and in the resurrection from the dead will neither marry nor be given in marriage.

36In fact, they can no longer die, because they are like the angels. And since they are sons of the resurrection, they are sons of God.

37Even Moses demonstrates that the dead are raised, in the passage about the burning bush. For he calls the Lord ‘the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob.’

38He is not the God of the dead, but of the living, for to Him all are alive.”

39Some of the scribes answered, “Teacher, You have spoken well!”

40And they did not dare to question Him any further.

41Then Jesus declared, “How can it be said that the Christ is the Son of David?

42For David himself says in the book of Psalms: ‘The Lord said to my Lord, “Sit at My right hand

43until I make Your enemies a footstool for Your feet.”’

44Thus David calls Him ‘Lord.’ So how can He be David’s son?”

45In the hearing of all the people, Jesus said to His disciples,

46“Beware of the scribes. They like to walk around in long robes, and they love the greetings in the marketplaces, the chief seats in the synagogues, and the places of honor at banquets.

47They defraud widows of their houses, and for a show make lengthy prayers. These men will receive greater condemnation.”

1And it came to pass, that on one of those days, as he taught the people in the temple, and preached the gospel, the chief priests and the scribes came upon him with the elders,

2And spake unto him, saying, Tell us, by what authority doest thou these things? or who is he that gave thee this authority?

3And he answered and said unto them, I will also ask you one thing; and answer me:

4The baptism of John, was it from heaven, or of men?

5And they reasoned with themselves, saying, If we shall say, From heaven; he will say, Why then believed ye him not?

6But and if we say, Of men; all the people will stone us: for they be persuaded that John was a prophet.

7And they answered, that they could not tell whence it was.

8And Jesus said unto them, Neither tell I you by what authority I do these things.

9Then began he to speak to the people this parable; A certain man planted a vineyard, and let it forth to husbandmen, and went into a far country for a long time.

10And at the season he sent a servant to the husbandmen, that they should give him of the fruit of the vineyard: but the husbandmen beat him, and sent him away empty.

11And again he sent another servant: and they beat him also, and entreated him shamefully, and sent him away empty.

12And again he sent a third: and they wounded him also, and cast him out.

13Then said the lord of the vineyard, What shall I do? I will send my beloved son: it may be they will reverence him when they see him.

14But when the husbandmen saw him, they reasoned among themselves, saying, This is the heir: come, let us kill him, that the inheritance may be ours.

15So they cast him out of the vineyard, and killed him. What therefore shall the lord of the vineyard do unto them?

16He shall come and destroy these husbandmen, and shall give the vineyard to others. And when they heard it, they said, God forbid.

17And he beheld them, and said, What is this then that is written, The stone which the builders rejected, the same is become the head of the corner?

18Whosoever shall fall upon that stone shall be broken; but on whomsoever it shall fall, it will grind him to powder.

19And the chief priests and the scribes the same hour sought to lay hands on him; and they feared the people: for they perceived that he had spoken this parable against them.

20And they watched him, and sent forth spies, which should feign themselves just men, that they might take hold of his words, that so they might deliver him unto the power and authority of the governor.

21And they asked him, saying, Master, we know that thou sayest and teachest rightly, neither acceptest thou the person of any, but teachest the way of God truly:

22Is it lawful for us to give tribute unto Caesar, or no?

23But he perceived their craftiness, and said unto them, Why tempt ye me?

24Shew me a penny. Whose image and superscription hath it? They answered and said, Caesar’s.

25And he said unto them, Render therefore unto Caesar the things which be Caesar’s, and unto God the things which be God’s.

26And they could not take hold of his words before the people: and they marvelled at his answer, and held their peace.

27Then came to him certain of the Sadducees, which deny that there is any resurrection; and they asked him,

28Saying, Master, Moses wrote unto us, If any man’s brother die, having a wife, and he die without children, that his brother should take his wife, and raise up seed unto his brother.

29There were therefore seven brethren: and the first took a wife, and died without children.

30And the second took her to wife, and he died childless.

31And the third took her; and in like manner the seven also: and they left no children, and died.

32Last of all the woman died also.

33Therefore in the resurrection whose wife of them is she? for seven had her to wife.

34And Jesus answering said unto them, The children of this world marry, and are given in marriage:

35But they which shall be accounted worthy to obtain that world, and the resurrection from the dead, neither marry, nor are given in marriage:

36Neither can they die any more: for they are equal unto the angels; and are the children of God, being the children of the resurrection.

38For he is not a God of the dead, but of the living: for all live unto him.

39Then certain of the scribes answering said, Master, thou hast well said.

40And after that they durst not ask him any question at all.

41And he said unto them, How say they that Christ is David’s son?

42And David himself saith in the book of Psalms, The LORD said unto my Lord, Sit thou on my right hand,

44David therefore calleth him Lord, how is he then his son?

45Then in the audience of all the people he said unto his disciples,

Summary

When the chief priests and elders demand to know by what authority Jesus acts, He counters by asking whether John's baptism was from heaven or from men, and their refusal to answer lets Him withhold His answer too (vv. 1-8). He tells the parable of tenants who beat and kill the servants a vineyard owner sends for his share of the fruit and finally murder his own son, warning the leaders that the vineyard will be taken from them and given to others (vv. 9-19). Spies sent to trap Him ask whether it is lawful to pay taxes to Caesar; Jesus has them produce a coin and tells them to give Caesar what is Caesar's and God what is God's, leaving them speechless (vv. 20-26). Sadducees, who deny the resurrection, pose a case about a woman widowed by seven brothers to mock the idea of life after death; Jesus answers that the resurrected neither marry nor die, and that God's word to Moses at the bush proves God is the God of the living, not the dead (vv. 27-40). He then asks how the Messiah can be both David's son and David's Lord, and warns the crowd to beware the scribes who love status and public honor while exploiting widows (vv. 41-47).

Authorship & Background

Author: Luke (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 20 records the confrontations of Passion Week in the Temple. The religious leaders challenge Jesus' authority (vv.1-8), Jesus tells the Parable of the Wicked Tenants (vv.9-19), they try to trap Him with questions about taxes (vv.20-26) and resurrection (vv.27-40), Jesus asks His own question about David's Lord (vv.41-44), and warns against the scribes (vv.45-47).
Classification: Gospel — Narrative
Key Themes: a counter-question about John's baptism that traps the very men demanding to know Jesus' own authority, a parable of wicked tenants who beat the servants and finally kill the son, aimed unmistakably at the leaders listening to it, a tax question with no safe answer resolved by transcending the trap entirely, render unto Caesar, an argument from the burning bush answering the Sadducees on resurrection with a clause Luke alone adds, for all live unto him, scribes conceding the point out loud and then daring to ask no more questions at all, and a warning against men who devour widows' houses placed immediately before the very widow who gives her last two mites in the next chapter
The Authority Question (vv.1-8): The chief priests demand to know by what authority Jesus teaches and cleanses the Temple. Jesus responds with a counter-question about John's baptism that traps them — they can't answer without either condemning themselves or angering the crowd.
The Wicked Tenants (vv.9-19): A parable clearly aimed at the religious leaders. God (the owner) planted Israel (the vineyard), entrusted it to leaders (tenants), sent prophets (servants) who were beaten and killed, and finally sent His Son — whom they killed. The vineyard will be given to others (the Gentiles/church).
The Tribute Question (vv.20-26): A brilliant trap — if Jesus says pay taxes, He loses the crowd; if He says don't pay, He's a rebel against Rome. His answer ("Render unto Caesar... and unto God") transcends the trap entirely.
The Sadducees and the Resurrection (vv.27-40): The Sadducees bring the case of seven brothers, and Luke's version of the answer is the fullest of the three Gospels. The children of this world marry, but they which shall be accounted worthy to obtain that world neither marry, nor are given in marriage, neither can they die any more. Then the argument from the burning bush, which the Sadducees accepted as scripture, and the clause Luke alone adds, for all live unto him. Some of the scribes concede the point out loud, Master, thou hast well said, and Luke notes the result, after that they durst not ask him any question at all.
David's Son, and Beware of the Scribes (vv.41-47): He asks the question this time, how say they that Christ is David's son? and puts Psalm 110 against it, David therefore calleth him Lord, how is he then his son? No answer is recorded. Then, in the hearing of all the people, a warning about the men he has been debating: beware of the scribes, which desire to walk in long robes, and love greetings in the markets, and the highest seats in the synagogues. The charge underneath the vanity is which devour widows' houses, and for a shew make long prayers, and Luke places it immediately before the widow with two mites.

Map & Geography

  • The Temple in Jerusalem: Jesus continued teaching daily in the Temple courts during Passion Week. The religious leaders challenged His authority here.
  • The vineyard (v.9): The Parable of the Wicked Tenants references a vineyard — a common feature of the Judean and Galilean landscape.

Places on this map

  • JerusalemJerusalemA hill town about 2,500 feet up in the central spine of Judah, roughly 35 miles inland from the Mediterranean and 15 miles west of the Jordan. It sits on no trade route and has no river, which is why it mattered as a fortress and a sanctuary rather than as a market. Wikipedia
  • Judeasouthern Israel and the West BankThe southern hill country around Jerusalem, high, stony and dry, with the wilderness on one side and the Philistine plain on the other. Poor farming and hard access are what let it stay independent longest. Wikipedia
  • Galileenorthern IsraelThe northern hill country above the Jezreel Valley, wetter and greener than Judah and crossed by the trade road to Damascus. That traffic is why Judeans considered it mixed and provincial, and why Jesus grew up hearing more than one language. Wikipedia

Setting: About AD 30, the week before Passover, in the temple courts at Jerusalem. Jesus has ridden into the city as king, wept over it, and driven the traders out of the temple (19:28–46). Now he teaches there every day, and the leaders are looking for a way to destroy him, held back only because the people hang on his words (19:47–48). Chapter 20 is a series of public contests. One group after another, chief priests, scribes, elders, spies and Sadducees, comes with a question meant to discredit him or trap him into a charge. Each time he answers so well that they fall silent. Then he asks a question of his own, and closes with a warning about the scribes.

The Authority of Jesus Challenged (vv1–8)

  • "Teaching the people in the temple and preaching the gospel" (v1). Luke frames even this week of conflict as gospel preaching. The temple courts were vast, with shaded colonnades along the walls where teachers gathered listeners.
  • "The chief priests and the scribes with the elders" (v1). These three groups made up the Sanhedrin, the Jewish ruling council in Jerusalem. Jesus had named all three in his first prediction of his death (9:22). They are coming as an official delegation.
  • "By what authority you do these things?" (v2). "These things" means above all clearing the temple, which was under the high priest's control. In their eyes he has no credentials: no priestly office, no rabbinic training under a recognized teacher, no appointment from the council. The question is meant to expose him as a fraud or provoke him into a claim they can use.
  • "Was the baptism of John from heaven or from man?" (v4). This is not a dodge. Answering a question with a question was normal in rabbinic debate, and this one goes to the heart of the matter. John had pointed to Jesus as the one coming after him (3:16). If John's authority came from God, so does the authority of the one he pointed to. "Heaven" is a reverent way to say God.
  • "If we say, 'From heaven'... But if we say, 'From man'" (vv5–6). Luke lets us overhear them. They do not ask which answer is true, only which answer is safe. "All the people will stone us" shows how much the crowds revered John.
  • "They did not know where it came from" (v7). The official teachers of Israel, whose job was to judge true and false prophets, plead ignorance in front of the crowd. They have disqualified themselves as judges of Jesus.
  • "Neither will I tell you" (v8). Jesus does not owe an answer to men who will not answer honestly. But the parable that follows gives his answer anyway, to anyone with ears to hear.
  • Commentators: Henry: "Men often pretend to examine the evidences of revelation... when only seeking excuses for their own unbelief." He adds, "Those that bury the knowledge they have, are justly denied further knowledge." JFB notes the "dignity and composure" with which Jesus turns their question back on them. Guzik says the question was not an evasion, since John's testimony to Jesus carried God's authority, and that people who refuse the truth already given are not ready for more.

The Parable of the Wicked Tenants (vv9–18)

  • "A man planted a vineyard" (v9). Everyone would hear Isaiah 5:1–7, the song of the vineyard, where God plants a vineyard that yields only wild grapes and "the vineyard of the LORD of hosts is the house of Israel." Matthew and Mark include more of Isaiah's wording, the hedge, the winepress and the tower (Mark 12:1). In Jesus' version the problem is not the vine but the tenants.
  • "Let it out to tenants and went into another country for a long while" (v9). Absentee landlords leasing land to tenant farmers were common in first-century Galilee, and tenants paid rent with a share of the crop. Resentment and disputes were common too. The "long while" pictures the long history of Israel under its leaders.
  • "He sent a servant... another servant... yet a third" (vv10–12). The treatment gets worse each time: beaten, then beaten and shamed, then wounded and thrown out. The servants are the prophets. Israel's history records prophets rejected, beaten and killed (2 Chr 24:20–21; 36:15–16; Jer 26:20–23; Neh 9:26).
  • "What shall I do? I will send my beloved son" (v13). The owner's patience is almost beyond belief. "Beloved son" are the words of the Father at Jesus' baptism and transfiguration (3:22; 9:35). Jesus sets himself apart from every prophet before him. They were servants. He is the Son.
  • "This is the heir. Let us kill him, so that the inheritance may be ours" (v14). Some scholars think the tenants assumed that land with no heir could be claimed by those working it. Whatever their logic, the tenants' plan is madness, since the owner is still alive. It echoes Joseph's brothers: "Come now, let us kill him" (Gen 37:20).
  • "They threw him out of the vineyard and killed him" (v15). Jesus was crucified outside the city walls (Heb 13:12).
  • "He will come and destroy those tenants and give the vineyard to others" (v16). Matthew has the leaders themselves give this answer (Matt 21:41). In Luke the people hear it and gasp, "Surely not!" (literally "may it not be"). The "others" are the apostles and the new leadership of God's people, which will include the Gentiles. The fall of Jerusalem in AD 70 was the first act of this judgment.
  • "The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" (v17). Psalm 118:22, from the same psalm the crowd sang at the triumphal entry (19:38; Ps 118:26). "Cornerstone" is literally "head of the corner," either the foundation stone that set the line of the walls or the capstone at the top. The builders are the leaders. The stone they threw away is the one God makes central. Peter will quote this verse to the same council (Acts 4:11; 1 Pet 2:7).
  • "Everyone who falls on that stone will be broken... it will crush him" (v18). Jesus joins two other stone prophecies. Isaiah 8:14–15 describes a stone of stumbling over which many "shall fall and be broken." Daniel 2:34–35, 44 describes a stone cut without hands that strikes the world's kingdoms and turns them to dust. The verb for "crush," likmaō, means to winnow or scatter like chaff, and it is the very word used of that stone in the Greek of Daniel 2:44. To stumble over Christ is ruin. To have him fall in judgment is worse.
  • Commentators: JFB says that in "my beloved son" Jesus severs himself "from all merely human messengers, and claiming Sonship in its loftiest sense," and that the tenants' aim, to turn from servants into lords, is "the deep aim of the depraved heart." On v18 JFB sees the builders already "falling" on the stone and soon to be ground to powder in the fall of Jerusalem. Henry warns that the doom is awful both for those who reject the Son and for those who "profess to reverence Him, yet render not the fruits in due season." Guzik notes that the tenants neither owned nor built the vineyard, and he reads falling on the stone as the humbling of those who come to Christ, and being crushed as judgment on those who refuse him.

Paying Taxes to Caesar (vv19–26)

  • "They perceived that he had told this parable against them, but they feared the people" (v19). They understand the parable perfectly and respond by planning to do exactly what the tenants did.
  • "Sent spies, who pretended to be sincere" (v20). Mark says they were Pharisees and Herodians, two groups who normally disliked each other (Mark 12:13). The aim is to "deliver him up to the authority and jurisdiction of the governor," Pontius Pilate. Only Rome could carry out an execution, so they need a political charge.
  • "Teacher, we know that you speak and teach rightly" (v21). Flattery to lower his guard. Ironically, every word is true. He does teach rightly and shows no partiality.
  • "Is it lawful for us to give tribute to Caesar, or not?" (v22). The tribute was a head tax paid directly to Rome since Judea came under a Roman governor in AD 6. That year Judas the Galilean led a revolt against it, teaching that paying tax to a pagan ruler was disloyalty to God, the only true king (Acts 5:37). If Jesus says yes, he loses the crowd, who hated the tax and hoped he would free them from Rome. If he says no, he can be reported to Pilate as a rebel.
  • "He perceived their craftiness" (v23). The word panourgia means cunning, the kind willing to do anything. Paul uses it of the serpent's deceit of Eve (2 Cor 11:3).
  • "Show me a denarius. Whose likeness and inscription does it have?" (v24). The silver denarius of Tiberius showed his head with an inscription that called him son of the divine Augustus, and on the other side a title of high priest. For many Jews such an image was a graven image. Jesus does not have one. His questioners produce one in the temple courts, showing that they use Caesar's money already.
  • "Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's" (v25). "Render" means "give back," to pay what is owed. The coin bears Caesar's image, so it can be returned to him. Government has a real, God-given claim (Rom 13:1–7; 1 Pet 2:13–17). But humans bear God's image (Gen 1:27), so the whole person belongs to God. Caesar's claim is limited. God's is total. When the two conflict, "we must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29).
  • "They became silent" (v26). They cannot catch him "in the presence of the people." Even so, they will later tell Pilate that he forbade paying tribute to Caesar (23:2), which is the opposite of what he said.
  • Commentators: JFB: in its general form the saying was impossible "for sedition itself to dispute," yet it broke the snare, and the addition about God is "profound but to them startling." Henry: Jesus "reproved them for offering to impose upon him," and they found nothing to use with the governor or the people. Guzik notes the irony that the hand holding Caesar's coin would soon be pierced under Caesar's authority, and he stresses that God's image on people means they owe their lives to God.

Sadducees Ask About the Resurrection (vv27–40)

  • "Some Sadducees, those who deny that there is a resurrection" (v27). This is their only appearance in Luke's Gospel. The Sadducees were the priestly aristocracy, wealthy and politically powerful, and they controlled the high priesthood. They accepted the five books of Moses as their main authority and rejected the Pharisees' oral traditions. They denied resurrection, angels and spirits (Acts 23:8), and believed the soul died with the body.
  • "Moses wrote for us" (v28). Under the law of levirate marriage, if a man died without a son, his brother was to marry the widow and the first son would carry on the dead man's name and inheritance (Deut 25:5–10). Ruth and Boaz are a famous example (Ruth 4:5–10). "Levirate" comes from the Latin levir, "brother-in-law."
  • "Now there were seven brothers" (vv29–32). It is a made-up case, meant to make resurrection look ridiculous. The apocryphal book of Tobit tells of a woman whose seven husbands died one after another on their wedding nights (Tobit 3:8), and the Sadducees may have used this story against the Pharisees before.
  • "In the resurrection, therefore, whose wife will the woman be?" (v33). Their assumption is that the resurrection life, if there is one, would just be this life continued.
  • "The sons of this age marry... but those... worthy to attain to that age... neither marry nor are given in marriage" (vv34–35). "This age" and "that age" was the Jewish way of speaking of the present world and the world to come. Marriage belongs to this age. The resurrection is not a return to this life but a new kind of life.
  • "They cannot die anymore, because they are equal to angels" (v36). Only Luke gives the reason: marriage exists partly to continue the human race in a world of death, and in the resurrection there is no more death. People do not become angels. They become like angels in being deathless. "Sons of God, being sons of the resurrection" means they share the life of God, given by being raised.
  • What about marriage in heaven? Christians sometimes find this hard to hear. Jesus does not say spouses will be strangers, or that love ends. He says the institution of marriage, with its exclusive bond and its purpose of bearing children, will not continue. The love it points to, Christ and his church, will be fulfilled (Eph 5:31–32; Rev 19:7). Guzik notes that whatever we lose, we will not be disappointed.
  • "Even Moses showed, in the passage about the bush" (v37). Jesus answers from the books the Sadducees respected most. At the burning bush, God told Moses, "I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" (Exod 3:6). Chapters and verses did not exist yet, so a passage was named by its subject.
  • "He is not God of the dead, but of the living, for all live to him" (v38). God said "I am," not "I was," centuries after the patriarchs died. God does not call himself the God of people who no longer exist. His covenant with them did not end at their deaths, so they must still live to him and will be raised. Only Luke adds "for all live to him."
  • "Teacher, you have spoken well" (v39). Most scribes were Pharisees, who believed in the resurrection, so they are glad to see the Sadducees beaten. Then no one dares ask him anything more (v40).
  • Commentators: JFB calls the Sadducees "the materialists of the day," and says that if the patriarchs had no existence when God called himself their God, it "would be unworthy of Him" (Heb 11:16). Henry warns that "we wrong ourselves, and wrong the truth of Christ, when we form our notions of the world of spirits by this world of sense." Guzik describes the question as absurd, and makes the point that an absurd question does not become a good one by being asked of God.

Whose Son Is the Christ? (vv41–44)

  • "How can they say that the Christ is David's son?" (v41). Now Jesus asks the question. Everyone agreed the Messiah would be David's descendant (2 Sam 7:12–16; Isa 11:1). The blind man near Jericho called Jesus "Son of David" (18:38). Jesus does not deny it. He asks whether it is the whole truth.
  • "The Lord said to my Lord, 'Sit at my right hand'" (v42). Psalm 110:1, which Jesus and the Jewish teachers of his day accepted as written by David about the Messiah. In Hebrew, the first "Lord" is God's name, YHWH. The second is adoni, "my lord," a title for a superior. David, the greatest king of Israel, calls his descendant "my lord," someone above him, seated at God's right hand.
  • "David thus calls him Lord, so how is he his son?" (v44). In that culture a father did not call his son "lord." The son honored the father. For David to call his descendant "Lord," the Messiah must be more than a human king. The answer, which Jesus leaves hanging, is that he is both David's son by human birth and David's Lord as the Son of God (Rom 1:3–4).
  • The most quoted psalm. Psalm 110:1 is often said to be the Old Testament verse most quoted or echoed in the New Testament. Peter preaches it at Pentecost (Acts 2:34–35), Jesus alludes to it at his trial (22:69), and Hebrews builds on it (Heb 1:13).
  • Commentators: JFB says the difficulty "can only be solved by the higher and lower—the divine and human natures of our Lord," and notes that Jesus treats the psalm as inspired (Mark 12:36, "in the Holy Spirit"). Henry: "Christ, as God, was David's Lord; but Christ, as man, was David's son." Guzik ties the point to Revelation 22:16, where Jesus calls himself both the root and the descendant of David.

Beware of the Scribes (vv45–47)

  • "In the hearing of all the people he said to his disciples" (v45). The warning is for the disciples, but spoken publicly, so the crowd hears it too.
  • "Who like to walk around in long robes" (v46). Scribes were the experts in the law, and long, flowing robes marked them out as scholars. The robes were not the problem. Loving to be seen in them was.
  • "Greetings in the marketplaces... best seats... places of honor at feasts" (v46). People rose to greet a scribe in public and gave him titles. The "best seats" in synagogues were on a bench facing the congregation. Jesus warned the Pharisees about the same things (11:43; 14:7–11).
  • "Who devour widows' houses" (v47). Scribes were not supposed to charge for teaching the law, so many depended on gifts from supporters. Some exploited devout widows by persuading them to give more than they could, or by mismanaging estates they were trusted to oversee. The widows God promised to defend (Exod 22:22) were being swallowed up by the men who taught his law. The very next scene shows a poor widow giving her last two coins to the temple (21:1–4).
  • "For a pretense make long prayers" (v47). Long, public prayers made them look holy, which helped them win the trust of the people they cheated.
  • "They will receive the greater condemnation" (v47). Those who know God's law best, and use it for their own gain, will be judged more strictly (12:47–48; Jas 3:1).
  • Commentators: Henry: "Dissembled piety is double sin." JFB describes the scribes taking advantage of widows' "helpless condition and confiding character" while their long prayers made them seem far above money. Guzik observes that teachers were supposed to receive gifts rather than fees, and that some had taught that giving to a teacher was among the highest of good works, a teaching that served the teachers well.

Pointing to Christ

The leaders ask Jesus for his credentials, and the whole chapter gives them. He is the beloved Son sent after all the servants, the rejected stone that becomes the cornerstone, the Lord of the living who will raise the dead, and David's son who is also David's Lord, soon to sit at God's right hand. The tenants of the parable plot to kill the heir to keep the vineyard. Within days the leaders will do it. But the stone they reject is the one God has chosen, and the resurrection the Sadducees deny is about to happen to him first.

Reflection

  • Avoiding an Honest Answer (v.7) — When Jesus asks the religious leaders about John's baptism, they refuse to answer honestly because any answer would expose their own unbelief. Where are you avoiding an honest answer to something God is asking of you because the truth would require change?
  • Wanting the Inheritance Without the Owner (v.14) — In the parable, the tenants kill the son hoping to seize the vineyard for themselves, wanting the blessing without submitting to the one who owns it. Where do you want God's blessings while resisting His authority over your life?
  • The Rejected Stone Becomes the Cornerstone (v.17) — Jesus applies Psalm 118 to himself: the very one the builders rejected becomes the foundation everything else is built on. Where has the world's rejection of Christ made you doubt his place in your own life?
  • Give God What Bears His Image (v.25) — Jesus says to give Caesar what belongs to Caesar and give God what belongs to God, since Caesar's image is on the coin while God's image is on you. Are you giving God what actually belongs to Him — your time, your obedience, your worship?
  • The God of the Living (v.38) — Jesus argues for the resurrection by pointing out that God is not the God of the dead but of the living, meaning those who've died in faith are still alive to Him. How does the certainty of resurrection shape the way you face loss, aging, or your own mortality?
  • Position Used to Exploit, Not Serve (vv.46-47) — The scribes love recognition and status while quietly exploiting vulnerable people under a show of religion. If you have any authority — at work, at home, in your church — are you using it to serve others or to be served?
