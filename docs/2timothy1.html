Summary

Paul greets Timothy, his beloved son in the faith, and recalls praying for him constantly, longing to see him, and remembering the sincere faith passed down from his grandmother Lois and mother Eunice (vv. 1-5). He urges Timothy to fan into flame the gift of God given through Paul's laying on of hands, since God has given believers a spirit not of fear but of power, love, and self-discipline (vv. 6-7). Timothy should not be ashamed of the gospel or of Paul's imprisonment, but share in suffering for it, relying on God who saved and called believers by His own grace, now revealed through Christ's appearing, who abolished death and brought life to light (vv. 8-10). Paul, though suffering for his appointed role as preacher and teacher, is unashamed because he trusts God to guard what he's entrusted to Him (vv. 11-12), and he charges Timothy to hold to sound teaching and guard that same good deposit by the Holy Spirit (vv. 13-14). He closes by noting that many in Asia deserted him, but praises Onesiphorus, who searched him out in Rome and faithfully served him (vv. 15-18).

Authorship & Background

Author: Paul the Apostle (v.1). Written from prison in Rome, approximately AD 66-67. This is Paul's final letter — written shortly before his execution under Emperor Nero. Unlike his first Roman imprisonment (Acts 28, a rented house), this is a cold dungeon (4:13, 21). Paul knows death is near (4:6).
Recipient: Timothy — Paul's "dearly beloved son" (v.2). Still in Ephesus, facing opposition and perhaps growing discouraged. Paul writes to strengthen him for the challenges ahead.
Purpose: Paul's last will and testament to his spiritual son. He encourages Timothy to be bold (not ashamed), to guard the gospel deposit, to endure suffering, and to continue faithful ministry. The letter is deeply personal — a dying father's final words to his son.
Theme: Endurance in ministry despite suffering, opposition, and abandonment. "Be not ashamed" echoes throughout (vv.8, 12, 16).
Classification: Epistle — Pauline, Pastoral Epistle
Key Themes: Faith inherited through a grandmother and a mother, a gift needing to be stirred into flame, power and love and a sound mind against fear, a deposit committed and kept, and companions who turned away while one man did not
Greeting and a Remembered Household (vv.1-5): This is almost certainly the last of Paul's surviving letters, written from a harder imprisonment than the first. He recalls Timothy's tears and traces his faith through Lois and Eunice, grandmother and mother. Citing two women as the pedigree for a man holding contested office is unusual, and it is offered as genuine ground for confidence rather than as warmth before business.
Stir Up the Gift (v.6): The verb describes fanning a fire back into flame, which presupposes embers rather than ashes. The gift is present and needs rousing, not replacing. That Paul has to say it at all suggests Timothy had gone quiet under exactly the pressure the rest of the chapter goes on to name.
Not Fear, But Power, Love, and a Sound Mind (v.7): The alternative to fear comes in three terms, and the third is not what a list about courage would predict: a sound mind, self-command, clear judgment. Courage here is not a surge of feeling but the composure to keep thinking straight, which is the first thing a frightened leader loses.
Be Not Ashamed of the Testimony (vv.8-11): Shame is the chapter's real subject and it is named outright. Timothy is told to take his share of affliction rather than avoid it, on the ground of a purpose given “before the world began” and a Saviour who “hath abolished death”. Paul states his appointment as preacher and apostle in the same breath as his chains, declining to treat the two as contradicting each other.
I Know Whom I Have Believed (v.12): The confidence rests on a person rather than an outcome — “I know whom I have believed”, not what. Whether the thing kept is what Paul entrusted to God or what God entrusted to Paul, the grammar permits both, and the next verse turns the same term around so that Timothy is holding a deposit of his own.
Keep the Deposit (vv.13-14): The pattern of sound words is to be held onto, and “that good thing which was committed unto thee” is guarded by the Holy Ghost rather than by vigilance alone. The image is of goods left with a custodian: valuable, and not his. That is how the letter treats doctrine throughout — something received and passed on intact rather than developed.
Onesiphorus, and Those Who Turned Away (vv.15-18): The chapter ends with names. All those in Asia turned away, Phygelus and Hermogenes among them. Set against that, Onesiphorus searched Rome until he found Paul and “was not ashamed of my chain” — the same word as v.8, which is why the story sits here rather than in the closing greetings. He refreshed Paul often, and the wish for mercy on his household reads like a man who had learned to count his visitors.

Deep Dive

Setting: This is Paul's last letter. In 1 Timothy he was free and travelling, and had left Timothy in Ephesus to deal with false teachers (1 Tim 1:3). Now he is a prisoner in Rome again, and this time he expects to die (4:6–8). Most who hold that Paul wrote it date it to about AD 64–67, during the persecution Nero launched after the great fire of Rome in AD 64. Tradition holds that Paul was beheaded there not long after. Unlike his first Roman custody in a rented house with visitors coming and going (Acts 28:30–31), this imprisonment is lonely, hard to find and dangerous to visit (1:17). Timothy seems to be still in Ephesus (1:18; 4:19). Many critical scholars think the three Pastoral Letters (1–2 Timothy, Titus) were written later in Paul's name, pointing to differences in vocabulary and church order and to the difficulty of fitting them into Acts. The traditional view, held by most evangelical scholars, is that Paul wrote them after being released from the imprisonment at the end of Acts, perhaps with a secretary's help, and the deeply personal details of 2 Timothy are especially hard to explain as fiction. Chapter 1 sets the tone of the whole letter: an old soldier, near the end, urging his beloved son in the faith not to be ashamed, not to shrink back, and to guard the gospel.

Greeting (vv1–2)

  • "Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God" (v1). The usual opening, but it carries weight here. A man chained as a criminal still knows who sent him. His standing does not come from Rome's verdict or from popularity in Asia (v15), but from God.
  • "According to the promise of the life that is in Christ Jesus" (v1). This phrase is found in no other greeting of Paul's. A condemned man opens his last letter with life. The theme returns in v10, where Christ "abolished death and brought life and immortality to light," and in 2:8–11. Paul's own death is close, and the promise of life is what he is standing on.
  • "To Timothy, my beloved child" (v2). In 1 Timothy 1:2 he was "my true child in the faith." Here he is "beloved," agapētos. Timothy, son of a Jewish believing mother and a Greek father, joined Paul at Lystra about fifteen years earlier (Acts 16:1–3) and had worked beside him ever since (Phil 2:19–22). Paul probably led him to faith (1 Cor 4:17).
  • "Grace, mercy, and peace" (v2). Paul's usual greeting is "grace and peace." Only in the two letters to Timothy does he add "mercy" to the greeting. A young leader under pressure, and an old apostle facing death, both need mercy as much as anything.
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry: "The gospel is the promise of life in Christ Jesus; life is the end, and Christ the way." Guzik connects the unusual "promise of life" directly to Paul's expected execution, which made eternal life precious to him, and draws on Spurgeon's point that ministers need more mercy than anyone. JFB passes on Bengel's description of the letter as Paul's "last testament and swan-like death song," and reads the promise of life as meant to brace Timothy for trials and for a risky journey to Rome.

Guard the Deposit Entrusted to You (vv3–18)

  • "I thank God whom I serve, as did my ancestors, with a clear conscience" (v3). "Serve" is latreuō, the word for worship and priestly service. Paul does not see his faith in Jesus as a break from the God of Israel but as faithfulness to him (Acts 24:14; Phil 3:5). The "clear conscience" echoes his defence before the Sanhedrin (Acts 23:1). Accused of being a criminal, he knows he has lived honestly before God.
  • "As I remember you constantly in my prayers night and day" (v3). In a cold cell with time on his hands, Paul spends it praying for Timothy. This is how he shepherds from a distance.
  • "As I remember your tears, I long to see you" (v4). We are not told when Timothy wept. It may have been their last parting, perhaps when Paul was arrested. Compare the Ephesian elders weeping as Paul left them (Acts 20:37–38). "Long to see you" is a strong word of yearning, and the letter keeps returning to it: "Do your best to come to me soon" (4:9), "before winter" (4:21).
  • "Your sincere faith… first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice" (v5). "Sincere" is anypokritos, literally "unhypocritical": faith that is not an act. Acts 16:1 tells us Timothy's mother was "a Jewish woman who was a believer" and his father "a Greek." The father is not mentioned here, and was probably not a believer. Lois and Eunice taught Timothy "the sacred writings" from childhood (3:15). Their faith in Israel's God came to rest in Jesus, likely when Paul first preached in Lystra (Acts 14:6–7). The faith had to become Timothy's own ("now, I am sure, dwells in you"), but it was handed on through a home.
  • "Fan into flame the gift of God" (v6). The verb anazōpyreō, used only here in the New Testament, means "to kindle afresh" or "keep burning." It pictures stirring embers back into a blaze. It doesn't necessarily mean the fire had gone out, but it does mean a fire left alone dies down. Paul isn't scolding. He is reminding, and the gift needs tending.
  • "Through the laying on of my hands" (v6). In 1 Timothy 4:14 it was "when the council of elders laid their hands on you." Most think this was one occasion, Timothy's commissioning, with Paul and the elders together. The gift is God's equipping of Timothy for his ministry by the Spirit. Paul reminds him that his calling did not come from himself, which steadies a timid man.
  • "For God gave us a spirit not of fear but of power and love and self-control" (v7). "Fear" here is deilia, which is always negative in Greek: cowardice, timidity, not the healthy fear of God (phobos). "Spirit" may mean the Holy Spirit, who produces power, love and self-control, or the attitude God gives. The two readings come to much the same thing, since v14 names the Spirit directly. "Self-control" is sōphronismos, found only here: a sound, disciplined mind, the opposite of panic. The KJV's "sound mind" catches it. Power without love is harsh, love without power is weak, and both need a clear head.
  • "Therefore do not be ashamed of the testimony about our Lord, nor of me his prisoner" (v8). This verse is close to the theme of the whole letter. Shame was a powerful force in Roman culture. To be linked to a man in chains, and to a crucified Lord, was disgraceful. Paul uses "ashamed" three times in the chapter: Timothy should not be (v8), Paul is not (v12), and Onesiphorus was not (v16). Note Paul calls himself "his prisoner," the Lord's, not Nero's.
  • "Share in suffering for the gospel by the power of God" (v8). One compound verb, synkakopatheō, "suffer hardship together with." It returns in 2:3. Paul doesn't offer Timothy escape from suffering. He offers company in it, and God's power to carry it.
  • "Who saved us and called us to a holy calling, not because of our works" (v9). Verses 9–10 are tightly built and may be an early Christian confession that Paul quotes or shapes. Salvation is God's work from start to finish: his "purpose and grace," given "in Christ Jesus before the ages began." Before there was a Timothy, a Paul or a world, God had purposed grace in Christ (Eph 1:4). That is a firm floor to stand on when Rome is hostile.
  • "Manifested through the appearing of our Savior Christ Jesus" (v10). "Appearing" is epiphaneia, used for a god showing himself or an emperor's state visit. Caesar was hailed as "savior" and his arrivals celebrated. Paul gives both words to Jesus. In the Pastoral Letters epiphaneia can mean his first coming (here) or his return (4:1, 8; Titus 2:13). The grace planned in eternity has now stepped into history.
  • "Who abolished death" (v10). The verb katargeō means to make powerless, put out of action. Believers still die, but death has lost its sting and its final say (1 Cor 15:26, 54–57; Heb 2:14). "Brought life and immortality to light": "immortality" is aphtharsia, "incorruptibility," the life that cannot decay. The Old Testament had glimpses of resurrection (Job 19:25–27; Dan 12:2), but Christ's own rising brought it into full daylight.
  • "For which I was appointed a preacher and apostle and teacher" (v11). A herald announces, an apostle is sent with authority, a teacher explains. The same three-part description appears in 1 Timothy 2:7.
  • "But I am not ashamed, for I know whom I have believed" (v12). Not "what" but "whom." Paul's confidence rests on a person he knows. "I know" is in the perfect tense: he came to know him and still does.
  • "He is able to guard until that day what has been entrusted to me" (v12). The Greek is literally "my deposit" (parathēkē), a banking and legal word for something left in another's safekeeping. It can be read two ways, and the ESV gives the other in its footnote. Either it is what Paul has entrusted to God, his life, soul and work, which God will keep safe; or it is what God has entrusted to Paul, the gospel, which God will protect even when Paul dies. The same word in v14 and 1 Timothy 6:20 clearly means the gospel entrusted to Timothy, which supports the ESV's text. Both thoughts are true, and the verse holds them together. "That day" is the day Christ returns as Judge (v18; 4:8).
  • "Follow the pattern of the sound words that you have heard from me" (v13). "Pattern" is hypotypōsis, an outline or sketch, the model a builder works from. Paul's teaching had a recognizable shape, and Timothy was to keep it rather than redraw it. "Sound" means healthy, the opposite of the false teaching that spreads "like gangrene" (2:17). And it is held "in the faith and love that are in Christ Jesus." Right words held without faith and love become cold and harsh.
  • "By the Holy Spirit who dwells within us, guard the good deposit entrusted to you" (v14). Now the deposit is Timothy's. He is not the gospel's author but its trustee. And he does not guard it by willpower: the Spirit who lives in every believer does it through him. The Spirit in v7 and v14 brackets this paragraph. In 2:2 Timothy is to pass the deposit on to faithful men who will teach others, so the guarding is not just defensive.
  • "All who are in Asia turned away from me" (v15). Asia is the Roman province in western Turkey, with Ephesus as its capital, where Paul had spent three years and seen "all the residents of Asia" hear the word (Acts 19:10). "All" can't mean every believer, since Onesiphorus and his household are from there, and Timothy himself is there. It probably means those from Asia who could have stood with Paul at his arrest or trial, and chose not to. Compare 4:16, "all deserted me." Whether they left the faith or simply abandoned Paul is not said. Paul, like his Lord, was deserted by his friends (Matt 26:56).
  • "Among whom are Phygelus and Hermogenes" (v15). They are named nowhere else in Scripture, but Timothy plainly knew them. Paul names them to warn, not to gossip. A later legendary work, the Acts of Paul and Thecla, makes Hermogenes and Demas false companions of Paul, but that is a second-century story, not history.
  • "The household of Onesiphorus, for he often refreshed me and was not ashamed of my chains" (v16). His name means "bringing profit" or "help-bringer," and he lived up to it. "Refreshed" is anapsychō, used only here, literally to cool or revive, like a breath of air in a stifling cell. "Not ashamed" is the third use of the word in the chapter. Onesiphorus is the living picture of what Paul asks of Timothy in v8. Rome's "chains" (halysis) were literal.
  • "When he arrived in Rome he searched for me earnestly and found me" (v17). Rome had perhaps a million people, and after the fire of AD 64 Christians were being hunted. Asking officials where a prisoner of Christ was held could put a man under suspicion. He kept looking until he found him.
  • "May the Lord grant him to find mercy from the Lord on that day!" (v18). A wordplay: he found Paul, may he find mercy. "The Lord… from the Lord" is probably just Paul's way of speaking, though some see a distinction between the Father and Christ.
  • Was Onesiphorus dead? Paul blesses his "household" here and in 4:19, not Onesiphorus with them, and prays for mercy for him "on that day." Many readers, including some Protestants, conclude he had died, and Roman Catholic teaching has used v18 as an example of prayer for the dead (with 2 Maccabees 12:43–45). Others reply that he may simply have been away from home, still travelling or still in Rome, so his family was greeted separately, and that wishing someone mercy at the judgment does not require that he was dead. The text does not say, and the verse is too slender to build a doctrine on either way.
  • "All the service he rendered at Ephesus" (v18). His kindness in Rome was not a one-off. Timothy had seen it in Ephesus already. Faithfulness in distant places grew out of faithfulness at home.
  • Commentators: On v6 Henry says to "stir it up as fire under the embers," and JFB likewise reads the verb as "rekindle," the opposite of quenching the Spirit. Henry on v7: "The great hindrance of usefulness in the increase of our gifts is slavish fear." JFB reports Wahl's reading of sōphronismos as "the bringing of men to a sound mind," but follows Bengel and the English versions with "sober-mindedness"; the lexicon allows both, admonishing others or self-discipline, and gives self-discipline for this verse. Guzik counts more than twenty-five calls to boldness across the two letters and treats Timothy as naturally timid; that is an inference from the letters, though a widely held one (1 Cor 16:10). On v12, JFB rightly gives the Greek as "my deposit" and reads it as Paul's body, soul and spirit "deposited in God's safe keeping," while also noting the gospel entrusted to Paul. Guzik takes it the same personal way, and Henry: "Those who trust in Christ know whom they have trusted." On v14 Henry adds, "We must not think to keep it by our own strength, but keep it by the Holy Ghost." On Onesiphorus, Henry thinks he was probably away from home, and asks, "who told them that Onesiphorus was dead?" JFB agrees he was alive, arguing "His household would hardly retain his name after the master was dead," and that "Nowhere has Paul prayers for the dead." It names Bengel and Alford as taking the other view, so this is a real disagreement among Protestant commentators too. Guzik leaves the question alone. JFB suggests the Asians' desertion took place while they were in Rome, which fits the parallel in 4:16, though this is a guess.

Pointing to Christ

A prisoner facing death writes that Christ "abolished death and brought life and immortality to light." That is not wishful thinking from a cell. It rests on a Saviour who was himself deserted by his friends, bound, shamed and killed, and who rose. Because Jesus "endured the cross, despising the shame" (Heb 12:2), his people need not be ashamed of him or of one another's chains. And the Lord Paul knows is the one who guards: he keeps what we entrust to him, he keeps the gospel he has entrusted to us, and he will show mercy on that day.

Reflection

  • Stirring Up the Gift Within You (v.6) — Paul urges Timothy to actively fan into flame the gift God placed in him rather than letting it go dormant. What has God equipped you to do that you've let go cold from neglect?
  • Power, Love, and a Sound Mind (v.7) — Paul reminds Timothy that fear isn't from God; what He gives instead is strength, love, and self-control. Where is fear currently shaping your decisions more than these three things are?
  • Unashamed of the Gospel (v.8) — Paul calls Timothy to share in suffering for the gospel rather than shy away from association with it. Is there a setting in your life where you quietly downplay your faith to avoid discomfort?
  • Confidence in a Person, Not Just a Belief (v.12) — Even facing death, Paul's confidence rests not in what he knows but in whom he has trusted. Is your faith built on a real relationship with Christ, or mostly on ideas about Him?
  • Holding to Sound Teaching (v.13) — Paul tells Timothy to keep a firm grip on the pattern of truth he was taught. What are you actively doing to hold onto sound teaching amid competing voices?
