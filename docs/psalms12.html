Summary

A lament over the collapse of honesty and loyalty in society. David observes that the godly and faithful seem to have vanished, replaced by people who flatter and deceive their neighbors (vv. 1-2), and asks God to silence proud, boastful speech, particularly from those who claim their words give them power over others (vv. 3-4). Because of the oppression of the poor and needy, the LORD promises to rise up and provide the safety they long for (v. 5). God's own words are described as pure, like silver refined seven times over, utterly trustworthy compared to human speech (v. 6). The psalm ends with confidence that God will guard the needy from this deceitful generation, even while the wicked continue to strut about and vileness is celebrated among people (vv. 7-8).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Communal Lament
Attributed Author: David — "To the chief Musician upon Sheminith, A Psalm of David"
Key Themes: The disappearance of godly people, corrupt speech (flattery, lies, boasting), God's pure words contrasted with human deception, divine intervention for the oppressed
Historical Context: David laments a society where faithful people seem to have vanished and deceitful speech dominates. "Sheminith" (the eighth) again indicates a low-pitched musical setting. The central contrast is between human words (vv.2-4: flattery, lies, boasting) and God's words (v.6: pure as silver refined seven times). The psalm's great contribution is v.6 — one of the strongest statements of Scripture's purity in all the Bible. God's response (v.5) comes as a direct divine oracle mid-psalm: "Now will I arise." This is rare — God speaking directly within a psalm.
Help, LORD, for the Godly Man Ceaseth (v.1): Help, LORD, for the godly man ceaseth, for the faithful fail from among the children of men. One verse of assessment, and it is the same complaint as Isaiah 57:1 and Micah 7:2: not that the wicked are strong but that the good are running out. The verb is one of numbers dwindling rather than of defeat.
They Speak with a Double Heart (vv.2-4): They speak vanity every one with his neighbour, with flattering lips and with a double heart. The problem this psalm is about is speech, and the petition against it is specific to the organ, the LORD shall cut off all flattering lips, and the tongue that speaketh proud things. Then the boast is quoted and it is a claim of independence, with our tongue will we prevail, our lips are our own, who is lord over us.
Now Will I Arise, Saith the LORD (v.5): For the oppression of the poor, for the sighing of the needy, now will I arise, saith the LORD. One verse, and it is the only place in the psalm where God speaks. What moves him is named as a sound rather than an argument, the sighing of the needy, and what he promises is stated in their own terms, I will set him in safety from him that puffeth at him.
The Words of the LORD Are Pure Words (v.6): The words of the LORD are pure words, as silver tried in a furnace of earth, purified seven times. The verse is placed immediately after God's one speech in the psalm and immediately after four verses about human speech that cannot be trusted, so the comparison is the argument. And the image is technical: silver refined seven times is a specification, not a flourish.
Thou Shalt Preserve Them (vv.7-8): Thou shalt keep them, O LORD, thou shalt preserve them from this generation for ever. The promise is made without any claim that the conditions will improve, and the last verse of the psalm says so, the wicked walk on every side, when the vilest men are exalted. The psalm ends where it began, with the faithful in a minority, and what has changed in between is that God has been quoted.

Deep Dive

Setting: The title reads "To the choirmaster: according to The Sheminith. A Psalm of David." Sheminith ("the eighth") also heads Psalm 6 and probably means a lower register or octave, or possibly an eight-stringed instrument (see 1 Chr 15:21). No occasion is named. Matthew Henry places it in Saul's reign, when there was "a general decay of honesty and piety both in court and country," and David certainly knew what it was to be surrounded by flatterers and liars at Saul's court (1 Sam 18:22–26; 23:19–20; 24:9). But the psalm is deliberately general. It is a community lament for a whole society where truth has collapsed. Psalms 9–11 were about violence: the hunter's net, the lion's ambush, the drawn bow. Psalm 12 is about words: lies, flattery, boasting and the tongue used as a weapon. Against the corrupt words of people the psalm sets one pure thing: the words of the LORD. Its eight verses are built around God's own speech in v5, one of the few places in these early psalms where God himself speaks (compare 2:6–9). Before it come human lies, and after it comes the purity of God's word.

The Godly One Is Gone (vv1–2)

  • "Save, O LORD" (v1). One Hebrew word, hoshi'ah, "save!" It is the root of "Hosanna" ("save, please," 118:25) and of the name Jesus, "the LORD saves." The prayer is as short as a cry for help can be.
  • "For the godly one is gone" (v1). "Godly one" (chasid) means someone loyal, devoted, marked by chesed, the steadfast covenant love that God shows and that his people are to show back. It is the word behind 4:3, "the LORD has set apart the godly for himself." David feels he is the last one left. Elijah felt the same: "I, even I only, am left" (1 Kings 19:10), and God told him there were seven thousand others. The feeling is real, but it may not be the full picture.
  • "For the faithful have vanished from among the children of man" (v1). "Faithful" (emunim) means trustworthy, reliable people. It may also be read as an abstract noun, "faithfulness," as JFB suggests: truthfulness itself has disappeared. Micah would later make the same complaint: "The godly has perished from the earth, and there is no one upright among mankind" (Mic 7:2).
  • "Everyone utters lies to his neighbor" (v2). "Lies" here is literally "emptiness" or "vanity" (shav), the word used in the commandment against taking God's name "in vain" (Exod 20:7). It is speech with nothing true or solid behind it. And it is spoken "to his neighbor," the one person the law says to love as yourself (Lev 19:18) and not to bear false witness against (Exod 20:16).
  • "With flattering lips and a double heart they speak" (v2). "Flattering" is literally "smooth," slippery talk that slides past without catching on anything true. "A double heart" is literally "with a heart and a heart": one heart shown on the outside, another hidden inside. James would call this being "double-minded" (Jas 1:8). The worst damage of flattery is not that it says nice things, but that it cannot be trusted.
  • Commentators: Henry says David begs God's help because "there were none among men whom he durst trust," and adds that the psalm "is certainly calculated for a bad reign," kept for the church's use in every corrupt age. JFB reads "the faithful" as literally "faithfulness" and says its absence is shown "by the prevalence of deceit and instability." Guzik passes on Thomas Adams, quoted by Spurgeon: "A man without a heart is a wonder, but a man with two hearts is a monster."

Who Is Master Over Us? (vv3–4)

  • "May the LORD cut off all flattering lips, the tongue that makes great boasts" (v3). "Cut off" is covenant language for removal from God's people (Exod 31:14). The prayer is that God would silence the lying mouth. Flattery and boasting are paired. Both inflate, one inflating another person, the other inflating oneself.
  • "With our tongue we will prevail" (v4). The liars believe words are power. They are right that words are powerful (Prov 18:21, "death and life are in the power of the tongue"; Jas 3:5–8). Their mistake is thinking their words have no master. Propaganda, slander campaigns and manipulation all run on this belief.
  • "Our lips are with us; who is master over us?" (v4). "Our lips are with us" means "our lips are our own," as the KJV says, our allies, under our control. "Master" is adon, "lord." This is the central rebellion of the psalm. They claim to own their speech with no one to answer to. Pharaoh asked almost the same question: "Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice?" (Exod 5:2). Jesus answered it: "I tell you, on the day of judgment people will give account for every careless word they speak" (Matt 12:36).
  • Commentators: JFB says boasting, like flattery, is a kind of lying, and reads "lips" and "tongue" as standing for the people themselves. Guzik answers the question "who is lord over us?" with the believer's confession that Jesus is Lord, owning body, soul and spirit, so a Christian's lips are not their own (1 Cor 6:19–20). He also passes on Poole's suggestion that the boasting describes slanders spread to turn Saul against David.

I Will Now Arise (v5)

  • "Because the poor are plundered, because the needy groan" (v5). Now God speaks. The center of the psalm is God's answer. The reason he gives for acting is not that David asked well, but that the poor are being robbed and the needy are groaning. The lies of vv2–4 have real victims. Deceit is never just words. It plunders.
  • "I will now arise," says the LORD (v5). Psalms 3:7, 7:6, 9:19 and 10:12 all prayed "Arise, O LORD." Here God replies in the same words: "I will now arise." Here God himself takes up the cry and answers it. The word "now" matters. The waiting of Psalms 9–10 has an end. The phrase recalls Exodus 3:7–8: "I have surely seen the affliction of my people... I have come down to deliver them."
  • "I will place him in the safety for which he longs" (v5). The Hebrew of the last phrase is difficult. Literally it may read "the safety he pants for" or "from him who puffs at him." The second reading takes the verb as the one in 10:5, where the wicked "puffs" at his enemies. The KJV has "from him that puffeth at him." Either way the promise is that God himself will put the poor man in a safe place.
  • Commentators: JFB says God's promise to save the poor, whom the wicked sneer at, shows the writer's confidence. Guzik says David took God's word here as a personal promise, and quotes Spurgeon: "Think of God arising in his might." The Tyndale notes read the whole psalm as a community lament answered by God's own word.

Pure Words (vv6–8)

  • "The words of the LORD are pure words" (v6). The contrast is deliberate. Human words in vv2–4 are empty, smooth, double and boastful. God's words, including the promise just given in v5, are pure, with no mixture of falsehood. "Words" here is the term for a spoken saying or promise (imrah), which JFB notes literally means "sayings." The psalm is not mainly about Scripture as a book but about God's spoken promises. The same truth applies to all of them: "Every word of God proves true" (Prov 30:5; Ps 18:30; 119:140).
  • "Like silver refined in a furnace on the ground" (v6). In ancient metalworking, silver ore was heated in a clay crucible until the impurities rose and could be skimmed off (Prov 25:4; Mal 3:3). The phrase translated "furnace on the ground" is uncertain in Hebrew. It probably means a crucible set in or on the earth, or "refined for the earth," that is, until it is pure.
  • "Purified seven times" (v6). Seven is the Bible's number of completeness. Silver refined seven times has no dross left at all. God's promises have been tested by every kind of trial, and none has failed.
  • "You, O LORD, will keep them; you will guard us from this generation forever" (v7). The Hebrew has a small puzzle. It reads "you will keep them; you will guard him." "Them" may be the poor and needy of v5, or God's pure words of v6. "Him" would be the poor man of v5. The ancient Greek translation reads "us" in both lines, and the ESV gives "them" and then "us," following that tradition in the second line. All readings come to the same promise: God protects his people and his promises together.
  • "From this generation forever" (v7). "Generation" means a kind of people, the type described in vv2–4, not only those living at one time (compare Deut 32:5, "a crooked and twisted generation"; Phil 2:15).
  • "On every side the wicked prowl, as vileness is exalted among the children of man" (v8). The psalm does not end with the problem solved. It ends honestly: the wicked are still everywhere, strutting about, and what is worthless is held up as admirable. "Vileness" (zullut) is a rare word for worthlessness or cheapness. Isaiah warned, "Woe to those who call evil good and good evil" (Isa 5:20). The faith of the psalm is that God keeps his people even in such a world, not only after it is fixed.
  • Commentators: JFB reads "seven times" as "thoroughly" and says the wicked roam freely while "vileness and vile men are exalted." Guzik passes on Spurgeon: "Man's words are yea and nay, but the Lord's promises are yea and amen." He notes the variant "keep us" in v7 but follows VanGemeren in keeping "them," saying God preserves both his word and his people. He quotes Maclaren on v8: "A corrupt people will crown corrupt men and put them aloft." Henry says God will make good his promises and remain faithful even when things look worst.

Pointing to Christ

In a world of flattering lips and double hearts, Jesus is the one whose words were perfectly pure: "No one ever spoke like this man" (John 7:46), and "no deceit was found in his mouth" (1 Pet 2:22). He is not only a speaker of God's pure word; he is the Word made flesh (John 1:14), the promise of v5 in person. In him God did "arise" for the poor and needy, proclaiming "good news to the poor" (Luke 4:18). And Jesus answered the boast "who is master over us?" by becoming the servant of all, then being raised as the Lord to whom every tongue will confess (Phil 2:11). Because God's promises are all "Yes" in him (2 Cor 1:20), his people can live in a generation where vileness is exalted and still be kept.

Reflection

  • The Loneliness of Faithfulness (v.1) — David feels that godly people have vanished around him, leaving him surrounded by deception. When faithfulness feels isolating, do you remember that you are not as alone as it feels?
  • Flattering Lips, Double Hearts (vv.2-4) — David describes a culture where people speak smoothly while concealing false intentions. Are you careful to let your words match what is actually in your heart?
  • God Speaks Directly Into the Crisis (v.5) — In response to the cry of the needy, God declares that He will now arise and act. Do you trust that even a simple, wordless groan reaches God and moves Him to act?
  • Words Refined Like Silver (v.6) — In contrast to corrupt human speech, God's words are described as pure, tested seven times over. Are you building your convictions on words that have proven trustworthy, or on whatever voices are loudest around you?
  • God Preserves What Is His (v.7) — The psalm closes with confidence that God guards and keeps what belongs to Him. In a culture that often rewards the wrong voices, do you trust God to preserve what is faithful to Him?
