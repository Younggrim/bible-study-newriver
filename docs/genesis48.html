1Some time later Joseph was told, “Your father is ill.” So he set out with his two sons, Manasseh and Ephraim.

2When Jacob was told, “Your son Joseph has come to you,” Israel rallied his strength and sat up in bed.

3Jacob said to Joseph, “God Almighty appeared to me at Luz in the land of Canaan, and there He blessed me

4and told me, ‘Behold, I will make you fruitful and multiply you; I will make you a multitude of peoples, and will give this land to your descendants after you as an everlasting possession.’

5And now your two sons born to you in Egypt before I came to you here shall be reckoned as mine; Ephraim and Manasseh shall be mine, just as Reuben and Simeon are mine.

6Any children born to you after them shall be yours, and they shall be called by the names of their brothers in the territory they inherit.

7Now as for me, when I was returning from Paddan, to my sorrow Rachel died along the way in the land of Canaan, some distance from Ephrath. So I buried her there beside the road to Ephrath” (that is, Bethlehem).

8When Israel saw the sons of Joseph, he asked, “Who are these?”

9Joseph said to his father, “They are the sons God has given me in this place.” So Jacob said, “Please bring them to me, that I may bless them.”

10Now Israel’s eyesight was poor because of old age; he could hardly see. Joseph brought his sons to him, and his father kissed them and embraced them.

11“I never expected to see your face again,” Israel said to Joseph, “but now God has let me see your children as well.”

12Then Joseph removed his sons from his father’s knees and bowed facedown.

13And Joseph took both of them—with Ephraim in his right hand toward Israel’s left hand, and Manasseh in his left hand toward Israel’s right hand—and brought them close to him.

14But Israel stretched out his right hand and put it on the head of Ephraim, the younger; and crossing his hands, he put his left on Manasseh’s head, although Manasseh was the firstborn.

15Then he blessed Joseph and said: “May the God before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac walked, the God who has been my shepherd all my life to this day,

16the angel who has redeemed me from all harm—may He bless these boys. And may they be called by my name and the names of my fathers Abraham and Isaac, and may they grow into a multitude upon the earth.”

17When Joseph saw that his father had placed his right hand on Ephraim’s head, he was displeased and took his father’s hand to move it from Ephraim’s head to Manasseh’s.

18“Not so, my father!” Joseph said. “This one is the firstborn; put your right hand on his head.”

19But his father refused. “I know, my son, I know!” he said. “He too shall become a people, and he too shall be great; nevertheless, his younger brother shall be greater than he, and his offspring shall become a multitude of nations.”

20So that day Jacob blessed them and said: “By you shall Israel pronounce this blessing: ‘May God make you like Ephraim and Manasseh.’” So he put Ephraim before Manasseh.

21Then Israel said to Joseph, “Look, I am about to die, but God will be with you and bring you back to the land of your fathers.

22And to you, as one who is above your brothers, I give the ridge of land that I took from the Amorites with my sword and bow.”

Summary

As Jacob nears death, Joseph brings his two sons Ephraim and Manasseh to him, and Jacob formally adopts them as his own, giving them equal standing with his other sons in the inheritance (vv. 1-6). Jacob recalls Rachel's death and burial near Bethlehem before blessing the boys (v. 7). When Jacob crosses his hands to lay his right hand on the younger Ephraim instead of the firstborn Manasseh, Joseph tries to correct him, but Jacob insists he knows what he is doing, prophesying that the younger will become greater (vv. 13-19). The scene continues Genesis' recurring pattern of God's sovereign choice overturning the expected birth order.

Authorship & Background

Author: Moses (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 48 records Jacob's adoption and blessing of Joseph's two sons, Ephraim and Manasseh. This is a deathbed scene of enormous significance: Jacob elevates Joseph's sons to the status of his own sons — giving Joseph a double portion (the birthright blessing). More remarkably, Jacob crosses his hands and places the greater blessing on Ephraim (the younger) over Manasseh (the firstborn) — continuing Genesis's pattern of God choosing the younger over the elder (Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, Joseph over his brothers). This chapter explains why there is no "tribe of Joseph" but rather two tribes: Ephraim and Manasseh.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: two grandsons formally adopted as Jacob's own sons, elevating Joseph to a double portion no other son receives, a dying man's eyes too dim to see clearly yet his hands crossed deliberately rather than by accident, Joseph's protest at the crossed hands met with Jacob's own insistence, I know it, my son, I know it, a blessing formula, God make thee as Ephraim and as Manasseh, that becomes the standard blessing spoken over children for generations afterward, and the younger again placed above the elder in a pattern this book has repeated since Isaac and Ishmael
Jacob Recalls God's Promise (vv.1-7): Joseph brings his sons to the dying Jacob (v.1). "Israel strengthened himself" — rallying for this important act (v.2). Jacob recounts God's appearance at Luz (Bethel): "I will make thee fruitful... and will give this land to thy seed" (vv.3-4). He adopts Ephraim and Manasseh: "they shall be mine; as Reuben and Simeon" (v.5). He remembers Rachel's death near Bethlehem (v.7) — still grieving after decades.
The Blessing (vv.8-16): Jacob's eyes are dim (v.10) — echoing Isaac's blindness (27:1). But unlike Isaac, Jacob blesses "wittingly" — deliberately (v.14). Joseph positions Manasseh at Jacob's right hand (the place of greater blessing), but Jacob crosses his hands, placing his right on Ephraim's head (vv.13-14). The blessing itself (vv.15-16) is trinitarian in structure: "God, before whom my fathers... did walk" — "the God which fed me all my life long" — "the Angel which redeemed me from all evil."
The Crossed Hands (vv.17-20): Joseph objects — "Not so, my father" (v.18). Jacob refuses: "I know it, my son, I know it" (v.19). This is not senility but prophecy. Ephraim will be greater than Manasseh — fulfilled in Joshua's time and beyond. "In thee shall Israel bless, saying, God make thee as Ephraim and as Manasseh" (v.20) — a blessing formula used to this day.
Jacob's Final Words to Joseph (vv.21-22): "God shall be with you, and bring you again unto the land of your fathers" (v.21). Jacob gives Joseph "one portion above thy brethren" — Hebrew "shechem" — possibly referring to the plot at Shechem (John 4:5) (v.22).

Map & Geography

  • The setting remains Egypt (Goshen region), but the blessings look forward to Canaan — the land promise is central to Jacob's adoption of Ephraim and Manasseh.
  • Shechem (v.22) — Jacob gives Joseph a "portion" (Hebrew "shechem") above his brothers, likely referring to the plot of land purchased in 33:19. Joseph's bones are later buried here (Joshua 24:32).

Setting: Jacob has lived seventeen years in Egypt and is now about 147, near death (47:28). He has just made Joseph swear to bury him in Canaan, not Egypt (47:29–31). Joseph is in his mid-fifties, still governor, and his two sons, Manasseh and Ephraim, born before the famine (41:50–52), are young men of about twenty. Their mother is Asenath, an Egyptian, daughter of a priest of On. They have been raised as Egyptian nobility. This chapter settles whether they will belong to Egypt or to Israel. It is a deathbed scene, and Hebrews names it as the great act of faith of Jacob's whole life: "By faith Jacob, when dying, blessed each of the sons of Joseph" (Heb 11:21).

Jacob Blesses Ephraim and Manasseh (vv1–22)

  • "Your father is ill" (v1). Jewish tradition noticed that this is the first time Scripture mentions illness before death. Until now people simply "breathed their last." Joseph comes at once and brings his two sons.
  • "Israel summoned his strength and sat up in bed" (v2). The narrator switches to "Israel" as the old man gathers himself for one last act as head of the covenant family. What he is about to do matters enough to sit up for.
  • "God Almighty appeared to me at Luz" (vv3–4). Luz was the old name of Bethel (28:19). Jacob starts with God, not with himself. He recalls the two times God met him there: as a runaway with a stone for a pillow (28:10–22), and on his return, when God renamed him Israel and said, "Be fruitful and multiply… kings shall come from your own body" (35:9–12). Everything that follows rests on that promise: offspring, a "company of peoples," and "this land… for an everlasting possession." Jacob is in Egypt, but his mind is on Canaan.
  • "Ephraim and Manasseh shall be mine, as Reuben and Simeon are" (v5). Jacob adopts his two grandsons as his own sons. They will not be one tribe under Joseph's name but two tribes alongside their uncles. This is how Joseph gets the double share that normally went to the firstborn (Deut 21:17). Chronicles explains it plainly: Reuben lost his birthright because he slept with his father's concubine (35:22), and "his birthright was given to the sons of Joseph" (1 Chr 5:1–2). Naming Reuben and Simeon, the two eldest, may be pointed: Ephraim and Manasseh take the places they forfeited (compare 49:3–7). This is also why lists of the twelve tribes vary. Levi gets no land, and Joseph's two sons fill the gap.
  • "The children that you fathered after them shall be yours" (v6). Any later sons will be counted under Ephraim or Manasseh for their inheritance.
  • "To my sorrow Rachel died… on the way to Ephrath (that is, Bethlehem)" (v7). This seems to come from nowhere, but it explains everything. Rachel was the wife Jacob loved, and she died young, after only two sons (35:16–20). By adopting Joseph's two boys, Jacob gives Rachel, in a sense, the further sons she never lived to bear. An old man thinking about death thinks about the grave of the woman he loved, by the roadside near Bethlehem. Many centuries later Matthew hears Rachel weeping for her children near that same place (Matt 2:18).
  • "Who are these?" (v8). His eyes are failing (v10), so he may not recognize them. But the question also works as a formal start to the ceremony, like asking for identification before a legal act. Joseph answers, "They are my sons, whom God has given me here" (v9).
  • "The eyes of Israel were dim with age" (v10). The scene deliberately echoes chapter 27: another old patriarch, nearly blind, about to bless the wrong son, or so it seems. Isaac was deceived and blessed the younger by mistake. Jacob, the son who took that blessing by trickery, now blesses the younger on purpose, with his eyes open in a way that has nothing to do with sight.
  • "I never expected to see your face; and behold, God has let me see your offspring also" (v11). This is one of the most tender lines in Genesis. Jacob had given up on ever seeing Joseph again. God gave him more than he had dared hope.
  • "Joseph… bowed himself with his face to the earth" (v12). The second man in Egypt bows to the ground before his old father. The dreams said the family would bow to Joseph. Here Joseph bows to Israel.
  • Crossed hands (vv13–14). Joseph positions the boys carefully so that Manasseh, the firstborn, stands opposite Jacob's right hand, the hand of greater honor and strength. Jacob reaches across and puts his right hand on Ephraim, "crossing his hands." The verb can mean "acting wisely" or "deliberately," as the KJV has it: "guiding his hands wittingly." It was no accident of blindness.
  • "The God before whom my fathers… walked, the God who has been my shepherd… the angel who has redeemed me from all evil" (vv15–16). Jacob's blessing names God three ways. The God of his fathers, before whom Abraham and Isaac "walked," lived in his presence. The God who "has been my shepherd all my life long." Jacob was a shepherd himself and knew what it meant to watch flocks day and night (31:38–40). This is the first time Scripture calls God a shepherd, and it comes from a man whose life had plenty of wandering in it. And "the angel who has redeemed me." "Redeemed" is gaʾal, the word for a kinsman-redeemer who buys back a relative from debt or slavery and stands up for him (Lev 25:25; Ruth 3:13). It is its first use in the Bible. This "angel" is no ordinary messenger. The three lines are parallel, and the verb "bless" that follows is singular, so the angel is identified with God himself. Jacob is probably thinking of the man who wrestled with him at Peniel, where Jacob said, "I have seen God face to face" (32:30; compare Hos 12:3–4).
  • "In them let my name be carried on" (v16). Joseph's sons, half-Egyptian and raised at Pharaoh's court, are formally brought into Israel. They will carry the names of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, not their Egyptian grandfather's. Hebrews 11 says Moses later made the same choice, "refusing to be called the son of Pharaoh's daughter" (Heb 11:24), and JFB connects these boys to that verse.
  • "It displeased him" (vv17–18). Joseph thinks his father has made a mistake and tries to move his hand: "Not this way, my father." Joseph, who has seen God overturn every human expectation in his own life, still expects things to go by the usual rules here.
  • "I know, my son, I know" (v19). The doubled words are gentle and firm. Jacob has not muddled the boys. Manasseh will be great too, "nevertheless, his younger brother shall be greater." Genesis keeps doing this: Abel over Cain, Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, Joseph and Judah over Reuben, Perez over Zerah (38:27–30), and now Ephraim over Manasseh. God's choice does not follow birth order. And this time there is no scheming, no goat skins, no lies. Jacob, the great deceiver, now hands on God's choice openly.
  • How it came true. At the census in the wilderness Ephraim outnumbered Manasseh (Num 1:32–35), though by the second census the order was reversed (Num 26:34, 37). Joshua came from Ephraim (Num 13:8), the tabernacle stood at Shiloh in Ephraim's land, and after the kingdom split, Ephraim was the leading tribe of the north, so much that the prophets often call the whole northern kingdom "Ephraim" (Isa 7:2; Hos 11:8).
  • "God make you as Ephraim and as Manasseh" (v20). These two boys become the pattern for every blessing. To this day many Jewish fathers bless their sons on Friday evenings with exactly these words.
  • "God will be with you and will bring you again to the land of your fathers" (v21). Jacob is dying in Egypt, and he speaks of the return as certain. The promise God gave him at Beersheba (46:4) he now passes on to Joseph. Joseph will say the same thing to his own family on his deathbed (50:24).
  • "One mountain slope that I took from the hand of the Amorites with my sword and with my bow" (v22). The word for "mountain slope" is shekem, literally "shoulder" or "ridge," and it is a wordplay on the town of Shechem. The lexicon notes the pun. Jacob bought land at Shechem peacefully (33:19), and his sons Simeon and Levi slaughtered its men with the sword, which Jacob condemned (34:25–30; 49:5–7). No conquest by Jacob himself is recorded. Interpreters suggest that Jacob is speaking of a battle Genesis does not record, or that he takes responsibility for the family's capture of the town, or that he speaks prophetically of what his descendants will take. JFB suggests Amorites seized the land while Jacob was away and he won it back. What is clear is the outcome: Shechem fell in the territory of Joseph's sons, Joseph's bones were buried there (Josh 24:32), and by Jesus' time a field near Sychar was known as "the field that Jacob had given to his son Joseph," where Jesus sat by Jacob's well (John 4:5–6).
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry reads the angel as Christ: "Christ, the Angel of the covenant, is he that redeems us from all evil." Guzik also stresses that Jacob's whole testimony is about grace, a shepherd who kept a stubborn sheep, not a man's achievements. On the crossed hands Henry draws the lesson that God "often gives most to those that are least likely." Guzik notes that Ephraim means "fruitful" and Manasseh "making to forget" (41:51–52), and that "firstborn" in Scripture often means first in rank, which helps explain calling Christ "the firstborn of all creation" (Col 1:15) without making him a created being. JFB says Hebrews 11:21 singles this blessing out as Jacob's chief act of faith and identifies the "portion" with land near Shechem. The Tyndale notes point out that the younger-over-older pattern ran four generations: Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Ephraim.

Pointing to Christ

Jacob, the old cheat, ends his life confessing that God was his shepherd and his redeemer all along. Both words point to Jesus: "I am the good shepherd" (John 10:11), and the kinsman-redeemer who buys his people back with his own blood (1 Pet 1:18–19; Gal 4:4–5). The crossed hands show the same grace. God's blessing goes where he chooses, not by birth or rank, and it reaches two half-Egyptian boys and brings them fully into Israel. In the same way, Gentiles once "alienated from the commonwealth of Israel" are brought near and made heirs by the blood of Christ (Eph 2:12–13).

Reflection

  • Exceeding What Was Hoped For (v.11) — Jacob admits he never expected to see Joseph's face again, yet God let him live to see Joseph's children too. Where has God exceeded what you dared to hope for?
  • Blessing on God's Terms (v.14) — Jacob deliberately crosses his hands to give the greater blessing to Ephraim, the younger son, ahead of Manasseh, the firstborn. Are you willing to accept God's blessing coming in an order or shape you didn't choose?
  • A Lifetime of Shepherding (v.15) — Looking back over his whole life, Jacob testifies that God shepherded him every day of it, hard years included. Can you say the same about your own life so far?
  • A Personally Known Redeemer (v.16) — Jacob names the one who has redeemed him from all evil, describing a deeply personal relationship rather than an inherited belief. Is your faith your own lived experience of God's rescue, or something you've only heard about secondhand?
  • Faith for a Future You Won't See (v.21) — Jacob's dying words to Joseph look forward in faith to God's continuing presence and the family's eventual return to the promised land. What legacy of faith, rather than fear, are you leaving for those who come after you?
