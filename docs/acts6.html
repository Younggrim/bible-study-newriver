Summary

As the number of disciples grows, Greek-speaking widows are being overlooked in the daily distribution of food, so the apostles have the community choose seven men full of the Spirit and wisdom to oversee this ministry, freeing the apostles for prayer and the word (vv. 1-6). The word of God spreads, the number of disciples multiplies greatly, and even many priests become obedient to the faith (v. 7). Stephen, one of the seven, full of grace and power, performs great wonders, but members of a synagogue dispute with him and cannot withstand his wisdom (vv. 8-10). They stir up false witnesses who accuse him of speaking against Moses, the law, and the temple, and drag him before the council, where his face is seen to be like that of an angel (vv. 11-15).

Authorship & Background

Author: Luke (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 6 records the first internal conflict in the church (the neglect of Greek-speaking widows), the appointment of the seven deacons, and the introduction of Stephen — who will become the first Christian martyr in chapter 7. The church is growing rapidly and facing growing pains.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: a complaint from Greek-speaking believers that their widows were being overlooked in daily food distribution, a solution that hands the selection of seven men entirely to the congregation rather than to the apostles themselves, seven chosen with Greek names drawn directly from the community that felt neglected, growth recorded as the direct outcome of settling an internal dispute, and Stephen introduced as full of faith and power, his opponents unable to resist his wisdom and forced to resort to false witnesses instead
The Conflict (vv.1-4): Greek-speaking Jewish believers (Hellenists) complained that their widows were being overlooked in the daily food distribution, while Hebrew-speaking widows were being served. This was a cultural/linguistic divide within the church — not a doctrinal one. The apostles' solution was wise: delegate the practical ministry to qualified men so the apostles could focus on prayer and the Word. The twelve refuse to leave the word of God to serve tables and hand the selection to the congregation, look ye out among you seven men of honest report, full of the Holy Ghost and wisdom.
The Seven (vv.5-6): Seven men were chosen — all with Greek names, suggesting they were from the very community that felt neglected. The church didn't just address the problem; they empowered the affected group to lead the solution. Stephen and Philip are the most prominent of the seven.
The Word of God Increased (v.7): And the word of God increased, and the number of the disciples multiplied in Jerusalem greatly, and a great company of the priests were obedient to the faith. The result of settling an argument about food distribution is recorded as growth, and the group named as joining is the one with most to lose by joining.
Stephen (vv.8-15): Introduced as "full of faith and power" (v.8). He performed miracles and debated with such wisdom that his opponents "were not able to resist" (v.10). Unable to defeat him intellectually, they resorted to false witnesses and brought him before the Sanhedrin. His face appeared "as the face of an angel" (v.15).

Map & Geography

  • Jerusalem: The appointment of the seven deacons occurred in Jerusalem as the church grew. The Hellenistic (Greek-speaking) Jews and Hebraic (Aramaic-speaking) Jews were both part of the Jerusalem church.
  • The Synagogue of the Freedmen (v.9): A synagogue in Jerusalem attended by Jews from Cyrene, Alexandria, Cilicia, and Asia — showing the diversity of Jerusalem's Jewish population.

Deep Dive

Setting: Jerusalem, early-to-mid 30s AD. The church has survived threats, a flogging and the shock of Ananias and Sapphira, and it keeps growing, now well beyond the five thousand men of 4:4. Its members come from two backgrounds. Some are local Jews who speak Aramaic, and others are Jews born abroad who have settled in Jerusalem and speak Greek. Growth brings a practical strain, and the way the apostles handle it frees the church to grow even more. It also brings forward Stephen, whose arrest, speech and death (chapter 7) turn the story outward to Samaria and beyond.

Seven Chosen to Serve (vv1–7)

  • "When the disciples were increasing in number" (v1). Growth is good, and it also brings problems. Luke does not hide the church's troubles. "Disciples" appears here for the first time in Acts as the name for believers in general.
  • "The Hellenists… against the Hebrews" (v1). Both groups were Jewish believers. The Hellenists were Jews from the dispersion, born or raised in Greek-speaking cities around the Mediterranean, who used Greek in daily life and worship. The Hebrews were Jews of the land who spoke Aramaic and read Scripture in Hebrew. Language, culture and some suspicion divided them. Many devout Jews from abroad came to Jerusalem late in life to die and be buried near the holy city, which left a good number of widows without family nearby.
  • "Their widows were being neglected in the daily distribution" (v1). The Old Testament repeatedly commands care for widows, orphans and foreigners (Deut 14:29; 24:19–21; Isa 1:17). The church had a daily handout of food or money (compare 2:45; 4:35). With thousands of members and the apostles running it, some widows were being missed. Luke does not say it was deliberate. It may have been oversight, made worse by a language gap. A "complaint" (gongysmos) is grumbling or muttering, the word for Israel's complaints in the wilderness.
  • "The twelve summoned the full number of the disciples" (v2). The apostles do not deny the problem or brush it aside. They call the whole church together.
  • "It is not right that we should give up preaching the word of God to serve tables" (v2). "Serve" is diakoneō, the verb behind "deacon." "Tables" can mean food tables or money tables (the word gives "bank" in modern Greek). The apostles are not saying practical service is beneath them. Jesus had washed their feet and said he came "not to be served but to serve" (Mark 10:45, same verb). They are saying it is not their calling, and that doing it would pull them away from what is.
  • "Seven men of good repute, full of the Spirit and of wisdom" (v3). For a job handling food and money, the qualifications are spiritual: trusted by others, Spirit-filled, wise. Practical service in the church still needs spiritual people. Why seven is not explained. Jewish towns sometimes had boards of seven to manage public affairs, but that may be coincidence.
  • "We will devote ourselves to prayer and to the ministry of the word" (v4). "Ministry" is diakonia, the noun from the same root as "serve" in v2. Serving tables and serving the word are both service. The apostles pick prayer first, then preaching. The verb "devote" (proskartereō) is the same one used of the whole church in 1:14 and 2:42.
  • "What they said pleased the whole gathering" (v5). The church chooses, the apostles appoint. Different churches still read this pattern differently, but here the whole body and the leaders both have a part.
  • All Greek names (v5). Stephen ("crown"), Philip, Prochorus, Nicanor, Timon, Parmenas and Nicolaus are Greek names. Jews of the land sometimes had Greek names too (Andrew and Philip among the Twelve), but this list strongly suggests the church chose men from the group that had been overlooked. The best way to settle a complaint of neglect was to give that group's own trusted men charge of the whole fund.
  • "Stephen, a man full of faith and of the Holy Spirit" (v5). He is named first and described most fully, because the next chapter and a half is about him. "Philip" becomes the evangelist who takes the gospel to Samaria and the Ethiopian (ch 8; 21:8), not to be confused with the apostle Philip.
  • "Nicolaus, a proselyte of Antioch" (v5). A Gentile who had converted to Judaism and then believed in Jesus. Antioch in Syria, mentioned here for the first time, becomes the home of the first mixed Jewish-Gentile church (11:19–26) and Paul's base. Some early church writers, beginning with Irenaeus, connected Nicolaus with the "Nicolaitans" condemned in Revelation 2:6, 15. That is a late tradition with no support in the text, and other early writers defended him.
  • "They prayed and laid their hands on them" (v6). Laying on of hands had Old Testament roots. Moses laid hands on Joshua to share his authority with him (Num 27:18–23). It signals being set apart and given a task with prayer, not passing on magic power.
  • Were they deacons? The noun "deacon" (diakonos) is not used of them, and two of the seven, Stephen and Philip, are soon preaching rather than handling food. Many see them as the forerunners of the deacons whose qualifications Paul lists later (1 Tim 3:8–13), and the verb used here supports that. Others see them as a one-off team for a particular need. Either way they show a principle: the church shares out its work so that each part is done well.
  • "The word of God continued to increase" (v7). This is the first of several brief progress reports in Acts (9:31; 12:24; 16:5; 19:20; 28:31). A problem that could have split the church instead ended in growth.
  • "A great many of the priests became obedient to the faith" (v7). These were most likely ordinary priests, not the ruling chief priests. Thousands of them served in rotation at the temple, many of them poor and resentful of the Sadducee elite. It is striking that men whose lives revolved around sacrifice came to trust the once-for-all sacrifice of Jesus (Heb 10:11–12). "Obedient to the faith" shows that believing the gospel means submitting to it (Rom 1:5).
  • Commentators: Guzik, JFB and the Tyndale notes all see the Greek names as a wise, generous answer to the complaint. JFB thinks the complaint was "in all likelihood well founded." Guzik leans toward poor organization rather than prejudice, and sees an attempt by the enemy to divide the church along cultural lines. He also points out that "distribution" in v1 and "ministry" in v4 are the same Greek word, which is right: both are diakonia. Matthew Henry: "The word of God was enough to take up all the thoughts, cares, and time of the apostles," and "The persons chosen to serve tables must be duly qualified." JFB says it is "universally admitted" that this was the first institution of the deacons, which overstates the agreement, and calls the priests' conversion the "crowning triumph of the Gospel."

Stephen Is Seized (vv8–15)

  • "Full of grace and power" (v8). The table-server is also doing "great wonders and signs," the first person outside the Twelve said to do so. Some later manuscripts read "faith" here, but the best ones read "grace." Luke builds Stephen's portrait from these words: faith, the Spirit, wisdom, grace, power.
  • "The synagogue of the Freedmen" (v9). Freedmen (Libertinoi, a Latin loanword) were former slaves or their descendants. Many Jews had been taken to Rome as captives after Pompey conquered Judea in 63 BC and were later freed. A Greek inscription found in Jerusalem, the Theodotus inscription, records a synagogue built "for the reading of the Law and the teaching of the commandments," with guest rooms for visitors from abroad. Its builder's family name (Vettenus) is Roman, so some link it to this synagogue, though that cannot be proven.
  • "The Cyrenians… the Alexandrians… Cilicia and Asia" (v9). Cyrene was in North Africa (modern Libya) and Alexandria in Egypt, both with large Jewish communities. Cilicia, in south-eastern Asia Minor, had Tarsus as its chief city, home of a young Pharisee named Saul (21:39). The Greek is unclear whether this is one synagogue or several, but either way these are Hellenistic Jews, the same background as Stephen himself. He is debating his own kind.
  • "They could not withstand the wisdom and the Spirit" (v10). Jesus promised exactly this: "I will give you a mouth and wisdom, which none of your adversaries will be able to withstand or contradict" (Luke 21:15).
  • "They secretly instigated men" (v11). Losing the argument, they turn to slander. The word means to put someone up to something, to suborn. "Blasphemous words against Moses and God." Moses is put first. To these men the law and the temple were close to sacred in themselves.
  • "The people and the elders and the scribes" (v12). For the first time the ordinary people turn against the believers. Until now the crowds had protected them (2:47; 5:13, 26). Stephen is from the Hellenists, and his teaching on the temple seems to have alarmed even devout people who liked the apostles.
  • "False witnesses" (v13). The parallel with Jesus' trial is plain. False witnesses accused him of saying he would destroy the temple (Mark 14:57–58). Luke's Gospel leaves that charge out of Jesus' trial and tells it here, at Stephen's.
  • "This Jesus of Nazareth will destroy this place and will change the customs that Moses delivered to us" (v14). The charge is false in what it says Stephen meant, but it has a grain of truth twisted. Jesus did predict the temple's destruction (Luke 21:6), and he said, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up," speaking of his body (John 2:19–21). Stephen likely taught that Jesus is the true meeting place of God and man and that the temple was never meant to contain God (7:48–50). That is not an attack on Moses. It is the claim that Moses pointed to Jesus.
  • "His face was like the face of an angel" (v15). The council looked at him and saw something they could not explain. When Moses came down from Sinai his face shone from speaking with God (Exod 34:29–35). The man accused of speaking against Moses has a face like Moses'. It suggests calm, heavenly confidence and God's approval before he says a word.
  • Commentators: JFB and Guzik both suggest that Saul of Tarsus may have been among those from Cilicia who debated Stephen, which fits 7:58 but is not stated. JFB calls the radiance "a play of supernatural radiance attesting to all who beheld his countenance." Guzik, with the Tyndale notes, links it to Moses' shining face. Matthew Henry: "When they could not answer Stephen's arguments as a disputant, they prosecuted him as a criminal," and "What shall we say of man, a rational being, yet attempting to uphold a religious system by false witness and murder!" Guzik argues that the charges twisted Stephen's real message, that Jesus is greater than the temple and the customs, into an attack on them.

Pointing to Christ

A complaint about food becomes a picture of the servant Lord: he came to serve, and his church learns to serve one another so that no one is left out and his word keeps spreading. Then Stephen walks the road Jesus walked: wisdom no one can answer, false witnesses, a charge about destroying the temple, a hearing before the same council. The difference is that Stephen's face already shines. The temple he was accused of attacking was being fulfilled in the Jesus he preached, the true place where God meets his people.

Reflection

  • Noticing the Overlooked (v.1) — The Greek-speaking widows were being neglected in the daily distribution, not out of malice but through oversight. Who in your community might be quietly falling through the cracks?
  • Spirit-Filled Service (v.3) — The qualification for the practical task of serving tables was still being full of the Holy Spirit and wisdom. Do you treat your everyday responsibilities as Spirit-filled service, or only the visibly "spiritual" ones?
  • Priorities of Prayer and the Word (v.4) — The apostles delegated practical needs so they could devote themselves to prayer and the ministry of the Word. What would it take for you to protect time for those same two priorities?
  • Growth Follows Right Priorities (v.7) — When leadership focused on prayer and the Word while practical needs were entrusted to qualified servants, the word of God increased. Is there a structural change you need to make to free up what matters most?
  • Irrefutable Wisdom (v.10) — Stephen's opponents could not resist the wisdom and the Spirit by which he spoke, so they resorted to false accusation instead. Have you ever faced opposition simply because your faith couldn't be argued away?
  • Peace Under Pressure (v.15) — Stephen's face appeared like an angel's even as he stood before the council that would condemn him. How do you maintain peace when you're facing real hostility for your faith?
