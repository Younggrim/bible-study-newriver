Summary

A lament that turns to praise, written while David hid from Saul in a cave (superscription; 1 Samuel 22 or 24). He takes refuge under the shadow of God's wings until destruction passes, crying out to the Most High who sends help from heaven against those who trample him (vv. 1-3). Surrounded by enemies whose words are sharp as swords, he prays that God's glory would be exalted above the heavens even as they set a trap that will catch themselves instead (vv. 4-6). His heart grows steadfast and he resolves to sing and awaken the dawn with praise, declaring he will thank God among the nations for a steadfast love reaching to the heavens (vv. 7-10), and closes repeating his prayer that God be exalted above all the earth (v. 11).

Authorship & Background

Author: Multiple authors — primarily David (73 psalms attributed), plus Asaph (12), Sons of Korah (11), Solomon (2), Moses (1), Heman (1), Ethan (1), and anonymous. The Psalter was compiled over approximately 1000 years and served as Israel's hymnal and prayer book. The book is divided into five 'books' (1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150), paralleling the five books of Moses.
Classification: Individual Lament / Confidence Psalm
Attributed Author: David
Key Themes: Refuge under God's wings, steadfast heart, praise in the midst of danger, God's glory above all, faithfulness and mercy reaching to the heavens
Historical Context: The superscription places this psalm when David fled from Saul into a cave (1 Samuel 22:1 — Cave of Adullam, or 1 Samuel 24:1-3 — Cave of En Gedi). Set to "Do Not Destroy" (Al Tashheth), which may reference David's refusal to destroy Saul when he had the opportunity. In the cave — literally underground, hiding for his life — David's heart is "fixed" (steadfast) and he resolves to praise. This Miktam (possibly "golden psalm" or "inscription psalm") moves from desperate refuge-seeking to exuberant worship, demonstrating how faith transforms the darkest circumstances into occasions for praise.
Be Merciful unto Me (vv.1-3): Be merciful unto me, O God, be merciful unto me, for my soul trusteth in thee. The superscription places the psalm when he fled from Saul in the cave, which is either 1 Samuel 22 or 24, and the shelter imagery fits it, in the shadow of thy wings will I make my refuge, until these calamities be overpast. The last clause is about duration: the refuge is described as temporary because the trouble is.
My Soul Is Among Lions (v.4): My soul is among lions, I lie among them that are set on fire, even the sons of men, whose teeth are spears and arrows, and their tongue a sharp sword. One verse, and the animal and the weapon imagery are mixed deliberately: what makes the men dangerous turns out on inspection to be their speech.
The First Refrain (v.5): Be thou exalted, O God, above the heavens, let thy glory be above all the earth. The refrain appears twice, here and at verse 11, and it divides the psalm into a half about danger and a half about praise. What is asked for in it has nothing to do with the singer's situation, which is the point of putting it in the middle.
They Are Fallen Themselves (v.6): They have prepared a net for my steps, they have digged a pit before me, into the midst whereof they are fallen themselves. The self-defeating pattern of Psalm 7:15 and 9:15, stated in a single verse, and the psalm reports it as done rather than requested.
My Heart Is Fixed (vv.7-10): My heart is fixed, O God, my heart is fixed, I will sing and give praise. The word fixed means settled or established, and repeating it is the psalm's way of contrasting with the agitation of verse 4. Then the singer wakes the instruments and himself, awake, psaltery and harp, I myself will awake early. And the reach of the praise is deliberately wider than the cave, I will praise thee, O Lord, among the people, I will sing unto thee among the nations.
The Second Refrain (v.11): Be thou exalted, O God, above the heavens, let thy glory be above all the earth. The refrain again, unchanged, closing the psalm. Verses 7 to 11 appear again as the opening of Psalm 108, which is assembled out of parts of this psalm and Psalm 60.

Setting: "To the choirmaster: according to Do Not Destroy. A Miktam of David, when he fled from Saul, in the cave." This psalm sits in a run of David's psalms from his years as a fugitive (Psalms 52–59), and it follows straight on from the escape from Gath in Psalm 56. After slipping away from the Philistines, "David departed from there and escaped to the cave of Adullam" (1 Sam 22:1), a cave in the hills of the Shephelah, the low country southwest of Jerusalem. His family came to him there, and about four hundred men who were "in distress… in debt… bitter in soul" (22:2). There was a second famous cave later, at En-gedi by the Dead Sea, where Saul went in alone "to relieve himself" while David and his men hid deeper inside, and David cut off a corner of Saul's robe but refused to kill him (1 Sam 24:1–7). Guzik thinks Adullam fits best. Henry reads the title with En-gedi in mind. Either way, David is hiding underground from a king who wants him dead, a king David has already been anointed to replace (1 Sam 16:13). The psalm has two halves, each closed by the same refrain: "Be exalted, O God, above the heavens! Let your glory be over all the earth!" (vv5, 11). Verses 7–11 were later reused, almost word for word, as the opening of Psalm 108.

The Title

  • "According to Do Not Destroy" (title). The Hebrew is 'al tashcheth, "do not destroy," which the King James transliterates as Al-taschith. It heads four psalms (57, 58, 59, 75). Most scholars think it names a tune. Isaiah 65:8 quotes what sounds like a vintage song: "Do not destroy it, for there is a blessing in it," said of a good cluster of grapes. But the words also fit the story. At the second sparing of Saul, David told Abishai, "Do not destroy him" (1 Sam 26:9), using the same verb. Moses prayed, "O Lord GOD, do not destroy your people" (Deut 9:26). Henry offers both readings: David refusing to let his men destroy Saul, or, as Henry prefers, God refusing to let Saul destroy David, "for a blessing is in him," that is, the line that leads to Christ.
  • "A Miktam" (title). The second of the Miktam psalms in a row (56–60, with 16). The meaning is uncertain. Guzik follows the older view that it is a "golden" psalm. JFB simply points back to Psalm 16.
  • "In the cave" (title). A cave is a place of hiding, darkness and waiting. What is striking is that the psalm does not stay in the cave. By the end David is singing about the heavens, the clouds and the nations.

In the Shadow of Your Wings (vv1–3)

  • "Be merciful to me, O God, be merciful to me" (v1). The same plea that opened Psalm 56 (and 51), now doubled. David has no claim to make. He asks for undeserved kindness. Henry calls it "no vain repetition" and notes it is the tax collector's prayer (Luke 18:13).
  • "For in you my soul takes refuge" (v1). The verb chasah means to flee for shelter, and it is a favorite word in the Psalms (2:12; 7:1; 11:1). David is sitting in a literal refuge, a cave, but he names God as his refuge, not the rock around him. Boice, cited by Guzik, makes exactly that point.
  • "In the shadow of your wings I will take refuge" (v1). The picture is a mother bird gathering her chicks under her wings when danger comes (17:8; 36:7; 61:4; 63:7; 91:4). Jesus used it of himself over Jerusalem: "How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings" (Matt 23:37). Ruth came to take refuge under the wings of the God of Israel (Ruth 2:12), and she was David's great-grandmother. Henry suggests a second layer: the outstretched wings of the cherubim over the ark, where God's presence dwelt (80:1). Morgan, cited by Guzik, notes the change from Psalm 55:6. There David longed for wings like a dove to fly away. Here he hides under God's wings instead.
  • "Till the storms of destruction pass by" (v1). "Storms of destruction" is one Hebrew word in the plural, hawwot, "ruin, calamities" (JFB: "mischiefs," the same word for the destroying tongue in 52:2). The image is shelter until a storm blows over. David expects it to pass. Henry: "the storm will blow over… Though now distressed, I shall not always be so."
  • "I cry out to God Most High" (v2). "Most High" is 'Elyon, the name Melchizedek used when he blessed Abram as "God Most High, Possessor of heaven and earth" (Gen 14:19). Kidner, cited by Guzik, notices the link: God cared for Abram, another man without a settled home. From the bottom of a cave, David calls on the God who is highest of all.
  • "To God who fulfills his purpose for me" (v2). The verb is gamar, to complete or bring to an end, used only five times, all in the Psalms. BDB gives "God that completeth, accomplisheth, for me." Psalm 138:8 uses it the same way: "The LORD will fulfill his purpose for me." God had begun something in David when Samuel anointed him, and he would finish it (compare Phil 1:6). JFB: God "completes what He has begun." The ancient Greek translation read a slightly different word, "who deals kindly with me," which the Tyndale notes list as an alternative, but the Hebrew as it stands makes good sense.
  • "He will send from heaven and save me" (v3). David cannot see any help on earth. Henry: "Look which way he will, in this earth, refuge fails… but he looks for it from heaven." Clarke, cited by Guzik, suggests God would send angels if needed.
  • "He will put to shame him who tramples on me. Selah" (v3). "Tramples" is sha'aph again, the panting, snapping verb of 56:1–2. JFB: "that pants in rage after me." The Selah in the middle of a verse is unusual. Whatever it meant musically, it makes a pause right after naming the enemy, as Maclaren, cited by Guzik, observes.
  • "God will send out his steadfast love and his faithfulness!" (v3). Chesed (covenant love, loyal kindness) and 'emet (faithfulness, truth) are the great pair that run through the Old Testament from Exodus 34:6. Here they are pictured almost as two messengers sent down from heaven on a rescue mission. JFB links it to 43:3: "Send out your light and your truth; let them lead me." The pair comes back in v10, where they fill the sky.
  • Commentators: Henry builds the section around three steps: all David's dependence is on God (v1), all his desire is toward God (v2), and all his expectation is from God (v3). He says those who "make God their only refuge… may be sure of salvation, in his way and time." Guzik adds that David, a soldier, knew the value of high ground, and "God Most High" is the highest. JFB is brief here, reading "my soul" as "self, or life, which is threatened."

My Soul Is in the Midst of Lions (vv4–5)

  • "My soul is in the midst of lions" (v4). Lions still lived in the land in David's day. He had fought them as a shepherd (1 Sam 17:34–36), and Maclaren, cited by Guzik, wonders whether wild animals prowled near the cave. But the lions here are people, Saul and his men. The Tyndale notes say the figure stresses enemies too strong to escape without God stepping in (compare 35:17).
  • "I lie down amid fiery beasts" (v4). "Lie down" is a startling verb. David does not say he is crouching or watching through the night. He lies down, which sounds like sleep (compare 3:5; 4:8). The word translated "fiery beasts" comes from a verb meaning to blaze or devour, and the King James has "them that are set on fire." Henry: "They are set on fire, and breathe nothing but flame."
  • "The children of man, whose teeth are spears and arrows, whose tongues are sharp swords" (v4). The images pile up: beasts, fire, weapons. JFB: "the mingled figures of wild beasts… and weapons of war heighten the picture of danger." The deepest wound, as so often in these psalms, comes from words. Saul's court was full of men who slandered David (1 Sam 24:9: "Why do you listen to the words of men who say, 'Behold, David seeks your harm'?"). Henry: "A spiteful tongue is a dangerous weapon."
  • "Be exalted, O God, above the heavens! Let your glory be over all the earth!" (v5). In the middle of the lions, David suddenly turns to praise. He does not pray, "Lift me up." He prays, "Lift yourself up." God's "glory" (kavod, literally "weight," the heaviness of his real presence) is meant to fill the whole earth (Num 14:21; Isa 6:3; Hab 2:14). The Tyndale notes connect it to the seraphim's song in Isaiah 6. JFB says the refrain shows "the connection of his deliverance with God's glory": rescuing David would display God before the world.
  • Commentators: Henry draws the same contrast: "When David was in the greatest distress and disgrace he did not pray, Lord, exalt me, but, Lord, exalt thy own name," and he compares Jesus' prayer, "Father, glorify your name" (John 12:27–28). Guzik passes on Spurgeon's four comforts for anyone living among lions, ending with the Lion of the tribe of Judah, and Spurgeon's picture of David walking to the mouth of the cave and looking up at the open sky. Guzik notes that the worst weapon in the list is the tongue.

My Heart Is Steadfast (vv6–11)

  • "They set a net for my steps; my soul was bowed down" (v6). Back to danger for a moment. Hunters hid nets on paths and dug pits covered with brush to catch animals. David admits it got to him: "my soul was bowed down," bent over, discouraged. The psalm is honest about the weight.
  • "They dug a pit in my way, but they have fallen into it themselves. Selah" (v6). A familiar Bible pattern: the trap catches the trapper (7:15; 9:15; Prov 26:27; Esth 7:10). JFB points to those psalms. Henry sees it played out in the story itself: in the cave at En-gedi, "when Saul thought David should fall into his hands, he fell into the hands of David, and lay at his mercy." The verb is in the past tense, spoken as if done already. Guzik calls it the confidence of faith.
  • "My heart is steadfast, O God, my heart is steadfast!" (v7). "Steadfast" is nakon, from kun, "to be firm, established, fixed." It is the word for a building set firmly on its pillars (Judg 16:26) and for God's throne and David's kingdom being established (2 Sam 7:16). The King James has "My heart is fixed." The same soul that was "bowed down" in v6 is now set firm. Psalm 112:7 uses the same word of the righteous man: "his heart is firm, trusting in the LORD." Nothing outside has changed yet. He is still in the cave.
  • "I will sing and make melody!" (v7). "Make melody" is zamar, to sing with instruments, the root of mizmor, "psalm." JFB: "both with voice and instrument."
  • "Awake, my glory!" (v8). "My glory" probably means David's inner self, his whole being, or, as JFB and Henry take it, his tongue, the instrument of praise (compare 30:12, "that my glory may sing your praise," and 16:9, where the ESV renders the same word "my whole being"). Some scholars repoint the Hebrew to kaved, "liver," a Hebrew way of speaking of the deep inner person, but the sense comes out much the same. David is stirring himself up. Henry: "dull and sleepy devotions will never be acceptable to God."
  • "Awake, O harp and lyre!" (v8). The nevel (a larger harp, ESV "harp") and kinnor (a smaller lyre, the instrument David played for Saul, 1 Sam 16:23). Guzik wonders whether David even had them in the cave. Clarke, cited by Guzik, mentions a Jewish legend that David's harp hung over his bed and played by itself when the north wind blew at midnight, a story Clarke himself treats as fable.
  • "I will awake the dawn!" (v8). A lovely reversal. Normally the dawn wakes a sleeper. David will be up before the sun and wake the dawn with his singing. JFB: literally "I will awaken dawn," poetically expressing his zeal. Henry: "early in the morning, to begin the day with God." For a man who "lay down amid fiery beasts" (v4), waking up to sing is itself a sign of God's keeping.
  • "I will give thanks to you, O Lord, among the peoples; I will sing praises to you among the nations" (v9). From a hole in the ground, David vows to praise God in front of the whole Gentile world. It sounds absurd from where he sits. But his psalms have done exactly that for three thousand years. Henry: David "may be said to be still praising God among the people and singing to him among the nations; for all good people make use of his words." Kidner, cited by Guzik, links the vow to Romans 15:9, where Paul quotes the same thought from David (Ps 18:49) as proof that Christ came so "the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy."
  • "For your steadfast love is great to the heavens, your faithfulness to the clouds" (v10). The two messengers of v3 now fill the whole sky. A cave limits how far a man can see, as Guzik puts it, but David's song reaches the clouds. Psalm 36:5 says the same: "Your steadfast love, O LORD, extends to the heavens, your faithfulness to the clouds." Henry: "great beyond conception and expression."
  • "Be exalted, O God, above the heavens! Let your glory be over all the earth!" (v11). The refrain again, word for word. In v5 it came in the middle of lions. Now it comes at the end of a song. Kidner, cited by Guzik, describes the first as sung in defiant faith and the second in grateful love. Nothing has changed in David's circumstances between the two. What has changed is his heart.
  • Psalm 108. Verses 7–11 reappear as Psalm 108:1–5, and Psalm 60:5–12 makes up the rest of Psalm 108. Israel's worship leaders took a song from David's cave and a song from his battles and joined them into a new psalm for public praise. The words of one hard night became the church's words.
  • Commentators: Henry: "How strangely is the tune altered here!" He takes "fixed" as "prepared for every event, being stayed upon God," and adds "This should make us in love with prayer, that, sooner or later, it will be swallowed up in praise." Guzik passes on Morgan's line that a fixed heart is the secret of song, and stresses that David did not wait for things to improve before praising. JFB takes "my glory" as the tongue and reads vv9–10 together: "As His mercy and truth, so shall His praise, fill the universe." JFB has no separate note on v11.

Pointing to Christ

David in the cave was the true king, anointed but not yet crowned, hunted by the one on the throne, gathering the distressed and the indebted around him (1 Sam 22:2). Jesus was the true King in the same way: rejected, surrounded by enemies whose words were swords, and gathering to himself the poor and the burdened. His body was laid in a cave too, a tomb cut in the rock, and God "sent from heaven and saved" him by raising him from the dead. Horne, cited by Guzik, saw David's rescue as a picture of that resurrection morning, when the dawn really was awakened. Now the risen Son of David leads praise "among the nations" (Rom 15:9), and the One who longed to gather Jerusalem under his wings (Matt 23:37) shelters everyone who takes refuge in him until the storms of destruction pass by.

Reflection

  • Refuge Until the Storm Passes (v.1) — David takes shelter under God's wings, not expecting the danger to vanish instantly but trusting it will eventually pass. When you're in a hard season, are you looking for God to remove it immediately, or willing to shelter in Him while it runs its course?
  • Worship Bigger Than the Cave (v.5) — Even hiding for his life, David's prayer lifts beyond his own trouble to ask that God be exalted above the heavens. Does your prayer life stay focused only on your problems, or does it also make room for God's greater glory?
  • A Steadfast Heart Regardless of Circumstances (v.7) — David declares his heart fixed and ready to sing while he's still hunted and hiding. Is your inner stability tied to your circumstances actually being stable, or to something deeper?
  • Praise Before the Day Gets Ahead of You (v.8) — David resolves to wake the dawn itself with his praise rather than let anxiety set the tone for his morning. What usually gets your first attention in the morning — worry about the day, or gratitude toward God?
