Summary

Elihu, a younger bystander, presents himself as Job's equal — formed from the same clay, no more terrifying than Job himself (vv. 1-7) — then quotes back Job's claim to be innocent yet treated as God's enemy (vv. 8-11). Countering Job's charge that God never speaks, Elihu insists God speaks through dreams and night visions that turn a person from pride (vv. 14-18), and through pain that chastens the body while preserving the soul from the pit (vv. 19-22). If a mediating messenger pleads a person's uprightness, God is gracious and finds a ransom, restoring health and favor (vv. 23-28) — one of the book's clearest anticipations of Christ. Elihu closes by inviting Job to answer, since God does all this to rescue and enlighten a life, not destroy it (vv. 29-33).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown (see Chapter 1 notes for full discussion).
Historical Context: Chapter 33 is Elihu's first substantive speech directed at Job. Having spent chapter 32 introducing himself, Elihu now addresses Job's central complaint: that God does not speak, does not answer, and does not explain His actions. Elihu responds with a crucial insight: God does speak — in dreams (vv.14-16), in pain (vv.19-22), and through mediating angels (v.23). Job has been crying "God doesn't speak to me!" and Elihu answers: "God is speaking to you — through the very suffering you endure." This is Elihu's most original contribution to the dialogue: suffering may be communication, not punishment. God may be saying something through pain, not merely doing something to Job.
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: Elihu positioning himself as Job's equal, made by the same Spirit and formed from the same clay, rather than as another distant accuser, Job's own words quoted back to him with apparent accuracy, I am clean without transgression, he counteth me for his enemy, a claim that God does in fact speak, just not the way Job expects, through dreams, through pain and through a mediating angel, one of the most Christological passages in the book naming a ransom found and a soul brought back from the pit, and an open invitation for Job to answer, the only one of the four speakers who actually offers him the floor
Elihu's Credentials (vv.1-7): Elihu positions himself as Job's equal — made by the same Spirit (v.4), formed from the same clay (v.6). He will not terrify Job as God might (v.7). He presents himself as an approachable mediator, unlike the distant God Job cannot find (23:8-9).
Elihu Quotes Job (vv.8-11): Elihu cites Job's claims: "I am clean without transgression" (v.9), "he findeth occasions against me" (v.10), "he counteth me for his enemy" (v.10), "he putteth my feet in the stocks" (v.11). These are fair paraphrases of Job's positions throughout the dialogue.
God Is Greater Than Man (vv.12-13): Behold, in this thou art not righteous, I will answer thee, that God is greater than man. Elihu has quoted Job accurately and now gives his one-line refutation, and it is not an argument so much as a statement of scale. Then the question he thinks Job's complaint amounts to, why dost thou strive against him? for he giveth not account of any of his matters.
God Speaks Through Dreams (vv.14-18): "For God speaketh once, yea twice, yet man perceiveth it not. In a dream, in a vision of the night" (vv.14-15). God opens ears, seals instruction, withdraws man from evil purposes, and hides pride — all through night visions. The first mode of divine communication is revelation.
God Speaks Through Pain (vv.19-22): "He is chastened also with pain upon his bed" (v.19). Physical suffering can be God's second mode of communication — not punishment for sin but prevention of greater evil. Pain keeps the soul "from the pit" (v.18). This is suffering as discipline, not retribution.
The Mediating Angel (vv.23-28): If there is "a messenger with him, an interpreter, one among a thousand, to shew unto man his uprightness" (v.23) — then God is "gracious unto him, and saith, Deliver him from going down to the pit: I have found a ransom" (v.24). This is one of the most Christological passages in Job: a mediator, a ransom, restoration of youth, return to righteousness. Elihu glimpses the gospel.
Twice, Even Three Times (vv.29-33): Lo, all these things worketh God oftentimes with man. The dream, the pain and the messenger are gathered up as one repeated method rather than three separate ones, and the purpose is stated, to bring back his soul from the pit, that he may be enlightened with the light of the living. Then Elihu turns from theology to procedure and offers Job the floor: mark well, O Job, hearken unto me, hold thy peace, and I will speak. If thou hast anything to say, answer me, speak, for I desire to justify thee. Of the four speakers in the book he is the only one who says that.

Setting: Elihu, the young man who burst into the debate in chapter 32, has finished explaining why he must speak. Now he turns to Job himself, calling him by name, something the three friends never did. Job had begged again and again for a hearing with God and for someone to stand between them: "There is no arbiter between us, who might lay his hand on us both" (9:33); "Oh, that I had one to hear me!" (31:35). He had complained that God would not answer him and that God watched him like an enemy. Elihu takes up exactly these complaints. His big idea in this chapter is that God does speak, through dreams and through pain, and that suffering can be God's mercy sent to pull a person back from the edge of death. It is the most hopeful thing anyone has said to Job so far, and it contains one of the most striking passages in the Old Testament about a mediator and a ransom (vv23–28).

Elihu Rebukes Job (vv1–7)

  • "But now, hear my speech, O Job" (v1). For thirty chapters the friends spoke at Job without ever using his name. Elihu addresses him directly and personally. He asks for a hearing, just as Job had asked his friends to listen (13:6).
  • "I open my mouth; the tongue in my mouth speaks" (v2). This sounds like padding, and in part it is. Elihu is still clearing his throat. But he is also stressing that what follows has been thought through before he says it.
  • "My words declare the uprightness of my heart" (v3). He promises sincerity. The friends had been accused by Job of speaking falsely for God (13:7). Elihu claims to speak straight.
  • "The Spirit of God has made me, and the breath of the Almighty gives me life" (v4). "Spirit" is ruach and "breath" is neshamah, the same pair as in 32:8. This is Genesis 2:7 in a sentence: God formed man and breathed life into him. Elihu claims no special status here. He is saying that he and Job owe their lives to the same Maker.
  • "Answer me, if you can... take your stand" (v5). Courtroom language. Job wanted to stand in court before God and argue his case (13:18; 23:4). Elihu offers himself as the one to argue with.
  • "I am toward God as you are; I too was pinched off from a piece of clay" (v6). The picture is a potter pinching off a lump of clay to shape a pot. Elihu is the same kind of creature as Job (compare 10:9: "you have made me like clay"). The older King James wording, "I am according to thy wish in God's stead," took the Hebrew to mean Elihu was the go-between Job had asked for. The Hebrew is literally "I am like your mouth toward God," and most modern translations take it as the ESV does: Elihu stands toward God exactly where Job stands. Either way, he is offering Job a fair hearing from an equal.
  • "No fear of me need terrify you" (v7). Job had pleaded with God: "withdraw your hand far from me, and let not dread of you terrify me" (13:21). Elihu echoes those words. He is not God, so Job need not be afraid of him. The friends had threatened Job with God's terrors (15:21; 18:11). The Tyndale notes add that Elihu promises gentleness here but becomes harsh later (34:7–9, 34–37).
  • Commentators: Henry reads v6 with the King James sense and has Elihu say, "I am, according to thy wish, in God's stead," answering Job's longing for an arbitrator: "O that one might plead for a man with God!" (16:21). He takes "formed out of the clay" to mean Elihu is no unequal match for Job. JFB likewise sees Elihu as the "daysman" (umpire) Job wished for and even calls him a type of Christ. It notes the two possible meanings of "pinched off": pressed together like a potter's clay, or cut off as a piece from a lump. Guzik is less generous. He finds the opening wordy and hears pride in it, Elihu presenting himself as the very advocate Job asked for. He passes on one scholar's summary: Elihu sees only a little, but sees that little clearly.

Elihu Rebukes Job (vv8–13)

  • "You say, 'I am pure, without transgression'" (v9). Elihu quotes Job, but not quite fairly. Job did say "I am blameless" (9:21) and "my prayer is pure" (16:17), and he swore he had not committed the sins listed in chapter 31. But he never claimed to be sinless. He asked, "Why do you not pardon my transgression?" (7:21), and spoke of "the iniquities of my youth" (13:26). Job claimed innocence of the kind of sin that could explain his suffering, not perfection.
  • "He finds occasions against me, he counts me as his enemy" (v10). This part is a fair quote. Job had said, "Why... count me as your enemy?" (13:24) and "He has... counted me as his adversary" (19:11). "Occasions" is a rare word, possibly meaning "pretexts" or, as JFB takes it, "enmities": grounds for hostility.
  • "He puts my feet in the stocks and watches all my paths" (v11). Almost word for word from 13:27. Job felt like a prisoner under constant guard, every step watched (7:20; 14:16).
  • "In this you are not right... for God is greater than man" (v12). Elihu's answer is simple and true as far as it goes. God is not a man to be put on trial. Job's words have overstepped. Yet it is not a full answer, and Job knew God was greater (9:2–12). His struggle was with what that great God was doing.
  • "Why do you contend against him, saying, 'He will answer none of man's words'?" (v13). This was Job's central complaint: "I cry to you for help and you do not answer me" (30:20; also 19:7; 23:3–5). Elihu's reply is that God has been speaking. Job simply has not recognized his voice.
  • Commentators: Guzik thinks Elihu did not listen carefully. He lists the places where Job admitted sin (7:21; 9:2; 13:26 and others) and argues that Job defended his integrity, not a claim of sinlessness. He grants that Elihu rightly captured Job's sense of being treated as God's enemy. Henry calls the words Elihu quotes things Job said in the heat of argument, which is fairer to both men. JFB draws from v12 that if God is greater, he would not watch a man as an enemy without cause, so Job must have sin needing correction. That is close to the friends' argument and not proven by the text.

Elihu Rebukes Job (vv14–22)

  • "God speaks in one way, and in two, though man does not perceive it" (v14). "One way, and in two" is a Hebrew way of saying "again and again." The problem is not that God is silent. It is that people do not notice. Elihu names two ways God speaks.
  • First, dreams and night visions (vv15–16). "When deep sleep falls on men." The phrase for "deep sleep" is the same word used when God put Adam to sleep (Gen 2:21) and when Abraham fell into a deep sleep before God made a covenant with him (Gen 15:12). God warned Abimelech in a dream (Gen 20:3), and Laban (Gen 31:24). Eliphaz claimed such a vision himself (4:12–16), and Job complained that God scared him with dreams (7:14). Elihu suggests those very dreams may have been God's voice. "Opens the ears" is literally "uncovers the ear," a Hebrew phrase for revealing something (1 Sam 9:15).
  • "That he may turn man aside from his deed and conceal pride from a man" (v17). The warnings have a purpose: to turn a person back before sin runs its course. "Conceal pride" is an unusual phrase, probably meaning to cut pride away or keep a man from it. JFB pictures pride as an open pit that God covers so a man will not fall into it.
  • "He keeps back his soul from the pit, his life from perishing by the sword" (v18). "The pit" (shachat) is the grave, the place of the dead. The last phrase may mean "perishing by the sword," or, as some translations read it, "crossing the river" of death, using a word that can mean a channel. The Tyndale notes say both fit. Either way, God's warning is rescue.
  • Second, pain (vv19–22). "Man is also rebuked with pain on his bed." The word "rebuked" here is the language of correction, the same root behind Proverbs 3:11–12: "do not despise the LORD's discipline... for the LORD reproves him whom he loves." Elihu's picture of the sufferer is Job himself: food turns loathsome (compare 6:7), flesh wastes away until the bones stick out (19:20), and life draws near "to those who bring death" (v22), probably the angels or powers of death, like the destroyer at the Passover (Exod 12:23) and the angel of plague (2 Sam 24:16).
  • This is Elihu's main contribution. The friends said suffering is God's punishment for sins already committed. Elihu says suffering can be God's warning and discipline, sent to keep a person from sin and death. That is a real advance, and the New Testament teaches it: "the Lord disciplines the one he loves" (Heb 12:6–11). It is not the whole answer for Job, whose suffering began with a heavenly challenge the friends and Elihu never saw (1:6–12), but it is true and important.
  • Commentators: Guzik passes on Spurgeon's picture of pain as the Shepherd's sheepdogs, sent to drive wandering sheep home, and G. Campbell Morgan's view that suffering here is educational, not merely penal. Henry says sickness and pain are not signs of God's wrath or of hypocrisy, but wise and gracious means that work patience, experience and hope. JFB reads the loss of appetite in v20 as affliction stripping away the desires that feed pride, and the "destroyers" of v22 as angels of death or death's pains. Guzik also passes on Adam Clarke's caution that not every dream is from God but some dreams have been.

Elihu Rebukes Job (vv23–33)

  • "If there be for him an angel, a mediator, one of the thousand" (v23). The word "angel" (malak) also means "messenger," human or heavenly. "Mediator" (melits) is the word used for the interpreter who stood between Joseph and his brothers (Gen 42:23). The lexicon gives "interpreter," and figuratively a go-between for God and man. This messenger's job is "to declare to man what is right for him," to explain what God is doing in the suffering and what the sufferer should do. "One of the thousand" means one rare and special among many. Job had longed for exactly this: "There is no arbiter between us" (9:33); "my witness is in heaven" (16:19); "I know that my Redeemer lives" (19:25).
  • "He is merciful to him, and says, 'Deliver him... I have found a ransom'" (v24). "Ransom" is kopher, the price paid to redeem a life that would otherwise be lost (Exod 21:30; Ps 49:7–8). It comes from the same family of words as the Day of Atonement, when sin was "covered." The text does not say what the ransom is. It might be the sufferer's repentance, or the mediator's own pleading. But the shape is unmistakable: someone stands between, shows mercy, and pays a price so the dying man can go free.
  • "Let his flesh become fresh with youth" (v25). The wasted body of vv19–22 is renewed, "fresh" like a child's. The picture recalls Naaman, whose flesh "was restored like the flesh of a little child" (2 Kings 5:14). Job will be restored in chapter 42.
  • "Man prays to God, and he accepts him; he sees his face with a shout of joy" (v26). The rescued man is welcomed back into God's presence. Seeing God's face is the blessing of Numbers 6:25 ("the LORD make his face to shine upon you"). "He restores to man his righteousness": he is put right with God again.
  • "I sinned and perverted what was right, and it was not repaid to me" (v27). The restored man testifies in public. He confesses his sin and marvels at grace: he did not get what he deserved. The Hebrew of the last phrase is literally "it was not made equal to me," the punishment did not match the wrong. This is the opposite of the friends' theology, where everyone gets exactly what he deserves.
  • "He has redeemed my soul from going down into the pit" (v28). "Redeemed" (padah) is the word used for buying back a firstborn son (Exod 13:13) and for God redeeming Israel from Egypt (Deut 7:8). Some ancient copies read "his soul" and "his life," making v28 Elihu's description instead of the man's song. The ESV follows the reading where the man himself speaks.
  • "God does all these things, twice, three times, with a man" (v29). God is patient. He warns more than once. The goal is always "to bring back his soul from the pit, that he may be lighted with the light of life" (v30).
  • "Be silent, and I will speak... I desire to justify you" (vv31–33). Elihu wants Job to answer if he can, and claims he would like to clear him. The Tyndale notes find this a little ironic, since Elihu joined the debate precisely because Job would not admit sin. But taken at face value it is the kindest line any of the counselors has spoken: he wants Job restored, not condemned. Then, before Job can reply, he asks him to keep quiet so he can teach him wisdom. Job does not answer.
  • Commentators: All three read vv23–24 as pointing to Christ. Henry: "when God speaks by afflictions, we are frequently so unversed in the language, that we have need of an interpreter," and such a one "is then one of a thousand, and to be valued accordingly." On v24 he says plainly, "Jesus Christ is that ransom." On v27 he urges confession: "We must confess the fact of sin (I have sinned) and not deny the charge, or stand upon our own justification." JFB calls the ransom an atonement and a covering, and notes the same word is used for the pitch covering Noah's ark (Gen 6:14). The lexicon lists "pitch" as a separate meaning of the same letters, so the link is a sound play on words rather than proof. JFB also links "one among a thousand" to the beloved who is "distinguished among ten thousand" (Song 5:10), which is a devotional reading, not the plain sense. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's delight in v24 as God's "Eureka!", the wonder of divine wisdom finding a ransom in Christ's substitution. He cautions that the passage does not really fit Job's situation, since Job's suffering was not caused by sin, but describes conversion beautifully: grace, rescue, renewal, prayer, joy and confession.

Pointing to Christ

Job cried out for an arbiter who could lay a hand on both God and man. Elihu describes one: a messenger, an interpreter, "one of the thousand," who is merciful and says, "Deliver him from going down into the pit; I have found a ransom." Elihu could not have known how fully that would be answered. Jesus is the "one mediator between God and men" who "gave himself as a ransom for all" (1 Tim 2:5–6). He did not only find a ransom. He became it: "The Son of Man came... to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). The rescued man's song in vv27–28 is the song of every believer: "I sinned... and it was not repaid to me. He has redeemed my soul." In Christ, the punishment did not match the sin because Christ bore it, and the one rescued from the pit will "look upon the light," the light of life (John 8:12).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik identifies v.24 as one of the most explicitly Christological verses in Job. The concept of a ransom ("kopher" = covering/atonement price) found by a heavenly mediator points directly to Christ's substitutionary work. Guzik notes that Elihu's insight about suffering as communication (not just punishment) is genuinely new in the dialogue and partially corrects the friends' rigid retribution theology.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • God's Voice in Dreams and Visions (vv.14-18) — Elihu insists God speaks in ways people often miss, opening ears and warding off destructive paths through the night. Have you considered that God might be speaking to you in ways you've been too busy or too hurt to notice?
  • Suffering as a Possible Form of God's Speech (vv.19-22) — Elihu suggests physical pain can be one way God gets a person's attention, not necessarily as punishment but as a wake-up call. When you're in pain, do you ever pause to ask what God might be saying rather than only asking why?
  • Longing for a Mediator Between Himself and God (v.23) — Elihu describes a rare intermediary who could declare a person's uprightness to God on their behalf. Who or what do you rely on to help you feel close to God when the distance feels too great to cross alone?
  • A Ransom Found, Not Earned (v.24) — The rescue Elihu describes originates entirely with God, who finds the ransom rather than waiting for the sufferer to produce one. Do you tend to treat God's grace as something you must earn, or as the gift Elihu describes it to be?
  • Discipline Aimed at Restoration, Not Ruin (vv.29-30) — Elihu insists God's purpose in painful discipline is to rescue a person from destruction, not to destroy them. Can you see the hard seasons of your life as aimed at your restoration rather than your ruin?
