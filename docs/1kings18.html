1After a long time, in the third year of the drought, the word of the LORD came to Elijah: “Go and present yourself to Ahab, and I will send rain upon the face of the earth.”

2So Elijah went to present himself to Ahab. The famine was severe in Samaria,

3and Ahab summoned Obadiah, who was in charge of the palace. (Now Obadiah greatly feared the LORD,

4for when Jezebel had slaughtered the prophets of the LORD, Obadiah had taken a hundred prophets and hidden them, fifty men per cave, providing them with food and water.)

5Then Ahab said to Obadiah, “Go throughout the land to every spring and every valley. Perhaps we will find grass to keep the horses and mules alive so that we will not have to destroy any livestock.”

6So they divided the land to explore. Ahab went one way by himself, and Obadiah went the other way by himself.

7Now as Obadiah went on his way, Elijah suddenly met him. When Obadiah recognized him, he fell facedown and said, “Is it you, my lord Elijah?”

8“It is I,” he answered. “Go tell your master, ‘Elijah is here!’”

9But Obadiah replied, “How have I sinned, that you are handing your servant over to Ahab to put me to death?

10As surely as the LORD your God lives, there is no nation or kingdom where my lord has not sent someone to search for you. When they said, ‘He is not here,’ he made that kingdom or nation swear that they had not found you.

11And now you say, ‘Go tell your master that Elijah is here!’

12I do not know where the Spirit of the LORD may carry you off when I leave you. Then when I go and tell Ahab and he does not find you, he will kill me. But I, your servant, have feared the LORD from my youth.

13Was it not reported to my lord what I did when Jezebel slaughtered the prophets of the LORD? I hid a hundred prophets of the LORD, fifty men per cave, and I provided them with food and water.

14And now you say, ‘Go tell your lord that Elijah is here!’ He will kill me!”

15Then Elijah said, “As surely as the LORD of Hosts lives, before whom I stand, I will present myself to Ahab today.”

16So Obadiah went to inform Ahab, who went to meet Elijah.

17When Ahab saw Elijah, he said to him, “Is that you, O troubler of Israel?”

18“I have not troubled Israel,” Elijah replied, “but you and your father’s house have, for you have forsaken the commandments of the LORD and have followed the Baals.

19Now summon all Israel to meet me on Mount Carmel, along with the four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal and the four hundred prophets of Asherah who eat at Jezebel’s table.”

20So Ahab summoned all the Israelites and assembled the prophets on Mount Carmel.

21Then Elijah approached all the people and said, “How long will you waver between two opinions? If the LORD is God, follow Him. But if Baal is God, follow him.” But the people did not answer a word.

22Then Elijah said to the people, “I am the only remaining prophet of the LORD, but Baal has four hundred and fifty prophets.

23Get two bulls for us. Let the prophets of Baal choose one bull for themselves, cut it into pieces, and place it on the wood but not light the fire. And I will prepare the other bull and place it on the wood but not light the fire.

24Then you may call on the name of your god, and I will call on the name of the LORD. The God who answers by fire, He is God.” And all the people answered, “What you say is good.”

25Then Elijah said to the prophets of Baal, “Since you are so numerous, choose for yourselves one bull and prepare it first. Then call on the name of your god, but do not light the fire.”

26And they took the bull that was given them, prepared it, and called on the name of Baal from morning until noon, shouting, “O Baal, answer us!” But there was no sound, and no one answered as they leaped around the altar they had made.

27At noon Elijah began to taunt them, saying, “Shout louder, for he is a god! Perhaps he is deep in thought, or occupied, or on a journey. Perhaps he is sleeping and must be awakened!”

28So they shouted louder and cut themselves with knives and lances, as was their custom, until the blood gushed over them.

29Midday passed, and they kept on raving until the time of the evening sacrifice. But there was no response; no one answered, no one paid attention.

30Then Elijah said to all the people, “Come near to me.” So all the people approached him, and he repaired the altar of the LORD that had been torn down.

31And Elijah took twelve stones, one for each tribe of the sons of Jacob, to whom the word of the LORD had come and said, “Israel shall be your name.”

32And with the stones, Elijah built an altar in the name of the LORD. Then he dug a trench around the altar large enough to hold two seahs of seed.

33Next, he arranged the wood, cut up the bull, placed it on the wood,

34and said, “Fill four waterpots and pour the water on the offering and on the wood.” “Do it a second time,” he said, and they did it a second time. “Do it a third time,” he said, and they did it a third time.

35So the water ran down around the altar and even filled the trench.

36At the time of the evening sacrifice, Elijah the prophet approached the altar and said, “O LORD, God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, let it be known this day that You are God in Israel and that I am Your servant and have done all these things at Your command.

37Answer me, O LORD! Answer me, so that this people will know that You, the LORD, are God, and that You have turned their hearts back again.”

38Then the fire of the LORD fell and consumed the sacrifice, the wood, the stones, and the dust, and it licked up the water in the trench.

39When all the people saw this, they fell facedown and said, “The LORD, He is God! The LORD, He is God!”

40Then Elijah ordered them, “Seize the prophets of Baal! Do not let a single one escape.” So they seized them, and Elijah brought them down to the Kishon Valley and slaughtered them there.

41And Elijah said to Ahab, “Go up, eat and drink, for there is the sound of a heavy rain.”

42So Ahab went up to eat and drink. But Elijah climbed to the summit of Carmel, bent down on the ground, and put his face between his knees.

43“Go and look toward the sea,” he said to his servant. So the servant went and looked, and he said, “There is nothing there.” Seven times Elijah said, “Go back.”

44On the seventh time the servant reported, “There is a cloud as small as a man’s hand rising from the sea.” And Elijah replied, “Go and tell Ahab, ‘Prepare your chariot and go down before the rain stops you.’”

45Meanwhile, the sky grew dark with clouds and wind, and a heavy rain began to fall. So Ahab rode away and went to Jezreel.

46And the hand of the LORD came upon Elijah, and he tucked his cloak into his belt and ran ahead of Ahab all the way to Jezreel.

1Some time later, in the third year of the famine, the Lord’s message came to Elijah, “Go, make an appearance before Ahab, so I may send rain on the surface of the ground.”

2So Elijah went to make an appearance before Ahab.Now the famine was severe in Samaria.

3So Ahab summoned Obadiah, who supervised the palace. (Now Obadiah was a very loyal follower of the Lord.

4When Jezebel was killing the Lord’s prophets, Obadiah took 100 prophets and hid them in two caves in two groups of 50. He also brought them food and water.)

5Ahab told Obadiah, “Go through the land to all the springs and valleys. Maybe we can find some grazing areas so we can keep the horses and mules alive and not have to kill some of the animals.”

6They divided up the land between them to search it; Ahab went one way by himself and Obadiah went the other way by himself.

7As Obadiah was traveling along, Elijah met him. When he recognized him, he fell facedown to the ground and said, “Is it really you, my master, Elijah?”

8He replied, “Yes, go and say to your master, ‘Elijah is back.’”

9Obadiah said, “What sin have I committed that you are ready to hand your servant over to Ahab for execution?

10As certainly as the Lord your God lives, my master has sent to every nation and kingdom in an effort to find you. When they say, ‘He’s not here,’ he makes them swear an oath that they could not find you.

11Now you say, ‘Go and say to your master, “Elijah is back.”’

12But when I leave you, the Lord’s Spirit will carry you away so I can’t find you. If I go tell Ahab I’ve seen you, he won’t be able to find you and he will kill me. That would not be fair, because your servant has been a loyal follower of the Lord from my youth.

13Certainly my master is aware of what I did when Jezebel was killing the Lord’s prophets. I hid 100 of the Lord’s prophets in two caves in two groups of 50 and I brought them food and water.

14Now you say, ‘Go and say to your master, “Elijah is back,”’ but he will kill me.”

15But Elijah said, “As certainly as the Lord of Heaven’s Armies lives (whom I serve), I will make an appearance before him today.”

16When Obadiah went and informed Ahab, the king went to meet Elijah.

17When Ahab saw Elijah, he said to him, “Is it really you, the one who brings disaster on Israel?”

18Elijah replied, “I have not brought disaster on Israel. But you and your father’s dynasty have, by abandoning the Lord’s commandments and following the Baals.

19Now send out messengers and assemble all Israel before me at Mount Carmel, as well as the 450 prophets of Baal and 400 prophets of Asherah whom Jezebel supports.”

20Ahab sent messengers to all the Israelites and had the prophets assemble at Mount Carmel.

21Elijah approached all the people and said, “How long are you going to be paralyzed by indecision? If the Lord is the true God, then follow him, but if Baal is, follow him!” But the people did not say a word.

22Elijah said to them: “I am the only prophet of the Lord who is left, but there are 450 prophets of Baal.

23Let them bring us two bulls. Let them choose one of the bulls for themselves, cut it up into pieces, and place it on the wood. But they must not set it on fire. I will do the same to the other bull and place it on the wood. But I will not set it on fire.

24Then you will invoke the name of your god, and I will invoke the name of the Lord. The god who responds with fire will demonstrate that he is the true God.” All the people responded, “This will be a fair test.”

25Elijah told the prophets of Baal, “Choose one of the bulls for yourselves and go first, for you are the majority. Invoke the name of your god, but do not light a fire.”

26So they took a bull, as he had suggested, and prepared it. They invoked the name of Baal from morning until noon, saying, “Baal, answer us.” But there was no sound and no answer. They jumped around on the altar they had made.

27At noon Elijah mocked them, “Yell louder! After all, he is a god; he may be deep in thought, or perhaps he stepped out for a moment or has taken a trip. Perhaps he is sleeping and needs to be awakened.”

28So they yelled louder and, in accordance with their prescribed ritual, mutilated themselves with swords and spears until their bodies were covered with blood.

29Throughout the afternoon they were in an ecstatic frenzy, but there was no sound, no answer, and no response.

30Elijah then told all the people, “Approach me.” So all the people approached him. He repaired the altar of the Lord that had been torn down.

31Then Elijah took 12 stones, corresponding to the number of tribes that descended from Jacob, to whom the Lord’s message had come, “Israel will be your name.”

32With the stones he constructed an altar for the Lord. Around the altar he made a trench large enough to contain two seahs of seed.

33He arranged the wood, cut up the bull, and placed it on the wood. Then he said, “Fill four water jars and pour the water on the offering and the wood.”

34When they had done so, he said, “Do it again.” So they did it again. Then he said, “Do it a third time.” So they did it a third time.

35The water flowed down all sides of the altar and filled the trench.

36When it was time for the evening offering, Elijah the prophet approached the altar and prayed: “O Lord God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, prove today that you are God in Israel and that I am your servant and have done all these things at your command.

37Answer me, O Lord, answer me, so these people will know that you, O Lord, are the true God and that you are winning back their allegiance.”

38Then fire from the Lord fell from the sky. It consumed the offering, the wood, the stones, and the dirt, and licked up the water in the trench.

39When all the people saw this, they threw themselves down with their faces to the ground and said, “The Lord is the true God! The Lord is the true God!”

40Elijah told them, “Seize the prophets of Baal! Don’t let even one of them escape!” So they seized them, and Elijah led them down to the Kishon Valley and executed them there.

41Then Elijah told Ahab, “Go on up and eat and drink, for the sound of a heavy rainstorm can be heard.”

42So Ahab went on up to eat and drink, while Elijah climbed to the top of Carmel. He bent down toward the ground and put his face between his knees.

43He told his servant, “Go on up and look in the direction of the sea.” So he went on up, looked, and reported, “There is nothing.” Seven times Elijah sent him to look.

11Now you say, ‘Go, tell your lord, “Behold, Elijah is here.”’

12It will happen, as soon as I leave you, that Yahweh’s Spirit will carry you I don’t know where; and so when I come and tell Ahab, and he can’t find you, he will kill me. But I, your servant, have feared Yahweh from my youth.

13Wasn’t it told my lord what I did when Jezebel killed Yahweh’s prophets, how I hid one hundred men of Yahweh’s prophets with fifty to a cave, and fed them with bread and water?

14Now you say, ‘Go, tell your lord, “Behold, Elijah is here”.’ He will kill me.”

Summary

After three years of drought, God sends Elijah back to confront Ahab, meeting Obadiah along the way, a God-fearing official who had hidden a hundred prophets from Jezebel's purge (vv. 1-16). Elijah challenges 450 prophets of Baal to a contest on Mount Carmel: two altars, two sacrifices, and whichever god answers by fire is God (vv. 17-24). The prophets of Baal cry out and cut themselves for hours with no response, while Elijah rebuilds the LORD's altar, drenches it with water, and prays a simple prayer that brings down fire consuming the sacrifice, wood, stones, and even the water in the trench (vv. 25-38). The people fall down declaring, "The LORD, he is God," and Elijah has the false prophets executed (vv. 39-40), then prays for rain and outruns Ahab's chariot to Jezreel as the drought breaks (vv. 41-46).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Jeremiah or a prophetic school. Originally one book with 2 Kings in the Hebrew Bible. Covers approximately 120 years (970-850 BC) — from David's death through Ahaziah's reign.
Historical Context: Chapter 18 is the climax of the Elijah narrative and one of the most dramatic confrontations in all of Scripture. Three and a half years have passed since Elijah pronounced drought upon Israel (17:1; cf. James 5:17). The famine is severe — Ahab himself is searching for grass to keep the royal horses alive. Baal was the Canaanite storm god, supposedly the one who sent rain and fertility. The drought itself was God's polemic against Baal: if Baal controls the rain, why has it not rained? The confrontation on Mount Carmel is not merely a contest between prophets — it is a trial. Yahweh and Baal are on trial before Israel, and fire from heaven is the verdict. Mount Carmel rises 1,742 feet above the Mediterranean coast, visible for miles — a natural amphitheater for this cosmic courtroom. The 450 prophets of Baal and 400 prophets of Asherah represent the full institutional weight of state-sponsored idolatry. Elijah stands alone — one man against 850, backed only by the word of God. The chapter moves from confrontation to fire to rain: God vindicates His name, His prophet, and His covenant, then restores what the drought had taken away.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: A famine so severe the king himself searches for grass to keep his horses alive, a governor who hid a hundred prophets at personal risk introduced alongside the one prophet everyone is hunting, a question that names Israel's condition exactly, how long halt ye between two opinions, mockery aimed at exposing a god who might be asleep or on a journey rather than merely taunting his prophets, and twelve barrels of water poured out in a drought before any fire falls at all
Obadiah and Elijah (vv.1-16): The chapter opens with God's command to Elijah: "Go, shew thyself unto Ahab; and I will send rain." The drought ends not because Israel has repented, but because God sovereignly chooses to act. Obadiah is introduced — a fascinating figure: the governor of Ahab's household who "feared the LORD greatly" (v.3). He had hidden 100 prophets from Jezebel's purge, feeding them at personal risk. Obadiah represents the faithful remnant serving in hostile territory. His fear of being sent to Ahab with Elijah's message reveals the danger: Ahab has searched every nation for Elijah (v.10). Elijah's oath — "As the LORD of hosts liveth, before whom I stand" — assures Obadiah he will not disappear. When Ahab meets Elijah, his accusation is revealing: "Art thou he that troubleth Israel?" (v.17). The guilty always blame the righteous for the consequences of their own sin. Elijah reverses the charge: "I have not troubled Israel; but thou" (v.18).
The Challenge on Carmel (vv.17-24): Elijah's challenge is brilliantly constructed. He demands all Israel gather — this must be public, undeniable. His question cuts to the heart: "How long halt ye between two opinions?" (v.21). The Hebrew "pasach" means to limp, to hobble — Israel is limping between Yahweh and Baal, unable to walk straight with either. The people's silence is damning — they have no answer because they know their compromise is indefensible. The test is simple and fair: two bulls, two altars, no fire. "The God that answereth by fire, let him be God" (v.24). Fire was associated with both Yahweh (Exodus 3:2; 13:21; 19:18) and Baal (as storm-god, lightning). The contest is on Baal's supposed terms — and he will still fail. The meeting comes first, and the opening line is Ahab's: Art thou he that troubleth Israel? Elijah returns the charge without softening it, I have not troubled Israel, but thou, and thy father's house, in that ye have forsaken the commandments of the LORD. The drought is placed at the king's door before the contest is proposed.
Baal's Failure (vv.25-29): Elijah gives Baal's prophets every advantage: they go first, they are many, they have all day. Their worship escalates from calling (v.26) to frantic leaping to self-mutilation with knives (v.28). Elijah's mockery is savage and deliberate: "Cry aloud: for he is a god; either he is talking, or he is pursuing, or he is in a journey, or peradventure he sleepeth" (v.27). The ESV renders one phrase as "he is relieving himself" — a crude but accurate translation of the Hebrew. Elijah is not merely taunting; he is exposing the absurdity of a god who can be absent, distracted, or asleep. The result after hours of frenzy: "there was neither voice, nor any to answer, nor any that regarded" (v.29). Baal is nothing. Silence is his only response because he does not exist.
Yahweh's Fire (vv.30-40): Elijah's actions are deliberate and symbolic. He repairs the broken altar of the LORD — Israel's worship has been in ruins. He uses twelve stones "according to the number of the tribes" — this is about all Israel, not just the northern kingdom. The name "Israel" is invoked (v.31) — the covenant name, the identity God gave to Jacob. Then Elijah does something extraordinary: he drenches everything with water. Twelve barrels total (four, three times). In a drought, water is precious — this is extravagant faith. It also eliminates any accusation of trickery. His prayer is brief — perhaps 30 seconds compared to hours of Baal worship. He asks for one thing: "that this people may know that thou art the LORD God, and that thou hast turned their heart back again" (v.37). The fire falls and consumes everything — sacrifice, wood, stones, dust, and water. The people's response is immediate: "The LORD, he is the God; the LORD, he is the God" (v.39). The prophets of Baal are executed at the brook Kishon — the penalty for leading Israel into apostasy (Deuteronomy 13:1-5).
The Return of Rain (vv.41-46): With idolatry judged, rain can return. Elijah prays with his face between his knees — a posture of intense intercession. He sends his servant to look seven times before the cloud appears — persistence in prayer. The cloud is "like a man's hand" — small, but enough for faith. The chapter ends with a stunning image: "the hand of the LORD was on Elijah; and he girded up his loins, and ran before Ahab to the entrance of Jezreel" (v.46). Elijah outruns a chariot — approximately 17 miles — by supernatural power. The prophet who called fire now runs in the rain.

Setting: About 870–867 BC, in the reign of Ahab over the northern kingdom of Israel. Three years earlier Elijah announced that there would be "neither dew nor rain" except by his word (17:1), and then vanished. God hid him by the brook Cherith and then in the home of a Gentile widow in Zarephath, in Jezebel's home country. Meanwhile Jezebel, the Phoenician queen, has been hunting down and killing the LORD's prophets, and Baal worship has become the royal religion. Baal was the storm god, believed to ride the clouds and send the rain. After three rainless years, the question hanging over Israel is plain: who really controls the sky? Chapter 18 answers it on Mount Carmel, in the most dramatic contest in the Old Testament.

Elijah Confronts Ahab (vv1–19)

  • "In the third year" (v1). Counted from the start of the drought or from Elijah's move to Zarephath, it is the third year. James and Jesus both say the drought lasted "three years and six months" (James 5:17; Luke 4:25). The two fit easily if the drought had already begun before Elijah went to Zarephath, or if the dry season before the first missed rains is counted. JFB dates the "third year" from six months after the first warning.
  • "Go, show yourself to Ahab, and I will send rain" (v1). The same God who said "hide yourself" (17:3) now says "show yourself." Elijah's movements are set by God's word, not by his own sense of safety. Notice also that the rain is promised before the contest. Carmel is not a gamble. God has already said what he will do.
  • "Obadiah, who was over the household" (v3). He was the palace manager, the king's chief steward (compare 4:6). His name means "servant of the LORD" ('Ovadyahu), and the narrator stops the story to say he "feared the LORD greatly." Most scholars do not think he is the prophet who wrote the book of Obadiah, which seems to come from a later time, though some older writers made the link.
  • "Hid them by fifties in a cave" (v4). Carmel and the hills of Israel are limestone, honeycombed with caves. Splitting the prophets into two groups meant one discovery would not kill them all. The "prophets" here were probably members of the prophetic groups or "sons of the prophets" known since Samuel's day (1 Sam 10:5; 2 Kgs 2:3). Feeding a hundred men in a famine, under Jezebel's nose, was costly and dangerous.
  • "Perhaps we may find grass and save the horses and mules" (v5). The king's concern in a famine is his animals. That was not only callous. Horses meant chariots, and chariots meant military power. An Assyrian inscription of Shalmaneser III records that Ahab brought 2,000 chariots to the Battle of Qarqar in 853 BC, more than any other king in the coalition. Still, the picture of a king searching for grass for his horses while his people starve says a great deal.
  • "Is it you, my lord Elijah?" (v7). Obadiah falls on his face. He calls Elijah "my lord" and Ahab "your lord" (v8). Elijah's reply quietly draws the line: Ahab is Obadiah's master, not Elijah's.
  • "How have I sinned...?" (v9). Obadiah is terrified. Ahab had searched "every nation or kingdom" for Elijah and made their rulers swear they did not have him (v10). Treaties of the time often required kings to hand back fugitives. If Obadiah announced Elijah and then Elijah disappeared again, Obadiah would be the one to die.
  • "The Spirit of the LORD will carry you I know not where" (v12). Obadiah assumes the Spirit might whisk Elijah away. The sons of the prophets later assume the same about Elijah (2 Kgs 2:16). It shows what people believed about this mysterious prophet, who appeared and disappeared without warning.
  • "I your servant have feared the LORD from my youth" (v12). Obadiah reminds Elijah of his record (v13). He is not boasting, only pleading that a faithful man should not be thrown away.
  • "As the LORD of hosts lives, before whom I stand" (v15). Elijah repeats the oath of 17:1, adding "of hosts," the LORD of heaven's armies. He keeps his word: "I will surely show myself to him today."
  • "Is it you, you troubler of Israel?" (v17). The word for "troubler" ('oker) is a heavy one. It is the word Joshua used of Achan, whose sin brought disaster on the whole camp: "Why did you bring trouble on us?" (Josh 7:25). The valley where Achan died was called Achor, "trouble." Ahab blames the drought on the man who announced it.
  • "I have not troubled Israel, but you have" (v18). Elijah hands the charge straight back. The drought is the covenant curse for idolatry (Deut 11:16–17; 28:23–24). Ahab and "your father's house," the dynasty of Omri, have "abandoned the commandments of the LORD and followed the Baals." The plural "Baals" probably refers to Baal in his many local forms.
  • "450 prophets of Baal and the 400 prophets of Asherah, who eat at Jezebel's table" (v19). Asherah was the Canaanite mother goddess, often paired with Baal. These prophets were on the royal payroll, supported from the queen's own household. That 850 such men existed shows how far Jezebel had pushed. The prophets of Asherah are not mentioned again at the contest. The Greek translation includes them later, but the Hebrew does not, and JFB thinks they found a way not to come.
  • Commentators: Guzik reads Ahab's "troubler" charge through Ahab's own beliefs: if Baal sends rain and Elijah has offended him, Elijah is to blame. Henry answers: "those who cause God's judgments do the mischief, not he that foretells them." Henry also sees Obadiah as proof of how "God raises up friends for his ministers and people" even in a wicked court. Guzik, drawing on Spurgeon, honors Obadiah's courage and on Meyer notes Ahab's care for his horses over his people. JFB calls Obadiah "a secret and sincere worshipper" who stayed at court because he could do good there.

The Prophets of Baal Defeated (vv20–40)

  • Mount Carmel (v20). Carmel is the ridge that runs about 15 miles from the Mediterranean coast (where Haifa is today) southeast toward the Jezreel Valley. It is green and wooded, and catches the sea rains. It sat near the border with Phoenicia and was a known holy place. An altar of the LORD had once stood here (v30), and Baal was also worshiped on its heights. If Baal could win anywhere, it was here. Tradition places the contest at el-Muhraqa, "the place of burning," on the southeastern end of the ridge, with a view over the Kishon and the plain.
  • "How long will you go limping between two different opinions?" (v21). The verb is pasach, "to limp." It is used of Mephibosheth, who became lame (2 Sam 4:4). The same verb appears in v26, where Baal's prophets "limped around the altar" in their ritual dance. Elijah's question has a sting: Israel's wavering is its own clumsy dance between two gods. "Opinions" is a rare word that probably means "branches" or "divided paths." Older translations said "halt between two opinions," using "halt" in its old sense of "limp." The people wanted the LORD and Baal, the God of their fathers and the god of the weather. Elijah insists they cannot have both. "If the LORD is God, follow him; but if Baal, then follow him."
  • "The people did not answer him a word" (v21). Their silence says everything. They are not convinced either way, and perhaps afraid of the king.
  • "I, even I only, am left a prophet of the LORD" (v22). Elijah knows of Obadiah's hundred (v13), so this is not strictly a census. He means that he alone stands publicly on the LORD's side here, one against 450. The feeling of being the last one left will come back to haunt him in 19:10.
  • "The God who answers by fire, he is God" (v24). This was Baal's own ground. As the storm god, he was pictured holding a thunderbolt. Lightning was his specialty, so a fire-from-heaven test should have been easy. But fire from heaven was also the LORD's sign of accepting a sacrifice (Lev 9:24; Judg 6:21; 1 Chr 21:26; 2 Chr 7:1). The people agree: "It is well spoken."
  • "Choose... and prepare it first, for you are many" (v25). Elijah gives them every advantage: their pick of the bull, first turn, the whole morning.
  • "O Baal, answer us!" ... But there was no voice, and no one answered (v26). The Hebrew word for "answer" ('anah) runs through the whole scene (vv24, 26, 29, 37). The contest is about which God answers.
  • "Either he is musing, or he is relieving himself, or he is on a journey, or perhaps he is asleep" (v27). This is biting sarcasm. Baal stories really did picture him traveling, sleeping and dying. The word the ESV renders "relieving himself" most likely means "gone aside," a polite way to say he is in the toilet. Older versions softened it to "he is busy." Elijah is mocking a god made in man's image, with man's needs.
  • "Cut themselves after their custom with swords and lances" (v28). Ancient texts from Ugarit describe the god El gashing himself in mourning for dead Baal, and self-wounding was a known way of trying to move a god to pity. The law of Moses forbade it for Israel (Lev 19:28; Deut 14:1). The scene is terrible: sincere, bleeding, frantic men, and nothing.
  • "They raved on" (v29). The word is the same verb used for prophesying, here in a frenzied, ecstatic sense. "No one answered; no one paid attention." The triple silence of the verse is the point. A god who does not exist cannot listen.
  • "Until the time of the offering of the oblation" (v29). This is the evening offering at the temple in Jerusalem, mid-afternoon (Exod 29:38–41). In the northern kingdom, cut off from the temple, Elijah still keeps time by it. His sacrifice will rise at the hour the true worship of God was being offered in Jerusalem.
  • "He repaired the altar of the LORD that had been thrown down" (v30). Someone had wrecked it, probably in Jezebel's purge. Before Israel can be restored, the worship of God must be restored.
  • "Twelve stones, according to the number of the tribes" (v31). The northern kingdom had only ten tribes. Elijah builds as if the nation were still one people, the twelve sons of Jacob, the man to whom God said "Israel shall be your name" (Gen 32:28; 35:10). It echoes the twelve stones Joshua set up after crossing the Jordan (Josh 4) and the twelve pillars Moses set at Sinai (Exod 24:4). The division of the kingdom is not the last word in God's eyes.
  • "A trench... as great as would contain two seahs of seed" (v32). A seah was a dry measure, perhaps about seven quarts. The phrase may describe the volume of the trench, or the area of ground that much seed would sow. Either way, it was a sizable ditch.
  • "Fill four jars with water... a second time... a third time" (vv33–34). Twelve jars in all, again matching the tribes. Where did water come from after three years of drought? There is a spring near the traditional site that JFB says never fails, and the sea is in sight of Carmel. The point is that no one could later say there was a hidden fire or a trick. Elijah makes the test harder for himself.
  • "O LORD, God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel" (v36). Elijah's prayer is short, calm and reasoned, in sharp contrast to the hours of shouting before it. He names God by his covenant with the patriarchs, using "Israel" for Jacob as in v31. He asks for three things to be known: that the LORD is God in Israel, that Elijah is his servant, and that "I have done all these things at your word." The whole show was God's idea, not Elijah's.
  • "That you have turned their hearts back" (v37). The goal is not Elijah's vindication but Israel's repentance. "Answer me, O LORD, answer me" doubles the cry. The verb is again 'anah.
  • "Then the fire of the LORD fell and consumed... the stones and the dust, and licked up the water" (v38). The fire eats what fire cannot normally eat: rock, dust and water. This is not a natural lightning strike on a wet altar. It echoes the fire that fell at the first sacrifices at the tabernacle (Lev 9:24) and at Solomon's temple (2 Chr 7:1).
  • "The LORD, he is God; the LORD, he is God" (v39). In Hebrew, YHWH hu ha-'Elohim. It is also a play on Elijah's own name, Eliyahu, "my God is the LORD." The people finally answer the question of v21. This confession echoes Moses: "the LORD is God; there is no other besides him" (Deut 4:35, 39).
  • "Seize the prophets of Baal; let not one of them escape" (v40). Elijah executes them at the brook Kishon, the stream that runs along the foot of Carmel. This is hard for modern readers. The law of Moses required death for prophets who led Israel to other gods (Deut 13:1–5; 18:20), and Israel was a nation under covenant law, with God as its King. These men were also Jezebel's agents in killing the LORD's prophets (v4). The New Testament does not give the church the sword in this way. Jesus rebuked James and John when they wanted to call down fire like Elijah (Luke 9:54–55). The Kishon was also where God defeated Sisera's army long before (Judg 4–5).
  • Commentators: Henry: "It is dangerous to halt between the service of God and the service of sin." Guzik links the "limping" of v21 to the prophets' limping dance in v26 and passes on Adam Clarke's picture of a bird hopping from branch to branch. He also notes the many readings of Elijah's taunt as crude humor about bodily functions. JFB calls the attempt to blend the worship of God with Baal "an impracticable union" and describes Carmel as "a bold, bluff promontory." On the water, JFB points to a spring below the altar site, while the Tyndale notes allow either the sea or the Kishon. Guzik answers modern theories of a trick, such as hidden fuel, by pointing out that the fire consumed even the stones and water. He also notes that the people's confession was real but short-lived. No lasting revival followed.

The LORD Sends Rain (vv41–46)

  • "Go up, eat and drink, for there is a sound of the rushing of rain" (v41). Ahab has fasted all day watching the contest. Elijah sends him to eat. Elijah hears rain before there is a cloud. He is hearing God's promise of v1.
  • "He bowed himself down on the earth and put his face between his knees" (v42). Elijah, who stood tall before 450 prophets, now crouches low before God. The posture is the one of deep, humble prayer, and some have noted it resembles a woman in childbirth in the ancient world: Elijah is laboring in prayer for what God has promised. James makes this the model: "he prayed again, and heaven gave rain" (James 5:18).
  • "Go again,' seven times" (v43). God promised rain, but Elijah still prays, and keeps praying. Promise does not replace prayer. It invites it. Seven, the number of completeness, shows persistence to the end.
  • "A little cloud like a man's hand is rising from the sea" (v44). From the top of Carmel there is a wide view west over the Mediterranean, where Israel's rain comes from. A tiny cloud on the horizon is enough for Elijah. "Prepare your chariot and go down, lest the rain stop you." The Kishon plain turns to deep mud in heavy rain. That is how Sisera's chariots were swept away (Judg 5:21).
  • "The heavens grew black with clouds and wind, and there was a great rain" (v45). The drought ends not because Baal woke up but because the LORD spoke. The God who answered by fire also answers by water.
  • "Ahab rode and went to Jezreel" (v45). Jezreel was Ahab's second capital, a royal residence in the fertile valley about 17 miles from the traditional contest site. It is where Jezebel was waiting.
  • "He gathered up his garment and ran before Ahab" (v46). Elijah tucked his long robe into his belt, as men did for running or hard work. Royal runners commonly went ahead of a king's chariot (1 Sam 8:11; 2 Sam 15:1). Elijah outruns the horses to the city gate. The "hand of the LORD" was on him. It may be a gesture of respect to the king, offering Ahab a fresh start, as JFB thinks, or a herald going ahead to announce God's victory. Chapter 19 will show that Jezebel had no intention of being won over.
  • Commentators: Henry: "we must continue earnest in prayer, and not faint or give over," and "God will strengthen his people for every service to which his commandments and providence call them." Guzik, following Meyer, notes that the man who stood so boldly before Ahab bowed so low before God, and that God's promises call us to pray, not to sit back. He sees the small cloud as a picture of the small first signs of revival and passes on Spurgeon's encouragement to watch for them. JFB explains royal runners as a common sight in the East and reads Elijah's run as loyalty to the king, while noting the reform that followed did not last.

Pointing to Christ

Carmel asks the question every age must answer: "If the LORD is God, follow him." Elijah stood alone, rebuilt a broken altar for all twelve tribes, and asked God to turn the people's hearts back. Fire fell on the sacrifice instead of on the people. Jesus is the greater prophet who came "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17) and beyond him. On the cross, the fire of God's judgment fell on the true sacrifice, offered at the hour of the afternoon offering (Matt 27:46; Acts 3:1), so that sinners could be turned back to God. He did not call down fire on his enemies (Luke 9:55). He took it himself, and then poured out his Spirit like rain on a dry land (Acts 2:17; Isa 44:3).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes that Elijah's challenge exposes the impossibility of spiritual neutrality. "How long halt you between two opinions?" is not a question about choosing between two equal options — it is an exposure of the absurdity of trying to serve both Yahweh and Baal. Guzik notes that the twelve barrels of water represent Elijah's absolute confidence in God — he made the miracle harder to demonstrate that nothing is too hard for the LORD. The brevity of Elijah's prayer (63 words in Hebrew) versus the hours of Baal worship shows that true prayer depends on the God who hears, not the volume or duration of the one who prays.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • No Neutral Ground Between Two Loyalties (v.21) — Elijah confronts the people for wavering between the Lord and Baal instead of committing fully to one or the other, and they have no answer for him. Where in your own life are you trying to hold a middle position that God is actually calling you to abandon?
  • Making the Miracle Harder on Purpose (vv.33-35) — Elijah drenches the sacrifice and altar with precious water during a drought, removing any possible doubt that the coming fire is anything but God's own doing. Are you tempted to make things easier for God to act, rather than trusting Him fully with a situation that looks genuinely impossible?
  • A Short Prayer Aimed at God's Glory (v.37) — After the prophets of Baal spend hours screaming and cutting themselves, Elijah offers a brief prayer asking only that the people would know the Lord is God. Is your own prayer more focused on being heard at length or on God's name being made known?
  • An Immediate, Unified Response to God's Answer (v.39) — The moment fire falls from heaven, the entire watching crowd falls on their faces and declares that the Lord alone is God. When God makes Himself unmistakably clear in your life, how quickly do you respond with that same wholehearted acknowledgment?
  • Persisting in Prayer Even After the Promise (vv.42-44) — Elijah already knows God has promised rain, yet he keeps praying and sending his servant to look seven times before the first cloud appears. Do you keep praying and watching for what God has promised, or do you stop seeking once you believe the outcome is settled?
