1Some time later, the king of the Ammonites died and was succeeded by his son Hanun.

2And David said, “I will show kindness to Hanun son of Nahash, just as his father showed kindness to me.” So David sent some of his servants to console Hanun concerning his father. But when they arrived in the land of the Ammonites,

3the princes of the Ammonites said to Hanun their lord, “Just because David has sent you comforters, do you really believe he is showing respect for your father? Has not David instead sent his servants to explore the city, spy it out, and overthrow it?”

4So Hanun took David’s servants, shaved off half of each man’s beard, cut off their garments at the hips, and sent them away.

5When this was reported to David, he sent messengers to meet the men, since they had been thoroughly humiliated. The king told them, “Stay in Jericho until your beards have grown back, and then return.”

6When the Ammonites realized that they had become a stench to David, they hired twenty thousand Aramean foot soldiers from Beth-rehob and Zoba, as well as a thousand men from the king of Maacah and twelve thousand men from Tob.

7On hearing this, David sent Joab and the entire army of mighty men.

8The Ammonites marched out and arrayed themselves for battle at the entrance of the city gate, while the Arameans of Zobah and Rehob and the men of Tob and Maacah were by themselves in the open country.

9When Joab saw the battle lines before him and behind him, he selected some of the best men of Israel and arrayed them against the Arameans.

10And he placed the rest of the troops under the command of his brother Abishai, who arrayed them against the Ammonites.

11“If the Arameans are too strong for me,” said Joab, “then you will come to my rescue. And if the Ammonites are too strong for you, then I will come to your rescue.

12Be strong and let us fight bravely for our people and for the cities of our God. May the LORD do what is good in His sight.”

13So Joab and his troops advanced to fight the Arameans, who fled before him.

14When the Ammonites saw that the Arameans had fled, they too fled before Abishai, and they entered the city. So Joab returned from fighting against the Ammonites and came to Jerusalem.

15When the Arameans saw that they had been defeated by Israel, they regrouped.

16Hadadezer sent messengers to bring more Arameans from beyond the Euphrates, and they came to Helam with Shobach the commander of Hadadezer’s army leading them.

17When this was reported to David, he gathered all Israel, crossed the Jordan, and went to Helam. Then the Arameans arrayed themselves against David and fought against him.

18But the Arameans fled before Israel, and David killed seven hundred charioteers and forty thousand foot soldiers. He also struck down Shobach the commander of their army, who died there.

19When all the kings who were subject to Hadadezer saw that they had been defeated by Israel, they made peace with Israel and became subject to them. So the Arameans were afraid to help the Ammonites anymore.

Summary

When the Ammonite king Nahash dies, David sends envoys to console his son Hanun, but Hanun's officials convince him the men are spies, so he shaves off half their beards, cuts their garments at the hip, and sends them home in humiliation (vv. 1-5). Realizing they have angered David, the Ammonites hire Aramean mercenaries; Joab leads Israel's army and splits his forces to fight the Ammonites and Arameans on two fronts at once, putting both armies to flight (vv. 9-14). Hadadezer regroups the Arameans for a second battle, but David personally crosses the Jordan and leads Israel to a decisive victory, after which the Arameans make peace with Israel and refuse to help Ammon again (vv. 17-19).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Nathan and Gad (1 Chronicles 29:29). Originally one book with 1 Samuel in the Hebrew Bible. The book covers David's entire reign — from his lament over Saul's death to the purchase of the threshing floor for the future Temple (approximately 1010-970 BC).
Historical Context: Chapter 10 records the war with Ammon and Syria that sets the stage for David's great sin in chapter 11. David sends ambassadors to comfort Hanun, the new Ammonite king, after his father's death — an act of diplomatic kindness (hesed). But Hanun's advisors convince him that David's envoys are spies. Hanun humiliates them by shaving half their beards and cutting their garments to expose them — a devastating insult in the ancient Near East. This provokes war. The Ammonites hire Syrian (Aramean) mercenaries — 33,000 troops from multiple kingdoms. Joab faces a two-front battle and divides his forces, entrusting one wing to Abishai with the memorable words: "Be of good courage, and let us play the men for our people, and for the cities of our God: and the LORD do that which seemeth him good" (v.12). Both enemies flee. The Syrians regroup with reinforcements from beyond the Euphrates, but David personally leads Israel to a decisive victory at Helam, killing 700 charioteers and 40,000 horsemen. The Syrian coalition collapses permanently. This chapter is crucial narratively because the Ammonite war continues into chapter 11 — and it is during this ongoing siege of Rabbah that David stays home in Jerusalem and falls into sin with Bathsheba.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: Ambassadors sent in genuine kindness reinterpreted by advisors as spies sent to search out the city, beards shaved and garments cut at the hips as a humiliation calculated to provoke war rather than merely insult, Joab facing enemies in front and behind and dividing his best troops toward the greater threat, a speech to Abishai pairing mutual support with a trust that leaves the outcome to the LORD, and a Syrian empire's full reinforcement still ending in defeat that leaves Ammon isolated for the siege still to come
David's Kindness Rejected (vv.1-5): Nahash king of Ammon dies, and David sends condolences to his son Hanun — honoring a relationship of mutual "kindness" (hesed) between their houses. The nature of Nahash's kindness to David is not specified — perhaps he supported David during his conflict with Saul (the enemy of my enemy). Hanun's princes poison his mind: David's ambassadors are spies sent to "search the city, and to spy it out, and to overthrow it." Hanun believes them and humiliates David's servants: shaving half their beards (destroying their masculine honor) and cutting their garments at the hips (exposing them shamefully). In ancient Near Eastern culture, this was an extreme insult — equivalent to a declaration of war. David shows compassion to his humiliated men: "Tarry at Jericho until your beards be grown" — allowing them to recover their dignity privately.
The Two-Front Battle (vv.6-14): The Ammonites realize they have made David their enemy ("they stank before David" — KJV) and hire massive Syrian reinforcements: 20,000 from Beth-rehob and Zobah, 1,000 from Maacah, and 12,000 from Tob — 33,000 mercenaries plus the Ammonite army. Joab faces enemies both in front (Ammonites at the city gate) and behind (Syrians in the open field). His tactical response is brilliant: he takes Israel's best troops against the Syrians (the greater threat) and gives Abishai the rest against the Ammonites. His speech to Abishai (vv.11-12) combines practical strategy (mutual support) with theological trust: "the LORD do that which seemeth him good." Joab fights with skill but leaves the outcome to God. The Syrians flee before Joab; seeing this, the Ammonites flee before Abishai into their city. Joab returns to Jerusalem — the siege of Rabbah will resume later (ch.11).
The Decisive Syrian Defeat (vv.15-19): The Syrians refuse to accept defeat. Hadadezer summons reinforcements from beyond the Euphrates — the full might of the Aramean empire. David personally leads all Israel across the Jordan to Helam. The battle is decisive: David kills 700 charioteers and 40,000 horsemen and mortally wounds Shobach, the Syrian commander. All of Hadadezer's vassal kings make peace with Israel and become subjects. The Syrian threat is permanently eliminated: "the Syrians feared to help the children of Ammon any more." This leaves Ammon isolated for the siege that follows in chapter 11.

Setting: David's reign in Jerusalem, around 990 BC. Chapter 8 summed up David's wars in a few lines, including victories over Ammon's neighbors, Hadadezer of Zobah and the Syrians (8:3–8, 12). This chapter tells one of those wars in full, and it does so for a reason: the war it starts is still going on when David stays home in chapter 11 and sins with Bathsheba. The Ammonites lived east of the Jordan, descendants of Lot (Gen 19:38), with their capital at Rabbah, modern Amman in Jordan. Their king Nahash is the man who once threatened to gouge out the right eye of every man in Jabesh-gilead until Saul rescued the town (1 Sam 11:1–11). Chapter 9 showed David's hesed, his loyal kindness, gladly received by Mephibosheth. This chapter opens with the same kindness, offered to a foreign king and thrown back in his face.

David Defeats Ammon and Syria (vv1–19)

  • "After this the king of the Ammonites died" (v1). "After this" is a loose link, not a timestamp. Chapters 8 to 10 are arranged by theme more than strict order. The Tyndale notes raise the puzzle that Hadadezer, beaten in 8:3–8, is back in force here (v16). Either he rebuilt and rebelled again, or chapter 10 tells in detail a war that chapter 8 only summarized. Many scholars prefer the second view.
  • "I will deal loyally with Hanun the son of Nahash, as his father dealt loyally with me" (v2). Both verbs translate the Hebrew phrase "to do hesed," the same covenant kindness David showed Mephibosheth (9:1, 3, 7). We are not told what Nahash did for David. A likely guess is that Nahash, Saul's enemy, helped David while Saul was hunting him, perhaps when David sheltered with the king of Moab nearby (1 Sam 22:3–4). Later, during Absalom's revolt, "Shobi the son of Nahash from Rabbah" brought David food and beds (17:27–29), so at least one son of Nahash remembered the friendship. The name Hanun comes from the Hebrew root for "gracious" or "favored," an irony in a man who answers grace with insult.
  • "Sent by his servants to console him concerning his father" (v2). Official condolences between kings were normal diplomacy. They were also a way of confirming a treaty with a new ruler. David's gesture is both personal and political, and nothing in the text suggests it was insincere.
  • "Has not David sent his servants to you to search the city and to spy it out and to overthrow it?" (v3). Hanun's princes read the worst into the kindness. They had some reason to be nervous: David had just conquered Moab, Edom and the Syrians (ch 8). But the narrator gives no hint that David meant harm. Suspicion is the princes' own idea, and Hanun, new and insecure, takes their advice, as Rehoboam later takes the bad advice of his young men (1 Kings 12:8–11).
  • "Shaved off half the beard of each" (v4). In Israel and its neighbors a full beard marked a free adult man, and shaving it was a sign of mourning, grief or disgrace (Isa 15:2; Jer 48:37). Israelite men were told not to mar the edges of their beards (Lev 19:27). Shaving half made the shame visible and impossible to hide or fix. These were envoys, and in the ancient world, as now, to insult an ambassador was to insult the king who sent him.
  • "Cut off their garments in the middle, at their hips" (v4). The long robes were cut short to expose them from the waist down. This is how conquered prisoners were paraded into exile, "naked and barefoot, with buttocks uncovered" (Isa 20:4). Hanun treated David's peace mission as defeated captives.
  • "Remain at Jericho until your beards have grown and then return" (v5). David's first concern is for the men, not his own wounded pride. Jericho lay on the road from Ammon to Jerusalem just after the Jordan crossing. It had been under Joshua's curse (Josh 6:26) and was not rebuilt as a city until Ahab's day (1 Kings 16:34), but people lived around its great spring. It was a quiet place to wait out the shame, away from the court.
  • "They had become a stench to David" (v6). The Hebrew verb bāʾash means to stink, and so to become hateful or odious. It is a vivid idiom used elsewhere for being in bad odor with someone (Gen 34:30; 1 Sam 13:4; 27:12). The Ammonites know exactly what they have done. Rather than apologize, they reach for their wallets.
  • Hiring the Syrians (v6). The Ammonites buy an army of 33,000: 20,000 from Beth-rehob and Zobah, 1,000 with the king of Maacah and 12,000 from Tob. The parallel in 1 Chronicles 19:6–7 adds the price, 1,000 talents of silver, perhaps 30 tons or more. These were Aramean (Syrian) states to the north and northeast. Beth-rehob was near Dan at Israel's northern edge (Judg 18:28). Zobah was Hadadezer's kingdom in the Beqaa Valley region. Maacah lay south of Mount Hermon. Tob was the land east of the Jordan where Jephthah once gathered a band of outlaws (Judg 11:3).
  • "He sent Joab and all the host of the mighty men" (v7). David does not go himself this time. He sends his nephew and army commander Joab with the professional core of the army, the "mighty men" whose deeds are listed in 23:8–39.
  • "The battle was set against him both in front and in the rear" (vv8–9). The Ammonites stood at their city gate. The Chronicler places the camp near Medeba (1 Chr 19:7), a town some 20 miles southwest of Rabbah, though many think the gate in view here is Rabbah's own. The Syrians waited in the open country. When Joab advanced on the city, he found the hired army behind him. It was a trap with Israel in the middle.
  • Joab splits his army (vv9–10). He takes the best troops against the Syrians, the more dangerous professional force, and gives the rest to his brother Abishai to hold the Ammonites. Then he arranges mutual help: "If the Syrians are too strong for me, then you shall help me, but if the Ammonites are too strong for you, then I will come and help you" (v11). Each half watches the other's back.
  • "Be of good courage, and let us be courageous for our people, and for the cities of our God" (v12). The Hebrew doubles the verb "be strong" (ḥāzaq), the word God spoke to Joshua: "Be strong and courageous" (Josh 1:6–9). Speeches like this before battle were expected (Deut 20:2–4). Joab gives his men a reason bigger than themselves: their people, and towns that belong to God.
  • "And may the LORD do what seems good to him" (v12). This is the best line Joab speaks in the whole book. He plans carefully, fights hard and then leaves the result with God. Eli used almost the same words when he accepted God's judgment (1 Sam 3:18), and Jonathan had the same spirit: "nothing can hinder the LORD from saving by many or by few" (1 Sam 14:6). Joab is a hard, often ruthless man (3:27; ch 18), and this verse shows the faith that sat alongside it.
  • "They fled before him" (v13). Hired soldiers had little reason to die for Ammon. When the Syrians ran, the Ammonites ran too, back inside their walls (v14). Joab does not try a siege. JFB suggests the season was too far gone. He goes home, and the city is left untaken. That loose end is picked up in 11:1: "In the spring of the year, the time when kings go out to battle… David sent Joab… and they besieged Rabbah."
  • "Hadadezer sent and brought out the Syrians who were beyond the Euphrates" (v16). The first defeat was hired troops. Now the overlord himself, Hadadezer, mobilizes his whole network, including Arameans from Mesopotamia, beyond the great river. The title of Psalm 60 mentions David's wars with "Aram-naharaim and Aram-zobah," which may connect to this campaign. Helam is unknown. Most place it somewhere in the north of Gilead or Bashan, east of the Jordan.
  • "He gathered all Israel together and crossed the Jordan" (v17). This time the threat is big enough that the king leads in person, with the whole national army. When David goes out, God gives the victory. That detail matters for the next chapter, where David stays behind.
  • "The men of 700 chariots, and 40,000 horsemen" (v18). The parallel reads "7,000 chariots and 40,000 foot soldiers" (1 Chr 19:18). Numbers in ancient manuscripts were easily miscopied, and many think one of the two texts has suffered a copying slip. Others suggest that each writer counted a different part of the force, for example chariot crews and the men who rode with them. The general is Shobach, spelled Shophach in Chronicles. Killing the commander broke the coalition.
  • "They made peace with Israel and became subject to them" (v19). The smaller kings under Hadadezer switch sides and become David's vassals. God had promised Abraham's descendants land "to the great river, the river Euphrates" (Gen 15:18), and under David Israel's influence reaches that far for the first time. "The Syrians were afraid to save the Ammonites anymore." Ammon is left alone with its insult, and with Joab still to come.
  • Commentators: Matthew Henry turns the princes' suspicion into a proverb: "False men are ready to think others as false as themselves," and "Unfounded suspicion argues a wicked mind." Guzik makes the same point in his own way, that those who deceive are quick to assume others are deceiving them. JFB thinks Ammon's fear came from knowing that God's law excluded them from the assembly (Deut 23:3–6) and that David kept that law, though the text does not say so. Henry also mentions a view that David brought the insult on himself by courting an enemy of Israel, but he does not press it. On v5 Henry draws a gentle lesson: unjust reproaches, like the shaved beards, "after awhile… will wear off of themselves." All three admire Joab's speech. Henry sums it up: "let God's work be done by us, and then God's will be done concerning us," and he reads v11 as a picture of believers helping one another under temptation (Luke 22:32). Guzik observes that Joab plans for the strain of battle but never for defeat. He also sees the chapter as a quiet warning before chapter 11: the army needed David in the field, and God blessed him when he went, yet the next spring he stayed home. JFB, on v16, has David crossing the Jordan by forced marches to surprise Shobach. That is a reasonable picture, but it goes beyond what the text says.

Pointing to Christ

A king sends messengers of peace and comfort, and they are mocked, stripped and sent away in shame. Matthew Henry saw the same story in the gospel: the Son of David sent his servants and then his Son to his own people, and "they treated them shamefully" (Matt 21:33–41). Paul calls Christ's messengers "ambassadors for Christ," with God "making his appeal through us" (2 Cor 5:20). Kindness from the true King can be refused, and refusing it is a fearful thing. But the chapter also shows that the King cares for his humiliated servants and does not leave their shame as the last word. The same Jesus "endured the cross, despising the shame" (Heb 12:2) and gives his people honor in the end.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik notes that this chapter shows David's kindness being misinterpreted — a reminder that good motives do not guarantee good reception. He emphasizes Joab's speech (v.12) as a model of combining human effort with divine trust, and notes that this ongoing war with Ammon is the direct context for David's sin in chapter 11.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Kindness That Gets Misread (v.2) — David's genuine condolence to Hanun is real hesed, yet it gets reinterpreted as a threat. Has a sincere gesture of yours ever been thrown back at you as something suspicious, and how did you respond?
  • Paranoia Turns an Ally Into an Enemy (v.4) — Hanun's advisors convince him to humiliate David's men based purely on suspicion, manufacturing a war that didn't need to happen. Whose counsel are you listening to that tends to project fear onto situations rather than see them clearly?
  • Fighting Hard, Trusting Fully (v.12) — Joab tells Abishai to be courageous for their people and then leaves the outcome to whatever the Lord sees fit. Where in your life do you need that same combination — full effort paired with a released grip on the result?
  • Victory as Its Own Danger (v.19) — This chapter closes with Israel's enemies thoroughly defeated and at peace, setting up the ease in which David will fall in the very next chapter. Are you more spiritually alert during hardship than you are in seasons when everything is going well?
