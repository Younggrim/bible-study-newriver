Summary

Jesus warns against judging others hypocritically, using the image of a speck and a beam to show that self-examination must come before correcting someone else's fault, and cautions against giving what is holy to those who will trample it (vv. 1-6). He teaches his disciples to ask, seek, and knock, trusting that the Father gives good gifts to those who ask him, and summarizes the Law and Prophets in the command to treat others as they wish to be treated (vv. 7-12). Jesus calls his listeners to enter through the narrow gate rather than the easy, crowded road to destruction, and warns them to identify false prophets by their fruit rather than their appearance (vv. 13-20). He cautions that not everyone who calls him Lord will enter the kingdom of heaven, only those who do the Father's will, and closes with the parable of two builders — one wise, whose house on rock survives the storm, and one foolish, whose house on sand collapses (vv. 21-27). The crowds are astonished at his teaching, because he taught with authority rather than as their scribes did (vv. 28-29).

Authorship & Background

Author: Matthew (see Chapter 1 notes for full authorship details).
Historical Context: Chapter 7 concludes the Sermon on the Mount with some of Jesus' most famous and most misunderstood teachings — on judging, prayer, the narrow gate, false prophets, and the two foundations. The sermon ends with the crowd "astonished at his doctrine" because He taught "as one having authority" — not quoting other rabbis but speaking with His own divine authority.
Classification: Gospel — Narrative and Discourse
Key Themes: judging forbidden on the ground of reciprocity rather than relativism, with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again, a mote and a beam turned into a joke with a serious end, a rule stated positively for once rather than negatively, all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them, a test for false prophets based on produce rather than doctrine, ye shall know them by their fruits, and a closing parable of two builders facing the identical storm, the difference between them lying entirely in the foundation rather than the effort
Judge Not (vv.1-6): Judge not, that ye be not judged, and the reason given is reciprocity rather than relativism, with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again. Then the mote and the beam, which is a joke with a serious end: the objection is not to correcting a brother but to correcting him while unable to see. The instruction is sequential, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye, and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote. And immediately after it a saying that limits the whole passage, give not that which is holy unto the dogs, so this is not a prohibition on discernment.
Ask, Seek, Knock (vv.7-12): Three imperatives with three promises, and then the argument from ordinary fatherhood, what man is there of you, whom if his son ask bread, will he give him a stone? The premise about human nature is conceded in passing, if ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children. Then the sentence Matthew places as the summary of the whole sermon so far, all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them, with the note that this is the law and the prophets. The rule is stated positively, which is what distinguishes it from its many negative predecessors.
The Strait Gate and the False Prophets (vv.13-23): Two gates, two ways, and the numbers are given, many there be which go in thereat, and few there be that find it. Then false prophets, and the test offered is not doctrine but produce, ye shall know them by their fruits, with the observation that grapes do not come off thorns. The section ends on the most disquieting passage in the sermon: people who called him Lord, prophesied, cast out devils and did many wonderful works, and are told I never knew you. Not their works but their knowledge of him is what is at issue, and the sentence between the two is the criterion, he that doeth the will of my Father which is in heaven.
The House on the Rock (vv.24-29): The closing parable has two builders and one storm, and the difference between them is not effort or materials but foundation. Whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them, is likened unto a wise man which built his house upon a rock. Both houses face the same rain, floods and wind. Then Matthew's own note on the effect, and it is about authority rather than content, the people were astonished at his doctrine, for he taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes.

Deep Dive

Setting: The last part of the Sermon on the Mount, on a hillside in Galilee early in Jesus' ministry, around AD 28. Chapter 5 showed what kingdom righteousness looks like, and chapter 6 turned to motive: giving, prayer and fasting done for the Father, not for an audience, and a heart that seeks his kingdom first instead of worrying. Chapter 7 turns outward to how disciples treat others (judging, discernment, the Golden Rule) and then closes the whole sermon with a series of warnings. Each one puts two options side by side: two gates, two trees, two kinds of disciples, two builders. There is no neutral ground. The chapter ends with the crowds stunned by the authority with which Jesus spoke.

Judging Others (vv1–6)

  • "Judge not, that you be not judged" (v1). This may be the most quoted verse in the Bible, and it is often taken to mean that no one may ever say anything is wrong. The chapter itself rules that out: a few verses later Jesus tells disciples to spot false prophets by their fruit (vv15–20), and in 18:15–17 he tells them to confront a brother's sin. What he forbids is the harsh, fault-finding spirit that sets itself up in God's seat, condemns quickly, and looks for the worst in others (Rom 14:4; James 4:11–12).
  • "With the measure you use it will be measured to you" (v2). A "measure" (metron) was the scoop or container a merchant used to measure grain. A merchant who used a short measure on his customers will have the same short measure used on him. The passive "you will be judged" most likely means by God, at the final judgment. It echoes the Beatitude "Blessed are the merciful, for they shall receive mercy" (5:7) and the line in the Lord's Prayer about forgiving as forgiven (6:12, 14–15). Luke's parallel adds "good measure, pressed down, shaken together, running over" (Luke 6:38).
  • "The speck… the log" (vv3–4). The "speck" is a tiny chip of wood or a bit of chaff. The "log" is a beam, the kind that held up a roof. Jesus grew up as the son of a carpenter (13:55), and the picture is deliberately absurd: a man with a roof beam sticking out of his face, squinting to perform delicate eye surgery on someone else. The humor makes the point stick.
  • "You hypocrite" (v5). Hypokritēs was the Greek word for a stage actor, one who spoke behind a mask. It is the word Jesus used three times in chapter 6 for those who give, pray and fast for show. The fault-finder is acting a part: the righteous critic, while his own life goes unexamined.
  • "First take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly" (v5). This verse does not end with "and then leave your brother alone." It ends with helping him. The goal is that the speck does come out, but by someone who has dealt honestly with his own sin first and comes humbly (Gal 6:1). Self-judgment comes before any word to another.
  • "Do not give dogs what is holy, and do not throw your pearls before pigs" (v6). "What is holy" probably pictures meat offered as a sacrifice, which only priests could eat (Lev 22:10–16). Throwing it to the wild dogs that scavenged village streets would be unthinkable. Pearls were among the most valuable things in the ancient world (13:45–46), and pigs, unclean animals to a Jew, would not know them from pebbles. They would trample them and, finding nothing to eat, turn on the giver.
  • How v6 fits. The verse balances vv1–5. Disciples must not be harsh judges, but they must not be naive either. Some people meet the holy things of God with settled scorn and hostility, and there comes a point to stop pressing (10:14; Acts 13:46). The Tyndale notes list the main readings: some think the dogs and pigs refer to Gentiles, as Jews sometimes called them; others to hostile religious leaders; others to hardened unbelievers in general. The last fits best, since Matthew ends with a command to take the gospel to all nations (28:19). The earliest known use of the verse, in the Didache (a church manual from about AD 100), applies it to keeping the Lord's Supper for the baptized.
  • Commentators: All three agree that Jesus forbids a censorious spirit, not all judgment. JFB defines what is condemned as "that disposition to look unfavorably on the character and actions of others," and Henry says "Our own sins ought to appear greater to us than the same sins in others." Guzik points to David, who angrily condemned the rich man in Nathan's story without seeing that the story was about him (2 Sam 12). On v6, Henry writes, "Throw a pearl to a swine, and he will resent it, as if you threw a stone at him," and applies the verse to reproof and to the sacraments. JFB adds a fair warning against using the verse as an excuse to write people off and stop doing them good.

Ask, and It Will Be Given (vv7–11)

  • "Ask… seek… knock" (v7). The three verbs climb: asking for something, searching for it, then knocking at a closed door. In Greek all three are present imperatives, which describe ongoing action: keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking. The same point is made in Luke, where this saying follows the story of a friend who keeps knocking at midnight until the neighbor gets up (Luke 11:5–10).
  • Why prayer comes here. After the high demands of the sermon and the warning about judging, the question is how anyone can live this way. The answer is not more effort but asking. The God who sees in secret (6:6) and knows what his children need (6:8, 32) is the one to go to.
  • "Everyone who asks receives" (v8). This is a promise to disciples, those who call God "Father" (v11), and it is not a blank check. The Lord's Prayer has already shaped what they ask for: God's name, his kingdom, his will, daily bread, forgiveness and protection (6:9–13). Elsewhere the New Testament adds that prayer is made in faith (James 1:6), according to God's will (1 John 5:14), and not from selfish motives (James 4:3).
  • Bread and a stone, a fish and a serpent (vv9–10). The everyday comparisons are pointed. A round flat loaf looked a bit like a stone, and certain eel-like fish of the Sea of Galilee looked a bit like snakes. No father would trick his hungry child with something that looked like food but could break his teeth or bite him. Luke adds an egg and a scorpion (Luke 11:12).
  • "If you then, who are evil" (v11). Jesus states human sinfulness almost in passing, as a plain fact. Even sinful parents give good things to their children. The argument runs from lesser to greater: "how much more will your Father who is in heaven."
  • "Good things to those who ask him" (v11). Luke's version reads "give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him" (Luke 11:13). Luke names the best of God's good gifts. Matthew keeps the broader promise. Both say the Father's heart is generous.
  • Commentators: Guzik, Henry and JFB all read the three verbs as growing intensity. JFB adds that each fits a different need: "we knock for that from which we feel ourselves shut out." Henry writes that "those that would be rich in grace, must betake themselves to the poor trade of begging," and on vv9–11, "Denials in love are better than grants in anger," a reminder that a wise Father does not grant every request. Guzik stresses that persistence in prayer is not about wearing down a reluctant God, but about our hearts being brought into line with his.

The Golden Rule (v12)

  • "So whatever you wish that others would do to you, do also to them" (v12). The "so" ties this back to what came before: since the Father is so generous to his children, his children are to be generous to others. The name "Golden Rule" came much later, but it has stuck.
  • Positive, not negative. Similar sayings existed. The Jewish book of Tobit (4:15) says "What you hate, do not do to anyone." The Talmud tells of Rabbi Hillel, a generation before Jesus, summing up the Law to a Gentile: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor." Confucius taught a similar negative rule. Jesus puts it positively, and that changes everything. The negative form can be kept by doing nothing. The positive form sends a disciple looking for good to do, as the Samaritan did for the man by the road (Luke 10:30–37).
  • "For this is the Law and the Prophets" (v12). This phrase bookends the main body of the sermon. In 5:17 Jesus said he came not to abolish "the Law or the Prophets" but to fulfill them. Now, after showing what that fulfilment looks like, he sums it up. Later he will say the whole Law and Prophets hang on loving God and loving neighbor (22:37–40), and Paul says the same (Rom 13:8–10; Gal 5:14).
  • Commentators: Henry calls it "the golden rule of equity," and JFB the summary of "all relative duty." JFB adds a sharp point: it does not mean doing whatever one might selfishly wish for, but treating others with the fairness one would want if places were switched. Guzik notes that the positive form is far more demanding than Hillel's, and that no one keeps it perfectly, which drives a person back to grace.

The Narrow and Wide Gates (vv13–14)

  • "Enter by the narrow gate" (v13). From here to the end, Jesus calls for a decision. The Old Testament often set out two ways: "I have set before you life and death" (Deut 30:19), the righteous and the wicked of Psalm 1, and "the way of life and the way of death" (Jer 21:8). The Didache opens the same way: "There are two ways."
  • The pictures. A gate and a road go together. One gate is wide and its road easy, the Greek word meaning broad and roomy. The other gate is narrow and its road "hard," a word (thlibō) related to pressure and affliction, the same family as the word for tribulation. The narrow way is not hard because grace is stingy. It is narrow because it goes through one door, Jesus (John 10:9; 14:6), and because following him means taking up a cross (16:24).
  • "Those who enter by it are many… those who find it are few" (vv13–14). When someone asked Jesus "will those who are saved be few?" he did not answer with a number. He said, "Strive to enter through the narrow door" (Luke 13:23–24). The point is urgency, not statistics. The crowd is not a safe guide.
  • Commentators: Henry treats this as "the necessity of strictness in conversation" (that is, in conduct), and says plainly that those on the way to heaven are few compared with the rest. JFB stresses the self-denial needed to enter. Guzik passes on an old picture from Adam Clarke of a small door set within a great city gate, which only one person could pass through at a time, stooping.

A Tree and Its Fruit (vv15–20)

  • "Beware of false prophets" (v15). The two ways need guides, and some guides point the wrong way. Israel's history was full of prophets who said "Peace, peace" when there was no peace (Jer 6:14; 23:16–17). Jesus warns that they will come again (24:11, 24), and the apostles found it so (Acts 20:29–30; 2 Pet 2:1; 1 John 4:1).
  • "In sheep's clothing but inwardly are ravenous wolves" (v15). The picture is of a wolf disguised as one of the flock, getting close enough to devour. Ezekiel used the same image for Jerusalem's corrupt leaders: "like wolves tearing the prey" (Ezek 22:27). It is not about prophets wearing woolly cloaks. It is about appearance and reality.
  • "You will recognize them by their fruits" (vv16, 20). The phrase begins and ends the section. Grapes and figs were staple fruit in Israel. Thornbushes and thistles were weeds farmers fought every year (Gen 3:18). What grows on a plant shows what kind of plant it is. "Fruit" includes a prophet's character and conduct, but also what his teaching produces in the people who follow it. Deuteronomy gave Israel tests: a prophet who leads people after other gods is false even if his signs come true (Deut 13:1–5), and one whose words fail is false (Deut 18:21–22).
  • "Every tree that does not bear good fruit is cut down and thrown into the fire" (v19). These are almost exactly John the Baptist's words (3:10). Jesus and John preach the same judgment.
  • Healthy and diseased trees (vv17–18). The words mean good and rotten. Jesus is not saying no believer ever sins. He is saying that the root shows itself over time. A changed life flows from a changed heart, which is why he says in 12:33–35 "the tree is known by its fruit."
  • Commentators: JFB describes false prophets as having "a bland, gentle, plausible exterior" and compares them to those Ezekiel denounced. Henry says they may be known by the drift and effects of their doctrines, so the test covers both life and teaching. Guzik agrees, listing a prophet's manner of life, the content of his message, and what his message does to its hearers.

I Never Knew You (vv21–23)

  • "Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord'" (v21). The doubled title shows warmth and zeal. These people are not unbelievers in the usual sense. They use the right words. But words without obedience do not bring entry into the kingdom. "The will of my Father" has just been laid out in the sermon.
  • "On that day" (v22). This is the day of judgment, rooted in the prophets' "day of the LORD" (Joel 2:31; Zeph 1:14). The startling thing is who is on the judge's seat. A young teacher from Nazareth says that on the last day people will plead their case to him, and he will decide. No rabbi talked like this.
  • "Did we not prophesy in your name… cast out demons… do many mighty works?" (v22). They point to impressive ministry. Jesus does not deny they did these things. Spiritual power and public success are not the same as knowing Christ. Judas was sent out with the Twelve to heal and cast out demons (10:1–4), and Balaam prophesied truly (Num 24).
  • "I never knew you" (v23). In the Bible "know" often means a personal relationship, not just information (Amos 3:2; John 10:14; 2 Tim 2:19). The word "never" matters: these were not people who were once known and then lost. There never was a relationship.
  • "Depart from me, you workers of lawlessness" (v23). Jesus quotes Psalm 6:8, "Depart from me, all you workers of evil." "Lawlessness" (anomia) means living as if God's law does not apply. It is ironic: people who claimed to do great things in Jesus' name ignored his words. Matthew uses the same word for the weeds pulled up at the end of the age (13:41) and the Pharisees' inner life (23:28).
  • Commentators: JFB puts v23 memorably: "Our acquaintance was not broken off--there never was any." Henry warns that "a man might cast devils out of others, and yet have a devil, nay, be a devil himself," and Guzik agrees that "never knew" shows these people were never saved, not that they lost their salvation. The Tyndale notes add that the decisive issue is obedience, which flows from knowing Christ.

Build Your House on the Rock (vv24–27)

  • "Everyone then who hears these words of mine and does them" (v24). "These words of mine" refers to the whole sermon. Jesus has spent three chapters teaching and ends by saying that the response to his words decides everything. That is the language God uses of his own word in the Old Testament (Deut 28:1–2; Isa 40:8).
  • Two builders. Both hear. Both build. From the street, the houses may look the same. The difference is hidden underground: one on rock, one on sand. Luke adds that the wise man "dug deep" (Luke 6:48).
  • "The rain fell, and the floods came, and the winds blew" (v25). In the land of Israel, dry wadis (stream beds) look like good flat building sites in summer. When the winter rains come, flash floods can turn them into torrents in minutes. A house built on the sandy floor of a wadi would be swept away, while one set on bedrock at the edge would stand. The listeners knew this from experience.
  • What the storm stands for. The storm hits both houses alike. It pictures the trials of life that test what a person is built on, and above all the final judgment (Ezek 13:10–14, where God's storm knocks down a wall daubed with whitewash). The Tyndale notes see the storm mainly as judgment.
  • "And it fell, and great was the fall of it" (v27). The sermon that began with "Blessed are…" ends with a crash. There is no softening, no closing summary. The last words leave the hearer standing with the choice.
  • Commentators: Henry says "To hear Christ is not barely to give him the hearing, but to obey him," and that the hopes "built upon Christ the Rock will stand." JFB says hearing and doing his words is the rock, and mere profession the sand, adding that the audience knew sudden Eastern storms first-hand. Guzik points out that the difference between the houses only shows when the storm comes.

The Authority of Jesus (vv28–29)

  • "When Jesus finished these sayings" (v28). Matthew uses this formula five times, each closing one of Jesus' five great teaching blocks (7:28; 11:1; 13:53; 19:1; 26:1). Many see an echo of the five books of Moses: Jesus as the greater Moses, teaching from a mountain.
  • "The crowds were astonished" (v28). The word (ekplēssō) literally means "to be struck out of one's senses," to be overwhelmed. The crowds have been listening too (5:1; 7:28).
  • "As one who had authority, and not as their scribes" (v29). The scribes were experts in the Law, and their teaching leaned on quotations from earlier teachers: "Rabbi so-and-so says." Jesus quoted Scripture, but he also said six times "But I say to you" (5:22–44), set himself up as the final judge (v22), and made his own words the foundation of life (v24). That is the authority of God's own voice.
  • Commentators: JFB notes that the point is the manner of his teaching as well as its content, and that the translators' added word "one" (in "as one having authority") slightly weakens the statement. Guzik warns that astonishment is not the same as obedience. The crowds were amazed, but the sermon has just said that only doing his words counts.

Pointing to Christ

The sermon ends by pointing to the preacher. The one who says "Judge not" will himself be the judge on "that day." The narrow gate is a person (John 10:9), the good tree is the life his Spirit grows, and the rock the wise man builds on is his word. Paul writes, "no one can lay a foundation other than that which is laid, which is Jesus Christ" (1 Cor 3:11). The Father who gives good gifts gave the best gift in his Son, so that those who were once "evil" (v11) could be known by him and enter the kingdom.

Reflection

  • Dealing With Your Own Sin First (v.5) — Jesus says to remove the beam from your own eye before addressing the speck in someone else's. Are you more aware of other people's faults than your own?
  • Persistent Prayer (v.7) — The progression of ask, seek, and knock suggests increasing intensity, not a single request and giving up. How persistent is your prayer life?
  • Active Love, Not Passive Restraint (v.12) — The Golden Rule calls for actively doing good to others, not merely avoiding harm. What specific good would you want done to you that you're not doing for someone else?
  • Choosing the Unpopular Path (v.14) — Jesus says few find the narrow way that leads to life, while most travel the easy road. Where are you tempted to take the broad, easy path simply because it's what everyone else is doing?
  • Activity Without Relationship (v.23) — People who prophesied and did miracles in Jesus' name are told He never knew them, showing that religious activity without genuine relationship is worthless. How is your actual, personal, daily relationship with Jesus — not your church attendance or ministry involvement?
  • Hearing Versus Doing (vv.24-27) — The wise and foolish builders both heard the same words; the difference was obedience when the storm came. What teaching of Jesus have you heard repeatedly but not yet acted on?
