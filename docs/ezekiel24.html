1In the ninth year, on the tenth day of the tenth month, the word of the LORD came to me, saying,

2“Son of man, write down today’s date, for on this very day the king of Babylon has laid siege to Jerusalem.

3Now speak a parable to this rebellious house and tell them that this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Put the pot on the fire; put it on and pour in the water.

4Put in the pieces of meat, every good piece—thigh and shoulder—fill it with choice bones.

5Take the choicest of the flock and pile the fuel beneath it. Bring it to a boil and cook the bones in it.’

6Therefore this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Woe to the city of bloodshed, to the pot now rusted, whose rust will not come off! Empty it piece by piece; cast no lots for its contents.

7For the blood she shed is still within her; she poured it out on the bare rock; she did not pour it on the ground to cover it with dust.

8In order to stir up wrath and take vengeance, I have placed her blood on the bare rock, so that it would not be covered.’

9Yes, this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘Woe to the city of bloodshed! I, too, will pile the kindling high.

10Pile on the logs and kindle the fire; cook the meat well and mix in the spices; let the bones be burned.

11Set the empty pot on its coals until it becomes hot and its copper glows. Then its impurity will melt within; its rust will be consumed.

12It has frustrated every effort; its thick rust has not been removed, even by the fire.

13Because of the indecency of your uncleanness I tried to cleanse you, but you would not be purified from your filthiness. You will not be pure again until My wrath against you has subsided.

14I, the LORD, have spoken; the time is coming, and I will act. I will not refrain or show pity, nor will I relent. I will judge you according to your ways and deeds,’ declares the Lord GOD.”

15Then the word of the LORD came to me, saying,

16“Son of man, behold, I am about to take away the desire of your eyes with a fatal blow. But you must not mourn or weep or let your tears flow.

17Groan quietly; do not mourn for the dead. Put on your turban and strap your sandals on your feet; do not cover your lips or eat the bread of mourners.”

18So I spoke to the people in the morning, and in the evening my wife died. And the next morning I did as I had been commanded.

19Then the people asked me, “Won’t you tell us what these things you are doing mean to us?”

20So I answered them, “The word of the LORD came to me, saying:

21Tell the house of Israel that this is what the Lord GOD says: ‘I am about to desecrate My sanctuary, the pride of your power, the desire of your eyes, and the delight of your soul. And the sons and daughters you left behind will fall by the sword.’

22Then you will do as I have done: You will not cover your lips or eat the bread of mourners.

23Your turbans will remain on your heads and your sandals on your feet. You will not mourn or weep, but you will waste away because of your sins, and you will groan among yourselves.

24‘Thus Ezekiel will be a sign for you; you will do everything that he has done. When this happens, you will know that I am the Lord GOD.’

25And you, son of man, know that on the day I take away their stronghold, their pride and joy—the desire of their eyes which uplifted their souls—and their sons and daughters as well,

26on that day a fugitive will come and tell you the news.

27On that day your mouth will be opened to him who has escaped; you will speak and no longer be mute. So you will be a sign to them, and they will know that I am the LORD.”

Summary

God reveals to Ezekiel the exact day Nebuchadnezzar's siege of Jerusalem begins and marks it with two devastating signs (vv. 1-2). First, he tells the parable of a corroded boiling pot representing Jerusalem, whose filth cannot be cleansed and so must be set empty over the fire until it burns away (vv. 3-14). Second, in the most personal sign-act of the book, God takes the life of Ezekiel's beloved wife and forbids him to mourn her publicly, in the ordinary way (vv. 15-24). His restrained grief becomes a preview of how the exiles will react with stunned silence when news arrives that Jerusalem has fallen (vv. 25-27).

Authorship & Background

Author: Ezekiel son of Buzi (see Chapter 1 notes for full details).
Classification: Prophetic Sign-Act / Parable / Personal Prophecy
Key Themes: The siege begins, the corroded pot that cannot be cleaned, judgment by fire, the death of Ezekiel's wife, forbidden mourning as a sign, the end of Ezekiel's silence, the most costly prophecy
Historical Context: This chapter is precisely dated: "the ninth year, in the tenth month, in the tenth day of the month" (v.1) — January 15, 588 BC. This is the exact day Nebuchadnezzar's army begins the siege of Jerusalem (confirmed by 2 Kings 25:1 and Jeremiah 39:1). God reveals this to Ezekiel in Babylon on the same day it happens hundreds of miles away — supernatural knowledge confirming his prophetic authority. The death of Ezekiel's wife (vv.15-27) is the most personally costly sign-act in all of Scripture. God takes "the desire of his eyes" and commands him not to weep, not to perform mourning rituals, not to remove his turban or shoes. His silent grief prophesies the stunned, wordless shock the exiles will experience when news comes that Jerusalem has fallen. They will be too devastated to mourn normally.
The Date the Siege Began (vv.1-2): The chapter is dated to the day: the ninth year, tenth month, tenth day, which is 15 January 588 BC. That is the exact day Nebuchadnezzar's army closed on Jerusalem, as 2 Kings 25:1 and Jeremiah 39:1 independently record. Ezekiel is in Babylon, hundreds of miles away, and is told to write the date down. The point of the instruction is verification later, when news finally travels.
The Boiling Pot, Corroded Beyond Cleaning (vv.3-14): The parable takes an image the Jerusalem leaders had used of themselves. In Ezekiel 11:3 they called themselves the flesh in the pot, meaning protected. Here the metaphor is turned round: the pot is boiling, the choice pieces are being cooked, and the scum or rust on it will not come off. So the vessel is set empty on the coals until it burns, because the corrosion cannot be scoured out. The judgment is described as cleansing that failed and therefore became destruction.
The Desire of Thine Eyes (vv.15-18): The most personally costly sign-act in Scripture. God tells Ezekiel that his wife, “the desire of thine eyes”, will die, and forbids the mourning that custom and decency required: no weeping aloud, no removing the turban, no covering the lip. Verse 18 reports it in one sentence — I spake unto the people in the morning, and at even my wife died, and I did in the morning as I was commanded. The brevity is the effect.
Forbidden Mourning as Prophecy (vv.19-24): The people ask what it means, which is what the sign was for. The answer is that they will lose the sanctuary, “the desire of your eyes”, and their sons and daughters, and will not mourn either — struck too numb for ritual. Ezekiel's silent grief is a rehearsal of theirs. Making a prophet's bereavement into a public sign is the hardest thing God asks of anyone in the book.
When the News Comes, Thou Shalt Speak (vv.25-27): The chapter closes with a promise about Ezekiel's own voice. He has been under restraint since chapter 3, and when a fugitive arrives with word that the city has fallen, his mouth will be opened and he will no longer be dumb. That happens at 33:21-22, some two years later. So the siege's beginning and the prophet's release are tied together from the outset.

Deep Dive

Setting: Babylonia, on one exact day: the tenth day of the tenth month of the ninth year of King Jehoiachin's exile, which works out to about January 15, 588 BC. Ezekiel is living among the exiles by the Kebar canal, some 700 miles from Jerusalem by the travel route. For more than four years he has warned that the city would fall, and many of his hearers still believed it never would. On this day, God tells him the siege has begun. Chapter 24 is the end of the first half of the book. It gives two final signs about Jerusalem: a cooking pot set on the fire, and the sudden death of Ezekiel's wife. After this, Ezekiel says nothing more about Jerusalem until a survivor arrives with news of its fall (33:21). Chapters 25–32 turn to the surrounding nations in the meantime.

The Siege of Jerusalem (vv1–14)

  • "Write down the name of this day, this very day" (v2). "This very day" is said twice. Ezekiel is to put the date in writing so that when news finally comes from Judah, months later, everyone can check that the prophet knew the day the siege began. The same date is recorded in 2 Kings 25:1, Jeremiah 39:1 and 52:4. After the exile the Jews kept it as a fast day, the "fast of the tenth month" (Zech 8:19), and it is still observed in Judaism as the Tenth of Tevet. Henry notes that God sees all places as clearly as all times: what happened at Jerusalem was present to him in Babylon.
  • "The king of Babylon has laid siege to Jerusalem" (v2). The verb (samak) literally means "to lean on" or "to lay hands on." The Tyndale notes point out that it is the same verb used when a worshiper laid his hand on the head of an animal before it was sacrificed (Lev 1:4). Nebuchadnezzar lays his hand on Jerusalem, and what follows is pictured as a sacrifice.
  • "Utter a parable to the rebellious house" (v3). The word is mashal, a saying or proverb. Ezekiel may have acted it out with a real pot, or he may have only spoken it. Either way, it is a poem, a cooking song.
  • "Set on the pot… put in it the pieces of meat, all the good pieces, the thigh and the shoulder" (vv3–4). At first it sounds like a feast: the best cuts, "choice bones," "the choicest one of the flock" (v5). The leaders of Jerusalem had a saying of their own: "This city is the cauldron, and we are the meat" (11:3). They meant that the city's walls protected them as a pot protects its contents from the fire, and that they were the choice cuts, the best people. Ezekiel now takes up their proverb and turns it against them. Yes, they are the meat in the pot. But the pot is on the fire.
  • "Woe to the bloody city, to the pot whose corrosion is in it" (v6). The parable now turns sour. "Bloody city" is the title from 22:2 and from Nahum's oracle against Nineveh (Nah 3:1). "Corrosion" (hel'ah) is translated "scum" in older versions, but the word means rust or a crust that eats into the metal. The pot itself is corroded, and the corrosion is "in it" and will not come out. The corruption is not on the surface of Jerusalem. It is part of the city.
  • "Take out of it piece after piece, without making any choice" (v6). Literally, "no lot has fallen on it." When an animal was shared out, lots might be cast to decide who got which piece. Here there is no choosing. The people will be pulled out one after another, killed or taken captive, with no distinction between the "choice" cuts and the rest.
  • "She put it on the bare rock; she did not pour it out on the ground to cover it with dust" (v7). The law required that even the blood of an animal killed for food be poured out and covered with earth (Lev 17:13), because the life is in the blood and belongs to God. Jerusalem had shed human blood and left it lying openly on the rock. It did not even try to hide its murders. Blood left uncovered was believed to cry out to God for justice, as Abel's blood "cried" from the ground (Gen 4:10; Job 16:18; Isa 26:21).
  • "I have set on the bare rock the blood she has shed, that it may not be covered" (v8). God takes her own shamelessness and makes it the evidence. The blood she left in the open now stands openly before him and calls for judgment. JFB notes that God purposely let the guilt be as visible as possible, so that the link between the crime and the punishment would be plain.
  • "I also will make the pile great" (v9). God himself becomes the cook. "Heap on the logs, kindle the fire, boil the meat well, mix in the spices, and let the bones be burned up" (v10). The scene moves from cooking to burning. The meat is boiled away, and then the bones themselves are burned. This is the siege that lasted about eighteen months, until July 586 BC, with famine inside the walls (2 Kings 25:3; Lam 4:9–10).
  • "Then set it empty upon the coals… that its uncleanness may be melted in it, its corrosion consumed" (v11). Even after the contents are gone, the pot itself must go into the fire, because the rust is in the metal. That is the destruction of the city and the temple, not just the people. JFB compares it to the law of a house with spreading mold, which had to be torn down when cleaning did not work (Lev 14:43–45).
  • "She has wearied herself with toil; its abundant corrosion does not go out of it" (v12). The Hebrew is hard. The ESV takes it as Jerusalem exhausting herself with efforts that achieve nothing. Others, including some JFB mentions, take it as "she has wearied me with toil": God's long efforts to clean her have been wasted.
  • "Because I would have cleansed you and you were not cleansed" (v13). God had tried. Hezekiah and Josiah led reforms (2 Kings 18:4; 23:1–25). Prophets warned for generations. The law, the temple and the sacrifices were all means of cleansing. None of it took hold. "You shall not be cleansed anymore till I have satisfied my fury upon you." Judgment will do what grace was refused the chance to do.
  • "I will not go back; I will not spare; I will not relent" (v14). Three short negatives, one after another. Ezekiel's hearers kept hoping for a last-minute reprieve, as had happened when Sennacherib's army left Jerusalem in Hezekiah's day (2 Kings 19:35–36). This time there would be none. "According to your ways and your deeds you will be judged."
  • Commentators: JFB and Guzik both connect the pot with the leaders' proverb in 11:3, which Ezekiel throws back at them. JFB suggests the bones piled under the pot as fuel are the poorest people, who suffered first in the siege. Guzik reports Block's view that the plural form of "bones" used here elsewhere in Ezekiel always means human bones, which would make the image darker still, though most commentators take them as animal bones. Guzik and Feinberg prefer "rust" to "scum," which fits the Hebrew. Henry notes that the scum boiled up most when the fire was hottest: Jerusalem's sin came to the surface under God's judgment, as with Ahaz, who "in the time of his distress… became yet more faithless" (2 Chron 28:22). He writes, "There is little hope of those who are made worse by that which should make them better," and on v14, "The declarations of God's wrath against sinners are as inviolable as the assurances he has given of favour to his people."

Ezekiel's Wife Dies (vv15–27)

  • "I am about to take the delight of your eyes away from you at a stroke" (v16). This is the only time Ezekiel speaks of his wife. "Delight of your eyes" (machmad 'eyneykha) means the one he most loved to look at. "At a stroke" can mean "suddenly," and the word is often used of a plague or sudden blow. God warned him beforehand. That made it no less painful. Henry draws the lesson: "That is least safe which is most dear."
  • "Yet you shall not mourn or weep, nor shall your tears run down" (v16). In Israel, mourning was loud and public. Mourners wailed, tore their clothes, took off their headdress and sandals, put dust on their heads, covered the lower part of the face, and were brought food by neighbors, since they were not expected to cook (2 Sam 15:30; 19:4; Jer 16:7). Ezekiel is told to do none of it.
  • "Sigh, but not aloud" (v17). This is the tender line in the command. God does not forbid grief. He forbids the public rituals of grief. Ezekiel may groan in silence. JFB, citing Grotius, reads it as "lament in silence," not a ban on sorrow. Guzik sees here the understanding heart of God, who knows his servant's pain and does not rebuke the sigh.
  • "Bind on your turban, and put your shoes on your feet; do not cover your lips, nor eat the bread of men" (v17). Each instruction reverses a mourning custom. The turban stays on. The sandals stay on. The face is not covered. "The bread of men" is the food neighbors brought to a mourning household. Ezekiel is a priest (1:3), and the law already limited a priest's mourning (Lev 21:1–6), with the high priest forbidden it entirely (Lev 21:10–12). Ezekiel's restriction goes further still. It is a sign for the people.
  • "So I spoke to the people in the morning, and at evening my wife died. And on the next morning I did as I was commanded" (v18). These are some of the most restrained sentences in the Bible. There is no description of his grief, just a morning, an evening and the next morning. He preached in the morning, lost his wife by evening, and obeyed the next morning. Taylor, quoted by Guzik, says these verses give a rare glimpse of the tender heart beneath Ezekiel's stern exterior.
  • Why would God do this? Faithful readers have struggled with this, and the text does not explain it away. It does not say God killed Ezekiel's wife as a punishment. It says God took her, and that her death and Ezekiel's response became the message. Prophets in Scripture often carried their message in their own lives: Hosea in his marriage (Hos 1–3), Isaiah in his children's names (Isa 8:18), Jeremiah in being forbidden to marry at all (Jer 16:1–4). Ezekiel had already lain on his side for months and eaten siege rations (ch 4). Here the cost reached his home. What the text does show is a man who loved his wife, grieved her deeply and still trusted God.
  • "Will you not tell us what these things mean for us?" (v19). The exiles had learned by now that Ezekiel's strange actions meant something. Seeing a man not mourn his wife would have been shocking in any culture. They knew it had to be a sign.
  • "I will profane my sanctuary, the pride of your power, the delight of your eyes, and the yearning of your soul" (v21). Here is the meaning. Ezekiel lost the delight of his eyes. Israel will lose the delight of its eyes: the temple. The exiles took pride in it and longed for it. Many believed God would never let it fall (Jer 7:4). "Your sons and your daughters whom you left behind shall fall by the sword." Many of the exiles had family still in Jerusalem.
  • "You shall do as I have done… you shall not mourn or weep, but you shall rot away in your iniquities and groan to one another" (vv22–23). When the news comes, the exiles will not be able to mourn either. The reasons are debated. Some think the disaster would be too great for any ritual to match: grief beyond tears. Others think mourning in public would not be possible or safe in a foreign land. Others think mourning would be out of place because the judgment was deserved. "Rot away" (maqaq) means to waste away or pine. "Groan to one another" is the quiet, private grief of people who know they brought this on themselves. This matches Leviticus 26:39: "those of you who are left shall rot away in your enemies' lands because of their iniquity."
  • "Thus shall Ezekiel be to you a sign" (v24). Apart from the heading in 1:3, this is the only place in the book where Ezekiel's own name is used. The word "sign" (mopheth) is the same word used for the wonders in Egypt (Exod 7:3). The prophet himself has become the wonder. "When this comes, then you will know that I am the Lord GOD."
  • "On that day a fugitive will come to you to report to you the news" (v26). God promises a messenger from Jerusalem. That survivor arrived about three years later, in the twelfth year of the exile (33:21). Travel from Jerusalem to Babylonia took months.
  • "On that day your mouth will be opened… and be no longer mute" (v27). At his call, Ezekiel was told he would be mute, speaking only when God gave him a message (3:26–27). Most interpreters take this as a limited muteness: he could not speak freely or plead for the people, only deliver God's words. That restriction will end on the day the news comes. And so it did: "the LORD had opened my mouth… and I was no longer mute" (33:22). The book then turns from judgment to hope.
  • Commentators: Henry calls the two signs in this chapter "two sermons… both from Mount Ebal, the mount of curses," and says the calamities would be "so great that they should sink down under them into a silent despair." He draws several gentle lessons from the death of Ezekiel's wife: marriage suits a minister's calling, a wife may rightly be "the desire of the eyes," and "when the desire of our eyes is taken away with a stroke we must see and own the hand of God in it." JFB notes that this is "the only memorable event of his personal history which he records," and it was for the sake of his message. JFB also reads the exiles' silence not as indifference but as grief that could not be shown in public. Guzik, citing Feinberg, notes that even Jesus wept at the grave of Lazarus, so this was not a ban on sorrow but on loud mourning. He makes a contrast: Ezekiel's wife did not deserve to die, yet he did not mourn, while Jerusalem's fall was deserved and long announced, which should have made its loss even less surprising.

Pointing to Christ

Ezekiel lost the delight of his eyes so that his people would understand what was coming. The prophet's grief became a picture of God's grief over a city and temple he loved and was about to give up. Centuries later Jesus wept openly over the same city as he came near it, "Would that you, even you, had known on this day the things that make for peace!" (Luke 19:41–42), and foretold that its temple would fall again (Luke 21:6). But Jesus also said "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up," speaking "about the temple of his body" (John 2:19–21). The temple Ezekiel's hearers loved too much was taken away. The true dwelling of God with his people is Christ himself, and the body that was destroyed was raised. The fugitive in Ezekiel 24 brings bad news that opens the prophet's mouth for hope. The empty tomb is the news that opened the apostles' mouths for good.

Reflection

  • Marking the Exact Day (v.2) — God tells Ezekiel to write down the very date the siege of Jerusalem begins, hundreds of miles away, before any news could travel. Where have you seen God's timing prove exact in your own life, even when you couldn't verify it in the moment?
  • Mercy Exhausted Becomes Judgment (v.13) — God says He tried to cleanse Jerusalem and she would not be cleansed, so only the fire remains. Where might you be mistaking God's patience with you for permission to keep putting off the change He's calling for?
  • Asked to Surrender What You Love Most (v.16) — God takes Ezekiel's wife, "the desire of his eyes," and asks him to carry that loss as part of the message he preaches. Is there something precious to you that God is asking you to hold with open hands rather than a tight grip?
  • Grief Carried in Silence (v.17) — Ezekiel is told to sigh in silence, forbidden the normal outlets of mourning, so that his restrained grief itself becomes part of the prophecy. Is there a season where God is asking you to carry pain without needing everyone around you to see or understand it?
  • A Life That Preaches Louder Than Words (v.24) — Ezekiel himself becomes a sign to the people, his very life and losses functioning as the sermon. Does the way you live through hardship point people toward God, or does it just look like private tragedy?
