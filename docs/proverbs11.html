Summary

Solomon opens with God's demand for honest business dealings, calling dishonest scales detestable and just weights delightful to him (v. 1). Pride precedes disgrace while humility carries wisdom, and personal integrity guides the upright while the wicked are ultimately destroyed by their own wrongdoing (vv. 2-6). A city thrives when the righteous prosper, gossip destroys neighbors, and trustworthy people keep confidences while many advisors bring safety (vv. 9-14). Generosity is repeatedly praised: the one who gives freely still grows richer, while the stingy person heads toward poverty, and whoever refreshes others will be refreshed in return (vv. 24-25). The chapter closes by warning that trusting in riches leads to a fall, while the righteous flourish like a green branch, and whoever wins souls is called wise (vv. 28, 30).

Authorship & Background

Author: Primarily Solomon (1:1, 10:1, 25:1), with sections by Agur (ch.30) and King Lemuel (ch.31). Compiled over several centuries. Proverbs is wisdom literature — practical instruction for godly living. Hebrew title: 'Mishlei' — 'Proverbs/Comparisons.'
Historical Context: Chapter 11 continues the first Solomonic collection of antithetical proverbs (10:1-22:16). The dominant themes are integrity vs. deception in business (vv.1, 3, 5-6), the impact of the righteous and wicked on community (vv.10-11), the power of speech (vv.9, 12-13), the paradox of generosity (vv.24-26), and the ultimate worthlessness of riches without righteousness (vv.4, 28). The chapter is remarkable for its communal vision — individual righteousness or wickedness affects the whole city (vv.10-11). Virtue is never merely private.
Themes in This Chapter: Integrity and Honesty in Business (vv.1, 3, 18); The Community Impact of Righteousness (vv.10-11, 26); The Paradox of Generosity (vv.24-26); Riches vs. Righteousness (vv.4, 28); The Power of Speech (vv.9, 12-13)
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: commerce opened with a specific business practice named outright, a false balance is abomination to the LORD, but a just weight is his delight, a civic claim unusual for the collection, by the blessing of the upright the city is exalted, treating private virtue as having public effect, in the multitude of counsellors there is safety standing beside a warning against becoming surety for a stranger, the paradox the chapter is remembered for, there is that scattereth, and yet increaseth, and a closing image of the righteous as a tree of life, ending on the man who winneth souls being called wise
Weights, Pride and Integrity (vv.1-11): The chapter opens with commerce, a false balance is abomination to the LORD, but a just weight is his delight, which is one of the few proverbs that names a specific business practice. Then pride and shame, and a run on what riches cannot do, riches profit not in the day of wrath. The block closes on a civic note that is unusual in the collection, by the blessing of the upright the city is exalted, so a private virtue is described as having a public effect.
Counsel, Surety and the Company Kept (vv.12-21): A cluster on discretion and advice. He that is void of wisdom despiseth his neighbour, but a man of understanding holdeth his peace. A talebearer revealeth secrets, but he that is of a faithful spirit concealeth the matter. Then the verse that has shaped centuries of committee practice, in the multitude of counsellors there is safety, and beside it a warning against standing surety for a stranger. The block includes the merciful man doing good to his own soul and the cruel man troubling his own flesh, which is the collection's habitual argument that conduct rebounds on the actor.
Generosity, Greed and the Fruit of the Righteous (vv.22-31): The most memorable image in the chapter opens the block, as a jewel of gold in a swine's snout, so is a fair woman which is without discretion. Then the paradox the chapter is remembered for, there is that scattereth, and yet increaseth, and there is that withholdeth more than is meet, and it tendeth to poverty, followed by the liberal soul that shall be made fat and he that watereth being watered himself. The block closes on the fruit of the righteous as a tree of life, and he that winneth souls is wise.

Setting: The collection headed "The proverbs of Solomon" continues (10:1–22:16; see Proverbs 10 on how it was gathered and how to read a proverb). The ESV gives no new headings inside it, so the headings below simply group the sayings in order. Chapter 11 keeps the steady contrast of chapter 10 between the righteous and the wicked, but its focus shifts from the tongue to the marketplace and the town: honest scales, generosity and hoarding, the good or harm one person does to a whole city. Most sayings are still two-line contrasts, and the word "righteous" or "righteousness" appears more than a dozen times. Proverbs speaks of wealth without embarrassment, but this chapter keeps asking how it was gained and what it is trusted for.

Honest Scales and Upright Lives (vv1–8)

  • "A false balance is an abomination to the LORD, but a just weight is his delight" (v1). Before coins, payment was weighed out on a hand-held balance: two pans hanging from a crossbar, with stones as standard weights. The Tyndale notes point out that the shekel itself was a weight, from the verb "to weigh." A merchant could carry two sets of stones, a heavy one for buying and a light one for selling (Deut 25:13–15; Amos 8:5). "Just weight" is literally "a complete stone," full measure (JFB: "complete in measure"). "Abomination" (toʿebah) is the strongest word of disgust in the Old Testament, the same word used of idolatry. God cares as much about the kitchen scale as about the altar. Henry: "Nothing is more offensive to God than deceit in commerce," and a rigged balance is worse for cheating "under pretence of doing right most exactly."
  • "When pride comes, then comes disgrace, but with the humble is wisdom" (v2). In Hebrew the first line has a wordplay: zadon (pride) comes, then qalon (disgrace) comes. Pride always brings its companion. "Humble" (tsenuʿim) is a rare word, and its root appears elsewhere only in Micah 6:8: "walk humbly with your God." It suggests modesty, people who know their limits. JFB: "Self-conceit is unteachable; the humble grow wise." Compare 16:18.
  • "The integrity of the upright guides them, but the crookedness of the treacherous destroys them" (v3). JFB says "guide" is the shepherd's word for leading a flock. Integrity is not only a rule to keep. It is a guide that keeps a person on the road. The "treacherous" (bogedim) are those who betray trust.
  • "Riches do not profit in the day of wrath, but righteousness delivers from death" (v4). The second line repeats 10:2. "The day of wrath" can be any day of disaster, but the prophets use it of God's judgment (Zeph 1:15, 18: "Neither their silver nor their gold shall be able to deliver them"). JFB takes it as God's wrath. Guzik draws the application: invest in treasure that lasts (Matt 6:19–20).
  • "The righteousness of the blameless keeps his way straight, but the wicked falls by his own wickedness" (vv5–6). Two parallel sayings with the same lesson: right living clears the path ahead, and wrong living sets its own traps. The treacherous are "taken captive by their lust," snared by the very things they craved. Sin is its own punishment long before it is judged (5:22).
  • "When the wicked dies, his hope will perish" (v7). If all a person's hopes lie in this life, death ends them. JFB: "death cuts short all his plans." The second line, "the expectation of wealth," is one way to read a hard word (ʾonim), which can mean wealth, strength or wickedness. Guzik sums it up: their hope stops at the grave.
  • "The righteous is delivered from trouble, and the wicked walks into it instead" (v8). Sometimes it is the very trap the wicked set. Haman built a gallows for Mordecai and hanged on it himself (Esth 7:10). Daniel's accusers ended in the lions' den (Dan 6:24).
  • Commentators: Henry and Guzik both put the weight of v1 on God's interest in ordinary business, and Guzik notes the strength of the word "abomination." On v2 Guzik describes humility as an accurate view of God and self, and the Tyndale notes say much the same: pride is dishonest self-promotion, humility is honest self-assessment. JFB reads v4's "day of wrath" as God's own wrath.

Words, Neighbors and the City (vv9–15)

  • "With his mouth the godless man would destroy his neighbor, but by knowledge the righteous are delivered" (v9). The "godless" (chanep) is the hypocrite, the profane person who wears a religious face. His weapon is words: slander, flattery, false witness. The righteous escape because they see through it. Guzik takes "knowledge" as both discernment and knowing God.
  • "When it goes well with the righteous, the city rejoices" (vv10–11). A whole town feels the difference good and bad people make. When the wicked fall, there are shouts in the street, as when Athaliah fell and "all the people of the land rejoiced, and the city was quiet" (2 Kgs 11:20). "By the blessing of the upright a city is exalted" means their prayers, their good deeds and their good words. JFB: "blessing" implies "active benevolence." Jeremiah told exiles to "seek the welfare of the city" (Jer 29:7). Abraham's prayer for Sodom shows that even ten righteous people could have spared a city (Gen 18:32).
  • "Whoever belittles his neighbor lacks sense, but a man of understanding remains silent" (v12). "Belittles" means to despise, to look down on and say so (JFB: "reviles"). The wise person may see the same faults but holds his tongue.
  • "Whoever goes about slandering reveals secrets" (v13). The "slanderer" (rakil) is a talebearer, someone who walks from house to house trading in gossip (Lev 19:16 forbids it). JFB: he "peddles scandal." Someone who is "trustworthy in spirit" keeps a matter covered. The same verb as "love covers" in 10:12. The Tyndale notes point to 20:19: don't associate with someone who can't stop talking.
  • "Where there is no guidance, a people falls, but in an abundance of counselors there is safety" (v14). "Guidance" (tachbulot) is a nautical word from the root for a ship's ropes, steering, the skill of a pilot. JFB calls it "the art of governing." A nation without wise steering drifts onto the rocks. Rehoboam had counselors, but listened to the wrong ones and lost ten tribes (1 Kgs 12:6–16). Guzik: no one has all the wisdom, so several sound advisers protect good decisions (15:22; 24:6).
  • "Whoever puts up security for a stranger will surely suffer harm" (v15). Another warning against cosigning a loan, as in 6:1–5. Literally "he will be broken, broken," harm doubled. "Striking hands" was the handshake that sealed the deal. JFB says this kind of suretiship "may be lawfully hated." Proverbs is not against helping people. It is against promising what one can't control.
  • Commentators: Guzik reads vv10–11 as the public face of private character: a city lives better or worse according to the people in it. JFB gives v14's "counsel" a political sense, the craft of government. The Tyndale notes add that a nation's future rides on wise decisions.

Kindness, Cruelty and What God Delights In (vv16–23)

  • "A gracious woman gets honor, and violent men get riches" (v16). "Gracious" (chen) means charm and favor, the grace that wins goodwill. "Violent men" (ʿaritsim) are ruthless, the kind who seize. The verse may be a contrast (honor through grace, money through force) or a comparison (each holds tight to what it values). The Tyndale notes take it as a contrast: honor lasts, while ruthless wealth is a temporary prize. The Greek translation adds two extra lines here, but they are not in the Hebrew.
  • "A man who is kind benefits himself, but a cruel man hurts himself" (v17). "Kind" is chesed, loyal love, the word used of God's covenant love. Kindness comes back. Cruelty, literally, "troubles his own flesh." JFB: the cruel "injure themselves as well as others." Jesus: "Blessed are the merciful, for they shall receive mercy" (Matt 5:7).
  • "The wicked earns deceptive wages, but one who sows righteousness gets a sure reward" (v18). A wordplay: "deceptive" is sheqer, and "reward" is seker. The wicked man's pay is false, it fails to satisfy or slips away (JFB). The righteous are like farmers, and seed sown in righteousness always comes up (Gal 6:7–9; Hos 10:12).
  • "Whoever is steadfast in righteousness will live" (v19). JFB treats this as the conclusion drawn from v18. Life and death are the final wages of the two ways.
  • "Those of crooked heart are an abomination to the LORD, but those of blameless ways are his delight" (v20). The same pair of words as v1, "abomination" and "delight." God is disgusted by a crooked scale and a crooked heart alike, and delights in straight ones. Guzik notes that, for Christians, blamelessness before God comes through Christ.
  • "Be assured, an evil person will not go unpunished" (v21). "Be assured" is literally "hand to hand," like the handshake in v15. JFB reads it as the wicked joining hands together, uniting their strength, but still failing to escape. Guzik points to Babel as a picture of united rebellion that came to nothing (Gen 11). Either way, it is a pledge: justice will come. And "the offspring of the righteous" share in their deliverance.
  • "Like a gold ring in a pig's snout is a beautiful woman without discretion" (v22). The most vivid picture in the chapter. Nose rings were common jewelry for women (Gen 24:47; Isa 3:21). Pigs were unclean animals to Israel, rooting in filth. Gold in a pig's nose is wasted and grotesque, beauty attached to something that rummages in garbage. "Discretion" (taʿam) is literally "taste," the ability to judge what is fitting. The Tyndale notes define it as telling right from wrong and good taste from bad. JFB: "a hog disgusts less than a fair and indiscreet woman." The saying applies just as well to men, but it fits a book addressed to a son choosing a wife (31:30).
  • "The desire of the righteous ends only in good, the expectation of the wicked in wrath" (v23). The Tyndale notes say the short-term picture may look unfair, but the end will put it right. JFB treats the rest of the chapter, vv24–31, as an illustration of this verse.
  • Commentators: JFB and Guzik differ slightly on v21: JFB reads "hand to hand" as the wicked combining forces, which Guzik illustrates with Babel, while most translations take it as a pledge of certainty ("be assured"). On v22 the Tyndale notes, JFB and Guzik all make the same point: outward beauty cannot cover for a lack of judgment.

Generosity and Its Harvest (vv24–31)

  • "One gives freely, yet grows all the richer; another withholds what he should give, and only suffers want" (v24). "Gives freely" is literally "scatters," like a farmer sowing seed. Guzik picks up this picture: the sower who throws seed away looks foolish but reaps a harvest. The miser keeps everything and loses it. Paul builds on this: "whoever sows bountifully will also reap bountifully" (2 Cor 9:6). The Tyndale notes add 28:27.
  • "Whoever brings blessing will be enriched, and one who waters will himself be watered" (v25). "Brings blessing" is literally "a soul of blessing," a generous person. "Enriched" is literally "made fat," which in a hungry world meant thriving (JFB: "prospers"). In a dry land, water is life. The one who refreshes others is refreshed. JFB calls this "a common figure for blessing."
  • "The people curse him who holds back grain, but a blessing is on the head of him who sells it" (v26). Hoarding grain during a shortage to drive up the price was an old way of profiting from hunger (Amos 8:4–6). JFB: "the miser loses reputation, though he saves corn." Joseph stored grain and sold it in the famine, and Egypt blessed him (Gen 41:56–57; 47:25). Guzik draws a principle about fair dealing, not a rule about prices.
  • "Whoever diligently seeks good seeks favor, but evil comes to him who searches for it" (v27). "Diligently seeks" is the verb from 8:17 (shachar). Each person finds what they go looking for.
  • "Whoever trusts in his riches will fall, but the righteous will flourish like a green leaf" (v28). The issue is trust. JFB points to 1 Timothy 6:17: don't "set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches." The green leaf is the tree planted by streams of water (Ps 1:3; Jer 17:7–8).
  • "Whoever troubles his own household will inherit the wind" (v29). "Troubles" (ʿokher) is the word for Achan, who brought trouble on Israel by stealing from Jericho, and on his own family (Josh 7:25). JFB connects it with greed for gain. The man who wrecks his family through greed or temper ends with nothing in his hands but wind (Eccl 5:16). The fool ends as a servant to the wise.
  • "The fruit of the righteous is a tree of life" (v30). The "tree of life" from Eden (Gen 2:9; 3:22) is used four times in Proverbs, for wisdom (3:18), the fruit of the righteous (11:30), a desire fulfilled (13:12) and a gentle tongue (15:4). A righteous life feeds and heals those around it.
  • "And whoever captures souls is wise" (v30). The phrase "takes souls" (loqeach nephashot) usually means to kill (1 Kgs 19:10; Ps 31:13). Because of that, some translations (NRSV) follow the Greek: "violence takes away lives." But here the context is positive, and "take" can also mean to win or carry off, as Absalom "stole the hearts" of Israel (2 Sam 15:6, a different verb with the same idea). So most read it as winning people over, drawing them to wisdom and to God. Henry: the wise "by communicating his wisdom, wins souls," and "those that would win souls have need of wisdom to know how to deal with them." JFB: "winneth souls" means doing them good, the opposite of harming them. Guzik illustrates it from courtship, warfare, sport and a fowler catching birds.
  • "If the righteous is repaid on earth, how much more the wicked and the sinner!" (v31). "Repaid" can mean either reward or discipline. If even the righteous receive correction for their faults in this life, how much more will the wicked receive judgment? The Greek translation reads "If the righteous is scarcely saved, where will the ungodly and the sinner appear?" Peter quotes that Greek form in 1 Peter 4:18, applying it to Christian suffering as God's refining judgment that "begins at the household of God." JFB says the principle is confirmed in time, "not excluding future rewards and punishments." Guzik calls it a sobering verse.
  • Commentators: JFB reads the whole closing section as one thought: generosity, "by God's blessing, secures increase," while stinginess ends in want. Henry turns v30 toward the work of winning others, noting that it takes wisdom to know how to handle each person. Guzik ties v24 to sowing and reaping and v26 to fair dealing. The Tyndale notes connect vv24–26 with Paul's teaching on cheerful giving (2 Cor 9:6–9).

Pointing to Christ

Proverbs 11 holds up the honest scale, the generous hand and the one who "waters" and "wins souls." Jesus is all of them. He is the just weight, the one man whose life measured exactly true (1 Pet 2:22). He gave "freely" in a way no one else has: "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Cor 8:9). He is the spring who waters the thirsty (John 4:14) and the tree of life whose leaves heal the nations (Rev 22:2). He called fishermen to "catch men" (Luke 5:10). And Peter's use of v31 points to the cross: judgment began with God's own household and fell fully on the Son, so that sinners "scarcely saved" are saved all the same (1 Pet 3:18).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes v.24's paradox: "The one who gives freely gains more than the one who hoards." He notes this operates on multiple levels — financial, relational, spiritual. On v.22, he observes that the pig doesn't appreciate the gold, and beauty doesn't appreciate (or benefit from) the absence of discretion. The proverb is about wasted potential and absurd combinations.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Honest Business Practices Matter to God (v.1) — a false balance is called an abomination to the LORD, while accurate weights are His delight. Where in your work or financial dealings might you be cutting a corner that you'd call dishonest if someone did it to you?
  • Your Character Affects Your Whole Community (vv.10-11) — a city rejoices when the righteous prosper and is built up by the blessing of the upright. Do you see your own integrity or compromise as something that only affects you, or as something that ripples out to the people around you?
  • Guard What Others Trust You With (v.13) — a gossip reveals secrets, but a trustworthy person keeps a confidence. Are you someone people can safely tell things to, or does information leak out of you?
  • Character Outlasts Appearance (v.22) — a beautiful woman without discretion is compared to a gold ring in a pig's snout, jarring and wasted. Are you investing more in how you look than in the kind of character that actually endures?
  • Generosity Leads to Increase, Not Loss (vv.24-25) — one person gives freely and grows richer, while another withholds and ends up poorer. Where is fear of scarcity keeping you from the generosity God actually rewards?
