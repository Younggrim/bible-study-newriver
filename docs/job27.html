2“As surely as God lives, who has deprived me of justice—the Almighty, who has embittered my soul—

3as long as my breath is still within me and the breath of God remains in my nostrils,

4my lips will not speak wickedness, and my tongue will not utter deceit.

5I will never say that you are right; I will maintain my integrity until I die.

6I will cling to my righteousness and never let go. As long as I live, my conscience will not accuse me.

7May my enemy be like the wicked and my opponent like the unjust.

8For what is the hope of the godless when he is cut off, when God takes away his life?

9Will God hear his cry when distress comes upon him?

10Will he delight in the Almighty? Will he call upon God at all times?

11I will instruct you in the power of God. I will not conceal the ways of the Almighty.

12Surely all of you have seen it for yourselves. Why then do you keep up this empty talk?

13This is the wicked man’s portion from God—the heritage the ruthless receive from the Almighty.

14Though his sons are many, they are destined for the sword; and his offspring will never have enough food.

15His survivors will be buried by the plague, and their widows will not weep for them.

16Though he heaps up silver like dust and piles up a wardrobe like clay,

17what he lays up, the righteous will wear, and his silver will be divided by the innocent.

18The house he built is like a moth’s cocoon, like a hut set up by a watchman.

19He lies down wealthy, but will do so no more; when he opens his eyes, all is gone.

20Terrors overtake him like a flood; a tempest sweeps him away in the night.

21The east wind carries him away, and he is gone; it sweeps him out of his place.

22It hurls itself against him without mercy as he flees headlong from its power.

23It claps its hands at him and hisses him out of his place.

Summary

Job resumes his speech, swearing by God — who he says has denied him justice — that he will never speak wickedness or concede that his friends are right; he will hold to his integrity until he dies (vv. 2-6). He wishes his enemy's fate to match that of the wicked, since the godless have no real hope when God cuts their life short (vv. 7-10). Job then turns to describe that fate in detail: the wicked man's children die by the sword or hunger, his stored-up wealth passes to the righteous, his fragile house collapses, and terror sweeps him away suddenly like a flood or whirlwind (vv. 11-23).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown (see Chapter 1 notes for full discussion).
Historical Context: Chapter 27 continues Job's final speech in the third cycle. With Bildad reduced to six verses and Zophar silent, Job dominates the closing of the dialogue section. This chapter is notable for two reasons: (1) Job's passionate oath of integrity (vv.1-6), and (2) his description of the fate of the wicked (vv.7-23), which ironically uses the very language his friends have been using against him. Some scholars have questioned whether vv.13-23 should be attributed to Zophar (as his missing third speech), but in context, Job is likely turning his friends' own argument back on them: "You say the wicked are destroyed? Fine — then let my enemies be as the wicked, because I am not one of them."
Classification: Wisdom Literature
Key Themes: an oath of integrity sworn by the very God Job believes has wronged him, till I die I will not remove mine integrity from me, a refusal to concede his friends' accusations even under continued suffering, a description of the wicked man's fate, children by the sword, houses fragile as a moth's web, that sounds almost identical to what the friends themselves have argued, and Job turning that very theology back on his accusers as evidence of his own innocence rather than his guilt
Job's Oath of Integrity (vv.1-6): Job swears by the living God — the very God who has "taken away my judgment" and "vexed my soul" — that he will not lie, will not concede his friends' accusations, and will not abandon his claim of innocence. "Till I die I will not remove mine integrity from me" (v.5). This is extraordinary: Job swears by a God he feels has wronged him, yet he will not lie to appease that God or His defenders. His integrity is non-negotiable. His righteousness is his, and he will not surrender it to maintain a theological system.
The Fate of the Wicked (vv.7-23): Job describes what happens to the truly wicked: their children die by the sword (v.14), their wealth passes to the righteous (v.17), their houses are fragile as a moth's web (v.18), terrors overtake them (v.20), and the east wind sweeps them away (v.21). This sounds like the friends' speeches — and that is precisely Job's point. He knows what happens to the wicked. He is not one of them. He turns their own theology against them as evidence of his innocence.

Setting: In chapter 26 Job mocked Bildad's six-verse "help" and then outdid him with a hymn to God's power over the sea, the sky and the world of the dead, ending with the confession that all of it is only "a whisper" of God. Now Zophar's turn should come, and it never does. The friends have run out of words. Matthew Henry says they were "out of breath, and left him room to say what he would." So chapter 27 opens with a new introduction, "Job again took up his discourse," and Job speaks with no one interrupting. This chapter begins Job's closing speeches (chs 27–31): first this oath and his teaching on the fate of the wicked, then the poem on wisdom (ch 28), and finally his long defense before God (chs 29–31). Two things make chapter 27 hard. Job swears he is innocent by the very God he says has wronged him. And in vv13–23 he seems to describe the ruin of the wicked in words his friends could have spoken, which raises the question of who is really talking and what Job means.

Job Continues: I Will Maintain My Integrity (vv1–23)

  • "And Job again took up his discourse" (v1). "Discourse" is mashal, the word for a proverb or wise saying. The lexicon explains that the phrase "lift up a mashal" is used for Balaam's solemn oracles (Num 23:7, 18) and for taunt songs against proud nations (Isa 14:4; Hab 2:6), and it lists Job 27:1 and 29:1 with Hebrew wisdom sayings. JFB says the word points to wisdom "in poetic form" and that "continued" implies "elevation of discourse." The Tyndale notes add that Job is no longer answering the three directly but going back to the themes of his first lament (ch 3).
  • "As God lives, who has taken away my right" (v2). This is an oath, the strongest form of speech in Israel. The Tyndale notes point to other oaths by the living God (Ruth 3:13; 1 Kgs 17:1), and to careless or false ones (1 Sam 14:39, 45; Jer 5:2). What is startling is the God Job swears by. "My right" is mishpat, justice or a fair verdict. Job calls on God as the guarantee of truth while saying that God has denied him justice. He holds both at once: God is the only true judge, and God is treating him unfairly. Elihu will later pick up exactly this line and condemn it: "Job has said, 'I am in the right, and God has taken away my right'" (34:5).
  • "And the Almighty, who has made my soul bitter" (v2). The verb is marar, "make bitter." The lexicon notes that the same form with the same subject, Shaddai ("the Almighty"), appears in only one other place: Naomi's words, "the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me" (Ruth 1:20). The Tyndale notes make the same link and add the Shunammite woman whose "soul is in bitter distress" over her dead son (2 Kgs 4:27). Like Naomi, he does not deny God's control. That is the very reason it is bitter.
  • "As long as my breath is in me, and the spirit of God is in my nostrils" (v3). The language looks back to creation, when God "breathed into his nostrils the breath of life" (Gen 2:7). JFB says it shows Job's knowledge that "the living soul was breathed into man by God." Job means the oath to last as long as he does.
  • "My lips will not speak falsehood, and my tongue will not utter deceit" (v4). The lie Job refuses to tell is a confession. The friends have urged him to admit hidden sin so that God will restore him (11:13–15; 22:21–23). For Job, that would be lying. JFB puts it this way: to speak "deceit" would mean admitting guilt against his own conscience.
  • "Far be it from me to say that you are right" (v5). "Far be it" is chalilah. The lexicon explains that it comes from the word for "profane," so literally it means something like "to profanity!", an exclamation of horror. Abraham uses it when he pleads for Sodom: "Far be that from you! Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Gen 18:25). In chapter 26 Job's "you" was singular, aimed at Bildad. Here it is plural. He is now speaking to all three friends. The Tyndale notes observe that agreeing would mean joining in condemning an innocent man, a grave wrong (Exod 23:7; Prov 17:15).
  • "Till I die I will not put away my integrity from me" (v5). "Integrity" is tummah, wholeness or blamelessness. The lexicon says it appears only five times in the Bible, and four of them are in Job: God's words to Satan, "he still holds fast his integrity" (2:3), his wife's challenge, "Do you still hold fast your integrity? Curse God and die" (2:9), this verse, and Job's later plea that God would "know my integrity" (31:6). The word ties chapter 27 back to the prologue. Satan predicted Job would let go of his integrity. His wife urged him to. His friends have spent chapter after chapter trying to talk him out of it. Job says he will hold it until he dies. Henry: "The best preparative for death is perseverance to death in our integrity."
  • "I hold fast my righteousness and will not let it go" (v6). "Hold fast" is the same verb, chazaq, used in 2:3 and 2:9. Job is doing exactly what God said he was doing. His "righteousness" here is not a claim to be sinless. Job has admitted sin before (7:21; 13:26). He means he is in the right in this dispute: he has not committed the hidden crimes the friends charge him with (22:5–9).
  • "My heart does not reproach me for any of my days" (v6). "Reproach" is charaph, which the lexicon defines as to taunt or say sharp things against someone. Job's conscience does not taunt him. JFB paraphrases that his "heart" (conscience) reproaches "not one of my days." Henry adds a careful distinction: a healthy conscience must accuse when there is real sin, so no one should resolve never to feel guilty, but a person who has walked honestly may refuse to let the accuser load false guilt on him. Paul also knew a clear conscience is not the final word: "I am not aware of anything against myself, but I am not thereby acquitted. It is the Lord who judges me" (1 Cor 4:4).
  • "Let my enemy be as the wicked" (v7). This sounds like a curse, but JFB reads it as a statement: anyone who opposes Job's claim of innocence will be "accounted as wicked." Guzik passes on Francis Andersen's point that Israel's law punished a false accuser with the penalty he tried to bring on the accused (Deut 19:16–19). If the friends are wrong, they have become the guilty party. Henry takes it another way: the hypocrite's lot is so miserable that Job could wish nothing worse even on an enemy.
  • "For what is the hope of the godless when God cuts him off" (v8). "Godless" is chaneph, which the lexicon defines as "profane, irreligious." It is one of the friends' favorite words. Bildad: "the hope of the godless shall perish" (8:13). Zophar: "the joy of the godless but for a moment" (20:5). Job himself used it earlier in a way that matters here: "this will be my salvation, that the godless shall not come before him" (13:16). Job's whole hope is to stand before God. A godless man would not want that. "Cuts him off" is batsa', which the lexicon says means to cut or break off, used figuratively here of God cutting off a life.
  • "Will God hear his cry when distress comes upon him?" (v9). The godless man prays only when trouble comes, and then has no claim on God. Henry contrasts the cry of the penitent, which God hears, with the cry of one who clings to sin: "If I had cherished iniquity in my heart, the Lord would not have listened" (Ps 66:18).
  • "Will he take delight in the Almighty? Will he call upon God at all times?" (v10). This is Job's real argument. JFB notes that it alludes to Eliphaz, who promised Job, "then you will delight yourself in the Almighty and lift up your face to God" (22:26), if only he repented. Job turns it around. A hypocrite does not keep calling on God when God seems absent. Job has done nothing else for twenty-four chapters. He has argued, complained and pleaded, but always toward God. His persistence in prayer is his evidence that he is not godless. Henry: "The more comfort we find in our religion the more closely we shall cleave to it."
  • "I will teach you concerning the hand of God" (v11). The friends set themselves up as Job's teachers. Now he takes that role. "The hand of God" is the phrase Job used for his own suffering: "the hand of God has touched me!" (19:21). Henry reads "what is with the Almighty" as God's hidden purposes toward the wicked.
  • "Why then have you become altogether vain?" (v12). The Hebrew is a wordplay. The lexicon gives it literally as "why do you become vain with vanity," using hebel, the "vapor" or "breath" of Ecclesiastes. Their arguments are hot air. JFB explains that the friends have seen for themselves that the wicked are often afflicted, but they wrongly treat Job's afflictions as proof that he is wicked. Guzik makes a similar point: the friends knew true principles about God but applied them to the wrong man.
  • "This is the portion of a wicked man with God" (v13). This line is almost word for word the end of Zophar's second speech: "This is the wicked man's portion from God, the heritage decreed for him by God" (20:29). That echo is why vv13–23 are among the most debated verses in Job. Earlier, Job argued that the wicked often live long, die peacefully and are buried with honor (21:7–33). Now he seems to say the opposite. There are three main readings.
  • Who is speaking in vv13–23? First, many modern scholars think these verses (some say vv7–23, or vv8–23) are Zophar's missing third speech, and that the speaker's name dropped out in copying. The Tyndale notes mention this view, observing that otherwise Zophar has no speech in the third round. No manuscript supports a change, and the Bible as it stands gives the words to Job. Second, JFB reads them as Job's words but not his own view. Job states "what Zophar would have said," even more strongly than Zophar would, to show that the argument still does not prove Job guilty. JFB admits plainly that "These words are contrary to Job's previous sentiments." Third, Henry and Guzik read them as Job's own belief. Henry explains the difference between Job and the friends as one of timing. The friends held that judgment falls on the wicked quickly and visibly. Job held that it is often delayed, but it is still certain, because "reprieves are no pardons." Guzik makes a similar point: Job never doubted that God judges the wicked; he only denied that his own suffering was that judgment. This third reading fits the chapter: Job has just shown he is not godless (vv8–10), and now warns his accusers what the godless can expect (v7).
  • "And the heritage that oppressors receive from the Almighty" (v13). "Oppressors" are the ruthless, people who crush others. Job described them in 24:2–4, the ones who move boundary stones and take the widow's ox. The wicked man spends his life building an inheritance, and what he really inherits is judgment.
  • "If his children are multiplied, it is for the sword" (v14). JFB: "His family only increases to perish by sword or famine." Sword, famine and plague (v15) are the standard judgments of the prophets (Jer 14:12). It is painful that this begins with children, since Job's own died suddenly (1:18–19). Much of what Job describes here is what happened to him.
  • "Those who survive him the pestilence buries, and his widows do not weep" (v15). "Pestilence" is literally "death," which sometimes means plague (Jer 15:2). The widows do not weep, either because they too are overwhelmed or because no one is sorry he is gone. The psalmist uses the same picture for judgment on Israel: "their widows made no lamentation" (Ps 78:64). JFB suggests reading "their widows," meaning the sons' widows, and says the change from singular to plural is common in Hebrew poetry: "Polygamy is not implied."
  • "Though he heap up silver like dust, and pile up clothing like clay" (v16). JFB notes that "Many changes of raiment are a chief constituent of wealth in the East." Fine garments were stored wealth, given as gifts and payment. Naaman brought "ten changes of clothing" with his silver and gold (2 Kgs 5:5).
  • "He may pile it up, but the righteous will wear it, and the innocent will divide the silver" (v17). The wicked man's labor ends up in other hands. Proverbs says the same: "the sinner's wealth is laid up for the righteous" (Prov 13:22), and Ecclesiastes says God gives the sinner "the business of gathering and collecting, only to give to one who pleases God" (Eccl 2:26). JFB notes that vv16–17 are built as an inward-turning parallel: silver, clothing, clothing, silver.
  • "He builds his house like a moth's, like a booth that a watchman makes" (v18). Both pictures are of something flimsy. JFB: "The moth worm's house is broken whenever the 'raiment' is shaken out, so frail is it." The lexicon lists the moth as a picture of fragility (4:19) but adds that the original word here was probably "spider," which differs in Hebrew by a few letters. That would echo Bildad: the wicked man's trust "is a spider's web. He leans against his house, but it does not stand" (8:14–15). The "booth" is sukkah, the shelter of woven branches that a watchman put up in a vineyard or field at harvest time (Isa 1:8). The lexicon explains it as a temporary shelter, the same word used for the booths of the Feast of Tabernacles (Lev 23:42). It lasted a season and then fell down.
  • "He goes to bed rich, but will do so no more; he opens his eyes, and his wealth is gone" (v19). The Hebrew text reads "but he will not be gathered," which JFB explains as "buried honorably," the opposite of being "gathered to his fathers." The ESV's "will do so no more" follows a reading closer to the Greek, which spells the Hebrew verb slightly differently. Either way the point is how fast it happens. He lies down rich and wakes up poor, as Job did in a single day (1:13–19).
  • "Terrors overtake him like a flood; in the night a whirlwind carries him off" (v20). Bildad said the same: "Terrors frighten him on every side" (18:11). JFB: "Like a sudden violent flood." "Whirlwind" is suphah, a storm wind. The lexicon notes it is a common picture of God's judgment that sweeps people away like chaff (21:18; Prov 1:27; Hos 8:7). It is a different Hebrew word from the "whirlwind" out of which God finally speaks to Job (38:1), though there too a storm brings Job not ruin but God.
  • "The east wind lifts him up and he is gone" (v21). "East wind" is qadim. The lexicon explains it as the hot, violent wind off the desert, the sirocco, which withers crops (Gen 41:6) and brought the locusts on Egypt (Exod 10:13). Here it carries the wicked man off as though he weighed nothing. Job's own children died when "a great wind came across the wilderness" and struck their house (1:19).
  • "It hurls at him without pity" (v22). The ESV footnote explains that "It" could also be "He," meaning God, here and in v23. If the subject is the wind, the storm keeps battering him as he runs. If the subject is God, it is God who throws his judgments at him. The King James takes it that way, "For God shall cast upon him," and JFB explains the thing thrown as "thunderbolts." "Without pity" is the frightening phrase.
  • "It claps its hands at him and hisses at him from its place" (v23). Clapping and hissing were gestures of scorn over someone's ruin. "All who pass along the way clap their hands at you; they hiss and wag their heads at the daughter of Jerusalem" (Lam 2:15). The lexicon defines "hiss" (sharaq) as a sound made in derision over a fallen city or person (Jer 19:8; Zeph 2:15). It also notes that some scholars read the verb as plural, "they hiss," meaning people. Henry reads it that way: "Men shall clap their hands at him." The ESV keeps the singular, so the storm itself seems to jeer. JFB notes that "Job alludes to Bildad's words," likely Bildad's picture of the wicked man driven from his place and forgotten (18:17–21). The chapter ends with the wicked man mocked and gone, and the reader knows that people have done exactly that to Job (30:1, 9–10).
  • Commentators: All three read the chapter as Job's words, but they differ over vv13–23. Henry gives the clearest way through: Job and his friends agreed that God judges the wicked and disagreed only about when, since "reprieves are no pardons." Guzik is close to Henry. He passes on Elmer Smick's observation that Job feels God has denied him justice yet still knows God is just, and he warns against reading vv13–23 as Job admitting guilt, since Job's point is that his case only looks like judgment on the wicked. JFB is the most candid about the tension, saying the passage is "contrary to Job's previous sentiments," and solving it by treating it as Zophar's argument in Job's mouth. The Tyndale notes mention the modern view that vv9–23 are actually Zophar's lost speech, which none of the three commentators adopt. Guzik also passes on Adam Clarke's note that clapping and hissing were ancient signs of public disapproval, which the lexicon's examples bear out (Lam 2:15). Henry's point on conscience (v6) is the most useful pastoral note on the chapter. JFB comments only briefly on vv9, 13 and 21, giving cross-references there rather than notes.

Pointing to Christ

Job swears by the God "who has taken away my right," refuses to confess crimes he did not commit, and holds on to his integrity while his friends treat him as one of the wicked. JFB hears in that line "words unconsciously foreshadowing Jesus Christ," pointing to Isaiah's servant: "By oppression and judgment he was taken away" (Isa 53:8), a verse Philip explained to the Ethiopian as being about Jesus (Acts 8:32–35). But the likeness has limits, and they matter. Job's innocence was real but partial, and in the end he will repent of speaking beyond what he knew (42:3–6). Jesus was wholly innocent, and he did not demand his rights. "When he was reviled, he did not revile in return... but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23). Then the cross did something Job's chapter never imagined: the Righteous One took the portion of the wicked. He was cut off, stripped, and mocked by passers-by who "derided him, wagging their heads" (Matt 27:39), the same gesture of scorn as v23. Job said that the righteous would wear the wicked man's clothing. At the cross the soldiers divided the clothing of the righteous man (John 19:23–24). Job could only hold fast his own righteousness. In Christ, the guilty receive a righteousness that is not their own (2 Cor 5:21), and the heritage of the wicked is exchanged for "an inheritance that is imperishable" (1 Pet 1:4).

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes that Job's oath in vv.2-6 is one of the most remarkable declarations of personal integrity in Scripture. Job swears by God — acknowledging God's reality and sovereignty — while simultaneously charging God with injustice. This is not contradiction but honest faith. Guzik also notes that vv.13-23 deliberately echo the friends' language, turning their own arguments into evidence for Job's innocence.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • Wrestling With God Without Walking Away (v.2) — Job swears by the very God he feels has wronged him, refusing to abandon the relationship even as he argues with Him. Can you bring your hardest complaints to God rather than avoiding Him?
  • Integrity That Refuses a Shortcut (v.5) — Job could end his suffering by falsely confessing sins he didn't commit, but he refuses to trade his integrity for relief. Where might you be tempted to compromise your integrity just to make a painful situation easier?
  • A Clear Conscience as Its Own Defense (v.6) — Job says his heart will not reproach him as long as he lives, resting in the clarity of his own conscience rather than others' opinions. Whose approval are you chasing that you should instead seek from your own conscience before God?
  • The Certain, if Delayed, Fall of the Wicked (vv.13-23) — Job agrees that the wicked ultimately lose everything, even while insisting his own suffering proves nothing about his guilt. Are you able to trust God's justice even when its timing doesn't match your own?
