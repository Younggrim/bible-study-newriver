Summary

Jehoiakim becomes a vassal of Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon but later rebels, bringing raiding bands of Chaldeans, Arameans, Moabites, and Ammonites against Judah as the LORD's judgment for the innocent blood Manasseh had shed (vv. 1-7). His son Jehoiachin reigns only three months before Nebuchadnezzar besieges Jerusalem and he surrenders (vv. 8-12). Nebuchadnezzar strips the temple and palace of their treasures and deports Jehoiachin, the royal family, the officials, the craftsmen and smiths, and some ten thousand leading citizens to Babylon, leaving only the poorest people in the land (vv. 13-16). He installs Jehoiachin's uncle Mattaniah as a puppet king under the new name Zedekiah, who also does evil and eventually rebels against Babylon (vv. 17-20).

Authorship & Background

Author: Unknown, traditionally attributed to Jeremiah or a prophetic school. Originally one book with 1 Kings. Covers approximately 250 years (850-586 BC) from Elijah's translation through the Babylonian exile.
Historical Context: Chapter 24 records Judah's rapid collapse under three kings — Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin, and Zedekiah — spanning roughly the period 605-597 BC. After Josiah's death at Megiddo (609 BC), Judah's brief period of independence ended permanently. The ancient Near East was reshaped by Babylon's victory over Egypt at Carchemish (605 BC), which established Nebuchadnezzar as the dominant power. Jehoiakim initially served Babylon, then foolishly rebelled after three years (v.1), triggering a series of raids and ultimately Babylon's first major siege of Jerusalem (597 BC). This chapter records the first and second Babylonian deportations — the removal of Judah's elite, treasure, and Temple gold — reducing the once-great kingdom to a puppet state under Zedekiah.
The theological significance of this chapter cannot be overstated. The author repeatedly insists that these events happen "at the commandment of the LORD" (v.3) and because of "the sins of Manasseh" (vv.3-4). Babylon is not acting independently — Nebuchadnezzar is God's instrument of judgment, fulfilling what the prophets foretold (v.2). The chapter connects directly to Isaiah's prophecy in 2 Kings 20:17-18: everything Hezekiah showed to Babylon is now being taken by Babylon. It also fulfills the judgment pronounced in 2 Kings 21:12- 15 against Manasseh's abominations. The text makes clear that God "would not pardon" (v.4) — the blood of innocents cried out, and the time of reckoning had arrived. Judah's last kings are all evaluated identically: "he did that which was evil in the sight of the LORD." There is no reform, no repentance, no turning back. The exile is inevitable.
Classification: Historical Narrative
Key Themes: Rebellion against Babylon after three years of vassalage triggering raiders sent by the commandment of the LORD rather than mere political consequence, judgment traced explicitly back to Manasseh's innocent blood which the LORD would not pardon, an eighteen-year-old king surrendering along with his mother and officers rather than resisting, golden vessels Solomon made cut in pieces in the exact fulfillment of a much earlier prophecy, and ten thousand deportees leaving behind only the poorest people in the land
Jehoiakim's Rebellion and God's Judgment (vv.1-7): Nebuchadnezzar came against Judah, and Jehoiakim served as vassal for three years before rebelling — a catastrophic miscalculation. God sent bands of Chaldeans, Syrians, Moabites, and Ammonites to destroy Judah (v.2), "according to the word of the LORD, which he spake by his servants the prophets." The author drives home the theological point twice: this came "at the commandment of the LORD" (v.3) and was due to Manasseh's sins and innocent blood "which the LORD would not pardon" (v.4). Jehoiakim dies (the text notably does not say "slept with his fathers" — Jeremiah 22:18-19 prophesied he would have the burial of a donkey) and Jehoiachin succeeds him. Verse 7 marks the end of Egyptian influence: "the king of Egypt came not again any more out of his land" — Babylon now controls everything from the Nile to the Euphrates.
The First Major Deportation (vv.8-20): Jehoiachin reigns only three months before Nebuchadnezzar's forces besiege Jerusalem (v.10). The young king (eighteen years old) surrenders — "went out to the king of Babylon" (v.12) — along with his mother, servants, princes, and officers. Nebuchadnezzar strips the Temple of its treasures and "cut in pieces all the vessels of gold which Solomon king of Israel had made" (v.13) — the explicit fulfillment of Isaiah's prophecy (2 Kings 20:17). He carries away 10,000 captives: all princes, mighty men, craftsmen, and smiths — "none remained, save the poorest sort of the people of the land" (v.14). This was deliberate policy: remove the leadership, skilled workers, and warriors, leaving only those incapable of resistance. Nebuchadnezzar installs Mattaniah (Zedekiah) as puppet king, changing his name (v.17) — an act of dominion showing Babylon's absolute control. Zedekiah, like his predecessors, does evil — and the chapter ends with his rebellion against Babylon (v.20), setting the stage for the final destruction.

Setting: Judah, about 605–588 BC. Josiah is dead at Megiddo (23:29), and his sons are on the throne one after another as puppets of foreign kings. Pharaoh Neco had removed Jehoahaz and installed his brother Jehoiakim (23:34). Now a new power takes Egypt's place. In 605 BC the Babylonian crown prince Nebuchadnezzar crushed Egypt and the last Assyrian forces at Carchemish on the Euphrates (Jer 46:2), became king the same year, and swept down the coast. This chapter covers three kings in quick succession and the first great deportation to Babylon in 597 BC. Daniel was taken in the first wave (Dan 1:1–6), Ezekiel in the second (Ezek 1:1–2), and Jeremiah preached in Jerusalem through all of it.

Jehoiakim Reigns in Judah (vv1–7)

  • "Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon came up" (v1). His name in Babylonian is Nabu-kudurri-usur, "O Nabu, protect my heir." He ruled 605–562 BC and became the most powerful king of his age. Babylon's empire is often called "Chaldean" (v2), after the people from southern Mesopotamia who led it. The ESV prints no new heading here; the section that began at 23:36 continues.
  • "Jehoiakim became his servant for three years. Then he turned and rebelled" (v1). "Servant" is the standard word for a vassal king who paid tribute. The Tyndale notes explain the timing: in 601 BC Egypt fought Babylon to a bloody standstill near the Egyptian border, and Nebuchadnezzar had to go home and rebuild his army. Jehoiakim took that as his chance. Jeremiah had told him plainly to submit to Babylon (Jer 27:9–11). He chose Egypt instead.
  • "The LORD sent against him bands of the Chaldeans… Syrians… Moabites… Ammonites" (v2). While Nebuchadnezzar regrouped, he sent raiding parties, and Judah's old neighbors joined in. The text does not credit Babylon's strategy. It says "the LORD sent" them, "according to the word of the LORD that he spoke by his servants the prophets." JFB says the raiders were "only an instrument in executing the divine judgment."
  • "Surely this came upon Judah at the command of the LORD… for the sins of Manasseh" (vv3–4). The narrator repeats the reason given in 21:10–15 and 23:26. Judah did not fall by bad luck or bad foreign policy alone. The deepest cause was spiritual.
  • "For the innocent blood that he had shed… and the LORD would not pardon" (v4). Manasseh "filled Jerusalem with innocent blood from one end to another" (21:16). Jewish tradition says Isaiah was among his victims, sawn in two, and many see an allusion to that in Hebrews 11:37. That is tradition, not something Kings records. "Would not pardon" is strong language. Chronicles reports that Manasseh himself repented and was forgiven (2 Chr 33:12–13), so the point here is the nation, not his own soul. A whole generation took up his ways and never let them go. Jehoiakim himself shed innocent blood, murdering the prophet Uriah (Jer 26:20–23).
  • "So Jehoiakim slept with his fathers" (v6). Usually this formula comes with "and was buried with his fathers." Here the burial is missing. Jeremiah had said of Jehoiakim, "With the burial of a donkey he shall be buried, dragged and dumped beyond the gates of Jerusalem" (Jer 22:19; 36:30). Chronicles says Nebuchadnezzar bound him in chains to take him to Babylon (2 Chr 36:6). How these fit together is not certain. He died in December 598 BC, just as the Babylonian army arrived. Josephus says Nebuchadnezzar killed him and threw out his body. JFB takes the missing burial as fulfilling Jeremiah 22:19.
  • "The king of Egypt did not come again out of his land" (v7). This explains why no help came. Judah's kings kept looking south to Egypt, and the prophets kept saying Egypt was a broken reed (18:21; Jer 2:36; Ezek 29:6). Now the whole region "from the Brook of Egypt to the river Euphrates" belongs to Babylon. The Brook of Egypt was the dry riverbed marking the border, probably the Wadi el-Arish in northern Sinai. Those are the very boundaries God once promised to Abraham (Gen 15:18). The land promised to Abraham's children is now in a pagan king's hand.
  • Commentators: Henry introduces the chapter with Jehoiakim "placed there by the king of Egypt" and then "severely chastised for attempting to shake off the yoke" of Babylon. Guzik dates the first invasion to 605 BC, just after Carchemish, and explains the rebellion as Jehoiakim seizing the moment when Nebuchadnezzar had to return home. On his death, Guzik sets 2 Chronicles 36:6 beside Jeremiah 22:19 and passes on one older suggestion that Jehoiakim was bound, then released after promising to submit. JFB treats the missing burial as the fulfilment of Jeremiah's word.

Jehoiachin Reigns in Judah (vv8–9)

  • "Jehoiachin was eighteen years old… he reigned three months" (v8). His name means "the LORD establishes." Jeremiah calls him Coniah, and elsewhere he is Jeconiah (Jer 22:24; 1 Chr 3:16). He reigned from December 598 to March 597 BC, the second of Josiah's line to last only three months.
  • Eighteen or eight? 2 Chronicles 36:9 in most Hebrew manuscripts says "eight." Some Hebrew manuscripts and the Greek translation of Chronicles read "eighteen." Since Jehoiachin had wives when he was taken captive (v15), eighteen is almost certainly right. Guzik follows the common view that a scribe dropped the "ten" in Chronicles. JFB suggests instead that his father made him co-ruler at eight and he reigned alone at eighteen. The first explanation is simpler and more widely held.
  • "His mother's name was Nehushta the daughter of Elnathan" (v8). The queen mother held real power in Judah, which is why she is named with every king and is taken captive with her son (v12, 15). Jeremiah addresses her directly: "Say to the king and the queen mother: Take a lowly seat" (Jer 13:18). Her father may be the Elnathan son of Achbor who fetched Uriah back from Egypt for execution and later urged Jehoiakim not to burn Jeremiah's scroll (Jer 26:22; 36:12, 25), but the identification is not certain.
  • "He did what was evil… according to all that his father had done" (v9). Three months was long enough. JFB says he was untaught by experience and deaf to the prophets, and points to Ezekiel 19:5–7, where one of Judah's kings is pictured as a young lion who learned to devour men.

Jerusalem Captured (vv10–17)

  • "The city was besieged… Nebuchadnezzar… came to the city" (vv10–11). His generals began the siege, and then the king came himself. The Babylonian Chronicle, a cuneiform tablet now in the British Museum, records this event: in his seventh year Nebuchadnezzar "encamped against the city of Judah," took it on the second day of the month Adar (March 16, 597 BC), "seized the king," appointed "a king of his own choice," and took heavy tribute. It is one of the most precisely dated events in the Old Testament.
  • "Jehoiachin… gave himself up… himself and his mother and his servants" (v12). He surrendered rather than fight. That probably saved the city and the temple for another ten years. "In the eighth year of his reign" counts by Nebuchadnezzar's years. The Babylonian Chronicle says seventh. The difference comes from the two calendars counting a king's first partial year differently.
  • "Cut in pieces all the vessels of gold… which Solomon king of Israel had made, as the LORD had foretold" (v13). A century earlier Isaiah had told Hezekiah, after he showed Babylonian envoys his treasures: "All that is in your house… shall be carried to Babylon. Nothing shall be left" (20:17). That word now comes true. The Tyndale notes trace the treasure in three stages: some taken in 605 BC (Dan 1:2), most now, and the rest in 586 BC (25:13–17). Gold that Solomon made for God's glory is hacked into pieces for weight.
  • "He carried away all Jerusalem… 10,000 captives" (v14). "All Jerusalem" means the leading people, not every resident. Verse 16 breaks the number down: 7,000 soldiers and 1,000 craftsmen, plus officials and leading citizens, about 10,000 in all, likely a round figure. Jeremiah 52:28 gives 3,023 for this deportation, probably counting only adult men of Judah, or a separate group. Babylon took the people a nation needs to rebuild or rebel: soldiers, leaders and skilled workers.
  • "The craftsmen and the smiths" (vv14, 16). "Smiths" (masger) comes from a verb meaning "to shut," so it may mean locksmiths and metalworkers, or men who built fortifications. Without them Judah could not make weapons. Philistia once did the same to Israel (1 Sam 13:19).
  • "None remained, except the poorest people of the land" (v14). This is the first time this phrase appears. It comes again in 25:12. Jeremiah's vision of two baskets of figs belongs to this moment: the good figs are the exiles in Babylon, and the rotten ones are Zedekiah and those left in Jerusalem who thought they were the lucky ones (Jer 24).
  • Among the exiles. Ezekiel was taken in this group and dates his whole book by "the exile of King Jehoiachin" (Ezek 1:2). Mordecai's great-grandfather Kish was also carried away with Jeconiah (Esth 2:5–6). Jeremiah wrote these exiles a letter telling them to build houses, plant gardens and "seek the welfare of the city" where they were sent, for they would be there seventy years (Jer 29:4–10).
  • "Mattaniah, Jehoiachin's uncle… changed his name to Zedekiah" (v17). Mattaniah means "gift of the LORD." He was another son of Josiah, full brother of Jehoahaz (compare v18 with 23:31). Nebuchadnezzar renames him, as Neco renamed Jehoiakim, to show who is master. Zedekiah means "the LORD is righteous." Jeremiah, writing in these years, promises a true king from David's line whose name will be "The LORD is our righteousness" (Jer 23:5–6), almost certainly a deliberate contrast with the man who carried that name without living it.
  • Commentators: Guzik notes that Babylonian ration tablets found in Babylon list oil supplied to "Yaukin, king of Judah" and his sons, confirming the record. JFB lists the captives as warriors, "craftsmen and smiths, one thousand," and officials, priests and prophets, and gives the meaning of Zedekiah as "the righteous of God." Henry summarizes Jehoiachin and his court as men "forced to surrender at discretion" and "carried captives to Babylon."

Zedekiah Reigns in Judah (vv18–20)

  • "Zedekiah was twenty-one… he reigned eleven years" (v18). About 597–586 BC. Jeremiah 37–39 gives a close-up of him: weak rather than wicked by nature. He asks Jeremiah for a word from God in secret, keeps him from being killed, and then does what his officials want. "Behold, he is in your hands," he tells them, "for the king can do nothing against you" (Jer 38:5).
  • "He did what was evil… according to all that Jehoiakim had done" (v19). Chronicles adds that he "did not humble himself before Jeremiah the prophet," and broke the oath he swore in God's name to Nebuchadnezzar (2 Chr 36:12–13). Ezekiel, from Babylon, condemns the same broken oath (Ezek 17:15–19). Under Zedekiah the priests and people filled the temple with idols again (Ezek 8).
  • "Because of the anger of the LORD it came to the point… that he cast them out from his presence" (v20). This is a hard sentence. God's anger is described as the cause behind Zedekiah's folly. JFB explains it as God letting the king's policy run its course to ruin. Scripture holds both truths together: Zedekiah freely chose, and God was at work in that choice to bring the judgment he had announced.
  • "And Zedekiah rebelled against the king of Babylon" (v20). He did this around 589 BC, probably trusting a new Pharaoh, Apries (Hophra in Jer 44:30), to back him, as the Tyndale notes suggest. Jeremiah warned him over and over to submit and live (Jer 27:12–15). The next chapter tells what that rebellion cost.
  • Commentators: Henry calls these verses "the preparatives of the next reign (which was the last of all) for the utter ruin of Jerusalem." Guzik lists Zedekiah's sins: refusing Jeremiah, breaking his oath to Babylon and allowing idolatry in the temple. He also ties the coming exile to the seventy years of Sabbath rest the land would finally receive (2 Chr 36:21). JFB says neighboring states pressed Zedekiah to rebel, and he did it in spite of Jeremiah's warnings.

Pointing to Christ

Jeremiah pronounced a curse on Jehoiachin: "Write this man down as childless… for none of his offspring shall succeed in sitting on the throne of David" (Jer 22:30). It looked like the end of David's line. Yet Jehoiachin had sons (1 Chr 3:17), his grandson Zerubbabel led the return from exile, and his name stands in the genealogy of Jesus (Matt 1:11–12). No son of his ever reigned again in Jerusalem. The promise to David survived the curse anyway, in a Son born of David's line who would sit on a throne no Babylon could take. While a puppet king named "The LORD is righteous" ruled in Jerusalem, Jeremiah pointed past him to the true King whose name would be "The LORD is our righteousness" (Jer 23:6). Jesus is that King, and he gives his righteousness to people who have none of their own.

Commentary

  • Enduring Word (David Guzik): enduringword.com Guzik emphasizes the phrase "the LORD would not pardon" as one of the most sobering in Scripture. He notes that this does not contradict God's forgiving nature but demonstrates that corporate sin has corporate consequences that persist even when individuals repent. He also highlights how Babylon's deportation strategy systematically dismantled Judah's capacity for independence — removing precisely those people (leaders, warriors, craftsmen) needed to maintain a functioning state.
  • Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (1871): blueletterbible.org A critical and explanatory commentary on the whole Bible, more concise than Matthew Henry and closer to the Hebrew and Greek, with attention to historical and geographical detail. Public domain.
  • Matthew Henry's Exposition (1710): blueletterbible.org A devotional verse-by-verse commentary in the Puritan tradition, still among the most widely read three centuries on. Public domain.

Reflection

  • God Uses Unexpected Instruments (v.2) — Nations Judah despised — Chaldeans, Syrians, Moabites, Ammonites — became the very tools God used to bring judgment. Could God be speaking to you through a circumstance or person you'd never have chosen to listen to?
  • Some Sin Accumulates Past the Point of Pardon (v.4) — Individual repentance is always available, but the text is blunt that Judah's corporate guilt had reached a point the LORD would not pardon. Are you assuming a patience from God that ongoing, corporate sin may have already exhausted?
  • What You Show Off, You Eventually Lose (v.13) — The very temple treasures Hezekiah once paraded before Babylon's envoys were now carried off by Babylon's army. What are you displaying that wisdom would tell you to quietly guard instead?
  • God Is Present Even in Exile (v.14) — Among the ten thousand captives taken to Babylon was Ezekiel, who would receive some of Scripture's most dramatic visions of God's glory there, not in Jerusalem. If you're in a season of loss or displacement you never chose, could that be exactly where God meets you?
  • Reckless Rebellion Without the Strength to Back It (v.20) — Zedekiah, a mere puppet king, rebelled against Babylon without the resources to survive the consequences. Before you take a stand, are you certain it's courage rather than pride driving you?
